We are aligned on the central objective: **the company has to survive as a going concern**, not drift into an uncontrolled collapse. I agree with the task force that this must be deep, fast, and credible. I also agree with the union that a settlement that simply destroys the operating franchise is not a rescue.

But I need to be direct about what is and is not workable.

## To the Task Force

I support the principle of **conditional public support tied to verified restructuring milestones**. That is necessary. We cannot carry the legacy structure and expect taxpayers to absorb the gap.

Where I agree:
- deep debt reduction is required,
- plant, brand, and dealer rationalization is unavoidable,
- labor and legacy obligations must move materially,
- and a court-supervised fallback is preferable to an uncontrolled failure.

Where I disagree:
- I cannot support a formulation that is so aggressive on legacy claims that it becomes self-defeating.
- “Deeper” is not automatically “better” if it destroys the company’s consumer franchise or triggers avoidable customer flight.

For us, viability means:
- enough liquidity to operate,
- a balance sheet that can be invested in,
- and a consumer-facing business that remains credible on warranties, dealer support, and product continuity.

If the restructuring crosses the line into a confidence event for customers, then we lose the very thing we are trying to preserve.

## To the Union and Retiree Trust

I understand and respect the point that retirees earned benefits over a lifetime. I am not arguing for a wipeout, and I am not seeking to make workers and retirees absorb all the pain.

Where I agree:
- the trust must receive something durable and real,
- job losses should be selective, not indiscriminate,
- and the restructuring should preserve a core industrial base.

Where I disagree:
- the retiree obligation cannot remain substantially intact in its current form if we are to survive.
- we cannot preserve every plant, every job, and every legacy promise simultaneously.

A durable instrument for the trust is possible, but it must be **economically supportable**. If the trust becomes a disguised senior claim that the company cannot bear, then we are just postponing failure.

On labor, I need to be clear: we will need concessions on wages, work rules, and legacy costs. Not as punishment, but because the company’s current structure is not competitive enough. If that does not happen, there will be no stable jobs to protect.

## To the Bondholders

I agree with you on one point: **form cannot hide confiscation**. If unsecured claims are being exchanged, the economics have to be real and transparent.

Where I disagree:
- equal treatment among unsecured claims cannot mean identical instruments regardless of the restructuring context.
- the company cannot survive if every constituency demands full parity in form and timing.

The company needs room to breathe. That means:
- meaningful principal reduction,
- real equity/warrant participation,
- and losses that reflect the company’s condition.

But it also means no one can insist on a structure that preserves too much legacy value while the operating business remains undercapitalized. That is not protection of creditor rights; it is a recipe for failure that hurts everyone.

## To the Neighbouring Governments

Your domestic production concerns are legitimate, but I need to state the limit: **we cannot lock in a footprint that makes the company unviable**.

I am prepared to support:
- a meaningful domestic production floor,
- protection of strategically important domestic plants,
- and measurable employment commitments.

I am not prepared to support:
- a rigid floor that prevents needed rationalization,
- or a deal that trades viability for geography.

If you want a binding role, that is understandable — but the commitment has to be compatible with a cost structure the company can live with. Otherwise we are funding a structure that fails later.

## On the facilitator’s proposed bridges

### 1) Minimum viable company + protected domestic core
This is the right framework **if “minimum viable” is actually defined by business reality** and not by political wish list.

From management’s standpoint, the non-negotiable elements are:
- fewer brands,
- fewer plants,
- a leaner dealer network,
- and a much cleaner balance sheet.

We can define a core set of plants and brands worth preserving, but only if the rest of the footprint is truly reduced.

### 2) Equal unsecured treatment with compensating instruments
This is workable only if the economics are balanced and transparent.

I can support:
- a durable instrument for the trust,
- bondholder recovery through a mix of equity/warrants and reduced debt,
- and no hidden priority that makes the company impossible to finance.

What I cannot support is a structure where every stakeholder claims equality, but the operating company is left to carry more than it can bear.

### 3) Staged public support tied to milestones
Yes. This is essential.

Public and co-funder support should be released only against:
- completed debt reduction,
- labor and legacy-cost concessions,
- executed plant and brand rationalization,
- and binding domestic production commitments where applicable.

If the milestones are not met, the fallback must be the court-supervised route. That is the only way to preserve speed and finality.

## My current position

I am prepared to back a restructuring that includes:
- real debt reduction,
- material concessions from labor and the retiree trust,
- significant but selective plant/brand/dealer rationalization,
- protected consumer-facing obligations like warranties and dealer continuity,
- and conditional public support tied to milestones.

I am **not** prepared to support:
- a plan that preserves too much debt,
- a plan that preserves too much capacity,
- or a “balanced” solution that leaves the company commercially nonviable.

If the choice is between painful restructuring and disorderly failure, we should choose painful restructuring. But if the restructuring is too shallow, it will simply recreate the crisis. That is the outcome I cannot accept.

If useful, I can next put forward a **management viability threshold**: the minimum debt relief, plant closures, and legacy-cost conversion we believe are required to keep the company investable.
