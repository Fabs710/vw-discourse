=== SYNTHESIS ===

1. **Decision context**  
The restructuring is a time-compressed rescue of a collapsing carmaker in which the task force controls financing and therefore has the strongest leverage. The company must achieve a materially leaner balance sheet, deep operational rationalization, and a credible ownership/recovery structure within sixty days, or the fallback is an expedited court-supervised process that can impose terms. The decisive unresolved issue is how to allocate sacrifice among bondholders, labour/retiree obligations, public funders, and domestic-protection interests without destroying the company’s remaining viability.

2. **Stakeholder assembly**  
- **Government task force / lead funder**: highest formal power; controls bridge financing and the court-supervised fallback.  
- **Company management**: operational authority and credibility on viability, but dependent on funding.  
- **Industrial union & retiree health trust**: essential consent holder on labour concessions and legacy benefits; politically and morally strong.  
- **Unsecured bondholders’ committee**: large economic claim, dispersed but collectively significant; can withhold consent and litigate.  
- **Neighbouring governments / co-funders**: can supply additional support, but only if domestic plants/jobs are protected.  

3. **Final positions**  
- **Government task force**: Wants a deep, fast, and final restructuring with real sacrifice from all parties. It will support public funding only if the balance sheet is genuinely fixed, legacy claims are reduced, and plant rationalization is meaningful; otherwise it will impose terms through court-supervised restructuring.  
- **Industrial union & retiree trust**: Will accept concessions, but not a wipeout of the retiree trust or a disguised liquidation of the domestic industrial base. It wants durable, meaningful value for retirees and binding protection for a core set of plants and jobs.  
- **Company management**: Accepts that the footprint and balance sheet must shrink, but insists the plan must preserve customer confidence, dealer continuity, and enough capacity to remain investable. It supports conditional public funding and selective rationalization, but not a structure that makes the company commercially nonviable.  
- **Unsecured bondholders’ committee**: Will negotiate a real restructuring, but rejects being the primary shock absorber while another unsecured claim receives better economics under a different label. It demands equal economic treatment among similarly situated claims and meaningful upside participation.  
- **Neighbouring governments**: Will co-fund only if domestic production and employment are contractually protected, with measurable floors and governance/equity rights proportionate to their contribution. It will not finance a deal that shifts production and jobs away from its jurisdiction.  

4. **Genuine conflicts (structurally incompatible positions)**  
- **Retiree trust protection vs. bondholder parity**: the union wants a durable, meaningful trust recovery; bondholders reject any instrument that is economically superior without offsetting value.  
- **Domestic production floor vs. operational rationalization**: neighbouring governments want binding domestic capacity, while task force and management need enough closures to restore viability.  
- **Depth of sacrifice vs. political acceptability**: the task force wants very deep cuts; labour and co-funders resist a plan that appears to concentrate losses on workers, retirees, or domestic plants.  
- **Consent deal vs. imposed court path**: the task force views court-supervised restructuring as a credible fallback; several stakeholders see it as leverage or as a threat to their recovery expectations.  

5. **Areas of convergence**  
- All parties accept that the current structure is unsustainable and that the company must emerge viable.  
- All accept that sacrifice must be shared and that public funding cannot be open-ended.  
- There is broad support for conditional, milestone-based financing.  
- There is tentative agreement that different instruments can be used, provided their economic value is transparent and comparable.  
- There is some acceptance of a “minimum viable company + protected domestic core” concept, if the core is genuinely compatible with the cost base.  

6. **Key trade-offs for the decision-maker**  
- **Viability vs. preservation**: how much of the footprint, workforce, and legacy obligations can remain without recreating insolvency.  
- **Equal treatment vs. differentiated instruments**: whether similar claims can receive different paper if economics are comparable.  
- **Domestic protection vs. cost reduction**: what minimum domestic production share is politically necessary and operationally sustainable.  
- **Speed vs. completeness**: whether the deal can be finalized quickly without leaving critical disputes unresolved.  
- **Public money vs. private losses**: how much bridge support is justified before imposing terms through court.  
- **Finality vs. consent**: whether a consensual deal is worth extra concessions compared with the certainty of a court-imposed outcome.  

7. **Integrative pathways**  
- **Minimum viable company + protected core**: define a lean operating footprint with a contractually protected domestic core, while closing or consolidating the rest.  
- **Common unsecured pool with offsetting instruments**: treat the retiree trust and bondholders as economically comparable unsecured claimants, but vary instruments only if value is equalized through equity, warrants, or contingent value rights.  
- **Milestone-based funding**: release public and co-funder money only after verified debt reduction, labour concessions, plant closures, and domestic-commitment execution.  
- **Governance-for-capital swap**: give co-funders equity and governance rights in exchange for binding domestic production commitments and additional capital support.  

8. **Open questions**  
- What is the minimum acceptable bondholder recovery range?  
- What is the retiree trust instrument, and what is its economic value relative to bondholder recovery?  
- How many plants/brands/dealers must remain for a viable but politically acceptable footprint?  
- What exact domestic employment or production floor would satisfy co-funders?  
- How much debt reduction is required to keep the company investable?  
- Which milestones are mandatory before any public or co-funder tranche is released?  

9. **Recommended next step**  
Move immediately to a numbers-based term sheet with one integrated package: debt haircut range, retiree trust instrument valuation, bondholder recovery range, plant/brand closure list, domestic production floor, co-funder equity/governance terms, and milestone triggers for financing. If the parties cannot converge on those figures quickly, the task force should prepare to shift to the court-supervised route with a pre-specified fallback framework.

10. **Emergent insights that only appeared through multi-turn interaction**  
- The decisive issue is not simply “who loses,” but **whether the same economic value can be delivered through different instruments without creating hidden priority**; this emerged only after bondholders and the union each rejected form-over-substance solutions.  
- A nominal “domestic protection” deal is not enough; neighbouring governments made clear they need **measurable floors plus governance rights**, which materially changes the rescue architecture.  
- The task force’s strongest leverage is not just financing, but the credibility of a court-supervised fallback; this narrowed the bargaining space by forcing all parties to define what they could live with absent unanimity.  
- Management’s key concern is not only debt load but **customer confidence and dealer continuity**, meaning that a legally deep cut can still fail if it is operationally too abrupt.  
- The real bargaining axis is now a three-way tension among **viability, economic parity among unsecured claimants, and domestic political protection**; none of these can be fully satisfied, so the deal must explicitly choose a hierarchy rather than pretend all can be maximized.
