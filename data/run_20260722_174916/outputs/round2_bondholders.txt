We are prepared to negotiate a real restructuring, but let me be equally clear: **we will not accept a plan that uses unsecured bondholders as the primary shock absorber while a comparable unsecured claim is given better economics under a different label**.

## To the Task Force

We agree on the need for:
- deep restructuring,
- speed,
- and a company that is actually viable.

We do **not** agree that this gives you a blank check to impose whatever loss distribution is politically easiest.

Your repeated emphasis on “viability” is correct in principle, but it cannot become a cover for **confiscatory treatment of unsecured creditors**. If the company needs balance-sheet relief, then relief must be shared, and it must be allocated in a way that respects creditor ranking and economic parity among similarly situated claims.

What we will not accept:
- a near wipe-out of bondholders,
- while the retiree trust receives a structurally superior instrument without equivalent offsetting value to us,
- and then being told this is necessary for “shared sacrifice.”

If the court-supervised route is your fallback, then you should also understand that it is **not a license to subordinate unsecured creditors at will**. We are not threatened by the existence of a process; we are threatened by an outcome that ignores equal treatment.

## To the Union and Retiree Trust

We hear the point that retiree benefits were earned. We are not denying that morally, but legally and economically the trust is still a claimant against an insolvent company.

That means two things:
1. If the trust is treated more favorably than bondholders, there must be a **real, defensible reason**.
2. If there is no such reason, then bondholders must receive **compensating value**.

We will not accept a structure where the trust gets a durable equity-like instrument, preferred economics, or practical priority, and bondholders are told to swallow a steeper haircut because it is politically easier.

Also, let’s be precise: “durable” cannot just mean “different form.” If the trust receives a longer-dated or more flexible instrument that is economically worth more, then our recovery must be adjusted upward accordingly. **Form is not substance.**

We are open to a settlement that gives the trust a meaningful recovery, but not one that does so by quietly expropriating another unsecured class.

## To Management

We acknowledge that the company needs a sharper footprint and a cleaner balance sheet. We do not dispute the need for plant closures, brand reduction, and debt reduction.

But your framing still assumes the bondholders should absorb whatever is left after the politically protected constituencies are addressed. That is not acceptable.

If you want our consent, you need to answer three questions:
1. What is the **actual recovery range** for unsecured bondholders?
2. How does that compare economically to the retiree trust recovery?
3. What is the minimum debt reduction that still leaves the company investable **without over-allocating losses to unsecured creditors**?

We are also not persuaded by appeals to customer confidence as a reason to protect everyone else and dilute creditors. Customer confidence matters, but so does lawful creditor treatment.

## To the Neighbouring Governments

We understand your desire to protect domestic production and employment. But your concerns do not justify loading the cost onto unsecured bondholders.

If you are contributing capital, then yes, you are entitled to a proportionate equity position and governance voice. That is reasonable.

What is not reasonable is using co-funding to insist on a protected domestic footprint **and** then expecting unsecured creditors to fund the rest of the rescue through inferior treatment. Your domestic conditions cannot become another claim senior to ours in disguise.

## On the facilitator’s “equal unsecured treatment with compensating instruments”

This is the only framework so far that points in the right direction, but it has to be real.

Our position is simple:
- If the retiree trust is treated as part of the unsecured pool, then it should receive **no materially better economic recovery** than unsecured bondholders unless the difference is fully offset.
- If the trust gets a durable instrument with higher economic value, then bondholders must get **equivalent value** through equity, warrants, or additional consideration.
- If the restructuring leaves bondholders with only token upside while the trust is protected, we will reject it.

We are open to form:
- equity,
- warrants,
- contingent value rights,
- or a mixed package.

But we are not open to **a disguised priority transfer**.

## On the court-supervised route

Let me be blunt: if the task force pushes this into court, we will not pre-accept a worse outcome just because the process is expedited. A court process may be faster, but it is not automatically better for the public policy argument being made here.

And from our side, the court route is not a threat if the consensual offer is confiscatory. If the alternative is to accept materially worse treatment than another unsecured claimant, we would rather test the legal process.

## Our bottom line

We can support a restructuring only if it meets these conditions:
- **Meaningful recovery for unsecured bondholders**, not token consideration;
- **Equal economic treatment relative to comparable unsecured claims**, including the retiree trust, unless compensated;
- **Real upside participation** through equity or warrants;
- **A balance sheet deep enough to be viable**, but not achieved by concentrating losses unfairly on bondholders;
- **A fast process**, consensual or court-supervised, that does not become a vehicle for political favoritism.

If the proposal remains one where bondholders are asked to take the worst of it while others are wrapped in more favorable instruments, then we will oppose it. We are not here to fund a rescue that wipes out legitimate creditors and calls that fairness.
