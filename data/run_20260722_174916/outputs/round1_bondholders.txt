We agree on one thing only: **this company needs a real restructuring, not a delay tactic**.

But I need to be blunt — from the bondholders’ side, there is a line between a viable compromise and an expropriation dressed up as rescue.

### Where we agree
- **Viability matters.** If the company is dead, everybody recovers less.
- **Speed matters.** We are not interested in endless negotiation while value evaporates.
- **Shared sacrifice is necessary.** We understand that no stakeholder can be made whole.

### Where we disagree
#### 1. Bondholders cannot be the default dumping ground
You keep saying creditors must take “real losses,” and we accept that. But that cannot mean **disproportionate losses for unsecured bondholders while other unsecured claims are treated better**.

That is especially true for the retiree trust. If the trust is another unsecured obligation, then it cannot be elevated above comparable unsecured debt without a serious justification and compensation. We will not accept a structure where:
- bondholders are heavily written down,
- retirees are protected through a superior instrument,
- and we are told this is “balanced.”

If there is a premium for the retiree trust, then the bondholders must receive offsetting value as well. Equal rank means equal treatment, absent a legally defensible reason otherwise.

#### 2. “Shared pain” cannot mean a near wipe-out for financial creditors
A deep restructuring is one thing. A near-total dilution or wipe-out is another. We are not going to sign up for a transaction that gives us token value, vague contingent upside, and the bulk of the restructured equity to other parties.

If you want bondholders to accept a haircut, then the package must include:
- meaningful recovery value now,
- real upside participation,
- and a clear allocation of equity/warrants that reflects the losses imposed.

We are open on form — equity, warrants, contingent instruments — but not on substance. **Form cannot be used to conceal a confiscatory outcome.**

#### 3. Management’s definition of “viability” cannot mean overcutting creditors to preserve every other constituency
I understand the company needs plant rationalization and a leaner structure. But every time “viability” is invoked, it seems to translate into one thing: creditors give more, and others preserve more.

That is not acceptable. If the business plan requires sacrifice from bondholders, then the plan must also show:
- deep enough labor and legacy-cost changes,
- genuine cuts to excess capacity,
- and no preferential treatment of politically favored claims at our expense.

### On the court-supervised route
The visible threat of a court process cuts both ways. It may be expedited, but it is not a blank check for subordination of unsecured creditors. If you want a consensual deal, it has to beat our court alternative in real economic terms.

And let’s be clear: we are not “speculators” blocking a rescue. Many holders are ordinary savers and institutional investors who bought lawful debt. We are legitimate creditors. We will not be talked into accepting inferior treatment while being blamed for the company’s failures.

### What would move us
We could consider an exchange if it includes:
- **a materially meaningful recovery** relative to par,
- **upside participation** through equity or warrants,
- **equal treatment among unsecured claims**, including the retiree trust unless there is compensation for any priority,
- and **credible evidence** that the company’s restructuring is deep enough to be viable without using creditors as the shock absorber for everyone else.

### Bottom line
We are prepared to negotiate, but not to rubber-stamp a plan that socializes pain onto bondholders while protecting other unsecured constituencies. If the task force wants our consent, it needs to present terms that are not materially worse than the treatment given to comparable unsecured claims. Otherwise, we will take our chances in court rather than accept a confiscatory exchange.
