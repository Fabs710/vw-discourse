We agree on the core premise: **the company must emerge viable, not merely delayed in its failure**. And we agree that **every stakeholder has to share sacrifice**. That is not in dispute.

Where I need to be very clear is this: **viability cannot be preserved if the restructuring is either too shallow or too slow**. We are weeks from insolvency. Without a financing commitment tied to a credible restructuring, there is no enterprise left to protect — for workers, retirees, creditors, dealers, or taxpayers.

### On the union and retiree trust
I understand the argument that retiree health obligations represent earned commitments. I am not dismissing that. But I cannot support a structure that treats that legacy obligation as effectively untouchable while the operating company burns through cash.

If the trust claim is left largely intact in its current form, the balance sheet remains unworkable. That would not protect retirees in practice; it would simply postpone a disorderly collapse that ends worse for everyone. So yes, the trust needs a durable solution — but it must be a solution that the company can actually carry.

What I can support is:
- a **real conversion or reduction** of the retiree claim into a sustainable instrument,
- **some form of equity or contingent value** so retirees are not just written off,
- and a structure that preserves the business long enough to honor the rest of the obligations.

What I cannot support is a “balanced” outcome that leaves the legacy burden so large that the company fails anyway.

### On creditors
I also agree that creditors must take genuine losses. They cannot be shielded while labor bears the whole burden. That would be neither fair nor viable politically or economically.

But the reverse is also true: if the bondholders are asked to absorb major cuts, then labor and the retiree trust must also move materially. We need **shared pain on a scale that matches the company’s condition**. Anything less is arithmetic denial.

### On plants, brands, and dealers
Here the union and I partly align: I do **not** support a liquidation by another name. The goal is not to destroy the franchise. But we also cannot preserve every plant, every brand, and every dealer relationship simply because it is painful to close them.

We need:
- **fewer brands**, not just better marketing,
- **fewer plants**, with closures based on utilization and cost,
- and a **leaner dealer network** that reflects actual demand.

Those cuts are not ideological. They are the price of keeping the strongest assets alive.

### On process
Given the timeline, I do not believe we can afford endless consensual bargaining if it means drifting toward failure. I would prefer agreement, but only if it is real and quick. If not, then an expedited court-supervised process is a necessary tool, not a threat. It may be the only way to force finality before the money runs out.

### Bottom line
My position is:

- **Yes** to deep restructuring.
- **Yes** to meaningful losses for all stakeholders.
- **Yes** to protecting the core consumer-facing franchise: warranties, dealers, and the strongest brands.
- **No** to preserving legacy claims and excess capacity to the point that the company remains nonviable.
- **No** to any outcome that amounts to an uncontrolled liquidation.

If the discussion is serious, then we should be negotiating the scale of the cuts, the treatment of the trust, and the allocation of equity based on who is actually taking losses — not pretending the old structure can survive with minor adjustments.
