I’ll respond directly to each party and to the facilitator’s proposed bridges.

## First, the non-negotiable point

We are not financing a patched-up legacy structure.  
If the company emerges with an overburdened balance sheet, too much excess capacity, or claims that simply postpone insolvency, then the public is being asked to underwrite failure. That is not acceptable.

The rescue must produce:
- a genuinely viable post-restructuring company,
- deep and verifiable sacrifices from labour, creditors, and legacy claimants,
- and a fast, final process.

If those conditions are not met, we will use the court-supervised route and impose terms there. That remains the fallback and it is real.

## To the Union and Retiree Trust

I hear the argument that retirees earned their benefits. That is not in dispute. But the trust cannot be treated as sacrosanct if the company cannot carry it. An obligation can be morally understandable and still economically unsustainable.

What I will not accept:
- a solution that preserves the retiree trust largely intact while asking the company and taxpayers to absorb the cost,
- or a structure where labour concessions are deep but the legacy health claim is insulated.

What could be acceptable:
- a durable conversion of the trust claim into a secured or equity-linked instrument,
- some real value for retirees,
- but with economic terms that are supportable by the restructured company.

If the trust insists on near-full preservation, then that is not a restructuring; it is deferral. And we will not fund deferral.

## To the Bondholders

Your point about equal treatment among unsecured claims is noted, but it cannot be used as a veto over the restructuring architecture.

We are not prepared to accept a plan where unsecured bondholders are the only class forced to absorb the whole adjustment while other legacy claims are protected in substance. Nor are we prepared to let formal rank obscure economic reality.

However, let me be equally clear in the other direction: bondholders should not expect a better outcome by stalling. If the company goes through the court-supervised route, the goal will still be a deep restructuring, not a creditor-friendly escape from sacrifice.

What we need is:
- real principal reduction,
- meaningful equity or warrant participation,
- and a recovery level that reflects the company’s condition, not the aspirational preferences of creditors.

If your acceptable range is only one that leaves the balance sheet too heavy, then you are not negotiating a rescue.

## To Management

I appreciate the candor on plant rationalization and the need for deeper balance-sheet repair. But I want to be explicit: “viability” is not a slogan that justifies preserving management’s preferred footprint while shifting pain elsewhere.

You need to come back with:
- a sharper plant and brand reduction plan,
- a clear debt reduction target,
- and a credible explanation of why the post-deal company will not need another rescue.

If you cannot define the minimum viable balance sheet and footprint, then you are not yet offering a serious restructuring.

## To the Neighbouring Governments

We understand your domestic political concerns. But co-funding cannot become a mechanism to freeze an uneconomic footprint in place. If your money comes with a hard domestic production floor that prevents necessary rationalization, that conflicts with viability.

We can consider binding commitments on:
- a meaningful domestic production presence,
- protection of key strategic plants,
- and measurable employment support,

but not if those conditions prevent the company from becoming lean enough to survive. Domestic protection is not a substitute for restructuring depth.

Also: if you are contributing materially, an equity stake and governance voice are reasonable. That part is not controversial.

## On the facilitator’s proposed compromise blocks

### 1) “Minimum viable company + protected domestic core”
This is directionally useful, but only if the “core” is genuinely compatible with a viable capital structure and cost base. If “protected domestic core” becomes a disguised promise to keep too much capacity open, we will reject it.

### 2) “Equal unsecured treatment with compensating instruments”
This is closer to what we can work with, but only if the numbers are real.

A retiree trust instrument can be different in form, but not a hidden priority that leaves bondholders economically shortchanged beyond what the restructuring requires. Any form-based distinction must be matched by equivalent value.

### 3) “Staged public support tied to milestones”
This is the right principle. Public money must be conditional, limited, and released only against verified restructuring actions.

But I want to emphasize: milestones are not a substitute for depth. We will not keep tranches flowing just because parties are still talking. If the essential concessions are not locked in, financing stops.

## Bottom line from the task force

We are prepared to support:
- a deep debt restructuring,
- real labour and legacy-cost concessions,
- rationalization of plants, brands, and dealers,
- and a consensual deal if it is fast and credible.

We are not prepared to support:
- an open-ended subsidy,
- a plan that preserves too much debt,
- a plan that leaves the retiree trust effectively untouched,
- or a “domestic protection” scheme that makes the company unviable.

Bring back something that is materially deeper, materially faster, and materially more credible, or we will move to the court-supervised path.
