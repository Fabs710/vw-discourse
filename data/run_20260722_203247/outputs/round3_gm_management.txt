We are aligned on the basic frame: **the company cannot survive in its present form**, and a durable restructuring must reduce the legacy burden, shrink the footprint, and reset the balance sheet. Where we part company is on what counts as “durable” and what sacrifices are actually financeable without destroying the enterprise we are trying to save.

## To the Task Force

We accept that viability comes first and that the court-supervised route is a credible fallback. We also accept that the old burden structure cannot simply be repackaged and left in place.

But we need to be explicit: **a replacement instrument for the retiree obligation is only acceptable if it is bounded and financeable**. If it is too senior, too rich, or too cash-draining, it will recreate insolvency under another name. That would not be a rescue; it would be a delayed failure.

So, yes to:
- a material haircut to the legacy retiree obligation,
- a hybrid replacement using preferred equity and/or limited secured or quasi-secured paper,
- contingent upside if the company performs,
- substantial plant, brand, and dealer rationalization,
- meaningful labour concessions,
- and material unsecured creditor losses.

No to:
- any structure that makes the restructured company unfinanceable,
- any open-ended public subsidy,
- and any attempt to preserve pre-crisis recovery expectations for anyone.

## To the Union and Retiree Trust

We recognize the obligation to retirees is real and cannot be treated as disposable. We also recognize the specialist’s point that a pure symbolic promise is worthless.

But I have to be candid: **the trust cannot be protected in a way that crowds out the rest of the capital structure**. If the trust receives a priority-like instrument, the rest of the package must adjust accordingly, and that means bondholders take deeper haircuts and the operating company must be leaner still.

What we cannot accept is:
- preserving the full legacy claim,
- or giving the trust an instrument that is effectively senior enough to threaten the company’s survival.

We are open to a durable substitute, but not to an instrument that turns the restructuring into another insolvency trap.

## To the Bondholders

Your claims are contractual, and we are not dismissing that. Many holders are ordinary savers, and we understand why you resist being the residual dumping ground.

But the arithmetic is unavoidable: **this company cannot emerge with a lightly impaired unsecured stack**. There will be meaningful haircuts. There has to be.

At the same time, I want to be equally clear: if the retiree trust receives real value, bondholders will also receive a real recovery — likely a mix of equity and warrants. What you should not expect is parity with the trust, because the trust is not simply another unsecured trade claim; it is part of the legacy labour settlement that must be restructured to keep the business alive.

We can discuss the shape of your recovery. We cannot discuss near-full recovery.

## To the Neighbouring Governments

We hear you on domestic plants, employment, and the need for a proportionate equity stake if you co-fund. Those concerns are legitimate.

But we cannot lock in capacity that the market cannot support. **Domestic production commitments must be compatible with a smaller, competitive company**. If a plant is viable, we want it in the retained core. If it is not, we will not preserve it simply because the politics are difficult.

And on ownership: any equity must reflect actual contribution and risk. We can support proportional co-funder equity, but not governance rights that amount to a veto over necessary restructuring.

## Where management stands

We support a package with these elements:

1. **A materially smaller operating footprint**
   - fewer plants, fewer redundant brands, fewer dealer points.

2. **Real labour concessions**
   - wages, work rules, staffing flexibility, and productivity improvements.

3. **A reduced and converted retiree obligation**
   - preferably a hybrid of preferred equity plus limited secured or quasi-secured value,
   - with contingent upside,
   - but no open-ended cash burden.

4. **Meaningful unsecured creditor haircuts**
   - enough to restore solvency and investment viability.

5. **Bridge financing with finality**
   - no open-ended support,
   - and a court route if the parties cannot close.

## On the operating footprint

The specialist’s framework is the right one. We need to retain only the plants that are structurally competitive after the reset:
- high utilization potential,
- modern tooling,
- good logistics,
- product relevance,
- and the ability to support the future mix.

What we cannot do is keep underloaded, duplicative capacity open for political reasons and then pretend the company is fixed. It would not be.

At the same time, we are **not** looking to destroy the strongest brands or the healthiest dealer network. Our goal is to preserve the strongest parts of the franchise consistent with viability. That means hard choices, but not blind liquidation by another name.

## What we need next

If we are going to move this from principle to settlement, we need a draft term sheet with actual numbers on:
- plant closures and retained sites,
- brand rationalization,
- dealer reductions,
- labour cost savings,
- the haircut to the retiree obligation,
- the size and form of the replacement instrument,
- unsecured creditor recovery range,
- co-funding amount,
- and equity allocation.

## Bottom line

We are prepared to support a **smaller, viable company** with:
- real labour sacrifice,
- real creditor sacrifice,
- a real conversion of the retiree obligation,
- and a footprint that matches the market.

We will not support:
- a structure that recreates insolvency,
- a protected trust instrument that makes the rescue unfinanceable,
- or a political settlement that preserves excess capacity and destroys the going concern we are trying to save.

If you want a deal, bring a financeable one. If not, we should move quickly to the court-supervised path rather than burn through the last financing while arguing over unworkable protections.
