Below is a **plant-network and footprint view** of what a viable rescue would usually require in this kind of collapse. I’ll stay within operations and restructuring economics.

## 1) The operating logic
A rescuable footprint is usually built around four rules:

1. **Concentrate volume** into fewer plants.
2. **Keep only plants with strong utilization potential, low fixed cost per unit, and low retooling burden.**
3. **Close or consolidate duplicate, underutilized, or structurally high-cost sites.**
4. **Reduce the dealer and brand complexity that forces small runs and poor plant loading.**

For a distressed legacy carmaker, the biggest value leakage is almost always:
- too many plants for the demand base,
- too many platforms/brands,
- too much fixed labor and overhead,
- and too little product volume per site.

## 2) What a viable footprint typically looks like
A viable post-restructuring footprint is usually **30–50% smaller in output capacity** than the pre-crisis network, sometimes more if the company has serious brand overlap and weak product demand.

### Typical target characteristics
- **Plant utilization:** ideally above **70%** at retained assembly sites, with a path to **80%+** as volume normalizes.
- **Assembly footprint:** fewer plants, but each assigned a clear product family or platform.
- **Supplier base:** fewer logistics lanes, shorter hauling distances, lower complexity.
- **Brand portfolio:** reduced to the strongest, most defensible nameplates.
- **Dealer network:** trimmed to match lower unit volume and improved sales per outlet.

## 3) How to judge plants: the practical scorecard
If I were scoring the network, I would use these metrics:

### A. Utilization and volume concentration
- Current utilization
- Expected post-restructuring utilization
- Ability to absorb model migration from closed plants

### B. Fixed cost per unit
- Labor and overhead spread across units
- Plant-support cost
- Maintenance and energy burden

### C. Product fit
- Whether the site can build current/future platforms without major retooling
- Flexibility across models
- Compatibility with future powertrain mix

### D. Logistics
- Distance to suppliers and markets
- Rail/truck efficiency
- Inventory and inbound freight burden

### E. Labor productivity
- Units per labor hour
- Absenteeism, overtime dependence, shift flexibility
- Historical quality performance

### F. Capital intensity
- Reinvestment needed to keep the plant competitive
- Whether the site needs a major retool just to stay open

## 4) Which plants are operationally strongest
Without a plant list, I can only speak in general terms. In a network like this, the **strongest plants** are usually the ones that have most of the following:

- already high utilization,
- modern or recently upgraded equipment,
- flexible product architecture,
- good logistics access,
- lower legacy labor drag,
- and a product program that can survive in a smaller company.

The weakest plants are usually:
- single-model sites with low volume,
- older plants with high maintenance needs,
- locations requiring expensive retooling,
- plants with poor logistics or fragmented supplier support,
- and facilities kept open mainly for political reasons rather than economics.

## 5) What closures are economically necessary
Closures are economically necessary when a plant is:
- structurally underutilized,
- not convertible to future products at reasonable capex,
- duplicative of another site with better economics,
- or so burdened by fixed cost that it destroys margin even after the rescue.

Those closures are not “punishment”; they are the consequence of fitting a smaller demand base.

### Signs a closure is probably necessary
- utilization below roughly **50–60%** with no credible recovery path,
- high retooling cost relative to future volume,
- poor location vs. supplier/customer network,
- repeated quality or labor flexibility problems,
- no strategic product role in the surviving portfolio.

## 6) What can be saved
Plants are usually worth saving if they:
- can be loaded to strong utilization quickly,
- support key products or platforms,
- need limited incremental capex,
- and are geographically/logistically efficient.

In a rescue, the right approach is often:
- keep the best few assembly plants,
- consolidate stamping/powertrain/logistics where possible,
- and transfer product volume from weaker sites into the stronger ones.

## 7) Labor and supply-chain impact
A viable footprint means real pain downstream.

### Employment
- Assembly plant closures trigger not only direct job losses but also supplier and logistics losses.
- A rough planning rule is that **1 direct assembly job can support several supplier/logistics jobs** depending on the local cluster.
- So the total employment impact is often **multiples of the plant headcount reduction**.

### Suppliers
- Tier 1 and Tier 2 suppliers lose volume quickly when plants shut.
- The weakest suppliers tend to be those tied to one platform or one plant.
- The rescue often needs a supplier support/transition plan, but that does not eliminate the volume shock.

### Dealers
- Dealer reductions are often necessary because small sales volumes cannot support the old retail footprint.
- Dealer cuts are usually politically difficult but operationally necessary if the company is shrinking.

## 8) The core tradeoff
The operating tradeoff is simple:

- **Keep more plants** → more jobs and political acceptability, but worse utilization, higher fixed cost, and weaker long-term viability.
- **Close more plants** → better economics and survivability, but larger immediate job and supplier shock.

For a collapsing automaker, viability usually wins over preservation. If the footprint is not cut deeply enough, the company just delays failure.

## 9) My restructuring read
A credible rescue would usually need:
- **fewer assembly plants**
- **one or two core platforms per retained plant**
- **major dealer rationalization**
- **supplier consolidation**
- **higher labor flexibility**
- **and a product plan matched to much lower volumes**

In practical terms, that usually means retaining the **strongest 3–5 plants** in a North American legacy network of this size, not trying to preserve the whole map.
