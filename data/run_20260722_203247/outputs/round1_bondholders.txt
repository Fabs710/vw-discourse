We agree on one thing: this company cannot be kept alive on an unrestructured balance sheet. Delay is destroying value, and a cosmetic plan that simply pushes losses onto creditors is not acceptable.

But let me be equally clear about where we stand.

### Where we agree
- **There must be a real operational reset.** If plants, brands, and dealer capacity are excess to the market, they have to be cut.
- **The public cannot be the permanent plug for private losses.** We do not expect taxpayers to absorb everything.
- **The retiree trust and labour costs have to be addressed somehow.** No one here should pretend the current structure survives intact.

### Where we disagree
#### 1) “Meaningful creditor sacrifice” cannot mean a near wipeout
Unsecured bondholders are being asked to bear very large losses already. We are not a secured lender sitting on collateral, and we are not the equity that took upside risk. We are legitimate contractual creditors, including many ordinary savers, and we will not accept treatment that is materially worse than comparable unsecured claims without compensation.

If the task force and management want deep haircuts, then the package must still leave unsecured creditors with **real recovery value** — not token paper and a story about fairness.

#### 2) The retiree trust cannot be given a privileged rescue while unsecured creditors are diluted
I want to be direct here: we will not accept a structure where the retiree health trust is protected more generously than the bondholders while we are told to swallow the bulk of the write-down.

If the trust receives a more durable instrument — equity, a secured-style claim, or any other enhanced treatment — then unsecured bondholders must receive **comparable upside participation**, not just a nominal haircut and exit. Equal standing claims should not be turned into a hierarchy that rewards politically sensitive constituencies and punishes dispersed creditors.

#### 3) “Fairness” cannot be one-way
The union keeps invoking earned obligations, and I do not dismiss that history. But our claim is earned too. These bonds were purchased on legal terms, and many holders are retirees themselves. We reject any framing that casts bondholders as speculators blocking rescue. That is simply not true, and it is not a basis for confiscation.

### What we will require
If there is to be a consensual deal, it needs to include:
- **material recovery for unsecured creditors**, not symbolic crumbs;
- **equal treatment within the unsecured class**, especially relative to the retiree trust;
- **upside participation**, such as equity or warrants, if we are forced to take a haircut;
- **a clearly viable operating company**, with cuts driven by economics, not politics;
- and a timetable that does not use delay to pressure creditors into accepting worse terms.

### On process
We are not threatening collapse for its own sake. But we are also not volunteering to be the sole financing source for everyone else’s losses. If the proposal effectively says “bondholders bear the deepest sacrifice while other unsecured claims are protected and the public funds the gap,” then we will not consent.

And if the court-supervised route can impose a worse outcome on us, that is not a reason to accept confiscatory terms now. It is a reason for everyone to bring a fairer proposal to the table.

So our position is simple:
- yes to restructuring,
- yes to shared sacrifice,
- no to disproportionate treatment of unsecured bondholders,
- no to a retiree-trust preference masquerading as necessity,
- and no to a deal that leaves us with little more than a nominal claim while others preserve real value.
