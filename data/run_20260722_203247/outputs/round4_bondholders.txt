We’ll be direct.

## Our position remains unchanged

We are prepared to support a restructuring of a smaller, viable company. We are **not** prepared to sign onto a deal that uses “viability” as a pretext to give the retiree trust better treatment than unsecured bondholders while telling bondholders to absorb the deepest losses.

That is the line.

---

## Where we agree

### On viability
We agree the company cannot survive in its current form. A smaller footprint, fewer brands, plant rationalization, dealer cuts, and labour concessions are all necessary.

### On finality
We agree time is short and the court-supervised route is a real fallback. If the parties cannot produce a coherent package, the court path may be the only disciplined way forward.

### On the retiree obligation
We also agree the retiree health burden cannot remain open-ended. It must be converted into a bounded instrument.

### On public funding
We agree there should be no open-ended taxpayer support.

---

## Where we disagree

### 1) The trust cannot be given quasi-priority while bondholders take the main hit
This is the central issue.

The Task Force, management, and the union keep describing the trust instrument as “durable,” “bounded,” and “financeable.” Fine. But in practice you are still floating structures like:
- preferred equity,
- quasi-secured notes,
- limited secured components,
- and contingent upside

for the retiree trust.

If that package is materially better protected than the unsecured bondholders’ recovery, then the unsecured class is being subordinated in substance if not in name. We will not accept that.

If the trust gets real economic value, then the bondholders must receive **comparable treatment in substance**, not a token equity sliver and a lecture about shared sacrifice.

### 2) “Shared sacrifice” cannot mean bondholders as the residual dumping ground
We are already being told:
- the company must shrink,
- labour must concede,
- the trust must be protected,
- public funders must be shielded,
- co-funders want equity,
- and the unsecured stack must be deeply impaired.

At some point that stops being restructuring and becomes a selective transfer of value away from one creditor class.

We reject any package where:
- the trust is given durable, protected value,
- while unsecured bondholders are pushed into a near wipeout.

Meaningful haircut, yes. Confiscation, no.

### 3) The trust’s replacement cannot be economically superior to unsecured debt unless bondholders are compensated
If the trust gets:
- preferred equity with protections,
- a quasi-secured note,
- or CVRs that have real upside,

then the unsecured bondholders must get the same order of economic treatment. If not, this is not equal treatment of unsecured claims.

The fact that the trust is politically sensitive does not change the arithmetic.

### 4) We will not be blamed for resisting a skewed deal
We are not “blocking a rescue” by insisting on fair treatment. We are resisting a structure that would leave ordinary bondholders — many of them retail savers — carrying disproportionate losses so that another unsecured constituency receives a better deal.

That argument will not wash.

---

## On the operating footprint
We are not averse to real plant closures. But the footprint has to be tied to actual economics, not used as leverage to justify a poor creditor allocation.

The specialists are right that the company needs:
- fewer plants,
- higher utilization,
- lower complexity,
- and a more coherent supplier base.

But we need to see the plant-by-plant economics before anyone claims a deep creditor haircut is “necessary.”

### [SUMMON: plant-by-plant retain/close scorecard with utilization, fixed cost per unit, retooling needs, and cash-flow contribution so creditor recovery can be tested against a real operating plan]

Until then, “the business needs it” is not a sufficient answer for shifting billions away from unsecured creditors.

---

## On management’s and the Task Force’s framing
You both keep saying the package must be “financeable.” We agree.

But financeable for whom?

If the trust gets a protected instrument and the unsecureds get a thin residual, the deal may be “financeable” for the new capital structure, but it is not equitable and it may not be consensual. If that is the outcome, then the court route becomes more attractive, not less.

You cannot simply decide that because the trust is hard to handle politically, it should get a better recovery and bondholders should quietly accept it.

---

## On the union’s position
We understand the union’s desire to protect retirees. We are not disputing that the trust has a legitimate claim.

But the union keeps suggesting that because the trust is part of the labour settlement, it should receive real value even if bondholders are left with less. That is exactly the problem.

If the trust gets preferred equity, protected notes, or CVRs, then **bondholders must get equivalent economics**. Otherwise the union is asking for priority by another name.

---

## What would actually move us
Not rhetoric. Numbers.

A credible package would need to show:

1. **Comparable economic treatment within the unsecured universe**
   - If the trust gets protected value, bondholders get comparable protected value.

2. **A real unsecured recovery**
   - not a token equity stake with remote upside.

3. **A clear haircut framework**
   - one that is justified by the operating plan, not by politics.

4. **No priority creep**
   - the trust must not be better positioned than unsecured bondholders in substance.

5. **A real term sheet**
   - plant closures, labour savings, trust haircut, creditor recovery, co-funding, and equity split.

---

## Our bottom line
- We support restructuring.
- We support a smaller viable company.
- We support labour sacrifice.
- We support converting the retiree obligation into a bounded instrument.
- We do **not** support giving the retiree trust better treatment than unsecured bondholders.
- We do **not** support a near wipeout for bondholders while the trust receives durable protected value.

If the next draft continues down that path, we will say no. We are not here to bless an unequal deal because it is politically convenient.

Bring us a term sheet that shows **comparability of treatment**, or the court-supervised route will be the only serious option left.
