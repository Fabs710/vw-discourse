## 1. Decision context
The company is insolvent in substance and faces imminent collapse without external support. The legislature refused a dedicated bailout, so the executive’s task force is controlling bridge financing and has imposed a 60-day deadline for a viable restructuring plan. The parties must decide how to allocate losses across labour, retirees, unsecured creditors, public funders, and neighbouring governments; how much capacity to shed; what form the retiree health obligation takes; what recovery bondholders receive; and whether the deal closes consensually or through a court-supervised process.

## 2. Stakeholder assembly
- **Government Task Force (lead funder/controller of rescue financing)** — highest leverage; sets viability constraints and can force court route.
- **Company Management (new leadership)** — operational designer; must make the business financeable.
- **Industrial Union & Retiree Health Trust** — high legitimacy and blocking power over labour/retiree consent.
- **Unsecured Bondholders’ Committee** — dispersed but material creditor class with meaningful consent leverage.
- **Neighbouring Governments (co-funders)** — lower than federal task force, but can materially affect financing and domestic production commitments.
- **Expert: labour/pension restructuring special** — advisory role; high analytical value on trust structure.
- **Expert: industrial restructuring/operations spec** — advisory role; high analytical value on footprint feasibility.
- **Moderator/facilitator** — summarises and pressures convergence.

## 3. Final positions

### Government Task Force
The task force supports a materially smaller, viable company with deep legacy burden cuts, substantial labour concessions, and meaningful bondholder haircuts. It will accept a bounded retiree trust replacement only if it is financeable, subordinate enough not to recreate insolvency, and paired with no open-ended public subsidy or veto rights over restructuring.

### Company Management
Management agrees the company must shrink materially and can support plant rationalisation, dealer cuts, labour flexibility, and a bounded hybrid trust instrument. It rejects any trust structure that is effectively senior enough to crowd out the operating business, and it insists that the capital structure must be anchored in actual plant-by-plant economics.

### Industrial Union & Retiree Health Trust
The union accepts that the old footprint and legacy obligation cannot remain intact, but insists retirees receive a durable, real-value replacement rather than a hollow substitute. It wants a hybrid package—preferred equity, limited secured or quasi-secured paper, and/or CVRs—with anti-dilution and upside participation, and it opposes a deal where workers/retirees absorb the deepest cuts while bondholders remain relatively protected.

### Unsecured Bondholders’ Committee
Bondholders accept restructuring and meaningful haircuts, but reject any package that gives the retiree trust better substantive treatment than unsecured creditors. They demand comparability of treatment within the unsecured layer and warn they will resist a near-wipeout paired with a protected trust instrument.

### Neighbouring Governments
The neighbouring governments are willing to co-fund, but only if the rescue preserves a meaningful domestic industrial core through binding production commitments, employment protections tied to volume, and proportionate equity/governance rights. They oppose a restructuring that uses their capital to hollow out domestic capacity or gives them mere passive status.

### Expert: labour and pension restructuring special
The expert recommends a bounded replacement package for the retiree obligation: material haircut to the legacy claim plus a hybrid instrument (preferred equity, subordinated note, CVRs, or split package). The key is to give retirees durable value without creating a de facto senior claim that breaks financeability or triggers a court fight.

### Expert: industrial restructuring/operations spec
The expert’s position is that the firm likely needs a 30–50% smaller footprint, with retained plants operating above roughly 70% utilisation and closures focused on underutilised, high-fixed-cost, low-productivity, or politically convenient sites. A viable rescue requires fewer plants, fewer brands, dealer rationalisation, and a supply-chain simplification anchored in economics.

## 4. Genuine conflicts (structurally incompatible positions)
1. **Trust protection vs. bondholder parity**  
   The union wants a protected, durable retiree instrument; bondholders refuse any structure that gives the trust better substantive economics than unsecured creditors. Both cannot be fully satisfied if the trust is materially preferred.

2. **Domestic plant protection vs. economic pruning**  
   Neighbouring governments want binding domestic commitments; management, task force, and the operations expert insist on plant-by-plant economic closures where necessary. Some domestic sites may be politically important but economically weak.

3. **Size of the trust replacement vs. financeability**  
   The union wants real value and anti-dilution protections; the task force and management will not fund a structure that recreates insolvency. A richer trust package mechanically worsens creditor and operating-company capacity.

4. **Bondholder recovery vs. total loss allocation**  
   Bondholders want a real recovery floor; labour and the task force want broad sacrifice. If the trust gets meaningful value and co-funders also receive equity, bondholder recovery compresses.

5. **Consensual deal vs. court-supervised fallback**  
   Several parties prefer a negotiated outcome, but their positions are still far enough apart that the court route remains credible and may become necessary if numbers are not produced quickly.

## 5. Areas of convergence
- The company must emerge **smaller and viable**.
- The current legacy burden structure cannot remain intact.
- **Plant/brand/dealer rationalisation** is unavoidable.
- **Labour concessions** are required.
- The **retiree obligation must be converted into a bounded instrument**.
- **Bondholders will take meaningful haircuts**.
- **Public funding cannot be open-ended**.
- A **court-supervised fallback** is acceptable if consensus fails.
- All sides now recognise the need to move from principles to **a term sheet with actual numbers**.

## 6. Key trade-offs for the decision-maker
- **How much retiree value is enough to secure labour consent without making the capital structure unfinanceable?**
- **How deep must bondholder haircuts be to restore viability, while still avoiding a revolt from dispersed retail creditors?**
- **Which plants are truly indispensable to the post-restructuring business, and which closures are economically unavoidable despite political costs?**
- **How much domestic production can be preserved without forcing excess capacity or control rights that block restructuring?**
- **Whether to prioritise a consensual closing deal or use the court-supervised process to impose discipline if bargaining stalls.**

## 7. Integrative pathways
1. **Hybrid retiree package**
   - Material haircut to the legacy obligation.
   - Replacement via preferred equity plus limited subordinated/quasi-secured note and/or CVRs.
   - Hard cap on annual cash and total burden.
   - Anti-dilution protections, but no senior-like priority.

2. **Defined bondholder recovery package**
   - Equity and warrants, possibly a modest note component.
   - Enough value to make the unsecured class appear fairly treated relative to the trust.
   - No token recovery.

3. **Plant-by-plant economic pruning with domestic carve-outs**
   - Retain only sites that clear utilisation/fixed-cost/retooling hurdles.
   - Preserve a domestic core where economically defensible.
   - Tie retained domestic operations to binding production and employment commitments.

4. **Conditional co-funding**
   - Neighbouring governments receive proportionate equity/governance only for the reduced domestic footprint.
   - No veto over operational restructuring.
   - Public support exits on a clear timetable.

5. **Fallback discipline**
   - If the term sheet cannot meet viability thresholds, move to the court-supervised route with the same economic constraints.

## 8. Open questions
- What is the **maximum annual cash burden and total present value** the task force will tolerate for the retiree instrument?
- What **specific bondholder recovery range** would count as “comparable enough” to keep negotiations alive?
- Which **plants, brands, and dealer sites** are retained, closed, or consolidated?
- What is the **minimum viable domestic production core** acceptable to co-funders?
- How are **equity and governance** allocated among public funders, co-funders, the trust, and bondholders?
- Can the parties agree on a **single integrated term sheet** before the 60-day deadline?

## 9. Recommended next step
Proceed immediately to a **single bracketed term sheet** with quantified options for:
- plant retain/close/consolidate decisions,
- labour savings target,
- retiree trust haircut and replacement instrument,
- bondholder recovery range,
- public and co-funder capital amounts,
- equity/governance split,
- and a clear consensual-vs.-court fallback trigger.

## 10. Emergent insights that only appeared through multi-turn interaction
1. **The real fault line is not “save vs. liquidate,” but “how to treat the trust relative to unsecured bondholders.”**  
   Early rounds showed broad agreement on restructuring; later rounds revealed that the deal’s feasibility turns on whether the trust is seen as substantively senior.

2. **“Durable” became a contested word.**  
   The union used it to mean real economic value and protection from erosion; the task force and bondholders treated it as a warning sign for priority creep. That semantic gap was not visible in a single prompt.

3. **Political and economic logics diverged most sharply at the plant level.**  
   The operations expert made clear that some closures may be economically necessary even where governments want protection. The parties moved from abstract “smaller footprint” language to the need for plant-by-plant scoring.

4. **All parties converged on the need for boundedness, but not on where the cap should sit.**  
   This was especially true for the retiree obligation: everyone accepted it cannot remain open-ended, yet no one agreed on the maximum priority or cash burden.

5. **The court-supervised route became less of a threat and more of a coordination device.**  
   As positions hardened, the court path emerged as a credible way to impose consistency if the term sheet cannot be made internally coherent.

6. **The key negotiation variable shifted from principles to comparable economics.**  
   By round 4, parties were no longer debating whether the trust and bondholders both sacrifice; they were debating whether the sacrifice is economically comparable in substance. That is a more precise and more difficult problem than the initial framing suggested.
