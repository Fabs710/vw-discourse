From a labour and pension restructuring perspective, the core issue is how to convert the retiree health obligation from an open-ended legacy liability into a **durable, bounded, enforceable value package** that is credible to the union and retirees, but still financeable for a distressed automaker.

## Bottom-line assessment

A workable structure usually has to do three things at once:

1. **Cap the company’s future cash burden**
   - the legacy health promise cannot remain a straight unsecured operating obligation if the business is to survive.

2. **Give the retiree trust real, enforceable value**
   - not a symbolic promise, but an instrument with priority, collateral, or equity upside.

3. **Avoid over-securing the trust to the point that bondholders and new money are structurally excluded**
   - if the trust is given too much priority, the rest of the capital stack may become unfinanceable.

So the right answer is usually **not** “keep the full retiree promise intact” and also **not** “wipe it out.” It is a **conversion**.

---

## The most credible instrument forms

### 1) Preferred equity in the reorganized company
This is often the cleanest equity-based substitute.

**Why it works**
- It gives the trust a real economic stake.
- It does not create a fixed cash drain on day one.
- It aligns the trust with enterprise recovery.

**Key design point**
- It should usually be **nonvoting or limited-voting preferred**, with:
  - a fixed liquidation preference,
  - cumulative dividend rights if feasible,
  - conversion or participation rights if the company outperforms.

**Main risk**
- If the enterprise remains fragile, preferred equity may have uncertain near-term value.
- If the trust gets only equity with no downside protection, retirees may view it as too speculative.

### 2) Senior secured note or payment-in-kind security
This gives stronger protection than equity.

**Why it works**
- It provides a defined claim with enforcement rights.
- It can be structured with deferred cash payments or PIK accretion, reducing immediate cash pressure.
- It is more legible to retirees than pure equity.

**Main risk**
- If the instrument is too senior, it may crowd out the company’s ability to raise rescue financing.
- It can also function like hidden debt, which may simply re-create distress later.

### 3) Hybrid structure: reduced health obligation plus equity/security package
In practice, this is often the most viable solution.

A typical combination is:
- a **permanent reduction** in the legacy health obligation,
- plus a **capped equity or secured instrument** to preserve value,
- plus possibly a **contingent value right** tied to future performance.

This gives the trust something durable without leaving the company with an unsustainable promise.

---

## What the trust instrument should not be

### Avoid an unfunded promise with a longer runway
If the company simply defers the obligation, but does not reduce or capitalize it, then the problem is not solved.

### Avoid a full-priority secured claim on too much enterprise value
If the trust becomes effectively senior to everything else, then:
- unsecured bondholders may be wiped out,
- new rescue capital may hesitate,
- the operating company may become too levered again,
- and the whole rescue can fail.

### Avoid pure common equity unless the base case is strong
Common equity alone can be too volatile for a retiree-benefit substitute unless paired with:
- a minimum value floor,
- liquidation preference,
- or some secured component.

---

## A structurally sound design

The most defensible architecture, in general, would look like this:

### Step 1: Reduce the legacy health obligation
- Settle the current liability at a **discount** to face value.
- The discount reflects both the distressed condition of the company and the need to preserve enterprise value.

### Step 2: Grant the retiree trust a durable replacement instrument
Possible mix:
- **preferred equity** in the restructured company,
- or a **secured/ senior note** with long maturity and limited cash pay,
- or a **split package** of both.

### Step 3: Add contingent value if upside materializes
Examples:
- additional equity if EBIT or cash flow exceeds a threshold,
- warrants,
- or a contingent payment tied to asset sales or refinancing.

### Step 4: Ring-fence governance
The trust should have:
- clear reporting rights,
- anti-dilution protections within reason,
- limits on dividend leakage until minimum funding metrics are met,
- but not full control rights that would impede normal corporate governance.

---

## How this interacts with bondholders

This is where the tradeoff becomes acute.

### Established principle
If the trust is given an instrument with real economic seniority, then bondholders must usually absorb a larger haircut or accept less upside.

### Likely implication
There is no way to give the retiree trust meaningful protection **and** preserve near-parity recoveries for unsecured bondholders unless the company has far more value than described.

### Practical constraint
A rescue package can tolerate:
- one meaningful protected constituency,
- but not multiple classes each insisting on near-priority treatment.

So if the trust receives a secured-style instrument, bondholders will likely need:
- a larger principal reduction,
- longer maturity,
- lower coupons,
- and/or a smaller equity/warrant package.

---

## What “durable” should mean here

Durable does not mean “fully preserved forever.”

In this context, durable should mean:
- legally enforceable,
- funded or at least asset-backed,
- not dependent on annual political bargaining,
- and tied to a value source the reorganized company can realistically support.

That is the standard that matters.

---

## My view on feasibility

If the goal is to close within sixty days, the most feasible package is usually:

- **a cut in the retiree health obligation**, not full preservation;
- **a conversion of the reduced claim into preferred equity and/or a secured note**;
- **modest contingent upside rights** for the trust;
- **meaningful unsecured creditor haircuts**;
- and **clear operational downsizing** so the replacement instrument is actually supportable.

If instead the trust insists on a claim that is effectively senior to the reorganized company’s free cash flow, then the rescue likely becomes unfinanceable.

---

## Key implementation risks

1. **Valuation risk**
   - If the company is overvalued in the restructuring, the trust may be over-allocated and the plan may later fail.

2. **Priority conflict**
   - The trust instrument must be slotted carefully into the capital structure to avoid litigation and financing blockage.

3. **Governance complexity**
   - Too much trust control can paralyze the reorganized company.

4. **Political infeasibility**
   - If one group is seen as receiving hidden priority, other stakeholders may refuse to sign.

5. **Future dilution**
   - If equity is used, the trust needs anti-dilution and conversion protections, or the value can evaporate quickly.

---

## Concise recommendation

If I were designing this as a restructuring instrument, I would recommend:

- **reduce the retiree health liability materially**;
- **replace it with a hybrid of preferred equity plus a limited secured or quasi-secured note**;
- **add contingent value rights for upside**;
- **avoid full unsecured treatment and avoid full senior secured treatment**;
- **bind it to strict viability metrics and cash-flow limits**.

That is the most credible way to convert the obligation into durable value without sinking the company.
