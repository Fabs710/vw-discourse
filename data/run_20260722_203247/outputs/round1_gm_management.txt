We agree on the core objective: the company must emerge as a viable manufacturer, not as a prolonged claim on public support.

But I need to be explicit about what viability requires, because we do not have the luxury of saying “yes” to every legacy claim and still preserve the business.

### Where we agree
- **Real restructuring is unavoidable.** We accept that plants, brands, and dealer capacity have to be reduced if that is what the market can support.
- **Labour concessions are necessary.** Wage, work-rule, and flexibility changes must be part of the package.
- **The retiree trust cannot simply be ignored.** It must be addressed in a way that preserves some real value and credibility.

### Where we disagree
#### 1) The trust cannot stay intact in a form that breaks the company
I understand the point that this was an earned obligation. But if the current structure of that obligation prevents the company from surviving, then preserving it unchanged is not an option. We cannot fund a restructured company while leaving a liability in place that continues to make the business nonviable.

So yes, the trust needs treatment that is real and durable. But that does **not** mean it can be protected at the expense of the company’s survival or taxpayers. If a conversion into equity, a reduced claim, or another structured instrument is what makes the deal work, that is the discussion we have to have.

#### 2) Creditors must take meaningful losses too
I want to be equally clear that unsecured bondholders cannot be insulated while labour and retirees carry the main burden. That would be neither fair nor acceptable to the funder, and it would not be a credible basis for continued financing.

There has to be genuine haircuts for unsecured creditors. This is part of the capital reset. The company cannot come out burdened by a structure that assumes public money will make bondholders whole.

#### 3) Plant and brand cuts must be driven by viability, not politics
We are not pursuing a wholesale destruction of communities. But we also cannot preserve excess capacity simply because it is painful to close it. Any plant, brand, or dealer footprint we retain has to earn its place in a smaller, competitive company.

### What this means in practical terms
From our side, the non-negotiables are:
- a materially smaller operating footprint,
- lower labour costs,
- a genuine reduction or restructuring of the retiree health obligation,
- and meaningful creditor sacrifice.

What we can support:
- a durable structure for the retiree trust that gives it real value,
- continued employment where operations justify it,
- and a transition that protects consumer confidence, warranties, and the dealer network through the process.

### On process
We are not attached to a purely consensual path if it cannot deliver these outcomes quickly. If agreement fails, we have to be prepared to use the court-supervised route the funder has already put on the table. Delay is the real threat here. The company does not have weeks to spend protecting every legacy expectation.

So my message is simple:  
We are prepared to make the hard changes needed to keep this enterprise alive. But the trust, the unions, and the creditors all have to accept that the old capital structure is over. If any stakeholder insists on being made substantially whole while the company and taxpayer absorb the risk, that will not be part of the deal.
