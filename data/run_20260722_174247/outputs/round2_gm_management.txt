We can now work with the advisor’s framing, and it clarifies the central point: **the retiree trust must receive a real economic instrument, but not one that chokes the reorganized company.** That is the right test.

## Where I agree

### With the government task force
- **Viability comes first.** If the post-restructuring business cannot fund its operations and invest, then the rescue fails.
- **Deep sacrifice is unavoidable.** I am not defending the old structure.
- **Speed matters.** We do not have time for a theoretical plan.
- **Governance must change.** New leadership is only meaningful if the capital structure is also reset.

### With the union and retiree trust
- The trust is not a nuisance claim.
- Retirees should not be handed a hollow promise.
- A durable instrument is necessary if we want any credible settlement.

### With the bondholders
- Unsecured creditors should not be singled out for a total wipeout just because they are dispersed.
- If they are asked to take losses, they should receive something real in return, ideally equity-like upside.

### With the neighbouring governments
- If public money is coming from multiple jurisdictions, it should be tied to real commitments and a proportionate stake.
- I agree that domestic industrial value matters and cannot be treated as irrelevant.

## Where I disagree

### 1) I do not accept a trust structure that is “durable” only in name
The advisor is right that a retiree claim can be made durable through equity, preferred equity, or a contingent value right. But from the company’s perspective, **durable cannot mean senior enough or large enough to re-create insolvency**.

So:
- **Yes** to meaningful equity or preferred equity.
- **No** to a quasi-debt instrument with mandatory cash drains the business cannot support.
- **No** to a structure that is technically protected but economically suffocating.

If the trust claim consumes too much of the reorganized company’s value before operations recover, that is not protection — it is a delayed failure.

### 2) I do not accept preserving every domestic facility as a condition of rescue
I understand the neighbouring governments’ concern. But viability still governs. If a plant or dealer network is structurally uneconomic, it cannot be frozen in place by political demand.

What I can support is:
- preserving the strongest and most strategic domestic assets,
- maintaining a meaningful domestic footprint,
- and sequencing closures carefully to avoid needless damage.

What I cannot support is:
- a blanket commitment to keep all domestic capacity regardless of economics,
- or a rescue that substitutes geography for competitiveness.

### 3) I do not accept bondholders being effectively asked to fund everyone else’s claim
Bondholders are right that equal unsecured claims should not be treated arbitrarily. But their position goes too far when it implies they should preserve a recovery level that leaves no room for labour and retiree restructuring.

Unsecured debt is unsecured debt. In this situation, it must absorb real losses. The company cannot exit this process with a balance sheet that still pretends the old debt load is intact.

## What I think is the workable direction

The advisor’s recommendation is broadly the right structure: **a meaningful minority equity or preferred-equity allocation for the retiree trust, with anti-dilution protections, and possibly a contingent value element.**

That said, for management, the key questions are:
- how much of that instrument can the business support without strangling recovery,
- how much debt reduction remains after that allocation,
- and whether the resulting company has enough liquidity to execute the turnaround.

So our working position is:

### Retiree trust
- real value, not token value;
- likely **preferred equity or a protected equity position** is better than a large debt-like claim;
- anti-dilution protections are acceptable;
- a contingent value right could help bridge the gap;
- but the structure must be sized to fit post-restructuring cash flow.

### Bondholders
- meaningful haircut;
- upside participation through equity or warrants is reasonable;
- but no claim to near-par recovery.

### Labour
- wage/work-rule flexibility and legacy burden relief are necessary;
- but not a wholesale dismantling of the workforce;
- plant rationalization only where economics require it.

### Plants and brands
- preserve the strongest consumer-facing brands and the most competitive plants;
- cut weak brands and structurally uneconomic facilities;
- protect the dealer network where it supports the core franchise.

### Ownership
- post-restructuring equity should reflect actual sacrifice and new capital;
- management should not be insulated;
- public capital, the trust, bondholders, labour, and co-funders may all have stakes, but no one should get a free pass.

## Minimum acceptable terms from management

To be very explicit, management cannot support:

1. **A retiree-trust package that is too senior or cash-heavy to be financed**
2. **A plan that preserves unsecured bondholders too close to par**
3. **A structure that prevents necessary plant, brand, or dealer rationalization**
4. **A deal that leaves the company dependent on endless public support**
5. **Any path that risks uncontrolled liquidation**

## On process

I am not attached to out-of-court for its own sake. But I am also not interested in a court route as an ideological preference. The only question is whether the process gets us to a viable company in time.

If a consensual deal can deliver:
- deep enough debt reduction,
- a workable retiree instrument,
- real labour relief,
- and a leaner footprint,

then we should do it. If not, we should not hesitate to use the court-supervised route to lock in the necessary terms.

## Bottom line

I support the advisor’s core conclusion: **the retiree trust should receive a real equity or preferred-equity-based package with protections, not a hollow unsecured promise.**

But I will not support any version of that solution that makes the reorganized company unfinanceable. The test is not whether the trust feels significant in isolation. The test is whether the company survives, invests, and returns to competitiveness.

If we want a deal, the next step should be a term sheet with:
- a range for trust recovery,
- a range for unsecured creditor recovery,
- explicit plant/brand/dealer cuts,
- and a clear liquidity target for the reorganized business.
