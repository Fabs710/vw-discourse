We are prepared to move from principles to terms, but I want to be equally clear: **the Union and the Retiree Health Trust will not sign a plan that makes workers and retirees the residual loss sponge while creditors and public officials preserve the upside.** That is not shared sacrifice.

## Where we agree
We agree on the need for:
- a **real restructuring**, not a cosmetic patch;
- **speed**;
- a **leaner footprint** where capacity is truly uneconomic;
- **new governance discipline**;
- and a company that can survive without endless public support.

We also agree that the old structure cannot remain unchanged.

## Where we disagree
### 1) The retiree trust cannot be treated as a nuisance claim
You keep saying “durable, yes; untouchable, no.” I need to respond plainly: **durable is not enough if it means hollowing out what retirees were promised.**

The trust’s obligation is not some discretionary add-on. If the only way to “restructure” it is to turn it into a weak, speculative promise buried behind other claims, then you are asking retirees to absorb a loss they cannot recover from. That is unacceptable.

What we can consider is:
- a **meaningful equity stake** for the trust,
- or a **preferred/structured instrument** with real protections,
- or a **secured claim against future value**,
but not a token instrument that looks durable on paper and evaporates in practice.

### 2) Workers cannot be asked to carry disproportionate sacrifice
We have already accepted concessions in earlier rounds. So when you say labour needs deeper cuts, I need specifics.

We are willing to discuss:
- targeted wage moderation,
- work-rule flexibility,
- limited benefit adjustments for active workers,
- and plant rationalization where economics truly require it.

We are **not** willing to accept:
- a wholesale wage war,
- a plan that tears up the framework for remaining plants,
- or closures that go beyond what viability actually requires.

If the government wants labour cost relief, it must also require real sacrifice from unsecured creditors and management, not just pressure from the shop floor.

### 3) Bondholders must take real losses, but not in a way that forces labour to pay for them
We agree unsecured bondholders should not be wiped out by decree. We do **not** agree that bondholder protection should become the hidden priority of the restructuring.

If bondholders want a meaningful recovery, then:
- they must take a **substantial haircut**,
- they must accept **equity at risk**, not just paper claims,
- and they cannot demand that the trust and labour absorb more simply to preserve creditor optics.

## Our minimum acceptable direction
If you want our signature, the plan must include all of the following:

### 1) Retiree trust treatment
- A **real, enforceable stake** in the restructured company.
- Protection against future dilution or stripping.
- A claim that is **durable in substance**, not just labeled durable.
- If the trust is asked to accept reduced cash flow, it must receive equity or equivalent value that can actually grow with the company.

### 2) Jobs and plants
- Preserve **as many productive plants and jobs as possible**.
- Close only capacity that is genuinely uneconomic.
- No mass liquidation disguised as rationalization.
- Domestic industrial communities cannot be treated as expendable.

### 3) Labour concessions
- We can discuss flexibility, but not permanent dismantling of the wage framework.
- Any concessions must be paired with job security and a credible future for the workforce.

### 4) Creditor treatment
- Bondholders take meaningful haircuts.
- They may receive equity or warrants, but not a package that leaves them positioned as if the crisis never happened.
- No “selective fairness” where bondholders argue equality only when it protects them and forget it when retirees are involved.

### 5) Ownership and governance
- The post-restructuring equity must reflect real sacrifice.
- The trust and workforce need a meaningful position.
- Management does not get to keep control by default after presiding over this failure.

## On neighbouring governments
We are not opposed to co-funding, but let me be direct: **if public money is coming from multiple governments, then the deal cannot be written solely around creditor recovery and abstract balance-sheet purity.**

There must be:
- binding job and plant commitments,
- a serious domestic footprint,
- and a governance structure that respects the public and labour contributions.

If the co-funders want a proportional stake, fine. But that does not override the need for a fair treatment of retirees and workers.

## On the process
I will say this carefully: the threat of court is not a substitute for bargaining.

If the task force believes the court route can impose a fairer result, then it should be prepared to justify that outcome against the actual claims of labour and retirees. We are not intimidated by the process. What matters is substance.

If the out-of-court proposal is only “acceptable” because it forces workers and retirees to accept more than anyone else, then we will not sign it.

## One important clarification
I am not saying the trust must be preserved in its old form. I am saying it must be converted into something **durable and meaningful**. That is the line.

If your proposal gives:
- genuine value to the trust,
- preserved jobs and plants where viable,
- real creditor sacrifice,
- and a future for the workforce,

then we can keep talking.

If instead the plan asks us to endorse deep labour and retiree cuts while bondholders are protected and domestic capacity is hollowed out, then we are fundamentally opposed.
