[Expert: restructuring/valuation advisor — Round 2]
From a restructuring and valuation perspective, the key issue is not whether the retiree trust gets “something,” but whether the instrument has **credible economic value**, **priority appropriate to the enterprise’s cash-flow capacity**, and **enforceability consistent with the company’s survival**.

## Bottom line

A retiree trust can be made “durable” in three broad ways:

1. **Equity in the reorganized company**
2. **Preferred equity or a structured value-linked instrument**
3. **A combination of modest current value plus contingent upside**

For a distressed automaker with severe liquidity strain, the most workable answer is usually:

- **not** full cash payment,
- **not** a large senior secured-like claim that strains the company,
- but a **meaningful equity or preferred-equity package**, often with **downside protection and limited priority**, plus possibly a **contingent value right** tied to future performance.

The design must fit the company’s post-restructuring debt service capacity and working capital needs. If the trust’s claim is too large or too senior, it simply recreates insolvency in the new capital structure.

---

## What “genuinely durable” means in valuation terms

A trust claim is durable if it has:

- **real economic participation** in the reorganized enterprise,
- **protection against immediate dilution or stripping**,
- **a claim on upside** if the turnaround succeeds,
- and **a legal form that survives the reorganization**.

Durability does **not** require that the trust be paid in full in present value terms. In distressed restructuring, that is often impossible. It does require that the trust receive an instrument whose expected value is not merely nominal.

---

## Most viable structures, ranked

### 1) Preferred equity or a preferred-like trust certificate
This is often the cleanest compromise.

**Features:**
- senior to common equity,
- often with a fixed or target dividend only if cash allows,
- can accrue unpaid amounts,
- may have a liquidation preference,
- may convert to common equity under certain conditions.

**Why it works:**
- gives the trust a real, quantifiable claim,
- preserves flexibility for the operating company,
- avoids burdening the firm with heavy mandatory cash outflows.

**Risk:**
- if structured too richly, it becomes quasi-debt and weakens the post-reorg balance sheet.

### 2) Common equity with anti-dilution protections
This is simpler but less protective.

**Features:**
- ownership stake in the reorganized company,
- board or governance rights,
- anti-dilution protections for a period,
- possibly warrants or additional shares if performance milestones are met.

**Why it works:**
- aligns the trust with turnaround success,
- avoids fixed cash drains.

**Risk:**
- common equity alone can be too volatile and may not feel “durable” to beneficiaries unless the stake is large enough or protected.

### 3) Structured claim plus contingent value right
This is useful if the parties need to bridge valuation gaps.

**Features:**
- a modest immediate instrument, plus
- additional payments or equity if EBITDA, share price, or asset monetization exceeds thresholds.

**Why it works:**
- lets the company survive today,
- gives the trust a fair share of upside,
- reduces the need to overstate current value.

**Risk:**
- contingent instruments can be too uncertain if milestones are unrealistic or poorly drafted.

---

## What I would avoid

### 1) Large unsecured-style promise with long deferral
If the trust simply gets a large long-dated unsecured claim, it may look “durable” but may be economically weak and vulnerable to future distress.

### 2) A claim senior to the company’s ability to invest
If the trust gets priority that consumes too much free cash flow, the company will underinvest, and the restructuring will fail.

### 3) Too much cash funding at closing
Immediate cash outlays to the trust reduce liquidity, which is usually the scarcest resource in a rescue like this.

---

## Relative economics: what is likely feasible?

Because no actual valuation numbers were provided, I can’t give a precise percentage. But in a case like this, the trust’s recovery is typically constrained by:

- enterprise value after restructuring,
- required debt reduction,
- working capital needs,
- pension/healthcare legacy burden,
- plant rationalization costs,
- and the amount of cash the public sector is willing to supply.

If the reorganized business needs a heavily delevered balance sheet, then the trust’s recovery usually has to come mostly from **equity-like value**, not from debt-like claims. That means the trust can be “durable” in a real sense even if its present value is materially discounted.

---

## Practical recommendation

The most defensible structure is usually:

- **a meaningful minority equity stake for the retiree trust**, or
- **preferred equity with a liquidation preference and limited governance rights**, plus
- **protective covenants against dilution**, and
- **possibly a contingent value right** tied to turnaround performance.

That gives the trust real value and durability while leaving the operating company financeable.

## What I would need to give a tighter recommendation

To say whether the trust should get, for example, 5%, 10%, 15%, or more of reorganized equity, I would need:

- post-restructuring enterprise value range,
- forecast EBITDA and free cash flow,
- minimum liquidity requirement,
- debt capacity after restructuring,
- plant closure and severance costs,
- the size and legal priority of the retiree obligation,
- and whether the trust can accept illiquid, long-duration value.
