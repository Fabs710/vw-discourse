## 1. Decision context
A major automaker is in acute liquidity distress after a sales collapse and credit freeze. The state has provided bridge financing but has rejected the company’s initial plan as insufficiently aggressive, forced leadership change, and is now driving a deeper restructuring within a 60-day window. The core challenge is to allocate losses among unsecured bondholders, the industrial union/retiree trust, management, and public co-funders while preserving a viable going concern.

The restructuring must also resolve how to treat the retiree health obligation, how much labour and plant rationalization is required, whether neighbouring governments will co-fund, and whether the transaction proceeds consensually or through a court-supervised process.

---

## 2. Stakeholder assembly
Higher standing is assigned to stakeholders with greater leverage and legitimacy in the process:

1. **Government task force / lead funder** — highest leverage; controls financing and can force court-supervised restructuring.
2. **Company management under new leadership** — operational authority and implementation capacity.
3. **Industrial union & retiree health trust** — labour legitimacy and consent rights over legacy obligations.
4. **Unsecured bondholders’ committee** — financially significant but dispersed; consent matters.
5. **Neighbouring governments / co-funders** — important contingent capital providers with domestic industrial interests.

---

## 3. Final positions

### Government task force
The task force insists on a materially lighter balance sheet, real losses for unsecured creditors, and a reworked retiree obligation that preserves viability. It is open to public support only if sacrifice is shared and if any co-funding comes with real ownership/governance terms; otherwise it is prepared to impose terms through court supervision.

### Company management
Management supports deep restructuring, a leaner footprint, and a trust instrument that is meaningful but not so heavy that it recreates insolvency. It aligns with the task force on viability first, but insists that the final package must still leave enough liquidity and flexibility to execute the turnaround.

### Industrial union & retiree health trust
The union accepts restructuring, some labour flexibility, and selective plant rationalization, but rejects being the residual shock absorber. It wants the retiree trust converted into a durable, meaningful instrument — ideally equity, preferred equity, or equivalent protected value — and wants jobs and productive plants preserved wherever economically defensible.

### Unsecured bondholders’ committee
Bondholders accept real losses and some upside participation, but reject a near-wipeout or any structure that gives the retiree trust materially better treatment without compensation. They want equal treatment among unsecured claims or offsetting value if the trust receives a richer instrument, and they are prepared to resist the deal if they see the court route as more favorable.

### Neighbouring governments / co-funders
The neighbouring governments are willing to contribute, but only if the deal includes binding domestic production and employment commitments and a proportionate ownership/governance stake. They view domestic plant protection as a non-negotiable condition of support and will not fund a restructuring that shifts the burden onto their industrial base.

---

## 4. Genuine conflicts (structurally incompatible positions)
- **Retiree trust vs. bondholders:** the union wants a durable, protected instrument for the trust; bondholders will only accept that if they receive comparable economic treatment or compensation.
- **Viability vs. domestic protection:** neighbouring governments demand binding domestic plant/job guarantees, but management and the task force insist on closure/rationalization where economics require it.
- **Depth of sacrifice:** the task force wants deep concessions from labour and creditors; the union resists disproportionate labour/retiree losses.
- **Process leverage:** the task force is willing to use court-supervised restructuring to impose terms; bondholders and labour see this as leverage, not a fair settlement mechanism.
- **Ownership allocation:** all parties want stakes reflecting sacrifice, but they disagree on who gets protected equity, who gets dilution, and how public capital should be repaid.

---

## 5. Areas of convergence
- All parties agree the company needs a **real restructuring**, not cosmetic repair.
- There is broad agreement that the company must emerge **leaner and viable**.
- Everyone accepts that **some plant/brand/dealer rationalization** is likely unavoidable.
- There is shared acceptance that **new governance discipline** is necessary.
- All parties acknowledge that the **retiree trust needs real economic value**, not a hollow promise.
- There is consensus that **bondholders must take meaningful losses**, though not on how deep.
- Public co-funding is viewed as possible, but only with **conditions and accountability**.

---

## 6. Key trade-offs for the decision-maker
1. **Retiree protection vs. bondholder recovery**  
   A more protected trust instrument improves labour legitimacy but reduces room for unsecured creditor recovery.

2. **Domestic industrial commitments vs. operational viability**  
   Binding plant/job guarantees can secure political support but may constrain necessary rationalization.

3. **Out-of-court speed vs. court-supervised certainty**  
   A consensual deal is faster and less destructive, but court-supervised restructuring may be needed to force sufficient sacrifice.

4. **Public support vs. public control**  
   Co-funding can stabilize the deal, but may require ownership/governance concessions and limits on managerial discretion.

5. **Equity as compensation vs. cash preservation**  
   Giving more equity/rights to constituencies can bridge acceptability, but too much claims burden can impair the turnaround.

---

## 7. Integrative pathways
- **Preferred-equity / protected equity package for the retiree trust** with anti-dilution protection and possibly a contingent value right, rather than a large cash or debt-like claim.
- **Meaningful but reduced bondholder recovery** through equity and/or warrants, with explicit recognition that unsecured claims are being impaired.
- **Targeted labour concessions**: wage/work-rule flexibility, limited active-worker benefit adjustments, and selective plant rationalization only where clearly uneconomic.
- **Selective domestic commitments**: preserve strategically important domestic plants and output, but avoid a blanket freeze on all closures.
- **Proportionate public co-funding stakes**: neighbouring governments receive board/governance rights and equity commensurate with their contribution, tied to measurable domestic commitments.
- **Single integrated term sheet** with ranges, not fixed points, allowing final bargaining within a viability envelope.

---

## 8. Open questions
- What is the realistic post-restructuring enterprise value and minimum liquidity requirement?
- How large can the retiree trust’s protected instrument be before it becomes quasi-debt and destabilizes the reorganization?
- What recovery range is acceptable for bondholders if the trust receives preferred or protected equity?
- Which plants, brands, and dealer networks are truly uneconomic versus politically contested but viable?
- How much domestic production must be guaranteed to satisfy co-funders without undermining competitiveness?
- What ownership split best reflects sacrifice while preserving management accountability and public exit?
- Can a consensual deal be reached within 60 days, or is the court route inevitable?

---

## 9. Recommended next step
Move immediately to a **single integrated term sheet** with quantified ranges for: enterprise value, liquidity cushion, retiree trust recovery, bondholder recovery, labour concessions, plant/brand/dealer actions, and public co-funding/governance. The next round should test one whole package against viability, rather than negotiating each constituency in isolation.

---

## 10. Emergent insights that only appeared through multi-turn interaction
- The key unresolved issue is not simply “how much each party gets,” but **whether the retiree trust is made durable in a way that is economically real without recreating insolvency**. The advisor clarified that preferred equity or protected equity is more workable than cash-like or senior unsecured-style claims.
- The bondholders’ position hardened once the trust was framed as potentially receiving a **better unsecured-equivalent instrument**; their central concern is **relative treatment**, not just absolute recovery.
- The neighbouring governments shifted the bargaining frame by making **domestic industrial protection a condition precedent**, not just a preference. This means process choice alone cannot solve their concern.
- Management’s stance evolved from general viability language to a specific operating principle: **durability for the trust is acceptable only if it does not drain post-reorg cash flow**.
- The task force’s threat of court-supervised restructuring is significant, but the discussion revealed that **court can impose financial terms, not political guarantees**, especially around domestic jobs and production.
- The interaction exposed a three-way tension that a single prompt would miss: **trust durability, bondholder equality, and operating-company financeability** cannot all be maximized simultaneously; any workable deal must deliberately subordinate at least one of them.
