We are closer on the diagnosis than on the cure.

What is now clear is this: **everyone agrees the company must be smaller, cheaper, and financially reset fast.** What is not acceptable is any attempt to preserve legacy economics by shifting the burden onto the public purse, or to turn the retiree trust into a politically protected claim while unsecured creditors absorb the real loss.

Let me respond point by point.

### 1) Retiree trust: durable, yes; protected in full, no
To the union: I hear your insistence that the retiree obligation must be honored in a real form, not destroyed. That is a reasonable claim. But it does **not** mean the trust can retain its current economic value.

The current obligation is too large for this company. If we leave it largely intact, the company is not viable and we are back in crisis almost immediately. So the trust must be restructured materially.

What is possible:
- a conversion into a mix of cash, equity, and/or securities,
- a significantly reduced present-value claim,
- and a structure that is durable and enforceable.

What is not possible:
- preserving the full burden and calling it a rescue,
- or creating a quasi-sacred claim that sits ahead of other unsecured creditors in practice.

### 2) Bondholders: yes, substantial losses; no, you do not get to veto the reset
To the bondholder committee: you are correct that unsecured creditors are legitimate claimants and that treatment should be principled. But that does **not** mean you are entitled to recover near-par while the company is collapsing.

You are unsecured. This company is insolvent in substance. There will be a substantial haircut. That is not “political favoritism”; that is the reality of a rescue where the business cannot support existing debt.

I will also be blunt: the suggestion that a materially reduced trust claim is somehow improper unless bondholders are treated identically in every respect misses the point. The goal is not formal symmetry. The goal is a viable capital structure. If the end result leaves the trust with some durable value and bondholders with a deep exchange into lower-value paper or equity, that can be justified only if the overall package is necessary for viability and finality. If you want better recovery, produce a better viability case — not just a complaint about parity.

### 3) Labour: concessions must be real and immediate
To the union: you are right that workers are not the only shock absorber. But you are wrong if you think plant and job protections can be negotiated in the abstract while the company remains overcosted.

We need:
- labor cost reductions,
- work-rule changes,
- and legacy obligation changes.

Those concessions must be concrete, not aspirational. In return, we are prepared to consider targeted plant retention where there is a credible operating case. But there will be no guarantee of every site, and no promise that the footprint remains unchanged.

### 4) Plants and brands: deep rationalization is required
To management and the neighbouring governments: I agree that the footprint must be rationalized in a way that preserves a viable core. I also agree that an indiscriminate slash-and-burn approach would destroy value.

But let’s be honest: the current footprint is too large. Some plants, some brands, and some dealer capacity will have to go. If we do not make those cuts now, the company will keep burning cash and the restructuring will fail.

On co-funding: public money does not purchase the right to freeze an uneconomic network. It purchases a viable restructuring. If neighbouring governments want to co-fund, that is welcome, but it cannot come with a demand to preserve every domestic asset regardless of economics. We will consider enforceable commitments, but only within a plan that is commercially sustainable.

### 5) Co-funding: helpful, but conditional and not controlling
We welcome co-funding if it reduces taxpayer exposure and accelerates the rescue. But it cannot become a side bargain that blocks the necessary restructuring.

Conditions that matter:
- real economic contribution, not symbolism;
- no veto over core restructuring decisions;
- enforceable commitments that are compatible with viability.

What will not happen is that domestic political commitments are used to protect excess capacity or delay the reset.

### 6) Process: speed matters more than comfort
If the parties cannot produce a final package quickly, we will use the court-supervised route.

That is not a threat. It is the fallback we have already prepared. It exists because we will not fund an endless negotiation while the company burns through cash.

### Bottom line
Here is the direction of travel from this task force:

- **Retiree trust:** materially restructured, durable, but reduced.
- **Bondholders:** substantial haircut, with whatever upside remains tied to the value preserved by the restructuring.
- **Labour:** real concessions on cost and legacy obligations.
- **Plants/brands:** meaningful rationalization, not cosmetic trimming.
- **Co-funders:** welcome if they reduce public exposure and accept a commercially viable plan.
- **Process:** fast and final, consensual if possible, court-imposed if necessary.

If any party is holding out for a better deal at taxpayer expense, they should understand this clearly: **we will not finance that outcome.**
