We agree on the core objective: **the company has to emerge viable, quickly, and with enough liquidity to survive the next few weeks**. If that cannot be achieved, then none of the legacy claims are worth much anyway.

Where I strongly agree with both of you:
- **The old capital structure is not survivable.**
- **Sacrifice has to be shared.**
- **We need a credible reset of debt and legacy obligations, not just bridge financing.**
- **The timeframe is real.**

Where I need to be explicit is on what is and is not workable from the operating side.

### On labour and the retiree trust
I understand the union’s point that workers and retirees cannot be treated as the only shock absorber. That would be both unfair and operationally self-defeating. We need labour peace to keep plants running and preserve the franchise.

But I also have to say clearly: **the existing retiree obligation cannot remain in its current form**. It is too large for a company at this scale of collapse. If we simply “honor” it without material restructuring, we are not rescuing the company; we are preserving a claim the business cannot support.

So yes, I am open to a durable replacement structure — but it has to be one that the company can actually fund. That likely means:
- a significant reduction or conversion of the trust’s claim,
- some combination of cash, securities, and equity,
- and a balance that does not choke the restructured company on day one.

What I cannot support is a structure that preserves the full economic value of the retiree claim while asking the rest of the business to absorb the pain. That would leave us back here in months.

### On unsecured creditors
I agree with the task force that unsecured creditors must take a real haircut. The balance sheet is too leveraged and the market is not going to finance an unchanged capital structure. If creditors want a viable enterprise rather than a liquidation outcome, they need to accept that recovery has to come down materially.

To be blunt: **there is no path to survival that leaves unsecured bondholders close to par**.

### On plants, brands, and dealers
Here I want to be careful but direct. I support meaningful rationalization, including closures and brand simplification, because the current footprint is not affordable.

But I do not support indiscriminate cuts that damage the core franchise:
- We need to preserve the strongest brands.
- We need a viable dealer and service network.
- We need to protect consumer confidence in warranties and parts support.
- And we need to avoid a restructuring that looks like dismemberment, because that would accelerate customer flight.

So yes to deep cuts, but they must be targeted to eliminate chronic loss-makers and excess capacity, not to dismantle the business we are trying to save.

### On the court-supervised route
I have no ideological objection to a court process if it is the only way to get finality. My concern has always been consumer confidence and operational disruption. If a court-supervised restructuring is the fastest credible way to force shared sacrifice and secure funding certainty, then I can work within that.

What I cannot accept is endless bargaining that leaves us underfunded and drifting toward insolvency. If the only way to get the necessary reset is through an expedited process, then that is preferable to collapse.

### Bottom line
From management’s perspective, the non-negotiables are:
1. **Financing certainty**
2. **A materially restructured balance sheet**
3. **A labor settlement that preserves operations**
4. **A consumer-facing franchise that survives the transition**

So I support:
- deep creditor losses,
- real restructuring of the retiree trust,
- targeted plant/brand rationalization,
- and whatever process gives us finality fast.

I do **not** support:
- preserving claims the business cannot carry,
- cosmetic concessions,
- or a restructuring that protects one stakeholder class at the expense of viability.

If the union is prepared to discuss a durable conversion of the retiree claim, and if creditors are prepared to take substantial losses, then we may have a route to keep the company alive. Without that, we do not.
