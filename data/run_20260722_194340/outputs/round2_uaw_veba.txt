We hear the task force’s urgency, and we do not dispute the basic facts: the company is overleveraged, the old structure is not survivable, and the 60-day window is real.

But let me be equally clear: **a rescue that simply deepens the burden on workers and retirees while forcing the trust into an inferior position is not acceptable**. If that is the direction, we will not bless it just because time is short.

## Where we agree
- The company needs a **real balance-sheet reset**, not bridge financing dressed up as a plan.
- There must be **shared sacrifice**.
- The old footprint is too large; some **plant, brand, and overhead rationalization** is unavoidable.
- **Unsecured creditors must take meaningful losses**.
- A **court-supervised process** is not inherently off the table if it produces a fair, durable outcome.

## Where we disagree

### 1) The retiree health trust cannot be treated as disposable
The valuation advisor says the trust likely needs a material present-value reduction. We do not dispute that the obligation must be reworked. What we reject is any approach that turns a negotiated earned benefit into a near-wipeout while bondholders are then protected by political sympathy or clean equity participation.

The trust must be converted into something **durable and real**:
- equity,
- secured or senior securities,
- or another enforceable funding vehicle with actual value.

A promise without value is not a solution.  
A destruction of the trust is not a solution either.

### 2) The bondholders do not get to demand parity by forcing the trust down to their recovery level
We understand the pari-passu argument. But we are not going to accept a framework where the retiree obligation is treated as a bargaining chip to make unsecured bondholders feel equal.

There is an important distinction:
- **equal legal rank** does not automatically mean **identical economic treatment** in a court-supervised rescue,
- especially when the workforce has already taken concessions and the company’s viability depends on preserving labour peace.

That said, if bondholders are being asked for a 70%–90% haircut, then the trust must also receive a meaningful, enforceable recovery — not a token slice. We are not asking for privilege; we are insisting on **dignity and durability**.

### 3) Workers are not the first source of savings
Management says labor concessions must be concrete, and we agree that adjustments may be needed. But the burden cannot fall disproportionately on the workforce while executives, creditors, and public authorities preserve their preferred positions.

Any labor changes must be tied to:
- **specific plant retention and job commitments**,
- **clear investment in viable plants**,
- and a guarantee that rationalization is aimed at eliminating loss-making capacity, not gutting the industrial core.

If the plan cuts wages and rules but then closes too many plants or strips the footprint so far that the company and communities are permanently weakened, then the “viability” claim is not credible.

### 4) Rationalization must be targeted, not a liquidation by instalment
We accept the need to shrink. We do **not** accept blanket closures or a plan that sacrifices industrial communities to satisfy financing optics.

A viable restructuring should identify:
- which plants are core,
- which are genuinely uneconomic,
- and which can be retained with new terms and modest investment.

We want specific commitments, not vague assurances.

## On the numbers
The advisor’s haircut range is helpful as a warning, not as a bargaining demand.

If the company truly can only support a much smaller enterprise value, then yes, everyone in the unsecured stack must take a real hit. But that cuts both ways:
- the trust cannot be asked to absorb the entire burden,
- and the bondholders cannot insist on treating their recovery as the benchmark against which all others are measured.

What matters is whether the final package produces:
1. a **viable company**,
2. a **durable retiree claim with real value**,
3. **preserved jobs and plants where feasible**,
4. and **shared sacrifice that is visible and fair**.

## On process
If the task force is prepared to use the court-supervised route to impose a genuinely balanced outcome, we will evaluate it on substance. But if the court route is being used to:
- crush the trust,
- impose disproportionate labour concessions,
- or hand creditors cleaner treatment than workers and retirees,

then we will oppose that outcome.

## Our bottom line
We are prepared to accept:
- a major restructuring,
- some labour concessions,
- targeted plant rationalization,
- and real creditor haircuts.

We are **not** prepared to accept:
- destruction of the retiree health trust,
- wholesale sacrifice of jobs and plants,
- or a deal where workers and retirees are made junior to financial creditors in substance if not in form.

### What would move us
A workable package must include:
- a **durable replacement for the retiree trust with real value**,
- **binding plant and job commitments** for the viable core,
- **substantial creditor losses**,
- and an ownership structure that gives labour and the trust a genuine stake in the reorganized company.

If those elements are on the table, we can keep talking. If not, then the problem is not our flexibility — it is the design of the rescue.
