From a restructuring valuation and insolvency perspective, the core question is not what each stakeholder prefers, but what enterprise value the business can credibly support after a reset, and how that value must be allocated to restore financeability.

## 1) Threshold conclusion

This company appears **balance-sheet insolvent and cash-flow distressed**. In that setting, the relevant test is whether the restructured entity can support:

## 2) Minimum viable enterprise value

Without company-specific forecasts, I can only state this as a valuation range, not a precise number.

For a legacy automaker under severe recessionary stress, a plausible minimum viable enterprise value is typically the value needed to support:

- a normalized post-cut EBITDA base,
- less capex and working capital needs,
- and debt service at a conservative leverage ratio.

A credible post-restructuring capital structure usually requires **net debt no more than about 1.5x to 2.5x normalized EBITDA**, and in a highly cyclical auto business often closer to the low end of that range.

So, if the reorganized company can only support, for example, **$3–5 billion of normalized EBITDA**, then sustainable funded debt is likely only in the **$4.5–12.5 billion** range, depending on cyclicality, capex intensity, and liquidity cushion. That means the implied enterprise value is not the distressed pre-restructuring headline value; it is the value of a **smaller, cheaper, liquidity-backed going concern**. If the company cannot get to that footing, the going-concern value collapses toward liquidation or break-up value.

From a restructuring mechanics perspective, the parties are converging on the economically necessary result: **old equity wiped, unsecureds deeply impaired, legacy retiree obligation materially reduced, and the operating footprint shrunk**. ## 1) What the structure likely needs to look like

### A. Claim classes
At minimum, the plan will need separate treatment for:
- **government bridge financing / debtor-in-possession-style support**: likely superpriority or otherwise effectively senior;
- **unsecured bondholders**: a distinct class because their recoveries are being materially impaired;
- **retiree health trust obligation**: separate class or separate settlement vehicle, depending on legal form and funding mechanics;
- **trade claims** if material;
- **equity**: almost certainly cancelled.

The key point is that the **bondholders and the retiree trust cannot be casually lumped together** if the economics differ materially. If they are similarly situated under applicable law, disparate treatment creates confirmation and litigation risk. If there is a legal basis for different treatment, it must be documented clearly.

### B. Retiree trust treatment
A durable solution usually falls into one of three models:

1. **Capped cash settlement plus securities/equity upside**  
   The trust gets reduced cash and a contingent instrument. This preserves some value but reduces present value materially.

2. **New funding vehicle / replacement trust**  
   The legacy obligation is transferred into a new trust or funding arrangement with lower annual contributions, possibly supported by equity or warrants.

3. **Full or partial conversion into unsecured or subordinated paper**  
   This is simpler mechanically, but often less acceptable politically and may not protect retirees from future employer distress.

Given the facts, the economically realistic outcome is **not full preservation** of the legacy obligation. If the trust is kept whole in nominal terms, the company likely remains overburdened. So the trust should expect a **substantial present-value reduction**, even if the headline face amount is re-expressed in a more durable form.

### C. Bondholder recovery
For unsecured bondholders, the likely exchange is:
- old bonds cancelled,
- new notes with reduced principal,
- possibly some cash at closing,
- and equity or warrants for the balance.

If the enterprise value is as constrained as described, a **70%–90% haircut** remains the right order of magnitude. In practical terms, that means bondholders likely receive a mix of:
- a small amount of new debt,
- a minority equity stake,
- and perhaps warrants.

Anything materially richer risks leaving the company overlevered.

## 2) Priority and fairness issues

The central legal issue is whether the retiree trust can receive better economics than unsecured bondholders.

That turns on:
- the **exact legal status** of the trust claim;
- whether it is treated as a contract claim, a welfare-benefit obligation, or something with special statutory treatment;
- and whether the restructuring is consensual or imposed in a court-supervised process.

If the trust is legally pari passu with bondholders, then a materially better recovery for the trust is vulnerable unless:
- the trust provides something of separate value,
- the differential is part of a global consensual settlement,
- or the legal framework permits it.

If the trust has a distinct legal character, then the difference may be justifiable. But from a restructuring design perspective, the safest path is to ensure that any disparity is tied to a **documented legal difference or a negotiated value exchange**, not just political preference.

## 3) Ownership allocation

### A. Old equity
Old equity is almost certainly out of the money and should be cancelled.

### B. New equity
New equity generally belongs to the parties providing the new value:
- new money lenders,
- possibly the government if it is taking true economic risk,
- and possibly a portion to unsecured creditors and/or the retiree trust in exchange for concessions.

A common pattern in a deep restructuring is:
- **new money holders get control or a large majority**,
- **impaired creditors receive the remainder** through a plan allocation,
- legacy shareholders receive nothing.

### C. Relative equity split
Without a specific valuation, no honest advisor can give a precise split. But the governing principle is:
- **the more a constituency is cut on claim value, the more it can be asked to trade for equity participation**,
- subject to feasibility and governance requirements.

For this fact pattern, an economically plausible result is:
- **new money / public bridge provider**: large control stake or strong protective rights;
- **bondholders**: meaningful minority recovery through debt-plus-equity;
- **retiree trust**: smaller economic slice, potentially via a capped funding vehicle or warrant-like participation;
- **old equity**: zero.

## 4) Court-supervised versus consensual route

### Consensual out-of-court
Pros:
- speed if everyone signs;
- less stigma;
- lower direct legal cost.

Cons:
- needs near-unanimous stakeholder alignment;
- vulnerable to holdouts;
- difficult when the capital structure is deeply insolvent and there are many dispersed bondholders.

### Court-supervised
Pros:
- can bind dissenters;
- can impose a coherent class-based allocation;
- better suited to deep deleveraging and contested legacy claims.

Cons:
- more litigation risk;
- more process complexity;
- can delay execution if documentation is not ready.

Given the disputes here, a **court-supervised process is likely the more reliable mechanism** if agreement is not nearly complete. The task force’s financing leverage can bridge the company into that process, but it should not be assumed that mere bridge funding solves the allocation problem.

## 5) Plant, brand, and labor implications
Mechanically, the more severe the footprint reduction:
- the higher the recovery pressure on all financial claimants,
- the greater the need to restructure legacy obligations,
- and the more likely the plan can support a viable post-reorg capital structure.

That said, operational cuts should be tied to a **going-concern plan**. If cuts exceed what is needed to restore viability, they destroy value and worsen recoveries across all classes. The restructuring has to preserve enough scale to support the post-cut enterprise value assumed in the exchange.

The most defensible package is likely:

- **unsecured bondholders receive a deep haircut, likely 70%–90%**, in a mix of new notes, equity, and warrants;
- **retiree trust is materially restructured**, with a reduced present-value obligation and durable funding mechanics;
- **new equity is allocated primarily to new money and impaired creditor classes**, with control likely resting with the new financier(s);
- **court-supervised completion is the most reliable path** unless a near-total consensual lock-up is achieved quickly.

- Convert the retiree trust into a reduced, durable replacement vehicle with some cash, securities, or contingent upside.
- Use court process to force class discipline and bind dissenters.
- Best for speed, finality, and enforceability.

### Pathway B: Consensual global settlement with side protections
- Offer bondholders a transparent recovery band and equity participation.
- Give the trust a durable replacement claim with clear present-value reduction.
- Add binding but limited domestic production commitments to satisfy neighbouring governments.
- Use governance allocations to balance new money, public co-funders, and impaired creditors.
- Best if near-unanimous support can be assembled quickly.

### Pathway C: Split-value compromise
- Preserve a smaller “core” industrial footprint in domestic jurisdictions.
- Allow the trust somewhat better durability but cap its economics.
- Give bondholders a meaningful equity upside to offset haircut severity.
- Use co-funder money to bridge the gap and reduce immediate cash strain.
- Best only if the viability model can support both the footprint and the legacy obligations.

## 8. Open questions

- What is the exact legal status of the retiree trust claim?
- What enterprise value and normalized EBITDA will anchor recoveries?
- How large is the needed post-restructuring leverage reduction?
- Which plants and brands are economically indispensable?
- What domestic production commitments are binding but still consistent with viability?
- How much equity should co-funders receive for their contribution?
- Is there enough time to reach a consensual lock-up before the deadline?
- What precise exchange terms will satisfy both pari passu concerns and trust durability?

## 9. Recommended next step

Move immediately to a single integrated term sheet built around a court-ready exchange framework, with three anchored inputs:
1. a valuation range and leverage target,
2. class-by-class recovery bands,
3. a defined list of retained versus closed plants with explicit domestic production covenants.

That package should be tested first for financeability, then for legal durability, and only then for political acceptability. If consensus does not quickly emerge, the task force should be prepared to launch the court-supervised route.

## 10. Emergent insights that only appeared through multi-turn interaction

1. **The real fracture is not “labour vs. capital” but “two unsecured constituencies competing for finite value.”**  
   The transcript clarified that the retiree trust and bondholders are the decisive distributional fight, not simply creditors versus workers.

2. **Parity is a legal and political issue, not just a valuation issue.**  
   Bondholders repeatedly shifted from accepting losses to demanding comparable treatment once it became clear the trust might receive a more durable wrapper.

3. **Domestic production became a separate bargaining axis.**  
   Neighbouring governments turned the restructuring from a pure financial reset into an industrial-policy negotiation with employment covenants and governance rights.

4. **Court supervision emerged as the most credible coordination device.**  
   The parties’ positions diverged enough that the process itself became part of the solution, not just a fallback.

5. **The company’s viability now depends on three linked reductions, not one:**  
   debt reduction, retiree-obligation reduction, and plant-footprint reduction. The multi-turn exchange made clear that any one of these left alone would likely destabilize the whole package.

6. **Ownership is not a separate afterthought; it is the settlement mechanism.**  
   Across rounds, equity became the currency for distributing losses, control, and upside among new money, public funders, and impaired claimants.
