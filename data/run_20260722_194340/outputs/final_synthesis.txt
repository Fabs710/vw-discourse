## 1. Decision context

A large, systemically important automaker is weeks from failure amid collapsed sales, a cash crisis, and a rejected initial turnaround plan. The executive task force controlling bridge financing has forced leadership change and is using a 60-day window to extract a deeper restructuring that can preserve going-concern value while minimizing taxpayer exposure. The core decision is how to allocate losses across bondholders, labour, retirees, public funders, and ownership claims, and whether the outcome is completed consensually or through a court-supervised process.

## 2. Stakeholder assembly

- **Government task force / lead funder**: controls rescue financing and process leverage.
- **Company management**: newly installed leadership, focused on viability and operational reset.
- **Industrial union and retiree health trust**: represents labour and the legacy retiree obligation.
- **Unsecured bondholders’ committee**: dispersed financial creditors with large unsecured exposure.
- **Neighbouring governments / co-funders**: conditional public financiers seeking domestic industrial protection.

## 3. Final positions

### Government task force / lead funder
The task force’s position hardened around a true balance-sheet reset: old equity should be wiped, unsecured debt should take substantial losses, and the retiree trust must be materially restructured. It will support co-funding only if it does not preserve uneconomic capacity or give any stakeholder a veto over the required reset.

### Company management
Management converged with the task force on the central diagnosis: the current capital structure is dead and the company must be smaller, cheaper, and financeable. It supports deep creditor impairment, a material reduction of the retiree burden, and major but targeted footprint rationalization, while warning that any plan preserving too much legacy burden will simply delay failure.

### Industrial union and retiree health trust
The union accepts that the company needs a real reset, but insists the retiree trust remain durable and economically meaningful rather than being gutted for the sake of bondholder optics. It will accept labour concessions and plant rationalization only if the viable industrial core, jobs, and a real replacement for the trust are protected; it rejects being forced into economic parity that destroys the trust’s value.

### Unsecured bondholders’ committee
Bondholders accept that they are deeply impaired and that old equity is out, but they strongly object to being used as the main loss absorber while the retiree trust receives better effective treatment. Their core demand is comparable economic treatment within the unsecured stack, transparency on valuation, and no political subordination of creditor rights.

### Neighbouring governments / co-funders
The neighbouring governments are willing to help fund a rescue, but only if it preserves a credible domestic production and employment base in their jurisdictions. They want binding production commitments, a proportionate equity/governance stake for their contribution, and no use of their money to fund a deal that disproportionately sacrifices their plants.

## 4. Genuine conflicts (structurally incompatible positions)

1. **Retiree trust vs. bondholders**
   - Bondholders want pari passu or comparable economic treatment.
   - The union wants the trust preserved as a durable, real-value instrument, even if that produces different economics.
   - This is the central conflict because the value pool is finite and both claimants sit in the unsecured legacy stack.

2. **Economic rationalization vs. domestic protection**
   - Task force and management want plant/brand cuts based on viability.
   - Neighbouring governments want binding domestic plant and employment commitments.
   - These positions cannot both fully prevail if domestic capacity is one of the areas needing reduction.

3. **Public leverage vs. control**
   - Co-funders want equity and governance proportional to their risk.
   - The task force wants to preserve its control over restructuring design.
   - This creates a governance tension over who can shape the post-restructuring company.

4. **Speed vs. negotiated fairness**
   - The task force wants a fast final outcome, including court supervision if needed.
   - The union and bondholders both want the process to avoid discriminatory treatment.
   - Faster resolution may require imposing terms that some parties view as insufficiently negotiated.

## 5. Areas of convergence

- The company is not viable in its current form.
- A deep restructuring is unavoidable.
- Old equity should not survive.
- Unsecured creditors must take real losses.
- The retiree obligation must be materially changed.
- Labour concessions and plant rationalization are necessary.
- A court-supervised process is acceptable if it is the cleanest route to finality.
- The company needs a smaller, financeable operating footprint.

## 6. Key trade-offs for the decision-maker

1. **How much burden to place on bondholders versus the retiree trust**
   - More protection for the trust means deeper bondholder losses or more public support.
   - More parity with bondholders weakens the union’s demand for a durable retiree solution.

2. **How much plant capacity to preserve**
   - Preserving more domestic capacity helps labour and neighbouring governments but may leave the company too costly.
   - Cutting more capacity improves viability but risks political backlash and job losses.

3. **How much public money to commit**
   - More co-funding can improve financing and soften recoveries.
   - But public money without hard conditions risks subsidizing an unstable or politically distorted outcome.

4. **Out-of-court deal versus court-supervised process**
   - A consensual deal may reduce litigation and stigma.
   - A court process can overcome holdouts and impose class-based discipline.

5. **Who controls the reorganized company**
   - New money and public funders likely need meaningful control rights.
   - But too much control ceded to co-funders could complicate commercial management.

## 7. Integrative pathways

### Pathway A: Court-supervised, class-based restructuring
- Cancel old equity.
- Exchange unsecured bonds for a mix of new notes, reduced equity, and warrants.
- Convert the retiree trust into a reduced, durable replacement vehicle with some cash, securities, or contingent upside.
- Use court process to force class discipline and bind dissenters.
- Best for speed, finality, and enforceability.

### Pathway B: Consensual global settlement with side protections
- Offer bondholders a transparent recovery band and equity participation.
- Give the trust a durable replacement claim with clear present-value reduction.
- Add binding but limited domestic production commitments to satisfy neighbouring governments.
- Use governance allocations to balance new money, public co-funders, and impaired creditors.
- Best if near-unanimous support can be assembled quickly.

### Pathway C: Split-value compromise
- Preserve a smaller “core” industrial footprint in domestic jurisdictions.
- Allow the trust somewhat better durability but cap its economics.
- Give bondholders a meaningful equity upside to offset haircut severity.
- Use co-funder money to bridge the gap and reduce immediate cash strain.
- Best only if the viability model can support both the footprint and the legacy obligations.

## 8. Open questions

- What is the exact legal status of the retiree trust claim?
- What enterprise value and normalized EBITDA will anchor recoveries?
- How large is the needed post-restructuring leverage reduction?
- Which plants and brands are economically indispensable?
- What domestic production commitments are binding but still consistent with viability?
- How much equity should co-funders receive for their contribution?
- Is there enough time to reach a consensual lock-up before the deadline?
- What precise exchange terms will satisfy both pari passu concerns and trust durability?

## 9. Recommended next step

Move immediately to a single integrated term sheet built around a court-ready exchange framework, with three anchored inputs:
1. a valuation range and leverage target,
2. class-by-class recovery bands,
3. a defined list of retained versus closed plants with explicit domestic production covenants.

That package should be tested first for financeability, then for legal durability, and only then for political acceptability. If consensus does not quickly emerge, the task force should be prepared to launch the court-supervised route.

## 10. Emergent insights that only appeared through multi-turn interaction

1. **The real fracture is not “labour vs. capital” but “two unsecured constituencies competing for finite value.”**  
   The transcript clarified that the retiree trust and bondholders are the decisive distributional fight, not simply creditors versus workers.

2. **Parity is a legal and political issue, not just a valuation issue.**  
   Bondholders repeatedly shifted from accepting losses to demanding comparable treatment once it became clear the trust might receive a more durable wrapper.

3. **Domestic production became a separate bargaining axis.**  
   Neighbouring governments turned the restructuring from a pure financial reset into an industrial-policy negotiation with employment covenants and governance rights.

4. **Court supervision emerged as the most credible coordination device.**  
   The parties’ positions diverged enough that the process itself became part of the solution, not just a fallback.

5. **The company’s viability now depends on three linked reductions, not one:**  
   debt reduction, retiree-obligation reduction, and plant-footprint reduction. The multi-turn exchange made clear that any one of these left alone would likely destabilize the whole package.

6. **Ownership is not a separate afterthought; it is the settlement mechanism.**  
   Across rounds, equity became the currency for distributing losses, control, and upside among new money, public funders, and impaired claimants.
