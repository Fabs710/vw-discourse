From a restructuring valuation and insolvency perspective, the core question is not what each stakeholder prefers, but what enterprise value the business can credibly support after a reset, and how that value must be allocated to restore financeability.

## 1) Threshold conclusion

This company appears **balance-sheet insolvent and cash-flow distressed**. In that setting, the relevant test is whether the restructured entity can support:

- its **post-restructuring operating cash needs**,
- **adequate liquidity** through the cycle,
- and a **debt load consistent with normalized earnings**.

On the facts given, the old capital structure is not financeable. A viable restructuring likely requires:

- **deep unsecured creditor haircuts**,
- a **material reduction in the retiree health obligation’s present value**,
- and **operating footprint cuts** sufficient to raise EBITDA margin and lower breakeven volume.

## 2) Minimum viable enterprise value

Without company-specific forecasts, I can only state this as a valuation range, not a precise number.

For a legacy automaker under severe recessionary stress, a plausible minimum viable enterprise value is typically the value needed to support:

- a normalized post-cut EBITDA base,
- less capex and working capital needs,
- and debt service at a conservative leverage ratio.

A credible post-restructuring capital structure usually requires **net debt no more than about 1.5x to 2.5x normalized EBITDA**, and in a highly cyclical auto business often closer to the low end of that range.

So, if the reorganized company can only support, for example, **$3–5 billion of normalized EBITDA**, then sustainable funded debt is likely only in the **$4.5–12.5 billion** range, depending on cyclicality, capex intensity, and liquidity cushion. Anything materially above that would be vulnerable.

That means the implied enterprise value is not the distressed pre-restructuring headline value; it is the value of a **smaller, cheaper, liquidity-backed going concern**. If the company cannot get to that footing, the going-concern value collapses toward liquidation or break-up value.

## 3) Expected recoveries by claim class in a court-supervised process

Given the description, the company is likely **insolvent on a going-concern basis**, so recoveries would flow approximately as follows:

### Secured / superpriority financing
Not enough detail is given, but rescue funding advanced by the task force would typically sit at or near the top of the stack. Recovery likely near par if properly protected.

### Unsecured bondholders
For a company in this condition, unsecured bonds typically recover **well below par**, often in the **10% to 35%** range in a stressed automotive restructuring, sometimes higher if the enterprise value and government support are materially better than expected.

Given the facts here:
- severe distress,
- forced operational contraction,
- and a large legacy retiree claim competing for value,

I would expect unsecured bondholder recovery to cluster roughly around **15% to 30%** absent unusually strong post-restructuring earnings.

### Retiree health trust
If the trust is treated as an unsecured or quasi-unsecured legacy obligation, its recovery should be analyzed on present value, not face amount. In practice, that means a **significant PV reduction** is required.

A durable settlement might preserve some combination of:
- reduced cash payments,
- equity or warrants,
- or a capped claim.

But economically, I would expect the trust to be cut to a level broadly comparable to other unsecured recoveries unless there is a specific legal priority I am not addressing here. If it receives materially better economics than the bonds, that is a transfer decision, not a valuation outcome.

### Equity
The pre-restructuring equity is almost certainly **worth zero or effectively zero** in an insolvency-driven valuation unless there is a major new-money injection that leaves existing shareholders meaningful value, which appears unlikely from the facts given.

## 4) Haircut range required for financeability

To make the company financeable, the combined burden of unsecured bonds and retiree obligations must be cut to a level that the post-restructuring EBITDA can support.

In practical terms, that usually means:

- **unsecured bonds: 70% to 90% haircut**, depending on enterprise value and how much new money enters,
- **retiree health obligation: substantial present-value reduction, often 50%+ economically**, though the form may be stretched over time rather than an explicit headline haircut,
- **labor and footprint concessions: material enough to raise operating margin and remove loss-making capacity**.

If the company does not reduce fixed obligations enough to bring total post-restructuring leverage to a conservative level, the restructuring is not financeable and will likely fail again.

## 5) What the distribution implies

The task force’s direction is economically coherent if — and only if — it results in:

1. a **smaller operating footprint**,
2. a **lower legacy burden**,
3. and a **post-restructuring debt load aligned with conservative cyclical EBITDA**.

If the retiree trust is left too rich, then the bondholders will need even larger losses, or the company remains overlevered. If bondholders are protected too much, then the trust and/or public funding must absorb the difference. The value pool is finite.

My valuation-based assessment is:

- **Old equity is very likely wiped out**.
