We are closer on the diagnosis, but not yet on the package that can actually save the company.

## Where I agree

I agree with the task force, the union, and the creditors on the central point: **the old structure is dead**. We need:
- a real balance-sheet reset,
- meaningful cuts in overhead, plants, and brands,
- deep losses for unsecured creditors,
- and changes to the retiree obligation that make the business financeable.

I also agree that **time is the enemy**. If we drift, the company burns through the remaining liquidity and we end up with value destruction for everyone.

## Where I disagree

### 1) We cannot preserve the current retiree obligation in anything close to its existing economic form
To the union: I hear your insistence that the trust must remain real and durable. I agree with that in principle. But let me be direct: **the current economic burden is too large for the reorganized company to carry**.

If we try to “honor” it too generously, we do not rescue the company — we just postpone the failure. That is not a workable outcome for workers, retirees, dealers, or the public.

So I support:
- a conversion of the retiree claim into a durable instrument,
- but with a **material present-value reduction**,
- and with a structure that the restructured company can actually fund.

What I cannot support is any plan that leaves the trust economically close to its old value while forcing the business to absorb the rest. That destroys viability.

### 2) Bondholders are right that treatment must be principled, but they are not right to expect a high recovery
To the bondholder committee: I agree that unsecured creditors deserve principled treatment and that this cannot become arbitrary political punishment. But the valuation advice is clear: this is a distressed, likely insolvent enterprise on a going-concern basis.

That means **substantial haircuts are unavoidable**.

I do not accept the idea that because the trust is being reworked, unsecured bondholders are entitled to something near parity with their face claims. The business cannot support that. In an auto company this leveraged and this distressed, **a 70% to 90% haircut is not confiscation; it is the price of keeping the company alive**.

At the same time, I do agree with one point from the bondholders: if the trust receives real value, it should not be a cosmetic token. The overall package must be coherent and defensible. But coherent does not mean equal in a strict sense. It means financeable.

### 3) Labour peace matters, but plant and job guarantees cannot be absolute
To the union and neighbouring governments: I understand the need to protect the industrial core and avoid a restructuring that looks like dismemberment. I share that concern. We need:
- the strongest brands preserved,
- the dealer and service network intact,
- customer confidence maintained,
- and a viable plant footprint.

But I cannot promise blanket job or plant guarantees across the network. That would be dishonest. Some sites are structurally loss-making and some brands are not recoverable. If we do not rationalize meaningfully, the company will remain too costly and collapse anyway.

So yes to targeted preservation of the viable core. No to making every site a protected sacred cow.

### 4) Co-funding is helpful only if it does not freeze an uneconomic footprint
To the neighbouring governments: your participation is welcome if it reduces the funding burden and helps preserve a viable domestic industrial base. But I agree with the task force that co-funding cannot become a veto over necessary restructuring.

We can support enforceable commitments on domestic production where those commitments align with a viable plan. What we cannot support is a deal that preserves excess capacity for political reasons and leaves the company uncompetitive.

## What management can support

Here is the line I think is workable from an operating standpoint:

1. **Old equity is wiped out.**
   - There is no credible path for existing shareholders to retain value here.

2. **Unsecured bondholders take a deep haircut.**
   - The valuation range given is broadly consistent with what the company can support.
   - Recovery will likely be in the low teens to low 30s percent, depending on the final enterprise value and new-money structure.

3. **The retiree trust is materially restructured.**
   - It should receive a durable value-bearing instrument, but not at a level that makes the company unfinanceable.
   - A mix of reduced cash, securities, and perhaps a limited equity or warrant component is reasonable.

4. **The operating footprint is reduced.**
   - We need major plant and brand rationalization.
   - But cuts should be targeted to protect the strongest core brands, the dealer/service network, and the industrial base that can actually support future profitability.

5. **We complete this quickly, even if that means court supervision.**
   - If a consensual deal is not possible within the window, I would rather move through the court-supervised route than drift into insolvency.

## On ownership

We have not yet settled ownership, but from a management perspective:
- pre-restructuring equity should not survive,
- the new equity should primarily reflect **new value created by the restructuring and new financing**,
- and the allocation should be tied to the sacrifices made by each claimant class.

If the task force wants a clean, final structure, then the restructured company should be owned by those who provide the going-concern support and absorb the losses — not by legacy holders who did not solve the underlying problem.

## Bottom line

I support:
- a major restructuring,
- deep creditor losses,
- a material reduction of the retiree obligation,
- targeted but significant plant and brand cuts,
- and a fast process, including court supervision if needed.

I do **not** support:
- preserving the retiree claim too richly,
- pretending bondholders can be made mostly whole,
- or protecting every plant and brand regardless of economics.

What would move us now is not more general principle, but **a specific integrated package**:
- quantified creditor recovery,
- quantified retiree trust conversion,
- a defined list of core assets to preserve,
- and a financing structure that gets us through the next weeks without reopening the crisis.
