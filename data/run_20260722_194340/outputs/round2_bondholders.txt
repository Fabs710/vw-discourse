We appreciate the valuation advice, because it confirms the central point we have been making: **this is an insolvent, balance-sheet broken company, and there is no room for political storytelling that pretends otherwise**.

But let’s be equally clear about what that advice does **not** justify.

## Our position

### 1) We accept that the company needs a real reset
We are not denying the need for:
- plant and brand rationalization,
- a materially smaller operating footprint,
- labor and legacy-claim concessions,
- and a fast process.

We also accept that old equity is out of the money. Fine. That is not the issue.

The issue is whether the restructuring becomes a **fair, pari-passu allocation among unsecured claimants** or whether unsecured bondholders are used as the easiest bulk loss absorber while the retiree trust gets dressed up in a more politically acceptable wrapper.

That we will not accept.

---

## On the task force’s position

You say unsecured creditors must take substantial losses. We do not dispute that.

What we dispute is the implied assumption that **substantial** can mean **almost everything**, while the retiree trust gets a more durable instrument, better optics, or a better expected value because it is politically sensitive.

The valuation adviser gives a broad unsecured recovery range of roughly **15% to 30%**. That is a huge spread, and we are not going to let the lower end become a predetermined outcome simply because bondholders are dispersed.

If the company can support 30% for one unsecured constituency, then a materially worse result for another unsecured constituency must be justified by law, not convenience. If not, then you are asking us to subsidize the rescue through discriminatory treatment.

---

## On the union and retiree trust

We understand the union’s argument that the retiree obligation is an earned claim and not a handout. We are not saying it should be destroyed for free.

But we are absolutely opposed to the idea that the trust should be protected through a structure that leaves **bondholders economically subordinated in practice**.

If both are unsecured or economically similar legacy claims, then the default must be **comparable treatment in value terms**. If the trust gets:
- cash,
- equity,
- a replacement vehicle,
- or a durable funding stream,

then bondholders need **comparable consideration**, not a token stub or a forced wipeout.

The union keeps speaking about “dignity.” We speak about **contractual equality and recovery value**. Both matter. But dignity cannot become a rationale for preferential economics.

And let’s be candid: if workers are already making concessions, that does not entitle the trust to jump ahead of ordinary creditors. Those are separate claims. They do not cancel the legal rights of unsecured noteholders.

---

## On management’s proposed haircut range

You cite a 70%–90% haircut as if it were a neutral valuation outcome. It is not neutral; it is an outcome that depends entirely on how the value is allocated among competing unsecured claims.

We are prepared to take a real loss. We are **not** prepared to endorse a structure where:
- unsecured bonds are cut to the low teens,
- the trust receives better effective value,
- and the whole thing is then described as “financeable.”

Financeability is not a license for selectiveness. If the post-restructuring enterprise value is limited, then the reduction burden must be shared fairly across the unsecured stack.

If the company truly can only support that kind of haircut, then the right answer is not to isolate bondholders for the deepest damage while preserving the trust in a more favorable form. The right answer is to allocate recoveries on a principled basis.

---

## On the neighbouring governments

You want domestic production commitments tied to co-funding. That is your prerogative, but let’s not confuse public policy with creditor priority.

We are not here to underwrite a rescue that turns into an industrial-policy transfer at the expense of contractual creditors. If co-funding is needed, fine — but it must not be used to justify a restructuring that worsens unsecured recoveries in order to preserve politically favored plants.

If public money is buying domestic commitments, then say that plainly. But do not then ask bondholders to absorb the cost of those commitments through inferior treatment versus another unsecured claimant.

---

## On process

The court-supervised route is acceptable to us **only if it imposes disciplined, equal treatment among unsecured claimants**. If it becomes a mechanism to rubber-stamp a politically driven allocation, then it is not a rescue process; it is a confiscation process with a courtroom veneer.

We want this settled quickly too. But speed does not authorize unequal treatment.

---

## What we require

If you want our support, the package must include:

1. **Comparable economic treatment for unsecured bondholders and the retiree trust**
   - no stealth preference,
   - no disguised seniority,
   - no “durable” trust vehicle that leaves us worse off without legal justification.

2. **A real recovery for bondholders**
   - not a token equity slice,
   - not a wipeout,
   - and not terms materially worse than similarly situated unsecured claims.

3. **Transparency on valuation**
   - what enterprise value is being used,
   - what normalized EBITDA is assumed,
   - and how recoveries are being allocated.

4. **No political subordination of creditor rights**
   - urgency is real,
   - but urgency does not create a right to discriminate.

---

## Our bottom line

We are not blocking the rescue. We are blocking an **unfair** rescue.

If the final package gives the retiree trust materially better economics than unsecured bondholders, we will oppose it.

If the court process is used to force a principled pari-passu allocation and a defensible exchange with meaningful upside participation, then we can consider it.

But if the plan is:
- deep bondholder haircut,
- superior trust treatment,
- public support to paper over the inequity,
- and then a demand that we call it “shared sacrifice,”

then the answer from this committee is **no**.
