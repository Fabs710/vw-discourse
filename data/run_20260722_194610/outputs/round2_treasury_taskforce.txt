We are past the stage of broad principles. The issue now is whether the parties will accept a term sheet that creates a viable company, or whether we move to the court-supervised fallback and impose one.

Let me be direct.

## 1) On the overall shape of the deal
We need **deep, quantified sacrifice from everyone**:
- management: gone or heavily curtailed, with no comeback to the old governance;
- labour: meaningful wage, benefit, and workforce concessions;
- retiree trust: a real restructuring, not a protected island;
- unsecured bondholders: a substantial haircut and a meaningful conversion;
- plants/brands/dealers: fewer, leaner, and selected for viability, not politics.

If the deal does not produce a **materially lower fixed-cost base** and a **repaired balance sheet**, it is not a rescue. It is a delay mechanism.

## 2) On the retiree health trust
I disagree with both labour and bondholders if either suggests the trust is untouchable.

To labour: the trust cannot be treated as sacred if doing so pushes the company back into insolvency.  
To bondholders: the trust is not a reason to resist restructuring altogether.

My position is this:
- the trust should receive **restructured value**, not a full-face promise;
- that value can include **equity or a long-dated instrument**, but only at a level consistent with viability;
- it cannot be senior in practice to the extent that unsecured creditors are left carrying the whole burden.

## 3) On bondholder recovery
Bondholders will not get par. They will not get close to par.

A viable restructuring will require a **deep haircut** and **conversion of a large portion of unsecured claims into equity**. That is the price of keeping the enterprise alive. The fact that many bondholders are ordinary savers does not change the arithmetic. Neither does their size as a creditor class.

I will be blunt: if bondholders insist on “parity” with every other unsecured-like claim while refusing to take a serious hit, then they are helping force the court process. We are not financing a rescue that preserves old creditor economics and leaves the public to absorb the downside.

## 4) On labour, wages, plants, and jobs
We agree with management on the hard point: the company cannot keep everything.

But I reject any attempt to use “viability” as a blank check for indiscriminate plant closures or one-sided workforce cuts.

So the terms must be:
- **closures only where plants are structurally uncompetitive**;
- **retention of the strongest productive sites**;
- **workforce reductions, work-rule changes, and compensation adjustments** in exchange for the financing;
- **no preservation of unprofitable capacity just to avoid pain**.

Labour is right that cuts should be tied to a real industrial plan. But that industrial plan must be judged by one test: does it make the company financeable and profitable? If not, it is not a plan.

## 5) On neighbouring governments and co-funding
We welcome co-funding only on **binding, limited, and commercially relevant terms**.

I disagree with the idea that co-funders can purchase veto rights over the whole restructuring or dictate the footprint to preserve every domestic plant. That would turn emergency rescue financing into a political bargaining contest and would make the deal unworkable.

What co-funders can reasonably receive:
- a **proportionate equity stake** for their capital contribution;
- **governance rights limited to protecting their contribution**;
- **binding commitments on minimum domestic production or employment where commercially sustainable**;
- consultation rights on major footprint changes in their jurisdictions.

What they cannot receive:
- a de facto veto over needed closures;
- a rescue structure that forces the rest of the company to carry uneconomic domestic capacity indefinitely.

## 6) On mechanism: consensual vs court-supervised
Our preference remains a **consensual restructuring**, but only if it is real and fast.

Let me be explicit about the fallback:
- if the parties can reach a term sheet quickly that includes the necessary haircuts, conversions, and closures, we can continue bridge support;
- if they cannot, we will move to the **expedited court-supervised route** and seek to impose terms.

No one should mistake that for bluff. The financing clock is real, and the public cannot be the last equity investor in an unrestructured company.

## 7) The concrete anchor points we are prepared to work from
To end the ambiguity, here is the framework we expect the parties to negotiate against:

- **Bondholders:** substantial haircut plus meaningful equity conversion;
- **Retiree trust:** restructured claim with real but reduced value, likely a mix of equity/long-dated consideration, not a protected full-value claim;
- **Labour:** lower compensation burden, workforce reductions, and productivity concessions;
- **Plants/brands/dealers:** significant rationalization, with closures where economics require it;
- **Co-funders:** proportionate equity and limited governance rights, not veto power;
- **Management:** new leadership and no return to the old regime;
- **Process:** out-of-court if fast and comprehensive; court-supervised if not.

## 8) Where I am not flexible
I want to be very clear about the red lines:
- no open-ended subsidy;
- no deal that leaves the capital structure unviable;
- no special protection for any claimant class that forces taxpayers to fill the gap;
- no delay tactics dressed up as fairness;
- no rescue that preserves the old company under a new label.

If the stakeholders want public financing, they will accept a hard restructuring. If not, they should be prepared for the court process.
