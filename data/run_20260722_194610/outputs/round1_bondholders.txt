We agree on one thing: the old structure is broken, and a cosmetic rescue is worthless. But I need to be blunt — **“all stakeholders share sacrifice” cannot be code for making unsecured bondholders absorb a near-wipeout while others negotiate protected outcomes**.

### Where we agree
- The company needs a **real restructuring**, not a delay tactic.
- The **cost base and balance sheet** both have to be repaired.
- A **fast, final process** matters because value is disappearing every day.
- If a consensual deal is impossible, a **court-supervised process** may be the mechanism — we are not clinging to consensus as a principle.

### Where we strongly disagree
#### 1) The retiree trust cannot be treated as automatically superior to other unsecured claims
You keep describing the trust as if it must be preserved because it was negotiated with labour. I understand the politics, but **that does not make it a creditor class entitled to better treatment than similarly situated unsecured bondholders**.

If the trust gets materially better economics than the bondholders, then what you are really asking is for one unsecured class to subsidize another. That is not “shared sacrifice.” That is discrimination.

We will not accept:
- a structure where the retiree trust gets protected value while unsecured bondholders are shoved into a token recovery, or
- a deal where our claims are diluted without comparable consideration.

If the trust gets equity, warrants, or some other participation, then **bondholders must receive equivalent treatment on a fair basis**. Equal standing matters.

#### 2) We are not interested in funding a rescue that wipes out bondholders to preserve jobs
You say the company cannot keep all plants, all brands, and all jobs. Fair enough. But the answer cannot be: “therefore creditors take whatever is left.”

We are unsecured, yes. We are also legitimate contractual creditors — many are ordinary savers, not hedge funds. Framing us as speculators blocking a rescue is unacceptable. We did not cause management failure, and we should not be the first and deepest pool from which everyone else is paid.

#### 3) “Court-supervised” is not a threat that automatically moves us
We understand the leverage. We understand a court process can be completed without our consent. But do not assume that means we will volunteer to accept terms that are materially worse than what a court is likely to impose.

If the proposed exchange is genuinely close to what a court would deliver, and if it gives us **real participation and speed**, we can evaluate it. But if the offer is just a prepackaged wipeout dressed up as cooperation, we will resist it.

### What would move us
We are open to a settlement if it contains:
- **meaningful equity or warrants** for unsecured bondholders;
- **clear parity** with any recoveries given to the retiree trust, unless there is a legally defensible reason otherwise;
- **certainty and speed** of recovery, since time is working against all of us;
- a structure that does not leave us with far less than comparable unsecured claims.

### What will not work
- A token exchange that is effectively confiscatory.
- Better treatment for the retiree trust with no compensation to bondholders.
- A “shared sacrifice” story where unsecured creditors carry the bulk of the pain while others preserve their position.
- Any attempt to pressure us by calling us obstructionist when we are defending a valid creditor claim.

So our position is straightforward: **we are prepared to do our part, but not to be singled out for disproportionate loss.** If the rescue requires a deep restructuring, then make it deep and fair across unsecured classes. If not, then do not expect us to bless an unequal deal.
