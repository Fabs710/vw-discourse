We are prepared to engage on a hard restructuring. We are **not** prepared to sign off on a deal that treats workers and retirees as the easiest source of sacrifice while financial claims and management failures are handled more gently.

## Where we agree
- **The company needs a real restructuring.** No one here is arguing for cosmetic change.
- **The balance sheet and cost base must be repaired.**
- **Speed matters**, because delay destroys value and jobs.
- **A consensual deal is preferable** if it is fast, credible, and fair.
- **Some plant rationalization is unavoidable** if the company is to survive.

## Where we disagree — and strongly

### 1) The retiree health trust is not an ordinary unsecured afterthought
The task force keeps describing the trust as something that must be “restructured” down to whatever level fits the financing. That is not acceptable if it means the trust becomes a hollow shell.

This was a negotiated industrial obligation. Retirees earned it through their labor and the company’s own bargaining history. If you strip it down too far, you are not balancing sacrifice — you are pushing the burden onto people who already gave their working lives to the company.

What we will insist on:
- **real, durable value for the trust**, not a token promise;
- **equity, warrants, or other securities with actual upside and enforceability**;
- a structure that makes the obligation **survivable through the restructured company**, not just deferred until the next crisis.

We are open to changing the form. We are **not** open to destroying the substance.

### 2) Bondholder parity cannot become a veto over labour and retiree rights
The bondholders keep saying they must not be worse off than the trust because both are unsecured-like claims. That is too mechanical.

We are not asking for a free pass for the trust. We are saying the company’s industrial bargain cannot be rewritten as if retirees were simply another class of speculators. The trust is tied to earned benefits and workforce stability, not just a portfolio position.

If bondholders receive meaningful equity conversion, then yes, the trust must receive **real and commensurate value** as well. But that does not mean a rigid equality formula that forces the trust down to the lowest common denominator while ignoring the company’s history and the workforce’s sacrifice already made.

### 3) Labour concessions must be paired with job and plant commitments
We hear “lower compensation burden” and “workforce reductions.” That may be necessary in part. But we will not accept a blanket story that says viability justifies whatever closure plan the company prefers.

We need specifics:
- Which plants are truly nonviable?
- Which sites can remain productive with investment and new product allocation?
- How many jobs are being preserved, and on what timeline?
- What commitments are binding, not just aspirational?

If closures are unavoidable, they must be **the minimum necessary** and tied to a serious industrial plan, not used as a lever to maximize recoveries elsewhere.

### 4) Management must not be insulated while labour takes the hit
The task force says management is gone or heavily curtailed. Good. That needs to be more than rhetoric.

We will not support a deal where:
- the old leadership escapes accountability,
- the workforce bears the bulk of concessions,
- and the company then asks for labour peace as if that is a favour.

If there is sacrifice, it has to be shared by management, creditors, and the public financing structure too.

## What would move us

We can work from a term sheet if it includes all of the following:
- **A meaningful, enforceable equity or securities package for the retiree health trust**
- **A substantial haircut for unsecured bondholders, but not one that leaves the trust with a hollow recovery**
- **Job and plant retention commitments where facilities remain viable**
- **Wage and work-rule concessions only to the extent needed to support survival**
- **Governance protections so the trust’s claim is durable, not merely postponed**
- **No return of the old management regime**

## On neighbouring governments
We recognize their contribution and their domestic interests. But we do not accept a restructuring designed mainly around geographic politics while workers and retirees are treated as the flexible part of the equation.

If they co-fund, they should indeed receive a proportionate stake and voice. But not at the expense of turning domestic footprint concerns into a justification for deeper labour cuts or trust impairment.

## On process
We still prefer a consensual restructuring — **but only if the terms are real**. If the only “consensus” on offer is a deal that guts the trust and forces disproportionate concessions from workers, then we will not bless it simply because the clock is running.

A court-supervised process may be available, but we are not the ones threatening it. The question is whether the funders and creditors are prepared to accept a fair industrial settlement, or whether they intend to force a result that shifts too much pain onto labour and retirees.

## Bottom line
We are ready to do our part. But our bottom line remains unchanged:

- **No destruction of the retiree health trust**
- **No wholesale sacrifice of the workforce to protect creditor recoveries**
- **No plant plan divorced from industrial reality**
- **No deal that makes labour the residual claimant on everyone else’s failure**

If the task force wants our signature and labour peace, then bring a structure that gives the trust real value, preserves as many jobs and plants as possible, and shares sacrifice genuinely across all stakeholders.
