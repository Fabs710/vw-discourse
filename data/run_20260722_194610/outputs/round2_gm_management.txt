We are aligned on the **need for a real restructuring**, and I want to acknowledge that clearly. None of us can pretend the old structure is viable. The company needs a materially lower cost base, a repaired balance sheet, and a faster path to profitability. On that, I think there is broad agreement.

But I need to be equally direct about what we **cannot** accept.

## To the Task Force
Your framework is directionally right on necessity, but I disagree with any reading of it that assumes the company can simply be cut down by brute force without regard to what remains viable.

- **Yes**: deep sacrifice from management, labour, creditors, and the trust.
- **Yes**: meaningful rationalization of plants, brands, and dealer network.
- **No**: a deal that closes so much capacity, or strips so much value, that we end up with a shell that cannot compete.

The survival test has to be real. If the restructuring leaves us with no strong consumer-facing franchise, no credible product footprint, and no dealer confidence, then it is not a rescue — it is just a managed collapse.

On the retiree trust, I agree it cannot be treated as sacred in the sense of full face value. But I also agree with labour that it cannot be reduced to a hollow gesture. The right answer is **restructured value with durability**, not a token claim and not a protected seniority that crowds out the rest of the deal.

## To the Union and Retiree Trust
I understand and respect the argument that the trust reflects a negotiated industrial bargain. I am not dismissing that history.

Where I disagree is on the implication that preserving the trust’s substance necessarily means preserving it at a level that the business cannot support. It does not.

What the company can support is:
- a **real securities package** for the trust, likely equity or long-dated value-linked consideration;
- a structure that gives retirees **durable value**, not an empty promise;
- but **not** a claim so large that it prevents the balance sheet from being repaired.

I also need to be blunt on jobs and plants: I cannot promise to keep everything. We are going to have to close weak capacity. The question is whether we do that in a disciplined way that preserves the strongest brands, plants, and dealer network, or whether we try to protect too much and destroy the whole enterprise. I will not choose the latter.

## To the Bondholders
I agree with you on one important point: you should not be singled out for punishment while others are insulated. That would be neither fair nor financeable.

But I also disagree with the idea that parity means everyone gets treated the same regardless of the role each claim plays in the rescue. This company does not have the balance sheet to deliver anything near par to unsecured creditors, and it will not survive if that expectation drives the process.

So yes:
- **a substantial haircut**
- **meaningful equity conversion**
- **real participation in the upside**

But no:
- a demand for treatment that blocks the restructuring because it insists on creditor economics the business cannot support;
- no attempt to use legal formality to force an outcome that leaves the enterprise unfinanceable.

The real comparison is not who feels worst; it is whether the company emerges viable. If we do not get viability, nobody recovers much of anything.

## To the Neighbouring Governments
We recognize the importance of domestic plants and jobs in your jurisdictions, and if you co-fund, you should receive a proportionate stake and legitimate governance protections.

Where I disagree is on the idea that the restructuring can be governed primarily by territorial demands. We cannot preserve every site simply because it is politically important. Some capacity is not competitive enough, regardless of geography.

What I can support is:
- **binding commitments on minimum production or employment where commercially sustainable**
- **a proportionate equity stake**
- **limited governance rights tied to your contribution**
- **serious consultation on footprint changes**

What I cannot support is any arrangement that gives a co-funder a de facto veto over closures that are necessary for survival.

## On the process
Our preference remains a **consensual deal** if it can be struck quickly and credibly. But consensus is only valuable if the terms actually work.

If the parties cannot agree on:
- deep creditor haircuts,
- a real but reduced trust recovery,
- labour concessions,
- and a rationalized footprint,

then the court-supervised route becomes the only way to get finality in time.

I want to be explicit: management will engage constructively in either route, but we will not support a process that preserves the old company under new financing. That would fail the going-concern test, and that is the one outcome we cannot accept.

## Where I think the workable center still is
A viable package likely has to include:
- **substantial unsecured creditor conversion into equity**
- **a real but restructured retiree trust instrument, not a full-value claim**
- **labour concessions in wages, work rules, and headcount**
- **closures limited to structurally uncompetitive plants**
- **protection of the strongest brands, plants, and dealer network**
- **co-funder equity and limited governance rights**
- **new management and no return to the old regime**

That is the kind of structure that can preserve the enterprise, protect the core franchise, and give everyone at least something to stand on.

If we miss that center, the risk is not a tidy bargaining victory for one side. The risk is uncontrolled failure. And that is the only outcome I regard as fundamentally unacceptable.
