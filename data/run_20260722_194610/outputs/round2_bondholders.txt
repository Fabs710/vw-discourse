We appreciate the acknowledgement that this must be a real restructuring, not a delay tactic. But let me be equally direct: **the current framing still risks making unsecured bondholders the residual dumping ground while the retiree trust gets politically preferred treatment**. That is not acceptable.

## Our position

### 1) We agree on viability, but not on creditor discrimination
We agree with the task force and management that:
- the old capital structure is broken,
- the company needs a materially lower cost base,
- and a fast process matters.

But “viability” cannot be used as a blank cheque to **subordinate one unsecured creditor class to another**. The trust may have industrial history, but history does not create a legal or economic right to better treatment than equally unsecured bondholders.

If the trust receives equity, warrants, or long-dated value, then **bondholders must receive equivalent treatment on a fair basis**. Anything less is discrimination, not restructuring.

### 2) The retiree trust cannot be protected at our expense
To the union: we do not accept the claim that the trust’s negotiated character justifies better recovery than bondholders.

We are not asking to “destroy” retirees. We are saying:
- if the trust is unsecured in the restructuring,
- then it should be treated **in line with other unsecured claims**,
- unless there is a legally defensible ranking difference.

We will not accept a structure where:
- the trust gets a durable securities package,
- while bondholders are pushed into a token recovery or near-wipeout.

That is precisely the kind of unequal treatment that makes a consensual deal impossible.

### 3) “Deep haircut” is not a license for a confiscatory exchange
To management and the task force: yes, unsecured creditors must take a serious hit. We are not pretending par is available.

But there is a hard line between:
- a deep restructuring that gives us meaningful upside participation, and
- a confiscatory exchange that strips us down to a sliver while preserving preferred value for others.

We are open to:
- equity,
- warrants,
- contingent value rights,
- other upside-linked instruments,

but only if the package gives **real recovery value**, not a cosmetic conversion.

### 4) Court-supervised fallback cuts both ways
You keep saying the court route can be used to impose terms. That may be true. But do not assume that means bondholders should volunteer to accept the worst version of the deal.

If the court process is likely to produce:
- a fairer allocation across unsecured claims,
- clearer legal protections,
- and a more disciplined valuation,

then pushing us into a consensual exchange that is materially inferior is not a serious ask.

We are not bluffing. If the proposed deal is worse than what a court would impose, we will resist it.

### 5) On plants, jobs, and domestic footprint
We hear the political and industrial concerns. But bondholders should not be made to pay for domestic footprint commitments that are really public-policy objectives.

If neighbouring governments want to co-fund and secure domestic production, that is their bargain. It cannot be financed by **discounting our recovery further** to subsidize geography.

And to labour: we understand that job losses are painful. But the answer is not to load the burden onto financial creditors while demanding that the trust be protected because it is politically sensitive.

## What would move us
A settlement could move us only if it includes:

- **equal or economically equivalent treatment with the retiree trust** for similarly ranked unsecured claims;
- **meaningful equity or warrants** for unsecured bondholders;
- **no token exchange**;
- **clear valuation support** showing the recovery is not artificially depressed;
- **speed and finality**;
- and **no deal where bondholders are materially worse off than the trust without compensation**.

## What will not work
- A deal that gives the trust a preferred outcome and leaves bondholders with scraps.
- A “shared sacrifice” narrative that means creditors bear the bulk of the pain.
- A rescue that uses public leverage to force unequal treatment of legitimate contractual creditors.
- A restructuring that is only “consensual” because one side is being bullied into accepting a worse outcome than court would impose.

## Bottom line
We remain willing to engage constructively, but our core line is unchanged:

**No acceptance of materially worse treatment than comparable unsecured claims without compensation.**

If the rescue requires deep sacrifice, then it must be **fair sacrifice**. If not, we will not sign away legitimate creditor value simply to make the politics easier for others.
