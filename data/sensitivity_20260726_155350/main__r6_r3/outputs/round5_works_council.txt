Management, investors, and the family holding are still trying to shift the burden of proof onto the workforce while keeping closures and forced redundancies as a permanent threat. I reject that.

## To management
You say you need “material, durable, implementable” savings. I agree with the standard. I do **not** agree with your conclusion that this automatically legitimizes plant closures or compulsory redundancies.

The burden is on **you** to show, with plant-by-plant numbers, that the non-destructive route fails. So far you have not done that. You have asserted that internal measures “may not be enough,” but that is not proof. It is a preference for the harshest tool.

And let me be clear: if you cannot close the gap without threatening jobs and sites, then the problem is not labour resistance — it is a management plan that has not been properly designed.

## To the controlling family holding
You are right about one thing: a cosmetic compromise would be useless. But you are wrong to treat closures as a normal fallback that must remain on the table.

That is not responsible stewardship. It is a leverage strategy against the workforce. If you really care about long-term value, then you should support:
- binding site guarantees through 2030,
- binding job guarantees through 2030,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- no German plant closures,
- and real owner contributions.

Anything else means you are asking workers to finance competitiveness by accepting permanent insecurity.

## To institutional investors
You keep demanding proof that the no-closure route is sufficient. Fine — then demand proof from management, not concessions from labour.

You now have a clear test:
- plant-by-plant utilization,
- fixed costs by site,
- annual recurring savings by measure,
- owner and management contribution,
- product allocation commitments,
- and the residual gap.

But I will not accept a framing in which “not yet proven” becomes an excuse to keep closures alive. If the bridge is incomplete, the answer is to complete it — not to threaten shutdowns.

## To the regional state government
Your position is closer to ours, but it must become operational. A political rejection of closures is not enough if management keeps weaponizing them.

Use your leverage to secure:
- binding no-closure protection,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- binding investment and product commitments,
- and a real industrial plan that keeps every German plant open.

Do not let “economic reality” become a slogan for pushing workers into surrender.

## To the union
We are aligned on the essentials, and I support your insistence on reversibility. Any employee contribution must be:
- time-limited,
- fully reversible,
- and explicitly tied to binding guarantees.

But I want to be equally clear: we should not even begin from a premise that forced layoffs are an acceptable fallback. They are not.

## On the expert assessments
The experts confirmed what we already know:
- internal measures can contribute,
- but they may not fully substitute for footprint reduction if the gap is large.

That is exactly why management must prove the gap, not the workforce prove the opposite.

The expert also made an important point: a no-closure package can work if the gap is moderate and capacity can be adjusted internally. That is precisely why closures are not justified by default.

## What I accept
I accept discussion of:
- redeployment,
- early retirement,
- natural attrition,
- qualification,
- working-time flexibility,
- productivity gains,
- shift redesign,
- and a time-limited workforce contribution.

But only inside a settlement that contains:
- **binding site guarantees through 2030**
- **binding job guarantees through 2030**
- **no compulsory redundancies**
- **no German plant closures**
- **real co-determination in shaping the industrial plan**

## What I reject
I reject:
- closures as a standing fallback,
- compulsory redundancies,
- a “numbers first” process that leaves jobs exposed while management shops for a mandate,
- any weakening of co-determination,
- and any solution that shifts structural risk onto the workforce.

## My position in one sentence
If management wants labour to contribute, then management, owners, and investors must first stop treating German plants and German jobs as negotiable collateral.

If you want a real settlement, bring:
1. a quantified non-destructive savings bridge,
2. binding site and job guarantees through 2030,
3. management and owner contributions,
4. product and investment commitments,
5. and a genuine role for co-determination in the industrial plan.

Until then, my answer remains unchanged:

**No German plant closures. No compulsory redundancies. No dismantling of job security. No retreat from co-determination.**
