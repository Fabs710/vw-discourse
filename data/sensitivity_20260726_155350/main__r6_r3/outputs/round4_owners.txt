I will be direct.

The expert has confirmed what I already suspected: **a no-closure, no-forced-layoff package may help, but only if the competitiveness gap is not too large and if the company can materially cut fixed costs and capacity through internal means.** That is important. It also means the employee side cannot simply declare victory by repeating “no closures” as a principle. Principles do not close a cost gap.

## Where I agree
I agree with management, investors, and the expert on the core economic point:

- this is a **structural** problem;
- the solution must be **material and durable**;
- and a cosmetic compromise would destroy value.

I also agree with the works council, union, and state on one important practical point:

- a prolonged strike or governance deadlock would also destroy value.

So yes, both a weak deal and a destructive confrontation are unacceptable.

## Where I disagree with the employee-side position
The works council and union keep treating **no German plant closures** and **no compulsory redundancies** as if they were automatically compatible with a full competitiveness reset.

The expert did not say that. The expert said the opposite: **that is only feasible if the gap is moderate and the footprint problem is not too large.**

That matters.

So I do **not** accept the employee-side claim that:
- internal transfers,
- attrition,
- early retirement,
- working-time flexibility,
- productivity gains,
- and product allocation

are obviously enough. They may be enough. They may not be. That is exactly what still needs to be proven.

And I also do **not** accept the framing that keeping closures and redundancies as a fallback is “blackmail.” If the company has a real structural overcapacity problem, then any serious restructuring must retain a credible fallback until the numbers prove otherwise.

## Where I disagree with management
Management is still right on the need for hard restructuring, but it is not yet right on the method.

The expert made one thing clear: **if a closure-first approach causes deadlock, strikes, and governance failure, that is value destruction, not discipline.**

So I will not support management if it is effectively saying: “give us the right to force the issue, and we will sort the rest out later.” That is too risky for the enterprise and too likely to trigger the exact instability we must avoid.

What management still needs to do is the part it has not done convincingly enough:
- quantify the full gap,
- quantify the internal measures,
- show the residual gap after those measures,
- and only then argue for harder tools if needed.

## Where I disagree with the state
The state is right to reject deindustrialization by default. It is also right that jobs and sites matter regionally.

But the state cannot simply say “no closures” and assume the economics will take care of themselves. The expert just underlined that internal measures have limits, especially if true footprint rationalization is needed.

So I do **not** support any position that turns the blocking minority into a blanket veto on necessary adjustment. That would not be stewardship; it would be freezing the problem.

## On the expert’s assessment
The expert was the most useful voice in the room because it cut through rhetoric.

The key point is this:

> **The decisive question is the size of the gap after all non-destructive measures are quantified.**

That is the correct test.

So my position is:

- I am **open** to a no-closure, no-forced-redundancy settlement **if** the itemized bridge shows it is sufficient.
- I am **not** open to treating those red lines as valid regardless of the arithmetic.
- I am **not** open to a fake compromise that preserves uncompetitive structure under softer language.
- I am **not** open to a closure-first confrontation that destroys trust and value.

## What I want next
Before I support any final package, I want management to table a **proper quantified bridge**, with independent verification, showing:

1. current plant-by-plant utilization  
2. headcount structure and natural attrition potential  
3. annual savings from working-time flexibility  
4. annual savings from early retirement / redeployment  
5. annual savings from productivity and overhead cuts  
6. annual savings from management and owner contributions  
7. annual savings from product allocation and capacity rebalancing  
8. the **residual gap** after all of the above  
9. the exact point at which management says the no-closure route fails

That is the real decision point.

## My bottom line
My position remains unchanged, but now it is sharpened by the expert:

- **I support serious restructuring.**
- **I support quantified internal measures first.**
- **I support binding investment and product commitments.**
- **I support labour contributions if they are time-limited and reversible.**
- **I do not support a closure-first strategy.**
- **I do not support a package that pretends internal measures are sufficient without proving it.**
- **I do not support a long conflict that destroys value.**

If the numbers show that a no-closure settlement can fully restore competitiveness, I can support binding site and job guarantees through 2030, because that would protect the long-term asset.

If the numbers show a large residual gap, then the employee side and the state must stop acting as though closures are merely a management preference. At that point, we would need to decide whether they truly accept the economic reality or prefer to preserve a weaker company.
