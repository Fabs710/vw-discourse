I’ll be direct.

The facilitator’s “bridges” only work if they **do not cross our red lines**. For us, the core issue is still unchanged:

- **No German plant closures.**
- **No compulsory redundancies.**
- **No dismantling of the job-security framework.**
- **No restructuring imposed as a fait accompli.**

If a proposal cannot meet those conditions, it is not a compromise — it is workforce surrender.

## To Management
You keep asking for “structural certainty,” but what you are actually demanding is the right to break historic protections whenever the market gets difficult. I reject that.

Yes, there is a cost problem. Yes, there is overcapacity. No one denies that. But you have still not shown that the only way to address it is by threatening plant closures and forced layoffs.

What you have to answer is simple:

- How much savings come from **product allocation to German sites**?
- How much from **management and owner contributions**?
- How much from **working-time flexibility**?
- How much from **natural attrition, early retirement, redeployment, and qualification**?
- How much from **productivity and process improvements**?

Bring me a **real calculation**, not a closure threat wrapped in financial language.

If, after exhausting those tools, you still claim closures are necessary, then I will say it plainly: that is not proof of necessity. It is proof that management prefers the easiest weapon against labour.

## To the Controlling Family Holding
You say you do not want a reckless closure-first strategy. Good. Then stop talking as if closures are a normal benchmark of seriousness.

If you genuinely want long-term value, then support a package that protects the industrial base instead of destabilizing it. A settlement that triggers long strikes, destroys trust, and tears up co-determination is not value-preserving. It is value-destructive.

If you are serious about stability, then back:
- binding site guarantees through 2030,
- binding job guarantees through 2030,
- and a genuine industrial commitment to German production.

If you won’t support that, then you are not helping to build a durable solution; you are helping management pressure the workforce.

## To Institutional Investors
You keep asking for “verified structural cost reduction.” Fine. But stop pretending that only closures produce structure.

Structure can change through:
- shifts,
- working-time arrangements,
- internal transfer,
- attrition,
- qualification,
- product allocation,
- and reversible labour contributions.

If those measures are not enough in your view, then show me the numbers that prove it. Don’t just say “visible structural change” as if that automatically means site closure. It does not.

And let me be clear: a “phased agreement” is only acceptable if Phase 1 contains **no closures, no forced redundancies, and no permanent weakening of protections**. Otherwise it is just a delayed attack.

## To the Regional State Government
You ask what leverage the state would use. Use it now.

If your position is really no closures and no compulsory redundancies, then the state must enforce that through the supervisory board and through its political weight. Not later. Now.

And if management says the no-closure package misses some abstract target, then the answer is not to sacrifice jobs automatically. The answer is to demand from management and owners the missing contribution they are trying to shift onto workers.

The state should not act like a spectator while management tries to normalize structural damage. If the political and social costs matter, then your veto position matters too.

## To the Union
We are aligned on the essentials, and I want that stated clearly:
- no closures,
- no forced redundancies,
- real protection for the workforce.

Where I will be careful is this: any employee contribution must be **strictly time-limited, reversible, and tied to binding guarantees**. I will not accept a “temporary” sacrifice that becomes permanent erosion by another name.

That means if the union is discussing:
- working-time flexibility,
- moderation on wage claims,
- or productivity-based adjustments,

then the counterweight must be:
- site security,
- job security,
- and enforceable return provisions.

I will not ask workers to pay for management’s failure twice.

## On the facilitator’s three bridge models

### 1) Protected restructuring with measurable triggers
This is only acceptable if the “trigger” is **not** a backdoor to closures.

If the trigger mechanism means: “if the target is missed, closure comes back automatically,” then no. That is just a hostage clause.

If the trigger mechanism means: “if targets are missed, all parties reopen negotiation and management must first exhaust non-destructive alternatives,” then it is at least discussable.

But I repeat: the starting point must still be **no closures and no compulsory redundancies**.

### 2) Site guarantees in exchange for deeper internal restructuring
This is the only direction that comes close to acceptable, because it respects the red lines.

Yes, we can discuss:
- internal transfers,
- redeployment,
- qualification,
- shift flexibility,
- temporary adjustments,
- natural attrition,
- and product reallocations.

But those are acceptable only if they are part of a package with:
- binding site guarantees,
- binding job guarantees,
- and a protected co-determination role in steering the industrial plan.

### 3) Phased restructuring with a joint governance body
A joint body is not a problem in itself. But it must not become a mechanism for slowly normalizing closure logic.

We can discuss a joint steering structure if it:
- includes employee representation with real influence,
- cannot impose closures unilaterally,
- and cannot be used to bypass the supervisory board’s employee bloc.

If it is just a consultation club while management keeps closure rights in reserve, then it is not joint governance. It is stage-managed pressure.

## Final point
Management should stop saying “if you cannot prove equivalent savings, closures remain on the table.” That is not negotiation. That is coercion.

If the company wants the workforce to contribute, then it must first commit to not using the workforce’s livelihood as the fallback solution.

We are ready to negotiate a serious industrial future.  
We are **not** ready to negotiate the closure of German plants or the introduction of forced layoffs.

That is my position, and it has not moved.
