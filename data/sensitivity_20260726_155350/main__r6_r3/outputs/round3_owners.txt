## To management
I agree with you on the diagnosis: this is a structural competitiveness problem, not a temporary dip. I also agree that a cosmetic compromise would be value-destructive.

Where I do **not** agree is in the way you keep leaning on closures and compulsory redundancies as if they were the default proof of seriousness. They are not. They are the proof that a plan has failed to achieve acceptance under this governance structure. If you cannot get a durable solution through the supervisory board, the state’s blocking rights, and labour peace, then the “hard” plan becomes a value-destroying confrontation, not a restructuring.

So I need to be explicit:

- I support material and durable cost reduction.
- I support capacity reduction.
- I support a real labour-cost contribution.
- I do **not** support a closure-first approach.
- I do **not** support forcing through a plan that triggers prolonged conflict and reputational damage.

You still have not shown that the non-destructive tools are insufficient in quantified terms. Until you do, closures remain a last-resort failure case, not a preferred instrument.

## To the works council and union
I hear your red lines:
- no German plant closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- no dismantling of protections.

I understand why you say that. I also agree that the workforce should not be asked to carry the adjustment alone.

But I will not endorse a settlement that simply preserves all current structures and calls that a solution. If the package does not materially reduce the cost base and rationalise capacity, then it is not protecting jobs — it is postponing a worse reckoning.

So I agree with you on the **direction** of a negotiated settlement:
- working-time flexibility,
- redeployment,
- natural attrition,
- early retirement,
- productivity gains,
- product allocation,
- time-limited employee contribution.

What I do **not** accept is turning those tools into a substitute for measurable structural change. If you want no-closure, no-forced-layoff protection, then show a credible package that actually closes the gap.

## To the regional state government
I respect your role, and I agree that this company is not a generic asset. It has regional consequences and a special governance reality. I also agree that a destructive conflict would be damaging.

But I cannot accept the state treating closures and compulsory redundancies as categorically off limits if the economics require deeper adjustment. That would freeze the problem and shift the burden onto the company’s long-term viability.

At the same time, I do **not** want the state’s leverage to be used to force an economically weak compromise. The state should help make a durable solution possible, not shield the company from necessary correction. If you want no closures, then help produce a package with real numbers that can withstand governance and market scrutiny.

## To institutional investors
Your standard is the correct one: durability, competitiveness, and value creation. I share that.

Where I differ from the more closure-oriented view is this: a package that is economically tough but politically impossible is not value-maximising. The market does not reward theoretical discipline if implementation collapses into strikes, deadlock, and governance paralysis.

So I agree with you on the need for:
- verified structural cost reduction,
- real capacity change,
- and a path back to target margins.

But I do **not** accept closures as a benchmark in themselves. The real benchmark is whether the company regains competitiveness in a way that can actually be delivered in this governance environment.

## My position
I want a settlement that:

1. restores competitiveness and margins,
2. reduces overcapacity materially,
3. includes a real labour contribution,
4. is implementable under co-determination and the special statute,
5. avoids value-destructive instability.

What I will not support:
- a fake compromise,
- a frozen structure,
- or a plan that relies on escalation and coercion rather than governance.

## What I need next
If the employee side wants to keep closures and compulsory redundancies off the table, then bring a package with numbers:
- exact capacity reduction,
- exact cost reduction,
- exact labour contribution,
- exact management/owner contribution,
- exact investment and product commitments.

If that package is real and sufficient, I will support it.

If it is not sufficient, then the company still has a competitiveness gap — and I will not pretend otherwise.

So my position remains unchanged:
**I support serious restructuring. I do not support value-destructive instability. And I will not endorse a settlement that preserves uncompetitive structure under a softer name.**
