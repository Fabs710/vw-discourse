I will be equally clear: **I cannot support a restructuring path that starts from plant closures in Germany, compulsory redundancies, or a one-sided burden shift onto the workforce.** That would cross a line for the state as a long-standing anchor shareholder and political actor.

Where I do agree with management is this:
- The company clearly has a competitiveness problem.
- A mere postponement of adjustment would be irresponsible.
- We need a durable solution, not a cosmetic truce.
- Any package must protect the company’s long-term industrial viability.

But I disagree sharply with the conclusion that closures and major layoffs must remain the default answer.

From our perspective, the central question is not whether there must be adjustment — there must — but **how** that adjustment is designed. If you want the regional state to support a settlement, then it must meet three conditions:

1. **No plant closures in Germany**
   - Not as a bargaining chip, and not as an “open option” that hangs over the process.
   - The regional economic damage from a closure would be severe and lasting, and it would undermine trust in the company’s role as an industrial anchor.

2. **No compulsory redundancies**
   - Workforce reduction, if unavoidable, must come through natural attrition, internal transfers, early retirement, and other socially responsible instruments.
   - A negotiated transition is acceptable; forced job destruction is not.

3. **A credible industrial and investment commitment**
   - If employees are asked for sacrifices, they need binding commitments in return:
     - product allocation,
     - future investment,
     - site security,
     - and a real path back to competitiveness.

To management I would say: your position is too narrow if it assumes that only closures and layoffs can generate the needed savings. That may be the easiest accounting answer, but it is not automatically the best industrial or political answer. A company like this has other tools:
- capacity rationalisation without closure,
- working-time flexibility,
- temporary labor-cost adjustments,
- internal redeployment,
- productivity agreements,
- and a negotiated contribution from the workforce.

To the works council and union, I would say: I support your red lines on closures and compulsory redundancies, but the workforce should also be prepared to engage seriously on a **balanced and time-limited contribution** if — and only if — it is tied to binding guarantees and real investment. That is not surrender; it is responsible co-ownership of the company’s future.

What I will not endorse is a package where:
- German sites are weakened or closed,
- jobs are cut massively,
- the state loses meaningful influence,
- and the burden is pushed predominantly onto employees while management claims “competitiveness” as a blanket justification.

If there is a serious alternative package that preserves sites, protects the regional economic base, and restores competitiveness through negotiated means, then the state can be constructive. If not, then you should expect us to use every formal right we have.

So my message is: **we are open to restructuring, but not to deindustrialisation by another name.** Bring forward a plan that protects German employment and site continuity while delivering real competitiveness gains — otherwise, it will not have our support.
