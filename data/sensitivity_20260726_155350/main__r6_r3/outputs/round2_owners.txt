I will respond plainly.

## To management
I agree with your diagnosis more than with the employee side’s framing: the company does have a structural competitiveness problem, and a cosmetic compromise would be value-destructive. If German costs and capacity remain unchanged in substance, the company will continue to miss its margin target and weaken the core asset we all depend on.

Where I disagree with you is in the way you are still presenting the choice.

If you keep saying “closures and compulsory redundancies remain on the table” without first showing a negotiated path that can realistically pass co-determination, the state’s blocking rights, and labour resistance, then you are not solving the problem — you are increasing the risk of a prolonged confrontation that destroys value faster than any saved cost.

I do **not** support a reckless closure-first strategy. I need a package that is:
- materially effective,
- durable,
- and implementable under this governance structure.

So: yes, hard adjustment is necessary. No, I will not back an approach that looks economically neat on paper but triggers deadlock, strikes, reputational damage, or a governance breakdown.

## To the Works Council and Union
I understand and respect the political and industrial logic behind your red lines, but I do **not** accept them as absolute if they prevent a credible restructuring.

“No plant closures ever” and “no compulsory redundancies ever” may be understandable as bargaining positions, but they are not sufficient answers to a structural overcapacity and cost problem. If the result is that the company remains uncompetitive, then you are preserving jobs today at the expense of the company’s ability to sustain jobs tomorrow.

That said, I agree with you on one key point: employees must not be asked to carry the burden alone.

If you want the shareholder side to support a no-closure path, then it must be backed by:
- real and measurable capacity reduction,
- a meaningful labour-cost contribution,
- productivity gains that are enforceable, not aspirational,
- and a binding industrial plan with investment and product commitments.

Without that, “no closures, no layoffs” becomes a demand to freeze the adjustment onto the balance sheet indefinitely. I cannot support that.

## To the Regional State Government
I share your concern about regional industrial damage and I do not dismiss the role of the company as an anchor employer. But I cannot accept the idea that closures and compulsory redundancies are categorically off limits if the business case demands structural change.

If the state wants to prevent a destructive outcome, it should use its influence to help build a settlement that is economically real, not simply politically comforting. That means backing a package with:
- serious savings,
- real capacity rationalisation,
- and enough flexibility to survive implementation.

I agree that long-term value is more than a short-term accounting gain. But long-term value also cannot mean maintaining an uncompetitive footprint because the political cost of change is uncomfortable.

## To institutional investors
Your standard is the right one: the package must be durable and must restore competitiveness. I agree with that.

But I would caution against over-indexing on closures as the benchmark of seriousness. The real test is whether the final package changes the economics enough to restore margins and fund future investment, while remaining implementable in this governance environment.

A settlement that looks tough but cannot be delivered is not value-creating. A settlement that preserves everything unchanged is also not value-creating. We need something in between that is still materially decisive.

## My position
Let me be explicit about where I stand:

- I support a **serious restructuring**.
- I support **durable cost and capacity reduction**.
- I support a **negotiated solution** if it is real.
- I do **not** support a fake compromise that freezes the current structure.
- I do **not** support a closure plan that is likely to trigger prolonged and destructive instability without a credible implementation path.

## What I would need to see next
Bring forward a concrete package with:
1. quantified capacity reduction,
2. quantified labour-cost contribution,
3. quantified productivity and flexibility gains,
4. binding investment and product commitments,
5. a governance path that can actually pass.

If the employee side and state want to avoid closures and forced redundancies, they need to prove that their alternative delivers **equivalent durable savings** and not merely social reassurance.

If they cannot, then management’s restructuring case remains the stronger one in principle — but it still must be converted into a form that can actually survive the company’s governance reality.

I am not moving on the core objective: restore competitiveness, protect long-term enterprise value, and avoid value-destructive instability.
