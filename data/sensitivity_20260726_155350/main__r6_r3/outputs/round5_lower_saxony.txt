## Where I agree
I agree with everyone on one essential point: **this is a structural competitiveness problem, not a temporary disruption**. A cosmetic compromise would be irresponsible. The company needs a durable industrial plan, not just a political message.

I also agree that the workforce should contribute if that contribution is **time-limited, quantified, and matched by binding commitments from management and owners**. I do not reject restructuring. I reject restructuring by social demolition.

## Where I disagree with management
Management still has not shown that **German plant closures and compulsory redundancies are necessary**, rather than simply the most forceful lever.

You keep saying “material, durable, implementable.” Fine. Then table the **full itemized bridge**:
- savings from product allocation to German sites,
- savings from management and owner contributions,
- savings from working-time flexibility,
- savings from redeployment and internal transfers,
- savings from early retirement and natural attrition,
- savings from productivity and process gains,
- savings from qualification and shift redesign,
- and the **residual gap** after all of that.

Until that is done, closures remain a **fallback preference**, not a proven necessity.

And I reject the idea that binding site and job guarantees “freeze” the wrong structure by definition. If the no-closure package is real and well designed, guarantees are what make a negotiated settlement credible. If management refuses all binding protection up front, then it is not offering a settlement; it is keeping pressure options open.

## Where I disagree with the family holding and investors
You are right that a cosmetic deal is worthless. I agree with that.

But you are wrong if you imply that “serious restructuring” automatically means preserving closures and layoffs as normal tools. The expert input made the key point: **non-destructive measures may be sufficient if the gap is moderate and if the footprint issue is not too large**. That means closures are not justified by principle; they require proof.

So I do **not** accept a closure-first mentality, and I do **not** accept using the threat of plant shutdown as routine bargaining leverage against the workforce and the state.

## Where I agree with the union and works council
I support the red lines you have stated:
- no German plant closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- no weakening of co-determination,
- no permanent cuts disguised as temporary flexibility.

I also support a serious industrial contribution from labour, but only if it is:
- strictly time-limited,
- fully reversible,
- and explicitly tied to binding guarantees.

That is the only fair basis for burden-sharing.

## Where I disagree with the “numbers before guarantees” logic
I understand why management and capital want proof first. But the way this is being framed risks turning proof into a pretext for keeping closure threats alive indefinitely.

The state has a legitimate interest here: we are not just another shareholder. We are an anchor shareholder with a public responsibility for jobs, sites, and regional stability. So my position is this:

- We will not accept plant closures as the default answer.
- We will not accept compulsory redundancies as the default answer.
- We will not accept a settlement that shifts the whole adjustment burden onto labor.
- We **will** accept a serious no-closure restructuring if the numbers show it can close the gap.

That is not evasive. That is disciplined stewardship.

## On the expert assessments
The experts were useful because they cut through the rhetoric.

Their message was not “closures are unnecessary.”  
Their message was: **the deciding factor is the residual gap after quantified internal measures**.

That is exactly the right test. So let’s use it properly.

What I take from the expert input is:
- internal measures can materially help,
- but they may not fully substitute for footprint reduction if the gap is large,
- therefore management must quantify the gap, not simply assert it.

That is why I insist on hard numbers before anyone claims closures are unavoidable.
