From the perspective of dispersed institutional shareholders, the key issue is not who “wins” the confrontation, but whether the outcome restores **credible, durable competitiveness** and protects long-term equity value.

So I’ll be direct:

## Where we agree
I agree with management, and partly with the controlling family, on the core diagnosis:
- the German cost base is too high,
- the current capacity footprint appears misaligned with demand,
- and the company cannot solve a structural margin problem with a cosmetic patch.

I also agree with the state, works council, and union on one important point:
- a prolonged, destructive conflict would destroy value faster than it creates it.

## Where we disagree
I do **not** support an outcome that simply preserves the current structure and calls it a compromise.

That means:
- **No to a settlement that leaves the cost base structurally uncompetitive.**
- **No to guarantees that amount to freezing inefficiency.**
- **No to delay disguised as social responsibility.**

At the same time, I also do **not** support a plan that relies on headline closures and forced redundancies as the only lever, if that triggers a prolonged strike, governance deadlock, and operational disruption that wipes out the intended savings.

So from our side, the standard is simple:

## What we need to see
A package that delivers:

1. **Material, durable cost reduction**
   - not just one-off savings
   - not temporary wage deferrals
   - not vague productivity promises

2. **Clear capacity rationalisation**
   - the company should explain how it will remove overcapacity
   - if that can be done without plant closures, fine
   - if not, then the alternative must still be equally credible and enforceable

3. **A real path back to target margins**
   - we want to see a believable bridge to the return-on-sales target
   - not just strategic language about “future competitiveness”

4. **Deal certainty**
   - a structure that can actually be implemented under this governance regime
   - because a package that cannot pass co-determination and triggers endless conflict is not value-creating

## Our position on the current lines being drawn
### To the Works Council and Union
Your insistence on protecting jobs and sites is understandable, but **“no closures, no compulsory redundancies” as an absolute position is not financially credible unless you can show equivalent savings elsewhere**.

If the alternative is:
- weaker margins,
- continued overcapacity,
- and a permanent drag on returns,

then we cannot back that. Institutional capital will simply reprice the risk.

### To Management
You are right that the problem is structural. But you still need to show that your plan is:
- economically superior,
- implementable,
- and not so confrontational that it destroys more value than it saves.

A plan that is numerically strong but politically impossible is not a good plan.

### To the State and Family Owners
We understand the political and long-term ownership constraints. But those constraints do not remove the market reality:
- the company must earn its cost of capital,
- and it must compete against lower-cost producers.

If the controlling bloc wants shareholder support for a negotiated solution, it needs to back a package that is truly decisive.

## Our bottom line
We are **not** attached to closures as an ideological first choice. But we are also **not** willing to finance a status quo that keeps the company structurally impaired.

So our support is available only for a settlement that credibly delivers:
- durable savings,
- meaningful capacity adjustment,
- and a realistic return to competitive margins.

If the final package does not do that, then from a capital markets perspective it should not be supported.
