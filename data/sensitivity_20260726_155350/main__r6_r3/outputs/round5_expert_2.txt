From a forensic restructuring and industrial-finance perspective, the expert’s statement is directionally correct:

- a **no-closure / no-forced-redundancy** package can generate **material savings**,
- but it **usually cannot fully replicate** the fixed-cost relief of a **plant closure / footprint reduction** unless the gap is **moderate** and the company can absorb excess capacity internally.

What can be concluded from the record so far:

## 1) What the no-closure package can plausibly deliver
A package built on:
- working-time flexibility
- wage moderation / temporary employee contribution
- redeployment
- natural attrition
- early retirement
- shift redesign
- productivity improvement
- overhead reduction
- product allocation / complexity reduction

can reduce recurring cost in a meaningful way. In plant-level terms, the biggest savings usually come from:
- lower direct labor hours,
- fewer paid hours per unit,
- less overtime / weekend premium,
- lower indirect labor,
- reduced overhead,
- and some capacity rationalization via shifts rather than sites.

## 2) What it usually does not fully eliminate
If there is true structural overcapacity, internal measures often leave residual costs that are hard to remove without footprint reduction:
- site overhead,
- utilities / maintenance / logistics duplication,
- indirect labor,
- underabsorbed fixed manufacturing cost,
- and the strategic cost of too many plants for the volume base.

Those are the costs that closures usually address most decisively.

## 3) Timing matters
Even if the no-closure package is economically viable in the long run, it may still be weaker if management needs:
- rapid margin repair,
- durable capacity reset,
- or bankable savings within 12–24 months.

Internal measures tend to be:
- slower to realize,
- more dependent on compliance,
- and more exposed to renegotiation risk.

A closure/consolidation plan is usually:
- more disruptive,
- but more certain on fixed-cost removal.

## 5) For decision purposes
The key accounting test is:

**Residual recurring gap after all non-destructive measures**  
vs.  
**Incremental fixed-cost relief from footprint reduction**

If the residual gap is:
- **small to moderate**: a no-closure deal may be sufficient and more value-preserving.
- **large**: the no-closure deal is likely insufficient on an economic basis, even if socially preferable.

## 6) Bottom line
So the expert’s answer is not “closures are required” — it is:
**the sufficiency of a no-closure settlement depends on the size of the residual gap, the timing of savings, and the plant-level fixed-cost structure.**
