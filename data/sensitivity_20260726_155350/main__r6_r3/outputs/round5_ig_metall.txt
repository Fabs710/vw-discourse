## To management
You still have not made the case that **plant closures and compulsory redundancies are necessary** rather than simply preferred.

Saying the company needs a “material, durable, implementable” solution is not enough. We agree on the objective. We disagree on your method.

If you want us to accept a serious restructuring, then table the **full quantified bridge**:
- plant-by-plant fixed costs and utilization,
- savings from product allocation,
- savings from management and owner contributions,
- savings from working-time flexibility,
- savings from redeployment and internal transfers,
- savings from early retirement and natural attrition,
- savings from productivity and process gains,
- and the **residual gap** after all of that.

Until that is on the table, closures and forced redundancies remain a threat, not a proven necessity. And I will say it bluntly: if you keep them as a standing fallback while asking the workforce to surrender protection first, that is not bargaining in good faith.

## To the works council
I agree with your core red lines:
- no German plant closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- no dismantling of job security,
- no weakening of co-determination.

I also agree that the workforce should not be asked to bear the whole burden. Any contribution from employees must be:
- **time-limited**
- **fully reversible**
- **quantified**
- and tied to **binding guarantees**

Where I would sharpen the line is this: we should not accept a process where management keeps closure options alive as a pressure tactic while claiming it is merely “prudence.” That is exactly how permanent insecurity gets smuggled in.

## To the regional state government
Your stance is closer to ours, and that matters. But support in principle is not enough.

If the state opposes closures and compulsory redundancies, then it must use its leverage to make that real:
- insist on a quantified no-closure package,
- insist on management and owner contributions,
- insist on product commitments to German sites,
- insist on binding site and job guarantees through 2030,
- and insist that workers do not become the residual variable.

If management says the no-closure package misses the target, then the answer is not to open the door to layoffs. The answer is to close the gap where it belongs: management, owners, product planning, overhead, and internal restructuring.

## To the controlling family holding
You are right that a cosmetic compromise would be worthless. But you are wrong if you think credibility requires keeping closures alive as a normal bargaining tool.

That is not stewardship. That is leverage.

If you truly want long-term value, then support a package that protects the industrial base while making the restructuring real:
- binding site guarantees through 2030,
- binding job guarantees through 2030,

If you refuse that, then you are not anchoring the company. You are just preserving the option to pressure the workforce later.

## To institutional investors
You keep asking for proof that the no-closure route is sufficient. Fair enough. We are not asking you to accept faith-based restructuring.

But the burden is on management to prove the gap, not on workers to prove a negative. So demand the **numbers**:
- plant-by-plant utilization,
- fixed costs by site,
- annual recurring savings by measure,
- management/owner contribution,
- product allocation commitments,
- and the residual gap.

What I reject is the jump from “not yet proven” to “therefore closures must remain on the table.” That is not analysis; it is a preference for the harshest tool before the alternatives are exhausted.

## On the expert assessments
The experts said two things that matter:
1. Internal measures can materially help.
2. They may not fully substitute for footprint reduction if the gap is large.

That is precisely why the arithmetic must be shown. It is **not** a license to keep closures as a default fallback. It is a reason to quantify the bridge properly.

And the expert also confirmed something else: a no-closure package is feasible if the gap is moderate and if capacity can be adjusted internally. So no, closures are not automatically justified by the mere existence of a restructuring problem.

## On the bridge proposals

### 1) Phased no-closure agreement with evidence-based review
This is the most reasonable starting point — **if** the review cannot be turned into a backdoor closure trigger.

Phase 1 should contain:
- no closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- time-limited employee contribution,
- redeployment,
- early retirement,
- attrition,
- working-time flexibility,
- productivity measures,
- management/owner contributions,
- and investment/product commitments.

If targets are missed, the response must be **renewed negotiation and exhaustion of non-destructive measures first**. Not automatic escalation to closures.

### 2) Protection-for-transformation package
This is the direction closest to acceptable because it starts from protection, not destruction.

But protection must be real:
- binding site guarantees through 2030,
- binding job guarantees through 2030,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- no German plant closures,
- real product and investment commitments,
- quantified and shared burden from management, owners, and labour.

If the package asks workers for flexibility but does not protect them from structural insecurity, then it is not transformation. It is one-sided adjustment.

### 3) Joint governance body
A joint body can help only if it has substance:
- full transparency,
- real employee influence,
- access to plant-level economics,
- and no way to bypass co-determination.

If it is just a consultation club that delays conflict while preserving closure rights in the background, then it is not joint governance. It is window dressing.
