## Where we agree
- We agree with management, the family holding, and the expert that this is a **structural** competitiveness problem.
- We agree with the works council, union, and state that a prolonged strike or governance deadlock would be **value-destructive**.
- We agree that a cosmetic settlement is unacceptable.

## Where we disagree
### To the works council and union
Your red lines are clear:
- no German plant closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- no weakening of protections.

We understand the logic, but from an investor perspective those red lines are only acceptable **if the alternative bridge is proven to close the full recurring cost gap**. Right now, it is not proven. The expert’s point was decisive: a no-closure package may work only if the gap is moderate and the fixed-footprint problem is not too large.

So we do **not** accept a settlement based on faith that internal measures will be enough. We need:
- plant-by-plant output and fixed-cost data,
- quantified savings from attrition, redeployment, working-time flexibility, productivity, and overhead cuts,
- a clear residual gap,
- and a credible timetable for restoring return on sales.

Without that, “no closures” becomes a promise to preserve underutilized capacity, and that is not an investment case.

### To management
We agree you should not accept a cosmetic compromise. But you still have not provided the quantified bridge that proves closures are necessary. That matters.

You are right that internal measures do not automatically substitute for footprint reduction. But you have to show the actual residual gap after all non-destructive levers are exhausted. Until then, closures and forced redundancies remain a **fallback**, not a demonstrated necessity.

What we will not support is a closure-first posture that triggers deadlock, strikes, and implementation failure. That would destroy value faster than it creates it.

### To the state and family holding
We support your rejection of a reckless closure-first confrontation. But we do **not** support using blocking rights to freeze the company into a structurally uncompetitive footprint.

If no-closure is to be defended, it must be defended with numbers, not political vetoes. If the bridge is insufficient, then the burden cannot simply be dumped on capital markets by pretending the gap has disappeared.

## Our position
We are open to a no-closure package **only if** it is independently verified to do all of the following:
1. close the full recurring cost gap on a steady-state basis,
2. materially reduce capacity utilization pressure,
3. deliver durable margin repair,
4. include enforceable milestones and review gates,
5. avoid a long conflict that burns value.

If those conditions are met, then yes, we can support binding site/job guarantees through 2030, because they would protect a competitively viable asset.

If those conditions are **not** met, then we will not endorse a settlement that merely preserves the cost problem under softer language.
