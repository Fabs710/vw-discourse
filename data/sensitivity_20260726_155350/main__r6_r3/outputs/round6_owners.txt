## My position
The family holding supports a **serious restructuring** only if it is:
- **material**
- **durable**
- **implementable**
- and does **not** trigger value-destructive instability

That means I am **not** supporting a cosmetic compromise.  
But I am also **not** supporting a closure-first confrontation that risks strikes, governance paralysis, reputational damage, and permanent value erosion.

So the burden now is on the numbers, not on slogans.

---

## To management
I agree with your diagnosis that the company has a structural competitiveness problem. I also agree that internal measures may not be enough in every case.

Where I disagree is your tendency to keep closures and compulsory redundancies as the implied default before the bridge is actually proven. That is too blunt, and too risky.

If you want the family holding to support harder measures, then bring the **plant-by-plant bridge**:
- current utilization
- fixed costs by site
- labor-hours per unit
- savings from product allocation
- savings from working-time flexibility
- savings from redeployment and attrition
- savings from early retirement
- savings from overhead and management cuts
- savings from process productivity
- the residual recurring gap after all of that

Right now, that evidence is still missing. So I do **not** accept “closures first” as a valid starting point.

At the same time, I am not prepared to accept an arrangement that simply freezes the current footprint regardless of economics. If the bridge shows a large residual gap, then we need to face that honestly. But you do not get to assume the harshest tool before the arithmetic is done.

---

## To the works council
I agree with you on one important point: a cosmetic deal would be useless. You are also right that prolonged conflict would damage the enterprise.

Where I disagree is your insistence that “no closures and no compulsory redundancies” can just be declared without first showing a credible economic bridge. That is not a plan; it is a position.

The expert input made the key point: the decisive variable is the **residual gap after quantified internal measures**. I accept that test. You should too.

What I cannot accept is a package that protects every existing structure regardless of whether the economics support it. That would erode the company’s value over time, and that is unacceptable to us as owners.

So yes, I am open to binding protections **if** the numbers prove that a no-closure settlement is sufficient. But I am not willing to pretend sufficiency exists before it is demonstrated.

---

## To the union
I agree that any employee contribution must be:
- time-limited
- reversible
- quantified
- and tied to binding commitments

That is the only fair basis for burden-sharing.

Where I disagree is your framing that keeping closures in reserve is simply blackmail. It is not blackmail if the company is facing real structural overcapacity and the alternative has not yet been shown to close the gap.

I also do not accept the idea that wage moderation, flexibility, redeployment, and early retirement automatically substitute for footprint reduction. They may help a lot. They may not be enough. That is exactly why the bridge matters.

So: I am open to a serious labour contribution.  
I am not open to a permanent downgrade of the asset’s competitiveness disguised as protection.

---

## To the regional state government
I respect your role, and I understand your red lines. I also agree that the company should not be pushed into deindustrialization.

But I disagree with the implication that blocking power alone can resolve the economics. It cannot. If the no-closure package is to be defended, it must be defended by verifiable savings and capacity effects, not by veto.

You are right to insist on:
- investment commitments
- product allocation
- co-determination
- a fair labour contribution

You are not right if that becomes a blanket refusal to confront a structural cost problem.

So I support your preference for a negotiated no-closure route **only if** management proves it works. Otherwise, you are asking the company to carry an unproven structure indefinitely.

---

## To institutional investors
You are the most economically disciplined voices in the room, and I largely agree with the question you are asking: does the package restore margins and competitiveness?

Yes, that is the right question.

Where I would caution you is against assuming that closure is the benchmark of seriousness. It is not. The benchmark is whether the recurring gap is closed. If internal measures can do that, then a no-closure settlement is absolutely preferable from a value-preservation point of view.

But if they cannot, then I will not support a papered-over compromise that leaves the company underpowered. That would destroy value more slowly, but just as surely.

So I am aligned with your demand for proof, not with any automatic drift to the harshest tool.

## On the expert assessments
The expert input was useful because it cut through the noise.

The key point is this:  
A no-closure package may work if the gap is moderate and the footprint problem is not too large. That is the correct test.

What I take from the expert input is:
- internal measures can materially help,
- but they may not fully substitute for footprint reduction if the gap is large,
- therefore management must quantify the gap, not simply assert it.

That means:
- the employee side is not yet disproven,
- management is not yet proved right,
- and the state’s protection stance is not yet economically validated.

So the only responsible position is: **show me the numbers**.

## What I would support
I would support a package built on:
- internal redeployment
- early retirement
- natural attrition
- working-time flexibility
- qualification
- productivity and process improvement
- product allocation
- management and owner contribution
- a strictly time-limited employee contribution
- binding investment commitments

**provided that** the independent bridge shows the residual gap is materially closed and the company is restored to a credible margin trajectory.

In that case, I would be prepared to support:
- binding site guarantees through 2030
- binding job guarantees through 2030
- no compulsory redundancies
- and a no-closure settlement

That is a serious offer. It is not weakness. It is disciplined stewardship.

---

## What I reject
I reject:
- a closure-first strategy
- a settlement that keeps destruction as the easy fallback
- a cosmetic compromise that leaves the cost problem untouched
- and any package that permanently impairs the enterprise’s value or governance stability

I also reject the opposite extreme:
- a protected structure with no verified economic basis that simply delays adjustment and weakens the company over time

Neither extreme serves the family’s long-term ownership responsibility.
