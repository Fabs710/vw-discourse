Management, investors, and the family holding are still trying to turn an unresolved question into a justification for destructive measures. I reject that.

We now have the expert confirmation of what is obvious to anyone looking honestly at the plant-level reality: **internal measures can materially reduce the gap**. The only open question is whether management is willing to do the hard work of proving the residual gap plant by plant — or whether it prefers to keep closures and compulsory redundancies in reserve as leverage.

## To management
You keep saying “numbers first.” Fine. Then put the numbers on the table in a way that actually allows a serious test.

What you have **not** shown is that:
- every German plant is genuinely unsustainable,
- internal capacity adjustments cannot absorb the overcapacity,
- or that the residual gap remains so large that closures are unavoidable.

Until that is demonstrated, I will not accept your attempt to normalize closures as a “real option.” That is not prudence. It is pressure.

And let me be very clear: if you are unable to close the gap without threatening sites and jobs, that is not proof that workers must surrender. It is proof that management must finally develop a restructuring plan that does not start with social destruction.

## To the controlling family holding
You say you want value protection. Then stop treating closures as a routine fallback weapon.

A family owner that truly thinks long-term would support:
- binding site guarantees through 2030,
- binding job guarantees through 2030,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- no German plant closures,
- and real owner contributions.

Anything else means you are asking the workforce to absorb the downside while keeping coercive options open for later. That is not stewardship. It is leverage politics.

## To institutional investors
You are correct that a cosmetic deal is worthless. I agree with that.

But your insistence that closures remain available unless internal measures are proven sufficient is exactly how destructive restructuring gets smuggled in before the bridge is even completed. You are right to demand proof of sufficiency. You are wrong if you treat “not yet fully proven” as permission to keep shutdowns alive.

The burden is on management to show the bridge is insufficient. Not on the workforce to prove the negative.

## To the regional state government
You are closer to our position than the capital side is, but your role has to be more than symbolic.

If you oppose closures and compulsory redundancies, then use your governance power to make that opposition real:
- insist on a no-closure settlement,
- insist on binding job security,
- insist on investment and product commitments,
- insist on management and owner contributions,
- insist on co-determination in the industrial plan.

Do not accept the framing that plant closures are somehow the only serious answer simply because management prefers the bluntest instrument.

## On the expert assessments
The expert did **not** say closures are necessary. The expert said:
- internal measures can deliver meaningful savings,
- plant closures deliver more immediate fixed-cost relief,
- and the actual decision depends on the **residual gap**.

That is exactly why we need a proper bridge — but let’s not pretend the existence of a residual gap automatically authorizes destruction. The expert also made clear that a no-closure package can work when the gap is moderate and capacity can be adjusted internally. That is the relevant point here.

So yes, I accept the need for a **verified residual-gap model**.

What I do **not** accept is the conclusion management wants to smuggle in ahead of the evidence: that closures and forced redundancies are legitimate until proven otherwise.

- any attempt to bypass co-determination through a supposedly neutral “review” mechanism,

## On the procedural proposals
### Two-stage agreement with conditional protections
I can accept a two-stage process only if Phase 1 contains real protection:
- no closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- time-limited employee contribution,
- owner and management contribution,
- full transparency,
- and independent verification.

But I will not accept a Phase 2 that quietly reintroduces shutdowns as the easy answer if management dislikes the result. Any later discussion must still exhaust all non-destructive options first.

### No-closure settlement with a hard review gate
This is only useful if the “review” is not a trapdoor. A review cannot become a backdoor for permanent insecurity. If targets are missed, the first response must still be renewed negotiation and additional non-destructive measures.

### Partial guarantees
I reject partial guarantees that protect only selected sites or selected groups while leaving the rest exposed. That would divide the workforce and weaken the collective job-security framework. The principle must remain: **every German plant stays open, no forced layoffs, no co-determination rollback**.
