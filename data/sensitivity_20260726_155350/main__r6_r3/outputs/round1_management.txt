I hear the red lines you are drawing, but I need to be equally clear: **keeping every plant open, ruling out compulsory redundancies, and preserving the current cost structure is not a viable answer to the crisis we are in**.

We are facing:
- a shrinking European market,
- intense price pressure from lower-cost competitors,
- structurally too high German factory costs,
- and a brand that is missing its return-on-sales target.

If we respond with a package that merely postpones the adjustment, we will not restore competitiveness. That would put the company’s future products, investment capacity, and ultimately more jobs at risk.

Where I agree:
- Any solution has to be **durable**, not cosmetic.
- It has to be **negotiated**, because without an agreement we cannot implement the necessary savings cleanly.
- It has to include a **credible industrial plan** and investment commitments for the German sites.

Where I disagree:
- **No plant closures** as an absolute principle is not compatible with the scale of the problem.
- **No compulsory redundancies** as an absolute principle is also not realistic if we cannot achieve sufficient structural savings by other means.
- Temporary measures alone — working-time tweaks, short-term flexibility, limited redeployment — do **not** close the gap we have identified.

What management needs is not a symbolic contribution, but a **structural and sustainable reduction in cost and capacity**. That means all options have to remain on the table, including:
- plant closures,
- capacity reductions,
- workforce reductions,
- and meaningful labour-cost adjustments.

I am prepared to discuss alternatives to closures **if** they produce the same effect on a durable basis. But that requires real concessions:
- a material labour-cost contribution,
- measurable capacity reduction,
- and a package that gets us to a competitive cost base, not a temporary truce.

Let me be blunt: if the only answer is “no closures, no compulsory redundancies, no deep wage or cost changes,” then there is no solution to the crisis — only delay. And delay is exactly what we cannot afford.

So my position is this:
- We are open to a negotiated package.
- We are open to alternatives to closures.
- But we are **not** open to a cosmetic compromise that leaves the German cost base structurally uncompetitive.

If you want to avoid the sharpest measures, then bring forward a package that actually delivers the required savings and capacity adjustment. Otherwise, management will continue to pursue the necessary restructuring, and I will not pretend that the current structure can be preserved unchanged.
