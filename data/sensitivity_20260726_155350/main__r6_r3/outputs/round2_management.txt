I’ll respond directly.

## To the Works Council and Union

I understand your red lines, but I do **not** accept the proposition that **no plant closures, no compulsory redundancies, and no dismantling of existing guarantees** can coexist with a genuinely competitive restructuring.

That position may be understandable politically, but it does **not** solve the economic problem we face.

### Where I agree
- A cosmetic fix is useless.
- A destructive conflict would damage the company.
- Any solution must be durable and implementable.
- Employees can and should be part of the solution.

### Where I disagree
You are asking us to preserve all sites, preserve all guarantees, and avoid all forced adjustment — while also expecting a structural cost reduction large enough to restore margins. Those demands are not currently consistent with the scale of the problem.

The reality is:
- German factory costs are too high.
- The market is shrinking.
- Overcapacity exists.
- The brand is below target.
- We need funds for future products and technology.

If we simply rely on natural attrition, redeployment, temporary flexibility, and reversible concessions, we may get some savings — but not the **durable structural reduction** we need. That is the gap you still have not closed.

So let me be explicit:
- I am **open** to alternatives to closures.
- I am **not** open to a package that leaves the cost base basically intact and calls that a compromise.
- I am **not** prepared to rule out plant closures or workforce reductions in advance.

If you want to avoid closures, then show me a package with:
1. **substantial, verified capacity reduction**
2. **material labour-cost contribution**
3. **enforceable productivity gains**
4. **credible governance that survives implementation**
5. **a clear path to target returns**

Without that, you are asking management to accept a structural weakness and hope for the best. I will not do that.

## To the Regional State Government

I respect your role and your concern for the regional industrial base. But your position is too restrictive if it assumes that **no closures and no compulsory redundancies** can be the starting point regardless of economics.

That is not a restructuring strategy. That is a political boundary. I understand it, but it does not change the financial reality.

If the state wants to help secure a settlement, then it should help shape a package that:
- reduces overcapacity in a meaningful way,
- protects the company’s future investment,
- and gives the business a viable cost base.

What I cannot accept is using the state’s position to freeze the structure and postpone adjustment. That would be irresponsible to the company, to its employees in the long run, and to the owners.

## To the Controlling Family Holding

You are right on one point: a purely confrontational closure-and-layoff plan would be risky and could destroy value. I am not arguing for reckless confrontation.

But I do not accept the implication that management’s case becomes invalid simply because it is difficult to implement politically.

The test is not whether restructuring is comfortable. The test is whether it is necessary and whether it restores competitiveness. On that, the core diagnosis remains unchanged.

I also agree that a package must be implementable under co-determination. But “implementable” does not mean “fully acceptable to all existing interests without hard choices.” It means the package must be strong enough to survive governance and industrial realities.

So yes:
- I want a deal that preserves long-term value.
- I want a solution that can actually pass.
- I do **not** want a destructive stalemate.

But I will not weaken the restructuring simply to avoid tension. A company in this position does not regain strength by protecting every structure that caused the problem.

## To Institutional Investors

Your standard is the correct one: durability, competitiveness, and value creation. On that basis, I think your concern about cosmetic compromises is exactly right.

But I would caution against reducing the issue to a binary of either “closure-heavy restructuring” or “status quo in a different language.” That is too narrow.

What we need is a package that genuinely changes the economics. If that can be achieved through negotiated means without closures, fine. If not, then closures and stronger workforce reductions remain legitimate instruments.

What is not acceptable is an outcome that:
- avoids hard decisions,
- preserves overcapacity,
- keeps labour costs above target,
- and still expects the market to reward us.

That would be value-destructive.

## My minimum position

Let me state this plainly:

- We need a **material and durable** reduction in cost base and capacity.
- We need a **real labour-cost contribution**.
- We need **implementation certainty**.
- We need a structure that can restore the brand’s return-on-sales target and fund future investment.

What I am **not** prepared to accept:
- a purely temporary truce,
- a deal that protects all current capacity regardless of economics,
- or a package that leaves the company structurally uncompetitive.

## What I want next

If the employee side, state, and owners want to avoid the sharpest measures, then bring forward an **actual alternative package** with numbers:
- how much capacity comes out,
- where the savings come from,
- what labour contribution is made,
- what is binding versus voluntary,
- and why this package is sufficient to restore competitiveness.

If you cannot show that, then management will continue to regard deeper restructuring — including closures and compulsory redundancies if necessary — as on the table.

I am not backing away from that.
