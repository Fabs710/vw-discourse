Below is the forensic restructuring view, limited to the accounting and operating economics.

## 1) What can be concluded now
On the facts given, **a no-closure / no-compulsory-redundancy package may be capable of closing part of the cost gap, but it is not yet demonstrably sufficient** to replace a true footprint reset. Whether it is sufficient depends on the **size of the residual structural gap after quantified internal measures**.

That is the correct test. Not whether closures are ideologically desirable, but whether the **remaining annual run-rate gap** can be eliminated by:
- headcount attrition and early retirement,
- working-time changes,
- redeployment,
- productivity and overhead reductions,
- product allocation / mix changes,
- and any owner/management concessions that are real and recurring.

## 2) What the bridge must contain
A credible forensic bridge needs to be built **plant by plant and function by function**, not at group slogan level. At minimum:

### A. Plant-level operating bridge
For each German plant:
- current annual output
- current shift pattern and line utilization
- direct labor hours per unit
- conversion cost per unit
- site fixed costs:
  - depreciation
  - utilities
  - maintenance
  - site overhead
  - logistics/interface costs
  - local management and support functions
- planned model allocation by year
- expected volume change under the proposed settlement

### B. Workforce bridge
For each site and group function:
- current headcount, permanent and temporary
- age profile and retirement eligibility
- expected natural attrition
- early retirement potential
- redeployment absorbtion capacity
- contract-hour changes / short-time or flex-time assumptions
- wage moderation or one-time contributions, if any

### C. Group overhead bridge
- central SG&A reductions
- engineering and program overhead reductions
- management compensation reductions
- procurement savings
- logistics and footprint rationalization savings
- any owner-funded support or balance sheet contribution, if real and cash-funded

### D. Timing bridge
This is critical:
- year 1 savings
- year 2 savings
- year 3 savings
- steady-state run-rate savings
- one-off restructuring costs
- cash vs non-cash effects

A package that “saves” money only in later years may still fail if the company needs margin repair immediately.

## 3) Accounting treatment: what counts and what does not
### Counts as recurring structural savings
- permanent payroll reduction
- permanent overhead reduction
- permanent shift/line consolidation
- permanent model concentration
- permanent supplier/logistics simplification
- permanent headcount removal through attrition or retirement where the position is eliminated

### Counts only as temporary relief
- wage restraint with an expiry date
- deferred bonuses
- short-time arrangements
- one-time contributions
- temporary working-time reductions
- deferral of maintenance or capex, unless explicitly sustainable

### Must be separated from operating savings
- restructuring charges
- severance
- pension settlement costs
- site write-downs
- lease break costs
- inventory rationalization costs

A common mistake is to confuse **cash relief in the restructuring period** with **permanent structural cost reduction**. They are not the same.

## 4) Forensic assessment of the no-closure tools

### 4.1 Attrition and early retirement
These are useful where the workforce is aging and positions can be eliminated without replacement.

**Strengths**
- politically and operationally less disruptive
- can reduce direct payroll cost
- may lower severance and legal friction

**Limits**
- slow
- uncertain
- insufficient where overcapacity is large
- does not reduce plant fixed costs quickly

### 4.2 Working-time flexibility
This can reduce labor cost and smooth utilization.

**Strengths**
- fast to implement if agreed
- can align labor input with demand
- can reduce overtime and idle hours

**Limits**
- usually partial and time-limited
- does not eliminate structural site overhead
- can become reversible only temporarily

### 4.3 Redeployment and internal transfers
Useful if other sites or functions have real absorption capacity.

**Strengths**
- preserves employment
- reduces headcount at a specific plant
- can support skill reallocation

**Limits**
- shifts cost, it does not automatically remove it
- may increase training and logistics cost
- limited by vacancy availability

### 4.4 Productivity and process gains
Necessary in any case.

**Strengths**
- can improve unit cost
- can reduce scrap, rework, and indirect labor
- can offset part of inflation and energy cost pressure

**Limits**
- often overestimated
- implementation risk is high
- rarely enough on its own to solve true overcapacity

### 4.5 Product allocation / model concentration
This is the most important non-closure lever if it can be done.

**Strengths**
- can raise utilization
- can reduce per-unit overhead
- can improve supply-chain efficiency

**Limits**
- constrained by platform suitability, capex needs, and global product strategy
- if demand is weak, volume cannot be invented
- may still leave multiple sites underutilized

## 5) Residual gap logic
The key forensic question is:

> After all non-destructive measures, what annual recurring cost gap remains?

A simple bridge would look like this:

**Current annual run-rate cost base**
minus
**savings from attrition/retirement**
minus
**savings from working-time changes**
minus
**savings from redeployment**
minus
**savings from productivity/overhead cuts**
minus
**savings from product allocation/mix improvement**
minus
**management/owner contribution**
equals
**residual gap**

If the residual gap is:
- **small to moderate**, a no-closure settlement may be feasible.
- **large**, then the package likely does **not** fully substitute for footprint reduction.

## 6) What I would consider a credible sufficiency threshold
I cannot set an external absolute number without the company’s actual data. But from a restructuring-accounting standpoint, the no-closure package is only credible if it does all of the following:

1. **Eliminates the full structural annual gap on a steady-state basis**
2. **Does so within the required timetable**
3. **Does not rely mainly on one-off items**
4. **Does not preserve materially underutilized fixed assets**
5. **Shows that site fixed costs are brought down, not merely deferred**
6. **Provides verifiable, enforceable assumptions on volume and product allocation**

If the package cannot demonstrate those points, it is not a full substitute for structural downsizing.

## 7) What is missing right now
The material missing information is:
- plant-by-plant volumes and margins
- fixed-cost profile by site
- current capacity utilization
- age and retirement profile
- detailed product allocation plan
- quantified management/owner contribution
- the company’s target annual savings requirement
- the time horizon for restoring margin/ROS

Without these, no one can honestly say whether closures are necessary or unnecessary. The current discussion is at the level of **positions**, not yet at the level of a verified **gap bridge**.

## 8) Bottom-line forensic view
- The employee-side argument is **not yet disproved**, but it is also **not yet proven**.
- The management/capital-side argument that internal measures may be insufficient is **economically plausible**, especially if there is real structural overcapacity.
- The deciding factor is the **residual gap after quantified internal measures**.

### My professional conclusion
**A no-closure package is only viable if it can be shown, with plant-level numbers, that it fully closes the recurring cost gap and materially reduces capacity utilization pressure. If it cannot, then it is a delay mechanism rather than a restructuring solution.**
