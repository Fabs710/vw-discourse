### Independent industrial restructuring assessment

I can assess the **industrial feasibility** of the competing packages, but I cannot verify the missing plant-level figures from the conversation itself. On the information provided, my conservative judgment is:

## 1) The no-closure package is **plausible but unproven**
A restructuring based on:
- redeployment
- attrition
- early retirement
- working-time flexibility
- productivity gains
- product allocation changes
- overhead reduction
- time-limited employee contribution

can deliver **material savings** and some **capacity normalization** without closures. This is industrially feasible.

However, whether it is **sufficient** depends on the **size of the residual gap** after those measures. That is the key test. Without quantified plant-by-plant numbers, nobody can responsibly claim sufficiency.

## 2) Closures are the fastest way to remove fixed cost, but not always the only way
From an industrial economics standpoint:
- **plant closure** gives the clearest fixed-cost reduction and footprint cut
- **internal restructuring** is slower, more contingent, and often less certain
- **forced redundancies** can reduce labor cost faster than attrition, but they carry execution and governance risk

So management is correct that closures are a strong cost lever. But that does **not** mean they are automatically necessary. They are only justified if the non-destructive bridge leaves a **material residual gap** that cannot be closed in time.

## 3) The main issue is timing, not just arithmetic
Even if the no-closure route can eventually produce enough savings, it may fail on **timing**:
- fixed costs remain high while savings phase in
- labor-cost relief from attrition and early retirement is gradual
- productivity programs often take 12–24 months to show full effect
- product allocation changes depend on model cycles and capacity planning
- working-time measures are useful but usually not structural enough on their own

So a no-closure package can be economically credible only if:
1. the gap is **moderate**, not very large
2. the savings arrive **fast enough**
3. there is a **hard enforcement mechanism**
4. there is a **fallback sequence** if targets are missed

## 4) The governance structure makes the implementation risk high
Given the described veto positions:
- supervisory board co-determination
- family control
- state blocking minority
- union bargaining power
- strike capacity

a closure-led strategy is **hard to execute** and potentially prolonged. That makes a negotiated no-closure package more attractive from an implementation-risk perspective.

But the reverse is also true: a no-closure package that is not economically sufficient becomes a **time-buying exercise** and may simply defer the same conflict.

## 5) What would need to be verified to judge the bridge properly
To assess sufficiency, I would need:
- plant-by-plant utilization
- fixed cost by plant
- headcount by site and function
- labor-hours per vehicle by plant
- current and projected model mix
- savings from attrition and early retirement
- savings from working-time changes
- savings from redeployment/internal transfer
- productivity and overhead savings
- management/owner contribution
- the residual recurring gap after all measures
- timing of each saving stream
- comparison to a footprint-reducing scenario

Without that, any claim that closures are “unavoidable” or “unnecessary” is still an assertion.
