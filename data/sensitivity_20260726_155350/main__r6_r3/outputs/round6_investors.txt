We agree on the diagnosis: this is a structural competitiveness problem, not a temporary blip. We also agree that a cosmetic compromise would destroy value.

Where we **do not** agree is on the idea that a no-closure, no-forced-redundancy package should be accepted **without a hard, independent proof of sufficiency**.

## To management
You are right to insist on a material, durable, implementable solution. You are also right that internal measures may not be enough if the residual gap is large.

But you still have not justified keeping closures and compulsory redundancies as a standing fallback **before** the numbers are proven. “Not yet disproven” is not “necessary.” From a capital standpoint, that matters.

What we need is:
- plant-by-plant utilization,
- fixed costs by site,
- labor-hours per unit,
- savings from attrition, early retirement, redeployment, working-time flexibility, productivity, and overhead cuts,
- management and owner contribution,
- the residual recurring gap,
- and the timing to restore margins.

Until that bridge exists, I cannot support a settlement that preserves structural uncertainty and risks a prolonged margin drag.

## To the works council and union
We understand why you want binding site and job guarantees first. We also understand the value of avoiding destructive industrial conflict.

But I have to be blunt: **guarantees without proof of economic sufficiency are not value-protective**. If a no-closure package really closes the gap, then we can support strong protections. If it does not, then the company is simply buying time while the cost base remains uncompetitive.

Your current position goes too far when it treats closures as illegitimate in principle, rather than as a legitimate fallback if the verified residual gap remains material. That is not a financeable stance.

## To the regional state government
Your insistence on no closures is understandable politically, but as an investor we cannot endorse governance vetoes that preserve excess capacity indefinitely. Blocking rights do not change the economics.

If the no-closure route is sufficient, fine — we can support it. If it is not, then vetoing hard restructuring just shifts the burden onto shareholders and weakens the company’s long-term value.

## To the controlling family holding
We agree that cosmetic compromise is unacceptable, and we appreciate your willingness to wait for the bridge. But “closure as a real option” is only acceptable if it is used as a disciplined fallback after the internal route has been quantitatively tested — not as a bargaining threat from the start.

## Our position
We would support a **no-closure settlement** only if independent verification shows it:
1. closes the full recurring cost gap,
2. materially improves capacity utilization,
3. restores a credible margin path within the required time horizon,
4. and does not rely mainly on one-off measures.

If those conditions are met, then binding site and job guarantees through 2030 become supportable, because they would protect a competitively viable asset.

If they are not met, we will **not** back a papered-over deal that leaves margins structurally impaired.
