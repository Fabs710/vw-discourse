We are still at the same test: **does the eventual package credibly restore margins and competitiveness, or does it merely defer adjustment while preserving an uncompetitive cost base?**

From the capital markets side, that is the only standard that matters.

## Where I agree

I agree with management, the family holding, and the state on the diagnosis:
- the cost base is too high,
- structural overcapacity is real,
- and a cosmetic compromise would be value-destructive.

I also agree with the works council and union on one practical point:
- a prolonged strike or governance deadlock would destroy value quickly.

So yes, everyone is right about the risk of a bad outcome. The problem is that the proposed “solutions” still do not close the gap.

## Where I disagree

### To the Works Council and Union
Your red lines are clear:
- no plant closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- no dismantling of protections.

I understand the bargaining logic, but from an investor perspective that is **not sufficient** unless you can show, with numbers, that the alternative package delivers **equivalent durable savings and capacity reduction**.

So far, what I hear is:
- redeployment,
- attrition,
- early retirement,
- working-time flexibility,
- productivity measures,
- and a time-limited contribution.

Those may help, but they do not automatically add up to a restructuring of the scale needed. If they do not materially reduce the cost base, then they are delay dressed as compromise.

I also reject the idea that any mention of closures is automatically coercion. In a restructuring of this magnitude, they are a legitimate fallback if the non-destructive package is insufficient. They are not desirable, but they are not illegitimate either.

### To Management
You are right that the company needs durable structural change. But I am not giving you credit for “hardness” if the chosen route destroys implementation certainty.

If the plan is too dependent on plant closures and forced redundancies, then you may win the theory and lose the company in practice through strikes, deadlock, and governance failure. That would be value destruction, not discipline.

So I need to be clear:  
**we do not support a closure-first strategy as an end in itself.**  
We support whatever package actually restores margins, is durable, and can be implemented.

That said, you still have to show a quantified bridge from the current cost base to competitive returns. Without that, you are asking capital markets to trust a promise rather than a plan.

### To the Regional State Government
Your political constraints are real. But political discomfort is not the same as economic impossibility.

If you want to rule out closures and compulsory redundancies, then you need to show that the remaining tools are enough to restore competitiveness. We have not seen that demonstrated yet.

A state-backed package can be acceptable if it includes:
- real capacity reduction,
- credible labour-cost contribution,
- enforceable productivity gains,
- and investment commitments.

But if the state’s position becomes a blanket veto on all hard measures without an economically equivalent alternative, then it is simply protecting appearances while the underlying problem remains.

### To the Controlling Family Holding
You are right to reject a reckless closure-first confrontation. But I also agree with management on one thing: a package that preserves the current structure and asks investors to call that discipline is not acceptable.

The family should support a settlement that is:
- materially effective,
- durable,
- and implementable.

If that means accepting a slower or less dramatic adjustment than management originally wanted, fine. But it does **not** mean accepting a compromise that leaves the company structurally uncompetitive.

## What would move us

We do **not** need closures as a matter of principle. We do need:
- verified structural cost reduction,
- real capacity rationalisation,
- a meaningful labour contribution,
- and a believable path back to target margins.

That means I would look for:
1. quantified savings from non-destructive measures,
2. a clear statement of the remaining gap,
3. the exact point at which management says the no-closure route is insufficient,
4. and a fallback that is credible if the numbers do not work.

## My position on the bridge proposals

### 1) Protected restructuring with measurable triggers
This is only acceptable if the trigger mechanism is not just a delay device.

If the targets are missed, the response cannot simply be “wait and hope.” There must be a real consequence, including reopening the full option set. Otherwise, the “protection” is just a way to postpone hard decisions.

### 2) Site guarantees in exchange for deeper internal restructuring
This is the most credible no-closure path, but only if the internal restructuring is large enough to matter.

Site guarantees are fine if they come with:
- real productivity gains,
- measurable capacity reduction,
- and durable labour-cost improvement.

If not, they are just a promise to preserve an uncompetitive footprint.

### 3) Phased restructuring with a joint governance body
Useful in principle, but only if it produces outcomes and not endless renegotiation.

Capital markets will not reward another process that delays clarity for 12 to 24 months while margins stay under pressure.

## Bottom line

Our support is conditional and unchanged:

We will back a package only if it is:
- **material**
- **durable**
- **implementable**
- **margin-repairing**
- and **credible on capacity reduction**

If the no-closure path genuinely achieves that, fine. If not, then the company must not rule out harder measures simply because they are politically uncomfortable.

So the next round needs numbers, not slogans:
- exact cost gap,
- exact savings,
- exact capacity reduction,
- exact labour contribution,
- exact investment commitments,
- and a clear answer on what happens if the gap remains.

Until then, our position remains:  
**no support for a compromise that preserves the cost problem under a softer name.**
