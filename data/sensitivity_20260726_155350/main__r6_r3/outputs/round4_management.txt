## To the Works Council
I hear your red lines. I understand the political and human significance of jobs, sites, and co-determination.

But you still have not answered the central economic question: **how does the company achieve a structurally competitive cost base if closures and compulsory redundancies are ruled out in advance?**

You keep asking for an itemized bridge. Fair enough — but the bridge cuts both ways.  
I am asking for a **quantified, credible package** that closes the gap, not a list of instruments that sound acceptable in principle.

So let’s be precise:

- **Product allocation to German sites** helps only if it is profitable and sustainable.
- **Management and owner contributions** can help, but they will not cover a structural overcapacity problem on their own.
- **Working-time flexibility** may buy room, but it does not permanently remove excess capacity.
- **Attrition, early retirement, redeployment, qualification** are useful, but they are gradual and limited.
- **Productivity and process gains** are necessary, but they are not enough if the cost base remains too high.

So no, I do not accept the claim that these tools obviously substitute for structural adjustment at scale. They may contribute to it. They do not yet prove sufficiency.

And I will be equally clear: **binding site guarantees through 2030 are not acceptable if they freeze an uncompetitive footprint.** A guarantee that protects capacity we cannot afford is not protection; it is a delay mechanism.

## To the Industrial Union
You say employee contribution is not the issue. It is. It has to be material.

I appreciate that you are prepared to discuss time-limited contributions. But the key issue remains durability.  
A reversible concession is only meaningful if the company gets a lasting improvement in competitiveness. Otherwise the firm pays twice: once through concessions, and again when the structural problem remains.

So I ask again: what is the **maximum** labour-cost contribution you are prepared to support, in quantified terms, and what exact operational savings would it deliver? If the answer is “not much,” then you are not solving the problem — you are just easing it temporarily.

And I reject your framing that keeping closures available is “pre-paying for management failure.” No. It is maintaining a credible restructuring fallback when the scale of the problem may require it.

## To the Regional State Government
You want no closures and no forced redundancies. I understand that. But your position still does not close the economic gap.

You say the missing contribution should come from management, owners, pricing, product allocation, and internal restructuring. In part yes — but those sources are not infinite, and some are already constrained by market reality.

If the state wants to rule out closures, it has to accept responsibility for showing how the gap is fully covered. I have not seen that. What I have seen is a strong preference for protection, with no verified alternative of equal scale.

That is not enough.

## To the Controlling Family Holding
You are right that a reckless closure-first confrontation would be value-destructive if it led to deadlock and strikes. I am not asking for recklessness.

What I am saying is this: **the company must not be locked into an uncompetitive structure simply because the governance environment is difficult.** If the no-closure camp cannot deliver an equivalent structural effect, then the fallback has to remain real.

So I welcome your rejection of a cosmetic compromise. But I do not accept your implication that “non-destructive” automatically means “sufficient.” We still need the numbers.

## To Institutional Investors
You are the only ones here consistently asking the right question: does the package repair margins and competitiveness? On that point, I agree.

Where I disagree with you is on the temptation to treat closures as a neutral benchmark. They are not neutral in implementation. They are disruptive, and I know that. But they also remain a legitimate tool if the cost gap cannot otherwise be closed.

So I will not support a deal that simply repackages the status quo under more palatable language.

## On the bridge proposals

### 1) Protected restructuring with measurable triggers
This is only viable if the triggers have teeth.  
A reopen clause that merely sends us back into another round of symbolic bargaining is useless.

If Phase 1 does not deliver the required savings, then management must be able to move to harder measures. Otherwise the package is not a restructuring path; it is a holding pattern.

### 2) Protection-for-transformation package
I can work with this direction only if “protection” is conditional on actual transformation, not on hopes.  
That means:
- material labor cost reduction,
- meaningful capacity reduction,
- and verified profitability improvement.

Without that, protections become a one-way constraint on management while the cost problem persists.

### 3) Joint governance body
A joint body can help if it accelerates implementation.  
It is unacceptable if it becomes a procedural shield against hard choices.

No one should mistake more committees for more competitiveness.

## My position, unchanged
I want a solution that is:
- **material**
- **durable**
- **implementable**
- **cost-reducing**
- **capacity-reducing**
- and capable of restoring the brand’s return-on-sales target

What I will not accept is:
- a cosmetic compromise,
- a frozen cost base,
- or a settlement that rules out the tools needed to make the company competitive.

## What would move me
Bring me a package with:
1. exact cost reductions by measure
2. exact capacity reductions by site
3. exact labor contribution
4. exact management/owner contribution
5. exact investment commitments
6. a credible explanation of why that closes the gap

If that package is sufficient, I can support it.  
If it is not sufficient, then the company still has a structural problem — and I will not pretend it is solved.

Until then, closures and compulsory redundancies remain legitimate options if they are required to restore competitiveness.
