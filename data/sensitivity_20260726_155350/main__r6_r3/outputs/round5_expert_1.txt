I can address the industrial-finance question: whether a **no-closure package** can plausibly close the restructuring gap, and how it compares with a footprint-reducing plan.

## Short answer
A **no-closure / no-forced-redundancy** package can produce **meaningful savings**, but in a large German automotive operation it usually **cannot replicate the full economic effect of plant closure** unless the underlying gap is **moderate**, the company has **significant excess capacity that can be absorbed internally**, and the package includes **deep, quantified labor and complexity reductions**.

In practical terms:
- **Internal measures** can often cover a **material share** of the gap.
- **Plant closure / footprint reduction** tends to deliver the **largest structural fixed-cost relief** and the clearest capacity reset.
- Therefore, the key question is not whether the no-closure package saves money — it does — but whether it saves **enough, fast enough, and durably enough**.

## How the economics usually work

### 1) No-closure package: what it can do
A negotiated package without closures can reduce cost through:
- shorter working time / shift changes
- temporary wage moderation
- natural attrition
- early retirement
- redeployment to other sites/functions
- overhead reduction
- slower hiring
- process productivity gains
- product concentration

This can be substantial, especially if the company has:
- surplus headcount relative to volume
- underutilized shifts
- duplicative functions
- fragmented model complexity

### 2) What it usually cannot fully do
If the problem is **structural overcapacity across several sites**, then internal measures often leave:
- a residual fixed-cost burden
- too much indirect labor
- too much site overhead
- too much logistics duplication
- too much complexity in plant/product allocation

Those costs are hard to remove without **footprint reduction**.

## Timing constraint
This matters a lot.

A no-closure package tends to be:
- **faster to negotiate**
- **slower to realize full savings**
- **less certain on durability**
- **more dependent on behavior and compliance**

A closure or consolidation plan tends to be:
- **more disruptive**
- **politically harder**
- but **more immediate and decisive** in removing fixed cost

So if management needs margin repair within a short window, internal measures may be insufficient unless they are very deep.

## My conservative judgment
Without seeing the plant-level bridge, the most defensible conclusion is:

- If the residual gap after internal measures is **small to moderate**, a no-closure package may be sufficient.
- If the residual gap is **large**, then the no-closure package is likely to be **economically weaker** than a footprint-reducing restructuring, even if it is socially preferable.

## What would prove sufficiency
A no-closure package would need to show, in verified numbers, that it:
- closes the **full recurring gap**
- restores a **credible ROS trajectory**
- does so **within the required time horizon**
- does not rely mainly on **one-off savings**
- does not leave the company with **persistent excess fixed cost**
