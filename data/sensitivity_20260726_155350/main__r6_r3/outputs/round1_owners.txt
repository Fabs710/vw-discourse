I appreciate the clarity from all sides. Let me be equally direct from the shareholder/governance perspective.

We are not in the business of preserving structures for their own sake. We are in the business of protecting a long-term industrial asset. That means I share management’s core diagnosis: **the current German cost base and capacity footprint are not competitive enough**. If we pretend otherwise, we will erode value gradually and end up with a worse outcome for everyone.

At the same time, I do **not** support a reckless “close plants and hope for the best” approach. A value-destroying confrontation, reputational damage, or a settlement that triggers prolonged instability would also be unacceptable. This company is a long-term family asset, not a short-term trade.

Where I agree:
- The problem is structural, not cosmetic.
- A durable solution is needed, not a temporary truce.
- The German operations must regain competitiveness and margin.
- Any workforce contribution must be tied to a credible industrial plan.

Where I disagree with the employee side:
- “No plant closures ever” is too absolute for the scale of the problem.
- “No compulsory redundancies ever” is also too absolute if sufficient structural savings cannot otherwise be achieved.
- Site and job guarantees are not a substitute for competitiveness; they only make sense if they accompany a viable restructuring path.

Where I disagree with management:
- If the restructuring is framed only as closures, layoffs, and a one-sided demand on labour, you will provoke exactly the instability we must avoid.
- A plan that is technically aggressive but politically unworkable is not a serious plan.
- You need to show how savings can be achieved in a way that can actually survive co-determination, the state’s veto position, and labour resistance.

To the regional state: I understand the political and regional constraints, and I agree that this company should remain an industrial anchor in Germany. But “no closures” cannot be the only answer if the company’s economics do not support the existing footprint. The state’s role should be to help secure a viable industrial settlement, not to freeze all structural adjustment.

So from the holding’s perspective, the test is straightforward:

1. **Does the package restore competitiveness materially?**
2. **Is it durable, not temporary?**
3. **Can it pass the governance reality of this company without triggering a destructive stalemate?**
4. **Does it protect long-term enterprise value rather than merely shifting the pain around?**

If the answer is yes, we can support a negotiated restructuring, even if it is painful.

If the answer is no, then I will not support a settlement that preserves the status quo under the banner of compromise. That would be value-destructive.

My current position is therefore:
- I support a **serious restructuring**.
- I am open on the exact instruments.
- I am not open to a fake compromise that avoids all hard decisions while claiming victory.

If the works council, union, and state want to avoid closures and forced redundancies, then they need to show an alternative package that **actually delivers equivalent, durable savings and capacity reduction**. Not slogans. Not delay. Real numbers and real enforceability.

Otherwise, management’s restructuring case remains, in principle, the stronger one.
