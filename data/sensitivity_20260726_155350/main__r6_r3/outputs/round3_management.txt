I will be equally direct.

We are now far enough into this discussion that it is clear where the real fault line sits: **the employee side and the state want restructuring without closure risk; management and the capital side need restructuring that actually changes the economics.** That is the core issue, and I am not going to pretend it is smaller than it is.

## To the Works Council
I agree on one point: a cosmetic fix is worthless. On everything else, I disagree.

You keep repeating “no closures, no compulsory redundancies, no weakening of protections” as if those phrases themselves solve the structural problem. They do not. They describe what you want to preserve, not what the company needs to become competitive.

You say management has not proven necessity. I say the burden is not on me to prove every non-closure measure fails in abstract. The burden is on the employee side to show how your preferred model closes a **material and durable** cost gap at the scale required.

So far I have heard:
- redeployment,
- attrition,
- early retirement,
- working-time flexibility,
- productivity gains,
- product allocation.

Those are all useful instruments. They are **not yet a credible answer** to the full overcapacity and cost-base problem unless you can quantify them. I have still not seen a package that demonstrably gets us to competitive German unit costs and restores the brand’s return-on-sales target.

And let me be explicit: **binding site guarantees through 2030 are not acceptable if they freeze an uncompetitive structure.** A guarantee that protects the wrong capacity simply postpones the adjustment and makes the eventual correction harsher.

## To the Union
I appreciate that you are at least willing to discuss time-limited contributions. But your position still rests on the assumption that the workforce can contribute enough without structural site adjustment. That may be true. It may not be. You have not shown it yet.

You ask why the workforce should be the shock absorber. Because the cost base sits there. Because the overcapacity sits there. Because the margins sit there. That is not moralizing; that is industrial reality.

You say management keeps closures as a threat structure. I say management must keep closures available because, without that leverage, there is no guarantee of a durable correction. If the non-destructive package is genuinely sufficient, then prove it with numbers. Until then, the fallback has to remain real.

I am **not** prepared to accept a deal where labour’s contribution is reversible, but management’s savings are imaginary.

## To the Regional State Government
I hear your concern about regional industrial stability. I do not dismiss it. But your position still reads as if the state wants the company to solve a structural competitiveness problem while being prevented from using the tools that would actually force adjustment.

That is not a viable governance model if the gap is large.

You ask what leverage the state should use. The answer is simple: help shape a package that produces real savings. Do not use the blocking position to outlaw the hard choices before the economics have been closed. If you insist on no closures and no compulsory redundancies as a starting point, then you must also accept responsibility for showing how the missing savings are achieved elsewhere.

I have not seen that yet.

## To the Controlling Family Holding
You are right on one important point: a reckless closure-first approach would be value-destructive if it triggered deadlock and strike escalation without a credible implementation path. I do **not** want a symbolic showdown.

But I do not accept your implied middle ground either, namely that we should accept the current structure and hope for negotiated discipline. That is exactly how companies drift into permanent underperformance.

You say you want a materially effective and implementable package. Good. So do I. But “implementable” cannot mean “fully protected from hard adjustment.” If the package does not create enough lasting cost reduction, then it is not a restructuring. It is a pause.

## To Institutional Investors
Your framing is closest to the economic reality. I agree that the package must be durable, material, and implementable. I also agree that a plan which cannot survive the governance and labour environment is not value-creating.

Where I disagree is with any suggestion that visible structural change can be achieved without meaningful capacity rationalisation. Not all structure equals closures, but **some structure must come out**. If the no-closure camp cannot show that its alternative actually removes enough cost and capacity, then it is asking the market to fund a promise rather than a transformation.

That is not acceptable.

## My position
I will restate it plainly:

- We need a **material and durable reduction** in cost base and capacity.
- We need a **real labour-cost contribution**.
- We need **implementation certainty**.
- We need to restore the brand’s competitiveness and fund future investment to 2030.
- We do **not** need a cosmetic compromise that preserves the current problem under a softer name.

## On the suggested bridge models

### 1) Protected restructuring with measurable triggers
This is only useful if the triggers are not just another way to defer the real decision.

If the package misses targets and the response is merely “talk again later,” then the model fails. A review mechanism only matters if it has consequence.

### 2) Site guarantees in exchange for deeper internal restructuring
This can only be discussed if “site guarantees” do not mean permanent protection of every existing footprint regardless of economics. If there is a genuine internal restructuring that closes the cost gap, fine. But I am not signing up to a guarantee that prevents the company from correcting structural overcapacity.

### 3) Phased restructuring with a joint governance body
A joint body is acceptable only if it is designed to deliver outcomes, not to institutionalize veto-by-delay. I will not support a structure that turns every hard decision into endless re-litigation.

## What would actually move me
I remain open to a negotiated solution, but only if it comes with a package that shows, in numbers:

1. how much capacity comes out  
2. how much cost comes out  
3. what labour contributes  
4. what management and owners contribute  
5. why the result is sufficient to restore margins  

Until then, I will continue to regard closures and compulsory redundancies as legitimate and necessary instruments if they are required to close the gap.

## Bottom line
I am not backing away from the core objective: **restore competitiveness, fix the cost base, and secure the company’s 2030 investment capacity.**

If the employee side and the state want to avoid the sharper measures, then they need to bring a package that is not just politically acceptable but economically sufficient. Right now, I have not seen that.
