I agree with the core line from the Works Council: **there will be no acceptance of plant closures in Germany and no compulsory redundancies**. That is not a negotiable detail; it is the minimum requirement for any serious settlement.

Management’s framing that employees must absorb the pain to fix a structural problem is exactly what we reject. If the company has an overcapacity problem, then the answer cannot be to destroy jobs, break the collective framework, and shift all adjustment costs onto the workforce while management, owners, and capital markets remain insulated.

What we are prepared to discuss is a **fair, shared-sacrifice package** that keeps sites open and protects employment, for example:
- capacity reductions through natural fluctuation, transfers, and working-time instruments,
- temporary working-time adjustments,
- a negotiated employee contribution,
- forgoing bonuses or other cost components,
- qualification and redeployment instead of layoffs,
- and investment commitments for the future model mix.

But let me be equally clear: **a deal that depends on closures, forced redundancies, or the dismantling of long-standing protections is unacceptable**. If management insists on those points, then there is no basis for agreement, and the workforce will respond accordingly.

The burden of restructuring must be shared. If management and owners want the union to contribute to competitiveness, then they need to bring something real to the table too:
- binding guarantees for jobs and sites,
- a credible industrial plan,
- transparent figures on the cost problem,
- and concrete concessions from the company side, not just demands from labor.

So our position is this: **we are ready to bargain hard and constructively, but only on the basis of security, fairness, and co-determined transformation.** Anything else will face resistance.
