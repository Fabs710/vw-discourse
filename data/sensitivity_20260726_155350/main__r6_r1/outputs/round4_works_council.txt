I will be direct.

## Where I agree
I agree with the diagnosis that the current situation is serious:
- overcapacity exists,
- the market is under pressure,
- costs must come down,
- and a cosmetic fix will not be enough.

I also agree that a durable, negotiated solution is better than an endless conflict.

## Where I disagree
I do **not** accept the idea that “durable” automatically means:
- German plant closures,
- compulsory redundancies,
- or tearing up the historic job-security framework.

That is still the wrong starting point. It is not justified just because management repeats it often enough, and it is not made more credible by calling it “structural.”

## To management
You keep saying the company needs “meaningful capacity reduction” and “durable fixed-cost relief.” Fine. But you still have not shown that this requires closing German plants or threatening forced layoffs.

An expert has already said what I have been saying all along: **closures are not proven to be unavoidable on the record provided**. That matters. Because once a plant is closed, the damage is permanent.

So if you want the works council to take your case seriously, then stop hiding behind general claims and present:
- site-by-site economics,
- clear utilization data,
- the real savings from non-closure measures,
- and the exact reason why closures would be necessary **after** all alternatives are exhausted.

Until then, your position is not evidence-based; it is a preference for shifting the burden onto workers.

## To the family holding and investors
You speak about value preservation, but you are too quick to equate value with cutting plants and jobs. That is a narrow accounting view.

Value is also destroyed by:
- prolonged strikes,
- lost trust,
- damaged supplier networks,
- lower commitment,
- and the long-term weakening of the company’s industrial base.

A deal that keeps production running, preserves skills, and protects co-determination is not “soft.” It is value-preserving.

If you push for closures as the default, you may win a spreadsheet argument and lose the company’s social license.

## To the regional state
I welcome your rejection of closures and forced redundancies, but words are not enough. If you really mean to protect the regional industrial base, then use your leverage. Do not let management present a fait accompli and then call it “responsible restructuring.”

## On the expert and the audit
The expert is right on one important point: a non-closure package can work **if it removes enough fixed cost and capacity**.

But I need to be equally clear: that does **not** mean we should accept closures as the next logical step. It means management must prove, with hard numbers, that no-closure alternatives cannot work.

So yes, I support a joint audit **only if** its purpose is to verify non-closure solutions first, not to manufacture a pretext for shutting sites.

[SUMMON: jointly mandated independent site-by-site audit with binding access to plant economics, utilization, product allocation, indirect costs, and the savings potential of non-closure measures]

## What we can discuss
We are prepared to discuss:
- working-time flexibility,
- temporary shift adjustments,
- voluntary exits,
- internal redeployment,
- retraining and qualification,
- product reallocation,
- function pooling,
- and narrowly limited remuneration moderation.

But all of that is only acceptable inside a framework of:
- **no German plant closures**
- **no compulsory redundancies**
- **binding job and site guarantees through 2030**
- **preserved co-determination**
- **preserved collective protections**

Without those guarantees, there is no deal.

## What we will not accept
Let me be very clear on the lines that remain non-negotiable:
- no closure “in principle” but closure in practice later
- no “temporary” agreement that is really a trap
- no compulsory redundancies dressed up as “natural attrition”
- no agreement that weakens job security clause by clause
- no bypassing of co-determination
- no restructuring that makes workers pay for management’s strategic failure

## On the bridge options
### 1) “Non-closure first, checkpoint later”
This only works if the checkpoint is **not** a hidden closure trigger.

We can accept a protected period with:
- no plant closures,
- no forced layoffs,
- and a clearly defined contribution from the workforce.

But we will not sign up to a deal that already contains the threat of future closures if management misses targets. That is not security; that is delayed coercion.

### 2) “Protected consolidation”
This is the only language that comes close to something discussable — but only if it means **real capacity adjustment without shutting plants**.

That means:
- internal consolidation,
- product transfers,
- working-time changes,
- function pooling,
- and voluntary measures.

It does **not** mean shutting a plant by another name.

### 3) “Employee contribution in exchange for guarantees”
We are prepared to contribute, but only if the company side also contributes visibly and the guarantees are binding.

That means:
- investment commitments,
- future product allocation,
- management restraint,
- and no hollow promises.

## My position remains unchanged
The works council’s line is still:
- **every German plant stays open**
- **no compulsory redundancies**
- **co-determination stays intact**
- **job-security framework stays intact**

If management wants a restructuring that survives, it must be built on that basis.

## Bottom line
We are not blocking restructuring. We are blocking a restructuring that starts by sacrificing sites, jobs, and rights.

If the company can achieve the needed savings without closures and without forced layoffs, then it should show us that in numbers. If it cannot, then it has not proven its case — it has only proven that it prefers to impose the burden on the workforce.

I will not move from that position unless the other side brings a genuinely binding, quantitatively credible package that protects every German plant, avoids compulsory redundancies, and preserves co-determination through 2030.
