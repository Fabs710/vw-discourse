I will be equally direct.

## Where I agree
I agree with everyone on the basic diagnosis:
- the current situation is not sustainable,
- overcapacity is real,
- the cost base is too high,
- and a cosmetic solution will fail.

I also agree that a long conflict would damage the company. That is exactly why we need a settlement that is **actually structural**.

## Where I disagree
I do **not** accept the employee side’s attempt to turn “no closures, no compulsory redundancies, job guarantees through 2030” into the starting premise.

That premise may be understandable politically, but it does **not** answer the business problem. It does not restore competitiveness. It does not lower the fixed-cost base enough by itself. And it does not prove that the current footprint can support the returns this company needs.

You keep saying closures are “not proven unavoidable.” Fine — but that is not the same as saying they are off the table. The burden is not on management to pretend the answer is already known. The burden is on all sides to produce a package that closes the gap **in reality**, not in rhetoric.

## To the Works Council
You are right that a site-by-site business case is needed. I support that. But let’s be clear: demanding the numbers is not the same as proving that every site can remain open.

We need the audit precisely because the company has a structural problem. If the audit shows that some capacity is duplicative, subscale, or permanently too expensive, then I will not accept a decision that freezes the company in an uncompetitive structure just because it is uncomfortable to confront.

Your position is still:
- no plant closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- no meaningful erosion of legacy protections,
- and full job security through 2030.

That is not a restructuring package. It is a defensive line. If you want a serious negotiation, you need to engage with the possibility that structural reduction may be necessary.

## To the Union
You are right about one thing: strikes and mistrust destroy value. Absolutely. But value is also destroyed by preserving an inflated cost base and hoping future product cycles somehow fix it.

I do **not** accept the idea that the workforce can contribute only if nothing fundamental changes. If there is to be a deal, the employee contribution has to be real and durable.

That can include:
- working-time flexibility,
- remuneration moderation,
- shift adjustments,
- voluntary exits,
- redeployment,
- retraining,
- and function pooling.

But those tools alone are not enough if they do not produce a **permanent** reduction in fixed cost and capacity.

And I am clear on this: I will not sign up to a “protected” phase that is merely a delay mechanism, where the company absorbs concessions now and is then denied any structural options later.

## To the Regional State
You are right to defend the industrial base. I respect that. But defending the base does not mean freezing the business model.

If the company is to stay an industrial anchor, it must be competitive. That means the state cannot insist on preserving every existing structure and then expect the company to hit target margins anyway.

I support a serious no-closure test — but not a political veto on necessary change. If the audit shows a genuine non-closure path, I will take it seriously. If it does not, then the state should not stand in the way of deeper consolidation.

## To the Controlling Family
You are right that ownership and governance control are not up for barter. Agreed. But control also carries responsibility for hard decisions.

I do **not** accept a position that says: “We want enterprise value protected, but we won’t accept the instruments needed to protect it.” If the business needs structural change, then ownership cannot demand results while ruling out the means.

## To the Investors
You are right to insist that any settlement must be durable and economically meaningful. I share that. A cosmetic compromise is useless.

But you are also wrong if you think preserving all sites and all legacy protections is automatically value-preserving. It is not. A structurally weak company with a too-high cost base is not “stable”; it is deferred deterioration.

## On the audit
I support the audit, but let me be precise about its purpose.

[SUMMON: jointly mandated independent site-by-site restructuring and industrial-economics audit with execution-risk assessment and access to plant cost, utilization, product allocation, indirect overhead, and savings from closure and non-closure measures]

The audit must answer:
1. What is the exact savings gap?
2. How much comes from labor cost?
3. How much from excess capacity?
4. How much from indirect overhead and duplication?
5. What can non-closure measures realistically deliver?
6. What additional savings would closures or deeper consolidation add?

That last point is decisive. We are not doing theater. We are testing whether a no-closure route is genuinely sufficient.

## What management can support
I am not fixated on closures for their own sake. I am fixed on outcomes.

So yes, I am prepared to consider:
- partial site consolidation,
- product transfers,
- function pooling,
- shift reductions,
- working-time flexibility,
- voluntary exit programs,
- redeployment,
- retraining,
- bonus moderation,
- and targeted changes to legacy arrangements.

But only if they deliver:
- durable capacity reduction,
- meaningful labor-cost relief,
- lower fixed cost per vehicle,
- and a credible path back to the return-on-sales target.

That is the minimum.

## What I will not accept
I will not accept:
- a blanket ban on closures,
- a blanket ban on compulsory redundancies,
- a permanent job-security framework that leaves no room for structural adjustment,
- or a package that keeps the company uncompetitive while calling itself balanced.

I also will not accept a deal that tells management to fund future products and technology while refusing to fix the current cost base.

## Bottom line
I am not here to preserve the current footprint. I am here to restore competitiveness.

If the audit shows that a no-closure package can truly deliver the required structural savings, then I will evaluate it seriously. If it does not, then deeper consolidation remains on the table — including closures.

That is the reality. And that is where I stand.
