From an industrial-cost and restructuring perspective, the discussion has now converged on the right question:

**Can the company close its structural savings gap with a protected, non-closure package, or does durable competitiveness require German plant closure and/or compulsory redundancies?**

## What can be concluded now

### 1) The employee side has not yet been disproven
Based on the record you provided, **closures are not yet proven unavoidable**. That is a meaningful finding, but it is not the same as saying a no-closure solution will work.

### 2) The management side has not yet proven inevitability either
Management has asserted overcapacity and a weak cost base, but the material so far does not show:

- the exact residual cost gap,
- the incremental savings from closures versus internal consolidation,
- or why non-closure measures cannot bridge the gap.

### 3) The real issue is the size of the residual gap after non-closure measures
That residual gap is the deciding variable. In restructuring terms, the parties need to know:
- current fixed-cost burden,
- installed capacity,
- required volume,
- utilization by site,
- direct labor cost differences,
- indirect overhead burden,
- logistics and complexity costs,
- and the one-time vs recurring effect of each measure.

Without that, everyone is debating in abstractions.

## What a credible audit must quantify
A decision-useful audit should model at least three scenarios:

### Scenario A: Non-closure restructuring
Typical measures:
- shift reductions
- working-time moderation
- wage/bonus restraint
- voluntary exits
- redeployment
- retraining
- function pooling
- product reallocation
- procurement and overhead reductions

Output needed:
- recurring annual savings
- one-time costs
- employment effect
- capacity effect
- execution risk

### Scenario B: Protected consolidation without formal closures
Typical measures:
- specialization by site
- line transfers
- partial capacity reduction
- function centralization
- footprint simplification

Output needed:
- fixed-cost reduction
- logistics penalty or benefit
- investment needs
- employment effect
- whether sites remain economically viable with lower volume

### Scenario C: Closure/consolidation
Output needed:
- incremental savings versus Scenario B
- one-time closure costs
- severance and transfer costs
- supplier/logistics disruption
- brand and execution risk
- political and industrial-relations risk

Only if Scenario A or B leaves a **material residual gap** can closure become economically persuasive.

## Practical cost logic
In German automotive manufacturing, closures tend to save money mainly through:
- elimination of duplicate fixed overhead,
- lower indirect labor,
- reduced maintenance and utility overhead,
- better capacity utilization at remaining plants,
- and simpler logistics / planning.

But they also create:
- high one-off restructuring costs,
- severance and social-plan costs,
- transfer and ramp-up costs at receiving sites,
- risk of lost productivity during implementation,
- and potentially higher logistics costs if production is concentrated far away.

So closure is economically rational only if:
1. the recurring fixed-cost savings are large,
2. the one-off costs are recoverable in a reasonable payback period,
3. and the remaining footprint can absorb volume without new bottlenecks.

## What “protected consolidation” can realistically achieve
A no-closure package can be economically real if it delivers:
- lower labor input per vehicle,
- reduced weekly hours or more flexible shift structure,
- lower bonus/outstanding variable compensation,
- fewer internal layers and functions,
- higher specialization,
- lower complexity,
- and volume alignment.

But there are limits:
- If current installed capacity materially exceeds foreseeable demand, internal measures may not be enough.
- If indirect cost density is too high across multiple plants, preserving all sites can leave the company with structurally high fixed cost.
- If product allocation cannot be rationalized, site protection may merely defer the problem.

## My candid assessment
A **no-closure settlement is feasible only if the combined employee contribution plus internal consolidation closes most of the gap**. If the residual gap remains large, then management’s case for deeper consolidation becomes much stronger.

In plain terms:
- **Small-to-moderate gap**: protected consolidation may work.
- **Large, persistent gap**: closures or equivalent footprint reduction become likely.

## What evidence would settle the matter
The audit should require:
- plant-level variable and fixed cost structure
- utilization and capacity utilization assumptions
- labor productivity by site
- indirect headcount by function
- logistics and transfer costs
- product pipeline and demand forecast by platform
- one-time restructuring cost by scenario
- recurring savings by scenario
- sensitivity analysis for volume downside
