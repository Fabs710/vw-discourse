[Works Council (Betriebsrat) (Plant-level employee representation under co-determination) — Round 1]
As Works Council, I will be very clear from the outset:

We will not accept plant closures in Germany. We will not accept compulsory redundancies. And we will not accept the breaking of long-standing job-security commitments as the price of a restructuring that management failed to prepare properly.

The workforce did not create this crisis alone, and it cannot be made to carry the full burden. If the company needs a restructuring, then it must be a fair one: with binding site guarantees, protection of jobs, and a real co-determination role for employee representatives in shaping any transformation. That means alternatives must be examined seriously first — capacity adjustments, internal redeployment, working-time solutions, training and qualification, and negotiated contributions that preserve the industrial base.

Our priorities are straightforward:
- keep every German plant open,
- rule out forced layoffs,
- secure binding guarantees through 2030,
- protect co-determination and the existing job-security framework,
- and ensure that any savings are shared fairly, not imposed one-sidedly on employees.

If management wants cooperation, it has to come with a credible, reversible plan that protects people and locations. If it comes with a fait accompli — closures, mass cuts, or the end of the historic guarantees — then it will meet fierce resistance, including mobilization of the workforce.

[Industrial Union (Trade union and collective-bargaining counterpart) — Round 1]
I agree with the core line from the Works Council: **there will be no acceptance of plant closures in Germany and no compulsory redundancies**. That is not a negotiable detail; it is the minimum requirement for any serious settlement.

Management’s framing that employees must absorb the pain to fix a structural problem is exactly what we reject. If the company has an overcapacity problem, then the answer cannot be to destroy jobs, break the collective framework, and shift all adjustment costs onto the workforce while management, owners, and capital markets remain insulated.

What we are prepared to discuss is a **fair, shared-sacrifice package** that keeps sites open and protects employment, for example:
- capacity reductions through natural fluctuation, transfers, and working-time instruments,
- temporary working-time adjustments,
- a negotiated employee contribution,
- forgoing bonuses or other cost components,
- qualification and redeployment instead of layoffs,
- and investment commitments for the future model mix.

But let me be equally clear: **a deal that depends on closures, forced redundancies, or the dismantling of long-standing protections is unacceptable**. If management insists on those points, then there is no basis for agreement, and the workforce will respond accordingly.

The burden of restructuring must be shared. If management and owners want the union to contribute to competitiveness, then they need to bring something real to the table too:
- binding guarantees for jobs and sites,
- a credible industrial plan,
- transparent figures on the cost problem,
- and concrete concessions from the company side, not just demands from labor.

So our position is this: **we are ready to bargain hard and constructively, but only on the basis of security, fairness, and co-determined transformation.** Anything else will face resistance.

[Group & Brand Management (Executive management driving the restructuring) — Round 1]
I’ll be equally clear from the outset: **the current cost base and overcapacity are not sustainable, and we cannot preserve every structure, every site, and every guarantee unchanged if we are serious about the brand’s future.**

I do agree on one point: **we need a negotiated solution, not chaos.** We do not want a prolonged confrontation for its own sake, and we know the supervisory-board reality. But negotiation only has value if it produces **structural** change.

Where I disagree sharply is this:

- **No plant closures in Germany, as a blanket rule, is not credible.**
- **No compulsory redundancies, as an absolute red line, is not compatible with the scale of the problem.**
- **Preserving long-standing job-security guarantees unchanged through 2030 is not realistic** if they prevent the company from restoring competitiveness.

Management has to act on the actual numbers, not on hopes. German factory costs are too high, the market is shrinking, and our competitors are moving faster and cheaper. If we settle for a cosmetic package — natural attrition, training measures, temporary working-time tweaks, and vague investment promises — then we will still be carrying an uncompetitive cost base in 2027, 2028, and 2030. That would be irresponsible.

So let me state the management position plainly:

1. **We need a durable reduction in capacity and fixed costs.**  
   That can include plant closure, partial site consolidation, or other binding capacity cuts — but it must be real, not symbolic.

2. **We need a meaningful labour-cost contribution.**  
   That means discussing pay moderation, the removal or reduction of legacy protections that no longer fit the cost situation, and additional employee contribution beyond standard flexibility tools.

3. **We need certainty on the path to 2030.**  
   If the employee side wants guarantees, then those guarantees must be earned through a package that restores margin, not block the restructuring.

4. **We are prepared to discuss the form of the adjustment, not its necessity.**  
   If you can bring forward alternatives that achieve comparable, durable savings in capacity and cost — at the same scale, with the same reliability — then we can assess them seriously. But “no closures, no layoffs, no concessions” is not an alternative.

On the union’s point about fairness: I reject the idea that management, owners, and capital markets should somehow be insulated while labor carries the burden. This is a group-wide crisis, and everyone will have to contribute. But contribution does not mean equal pain in abstraction; it means each side takes the measures necessary to save the business.

So my message is simple:

- **We are not negotiating around the need for deep restructuring.**
- **We are willing to negotiate the instruments.**
- **We will not accept a deal that leaves the German cost base structurally uncompetitive.**

If the employee side is serious about safeguarding the company’s industrial future, then it should come to the table with a package that delivers real and durable savings — including on labor costs and capacity. Otherwise, we will have to pursue tougher measures.

## Industrial economics view: what is actually expensive?

In large automotive restructuring, the cost issue is usually a mix of:

### 1) Direct labor cost
German automotive labor is expensive in gross terms, though the gap versus other Western European countries is often **smaller than commonly portrayed** once productivity is considered. The bigger problem is frequently:
- premium pay structures,
- legacy guarantees,
- high indirect staffing,
- shift and overtime rigidity,
- limited flexibility in deployment.

### 2) Fixed factory overhead

- absenteeism,
- quality issues,
- launch delays,

- elimination of duplicate fixed overhead,
- lower indirect labor,

- high one-off restructuring costs,
- severance and social-plan costs,
- transfer and ramp-up costs at receiving sites,
- risk of lost productivity during implementation,

## 4. Genuine conflicts (structurally incompatible positions)

1. **No-closure guarantee vs. openness to closure**
   - Works council/union/state want a settlement that excludes closures and compulsory redundancies.
   - Management/family/investors want to preserve closure as a live option if needed.
   - These are structurally incompatible unless the audit proves that non-closure measures close the gap.

2. **Protection first vs. flexibility first**
   - Labor and state want binding protection up front.
   - Management and capital want structural flexibility up front, with protection conditional on outcomes.
   - This sequencing conflict is the central deadlock.

3. **What counts as “protected consolidation”**
   - Employee/state side interprets it as genuine site protection.
   - Capital/management view it as compatible with future deeper cuts if required.
   - The same term is being used for two different bargaining logics.

4. **Burden of proof**
   - Employees say closures have not been proven unavoidable.
   - Management says non-closure alternatives have not been proven sufficient.
   - Both are correct in a legalistic sense, but they imply different default paths.

## 5. Areas of convergence

- All sides agree the current arrangement is unsustainable.
- All sides prefer negotiated restructuring over open confrontation.

## 6. Key trade-offs for the decision-maker

1. **Industrial peace vs. structural certainty**
   - A protected non-closure deal could reduce immediate conflict, but may not deliver enough savings.
   - A harder structural deal could improve economics, but risks strikes, political backlash, and execution failure.

2. **Irreversibility vs. credibility**
   - Labor seeks irreversible guarantees against closures and layoffs.
   - Capital seeks irreversibility in the form of actual cost reduction and capacity alignment.
   - The decision hinges on which irreversibility is more valuable at this stage.

3. **Short-term cost vs. long-term competitiveness**
   - Preserving all sites may cost more initially.
   - Closing sites may improve recurring economics but impose large one-off costs and industrial-relations damage.

4. **Governance legitimacy vs. managerial autonomy**
   - Co-determination and the state’s role mean legitimacy matters as much as economics.
   - Management cannot simply “optimize” without coalition support.

## 7. Integrative pathways

### Pathway A: Audit-first, decision-gated settlement
Agree immediately on a strict independent site-by-site audit. No side pre-commits to closures or no-closures before the data are in. The audit models non-closure, protected consolidation, and closure scenarios with recurring savings, one-off costs, employment effects, and payback periods.

### Pathway B: Protected consolidation with hard thresholds
All German sites remain formally open for a defined period, with no compulsory redundancies during that period, while the company implements real internal capacity reduction. If predefined savings thresholds are not met, negotiations reopen on further measures based on verified numbers.

### Pathway C: Differentiated site-by-site settlement
Instead of a blanket package, some sites may remain protected while others enter special consolidation tracks if the audit shows severe underutilization. This reduces the all-or-nothing character of the dispute and may allow partial agreement.
