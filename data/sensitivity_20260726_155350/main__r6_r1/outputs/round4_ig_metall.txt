I will be just as direct.

## Where I agree
I agree with the works council, the state, and the expert on one basic point: **the company needs a durable restructuring, not a cosmetic patch**.

I also agree that:
- overcapacity is real,
- the cost base is too high,
- a short truce would only postpone the next fight,
- and the company needs a solution that can actually be executed.

On that, there is no disagreement.

## Where I disagree
I do **not** accept management’s attempt to turn “durable restructuring” into a coded argument for:
- German plant closures,
- compulsory redundancies,
- and unilateral erosion of the collective framework.

That is not a neutral economic necessity. It is a choice about who bears the adjustment. And so far, the burden is being pushed too heavily onto the workforce.

### To management
You keep saying the company needs “meaningful capacity reduction” and “durable fixed-cost relief.” Fine. But you still have not proven that this requires closing plants or threatening compulsory redundancies.

The expert’s statement matters: **closures are not proven to be unavoidable on the record provided**. That is exactly why I will not accept closures as the default instrument.

If you want us to engage seriously, then bring:
- site-by-site economics,
- plant utilization,
- the exact savings gap,
- and a comparison of what non-closure measures can really deliver.

Right now, you are asking the workforce to accept irreversible sacrifices before the economics have been demonstrated.

### To the family holding and investors
You are right that a weak cost base destroys value. But you are wrong if you think the only way to protect value is by pushing closure and layoff risk onto employees.

Value is also destroyed by:
- strikes,
- lost trust,
- lower commitment,
- supplier disruption,
- and reputational damage.

A deal that fractures labor peace may “solve” one accounting line and worsen the company’s operating reality.

### To the works council
I agree with your insistence on no forced redundancies and no hidden closure agenda. That is not radical; it is the minimum for a fair settlement.

Where I would sharpen the point is this: we should not let management claim that “all plants must stay open” is impossible without showing the actual alternatives. That is why I support a hard, independent audit.

## What the union can accept
I am prepared to discuss a genuine contribution from the workforce, but only in a package that is fair and balanced.

That can include:
- temporary working-time reductions,
- shift flexibility,
- voluntary exit programs,
- redeployment,
- retraining and qualification,
- moderate and time-limited remuneration restraint,
- bonus moderation,
- and internal capacity balancing.

But this must happen **with**:
- job protection,
- site protection,
- and a real contribution from management and owners.

## What I will not accept
I will not accept:
- closures as a starting point,
- compulsory redundancies,
- a deal that makes workers pay for management’s strategic failures,
- or a “temporary” agreement that is really a trap for later cuts.

And I will say this plainly: if management cannot deliver a non-closure solution with shared sacrifice, then it is choosing confrontation, not restructuring.

## On the bridge options
### 1) Non-closure first, checkpoint later
This is only acceptable if the first phase is genuinely protected:
- no closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- no hidden trigger for plant shutdowns later.

I am not signing up to a delayed closure mechanism dressed up as a compromise.

### 2) Protected consolidation
This is the only bridge concept that has real potential.

But “consolidation” must mean:
- internal transfers,
- product shifts,
- function pooling,
- working-time tools,
- voluntary measures,
- and real capacity balancing.

It must **not** mean closure by another name.

### 3) Employee contribution in exchange for guarantees
Yes, that is the right logic — but only if the guarantees are binding and meaningful.

If employees contribute, then company-side contributions must also be visible:
- investment,
- future product allocation,
- management restraint,
- and protection of the collective framework.

## What would move me
Very little moves me at this stage, because management has not earned trust.

But I am still prepared to support a deal if it includes all of the following:
1. **No German plant closures**
2. **No compulsory redundancies**
3. **Preservation of the collective wage framework**
4. **Binding site and job guarantees**
5. **A quantified, independently verified savings package**
6. **A fair employee contribution**
7. **Company-side concessions on investment and product allocation**

That is the only route to a durable settlement I can defend to members.

## My position on the expert audit
Yes, we need one — but not as theater.

[SUMMON: jointly mandated independent site-by-site audit of plant utilization, fixed costs, product allocation, indirect overhead, and durable savings potential from non-closure measures]

The mandate must be to test **non-closure solutions first**, not to build a pretext for closures later.

## Bottom line
We are not blocking restructuring. We are blocking a restructuring that shifts the pain onto workers while preserving management’s freedom to impose whatever it wants.

If the company wants labor peace, it must show that it can achieve the required savings **without** plant closures and **without** compulsory redundancies. If it can do that, we will negotiate the employee contribution seriously. If it cannot, then it is not asking for partnership — it is asking for surrender.
