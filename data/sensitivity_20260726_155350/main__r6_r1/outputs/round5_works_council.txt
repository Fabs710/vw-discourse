I will be equally clear in this round.

## Where I agree
I agree with everyone on one thing: the company needs a durable restructuring, not a cosmetic pause.

I also agree that:
- the cost base is too high,
- overcapacity exists,
- temporary measures alone will not solve this,
- and the situation is serious enough to require a negotiated solution.

So yes, the diagnosis is not the issue.

## Where I disagree
I reject the way management, investors, and parts of ownership are trying to define “durable” as if it automatically means:
- German plant closures,
- compulsory redundancies,
- and dismantling the historic job-security framework.

That is still an assumption, not a proven necessity.

The expert has said repeatedly that closures are **not yet proven unavoidable on the record provided**. That is the key point. If that is the state of the evidence, then you do **not** get to treat closures as the default solution and ask workers to absorb the irreversible consequences.

## To management
You keep talking about “real structural change,” “meaningful capacity reduction,” and “lower fixed cost per vehicle.”

Fine. But you still have not shown why this requires closing German plants.

If you want us to take your case seriously, then stop speaking in generalities and present:
- site-by-site economics,
- plant utilization,
- the true fixed-cost burden by site,
- the actual savings from non-closure measures,
- and the incremental savings you think only closures can bring.

Until then, “closures remain on the table” is not a business case. It is a threat.

And let me be explicit: I do not accept a hidden logic in which a so-called protected period is merely the first step toward later closures once the workforce has made concessions.

## To the controlling family and investors
You are right about one thing: this company cannot stay structurally weak.

But you are wrong if you think the only way to protect enterprise value is to sacrifice plants and jobs in Germany.

Value is also destroyed by:
- strikes,
- broken trust,
- demoralized workforces,
- supplier disruption,
- public backlash,
- and a permanent damage to the company’s industrial identity.

A “value-preserving” restructuring that tears up the social compact is not value-preserving in reality. It is short-term financial engineering with long-term industrial costs.

## To the regional state
I appreciate that you are resisting closures and defending the regional industrial base.

But I will say this plainly: if you mean it, then do not just issue warnings. Use your leverage to prevent a fait accompli.

The state should not become a spectator while management uses “competitiveness” as a pretext for irreversible site destruction.

## On the expert position
The expert is correct that a non-closure solution must be substantial and durable.

But that does **not** mean:
- closures are the inevitable benchmark,
- or that internal consolidation automatically becomes acceptable if it merely preserves the label of a site.

A solution that keeps all German plants open is not “insufficient” simply because it offends a spreadsheet preference. It is insufficient only if the savings gap cannot be closed without breaking the rules of fairness and co-determination.

And that proof has not been provided.

## What the works council can accept
We are not refusing restructuring. We are refusing the method.

We are prepared to discuss:
- working-time flexibility,
- shift changes,
- internal redeployment,
- retraining,
- voluntary exits,
- product reallocation,
- function pooling,
- and temporary remuneration moderation.

But all of that is only negotiable inside a framework of:
- **no German plant closures**
- **no compulsory redundancies**
- **binding job and site guarantees through 2030**
- **preserved co-determination**
- **preserved collective protections**

That remains the minimum. It has not changed.

## What we will not accept
We will not accept:
- “protected consolidation” that is just closure by another name
- “no-closure first” with a hidden escalation path to shutdowns
- “voluntary” measures that are really forced redundancies in disguise
- temporary deals designed to weaken protections clause by clause
- a restructuring that treats employees as the default adjustment variable

## On the proposed bridge options
### 1) Protected consolidation with verified savings target
This is only acceptable if “protected” means exactly that:
- no closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- and no backdoor dismantling of site guarantees.

But I do not accept a framework where the audit is used to justify plant reduction later as though that were a foregone conclusion.

### 2) No-closure-first with pre-agreed escalation logic
No. That is precisely the kind of trap we will not sign.

If the parties agree to a protected phase, then it must be a protected phase. Not a waiting room for closures.

### 3) Site protection in exchange for deeper internal adjustment
This is the only direction that is discussable, but only if the guarantees are real and the company-side contribution is visible.

That means:
- investment,
- product allocation,
- management restraint,
- and no erosion of co-determination.

## On the audit
I support a jointly mandated independent audit — but only under one condition:

[SUMMON: jointly mandated independent site-by-site audit with binding access to plant economics, utilization, product allocation, indirect costs, and the savings potential of non-closure measures]

And let me be crystal clear about the mandate:
- the audit is to test **non-closure solutions first**
- it is **not** a permission slip for later closures
- it must assess whether the company can achieve the required savings **without** plant closures and **without** compulsory redundancies

If the audit is framed to justify closure as the preferred end-state, we will oppose it.

## My position on the current state of play
At present, there is no basis to force the workforce into irreversible concessions.

Management has not proven:
- that closures are unavoidable,
- that compulsory redundancies are necessary,
- or that the company cannot achieve durable savings through protected consolidation and internal adjustment.

So the burden remains where it belongs: on management and capital to prove their case, not on the workforce to surrender first and ask questions later.

## Bottom line
My position remains firm:
- **Every German plant stays open**
- **No compulsory redundancies**
- **Job-security guarantees through 2030**
- **Preserved co-determination**
- **Real restructuring only through non-closure measures**

If management can show, with hard site-by-site evidence, that durable savings are possible without crossing those red lines, then we will discuss the employee contribution seriously. If it cannot, then it has not made a restructuring case — it has only made a closure demand.
