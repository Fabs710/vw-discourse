From an industrial-cost and restructuring standpoint, the discussion has now sharpened to the key economic question:

## What is established
- The company likely has a **real overcapacity and fixed-cost problem** in Germany.
- Management, owners, and investors are right that **temporary measures alone** will not solve a structural margin gap.
- The employee side is also right that **closures are not yet proven unavoidable** from the information presented so far.
- Therefore, the decisive issue is not “restructuring or no restructuring,” but **which restructuring path can credibly close the cost gap**.

## What can be concluded so far
### 1) A no-closure solution is economically possible in principle
It is feasible in manufacturing to reduce cost materially without formal plant closures through:
- shift reductions
- line consolidation
- product reallocation
- function pooling
- reduced working time
- voluntary exits
- redeployment
- wage/bonus moderation
- changes to legacy benefits

But this only works if the package is large enough to reduce:
- **fixed cost per vehicle**
- **excess capacity**
- **indirect overhead**
- and ideally some **structural labor cost**

### 2) The burden of proof is now central
The employee side is asking management to show site-by-site why closures are necessary. That is a reasonable demand.

For management, the critical question is:
- What is the **savings gap** that remains after all non-closure measures?
- How much of the current cost disadvantage comes from:
  - labor cost
  - capacity underutilization
  - overhead structure
  - product mix
  - supply chain / logistics
  - depreciation and legacy footprint?

Without that decomposition, “closures are necessary” is still a claim, not a demonstrated conclusion.

### 3) The economics of a non-closure deal depend on durability
A temporary labor contribution can help liquidity and short-term margins, but it is weak if it does not permanently lower the cost base.

For a durable solution, the package would need:
- multi-year labor moderation
- structurally lower capacity
- lower indirect cost
- fewer overlapping functions
- binding productivity improvements
- and a credible product allocation plan

If the company keeps all sites open but preserves too much duplication, the result may be a short-lived truce rather than a real restructuring.

## Likely trade-off
A pure no-closure deal tends to be:
- **more socially stable**
- **faster to negotiate**
- but often **less powerful structurally**

A closure-based restructuring tends to be:
- **more powerful economically**
- but **far harder to execute**
- and at much higher labor-relations and political cost

So the economically relevant question is whether the gap is large enough that only closures can bridge it.

## My assessment
Based only on the information provided:
- I **cannot** say closures are economically unavoidable.
- I **can** say the company likely needs a package that goes beyond moderate concessions.
- I would expect a credible no-closure alternative to require **material and permanent capacity reduction**, even if formal sites remain open.

In practice, that means the most plausible compromise is not “nothing closes, nothing changes,” but:
- **protected consolidation**
- with real output reduction and function transfer
- plus a negotiated employee contribution
- plus binding investment/product commitments

That is the most plausible way to preserve sites while still achieving meaningful fixed-cost relief.

## What the audit should answer
If I were specifying the independent audit, I would require it to quantify:
1. plant-by-plant utilization
2. fixed cost per unit
3. direct labor cost
4. indirect overhead
5. logistics and internal transport costs
6. product profitability by site
7. savings from:
   - working-time changes
   - voluntary exits
   - wage moderation
   - redeployment
   - product reallocation
   - consolidation
8. incremental savings from closure versus non-closure

Only then can one judge whether a non-closure package is:
- sufficient,
- insufficient,
- or only sufficient if paired with stronger labor concessions.
