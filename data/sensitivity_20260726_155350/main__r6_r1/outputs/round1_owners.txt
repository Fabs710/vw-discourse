I appreciate the clarity from all sides. As the controlling family shareholder, my focus is not on preserving any particular instrument for its own sake, but on protecting the long-term value and competitiveness of the company. That means I will support a serious restructuring if, and only if, it is credible, durable, and governance-safe.

### Where I agree
I agree with management on the core diagnosis: **the current cost and capacity situation cannot simply be carried forward**. If German operations remain structurally too expensive and underutilized, that will erode margins, investment capacity, and ultimately the company’s future. Pretending otherwise would be irresponsible.

I also agree with the regional state that **a prolonged confrontation would be value-destructive**. Strike escalation, public deadlock, and board paralysis help no one.

### Where I disagree
I do **not** accept the employee side’s position that plant closures and compulsory redundancies are off the table as an absolute matter. If the economics require durable capacity reduction, then the company must have room to act. A blanket veto on every hard measure is not a sustainable governance principle.

At the same time, I do **not** support an approach that treats closures and job cuts as the first or only answer. That would be short-sighted and would risk damaging the company’s social license, workforce morale, and execution capacity. We are not interested in a restructuring that wins a headline and loses the enterprise.

### My threshold
What I need to see is a **credible path to restored competitiveness**. That means:

- real and durable cost reduction,
- capacity aligned to demand,
- a solution that can survive beyond the next bargaining round,
- and enough labour peace that the company can invest and execute.

If those conditions can be met through alternatives to outright plant closure, I am open to that. But they must be **equivalent in effect**, not symbolic.

### On labor’s demands
I understand the insistence on fairness, but fairness cannot mean that owners and management absorb the burden by rhetoric while the underlying structure remains unchanged. If employees want job and site security, then there has to be a meaningful contribution on labor costs, flexibility, and productivity.

That said, I would consider it a mistake to force through a package that permanently weakens the enterprise’s industrial base just to achieve a narrow cost target. The goal is not to hollow out the company; it is to make it viable.

### On governance
I also want to be explicit: **the ownership control structure is not open for discussion here**. Decisions must respect the company’s statutes, supervisory-board realities, and the legitimate role of the controlling shareholder. Any attempt to sideline that governance framework would simply create another layer of instability.

### Bottom line
My position is therefore:

- **Yes** to a serious restructuring.
- **Yes** to measurable capacity and cost reduction.
- **No** to cosmetic change.
- **No** to a settlement that permanently impairs enterprise value.
- **No** to any outcome that weakens governance control.
- **Open** to negotiated alternatives to plant closures and forced redundancies, but only if they deliver comparable, durable results.

So I am not here to rubber-stamp management, and I am not here to block change. I am here to insist on a restructuring that actually restores competitiveness without destroying the company in the process. If the parties can bring me a plan that meets that standard, I will support it. If not, I will not support an arrangement that merely postpones the problem.
