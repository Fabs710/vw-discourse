I will be equally direct.

## Where I agree
I agree with management, the expert, the works council, and the union on the basic diagnosis:

- the current structure is not sustainable,
- the cost base is too high,
- overcapacity is real,
- and cosmetic measures will not solve this.

I also agree that the company needs a **durable** restructuring, not a short truce that merely postpones the next crisis.

And I agree with the expert on one critical point: we need **site-by-site, hard numbers**, not slogans.

## Where I disagree
I do **not** accept the attempt by management, investors, and parts of ownership to make **German plant closures** and **compulsory redundancies** the default answer.

The expert has said clearly that closures are **not yet proven unavoidable on the record provided**. That is not a technical footnote. It is the central point.

So no: you do **not** get to treat closures as the starting assumption and ask workers, the state, and co-determination stakeholders to absorb the irreversible damage first.

## To management
You are right that a company like this cannot survive on hope.

But you still have not shown, convincingly and site by site, why:
- all German plants cannot remain open,
- compulsory redundancies are necessary,
- internal consolidation, product reallocation, shift changes, and voluntary measures are insufficient,
- and why the remaining savings gap cannot be closed without shutdowns.

Until that proof exists, “closures remain on the table” is not a business case. It is leverage language.

I also reject the idea that a “protected phase” is acceptable if it is merely the first step toward later closures once the workforce has already made concessions. That is not restructuring. That is sequencing a broken promise.

## To the works council and union
I agree with your insistence that:
- forced redundancies are unacceptable,
- co-determination is part of the company’s legitimacy,
- and workers should not be made the default adjustment variable.

You are right to demand proof before irreversible steps.

But I will also be frank: I cannot support a position that assumes every existing structure must remain untouched regardless of the economics. If protected consolidation can deliver real savings, then it should be tested seriously.

That said, “test” does **not** mean “create a backdoor to closures.” I reject any hidden escalation logic that would turn a protected period into a waiting room for shutdowns.

## To the controlling family and investors
You are right that a weak cost base and excess capacity cannot be wished away.

But you are wrong if you think value is protected simply by forcing plant cuts and job losses. Value is also destroyed by:
- strikes,
- broken trust,
- supplier disruption,
- public backlash,
- and permanent damage to the company’s industrial identity in this region.

A “value-preserving” restructuring that tears up the social compact is not value-preserving in the long run.

## To the regional state
I support your refusal to be sidelined.

And I will say this plainly: the state will not accept a restructuring that guts regional employment or weakens our statutory role. That is not what this shareholding and this special law are for.

We are not a passive investor. We are a legitimate political and economic actor, and we will use that role to protect the regional industrial base.

## What I can support
I am prepared to support a serious restructuring if it contains all of the following:

1. **No German plant closures as the starting point**
2. **No compulsory redundancies**
3. **Preserved co-determination and statutory shareholder rights**
4. **A genuinely independent, site-by-site audit**
5. **Real capacity and cost reduction through non-closure measures first**
6. **Binding investment and future product commitments**
7. **A fair, time-limited employee contribution**

That is the framework I can defend politically and economically.

## What I cannot support
I cannot support:
- a package that treats closures as the easy solution,
- a restructuring that sidelines the state’s veto and supervisory role,
- or a deal that asks workers to carry the pain while management and ownership avoid meaningful responsibility.

And I cannot support any outcome that guts regional employment.

## On the audit
I support the audit, but its mandate must be precise.

[SUMMON: jointly mandated independent site-by-site audit with binding access to plant economics, utilization, product allocation, indirect costs, and the savings potential of non-closure measures versus consolidation or closure]

Its task must be:
- to test **non-closure solutions first**,
- to quantify the residual savings gap,
- and to determine whether durable competitiveness can be restored **without** German plant closures and **without** compulsory redundancies.

If the audit is framed as a closure justification exercise, I will oppose it.

## My bottom line
I am not here to block restructuring.

I am here to block a restructuring that sacrifices the regional industrial base, weakens co-determination, and pretends that irreversible plant destruction is the only path to competitiveness.

If management can prove, with hard numbers, that a non-closure package cannot work, then the burden of persuasion becomes much higher. But until that proof exists, closures are not an accepted solution. They remain an unresolved threat.

That is my position.
