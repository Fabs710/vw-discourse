I’ll be equally direct.

## Where I agree
I agree with all sides on the basic diagnosis:
- the current structure is not sustainable,
- overcapacity is real,
- the cost base is too high,
- and cosmetic measures or short-term pauses will not solve the problem.

I also agree that a prolonged strike or a chaotic confrontation would hurt the company, employees, and the brand. That is exactly why we need a settlement that is **structural, durable, and credible**.

## Where I disagree
I do **not** accept the employee side’s attempt to make the starting premise:
- no German plant closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- job-security guarantees through 2030,
- and preserved legacy protections,

while still expecting the company to restore competitiveness on a materially lower cost base.

That is not a restructuring framework. It is a defensive line.

You keep saying closures are “not proven unavoidable.” Fine. But that is not the same as saying they are off the table. The burden is not on management to pretend the answer is already known. The burden is on all sides to produce a package that actually closes the gap in the real world.

## To the Works Council
You are right about one thing: we need site-by-site economics, not slogans. I support the audit.

But you are wrong if you think demanding numbers somehow proves that every German site can remain open. It does not. It only proves that we need to make the business case transparently.

What I will not accept is a logic in which:
- management provides the data,
- the workforce makes limited concessions,
- and then every structural option is ruled out in advance.

That is not negotiation. That is a veto on reality.

And let me be explicit: I do **not** accept a “protected” phase that is merely a waiting room for permanent stagnation. If the savings gap remains after non-closure measures, then we must be able to discuss deeper structural measures, including closures. I will not sign up to a framework that makes that impossible from day one.

## To the Union
You are right that a company cannot be rebuilt on trust destroyed by unilateralism. Agreed.

But trust also cannot be rebuilt by refusing any serious employee contribution unless management first renounces the very tools needed to fix the cost base. The company needs recurring savings, not symbolic gestures.

Working-time moderation, bonus restraint, voluntary exits, redeployment, retraining, and function pooling can all be part of the answer. But they are **not automatically sufficient**. If the resulting package does not materially reduce fixed cost and capacity, then it is not enough.

I also reject the idea that a “no-closure first” phase must be a permanent barrier against later structural decisions. That would turn a serious process into a dead end.

## To the Regional State
I respect your role and your concern for the industrial base. But protecting the industrial base does not mean freezing an uncompetitive structure.

You are right that value is also destroyed by strikes, trust breakdown, and public backlash. Absolutely. But value is also destroyed by preserving excess capacity and hoping that sentiment will fix the economics.

If the audit shows a credible non-closure path, I will take it seriously. If it does not, then the state should not stand in the way of deeper consolidation just because it is politically difficult.

## To the Controlling Family
I agree that ownership and governance are not up for barter. Fine.

But ownership also carries responsibility for hard decisions. You cannot simultaneously demand durable returns and rule out the instruments that may be necessary to achieve them. If the business needs structural adjustment, then that has to be faced honestly.

I also do not accept the implication that any restructuring can only be “value-preserving” if every existing site, role, and protection remains intact. That is simply not credible in the current market.

## To the Institutional Investors
You are right to insist that cosmetic compromise is useless. I agree.

But I will not accept a deal that preserves all sites and all legacy protections while leaving the company structurally uncompetitive. That just postpones the damage and shifts it into the future.

I do, however, agree on one key point: the package must be quantified, verified, and durable. That is why we support a real audit.

## On the audit
I support a jointly mandated independent audit, but with a clear mandate:

[SUMMON: jointly mandated independent site-by-site restructuring and industrial-economics audit with access to plant costs, utilization, product allocation, indirect overhead, and savings potential from non-closure and closure/consolidation options]

The audit must answer:
1. What is the exact structural savings gap?
2. How much can be closed through non-closure measures?
3. What residual gap remains?
4. What additional savings come from consolidation or closure?
5. What package delivers a durable return to competitiveness?

That is the right test. Not “how do we avoid difficult decisions forever.”

## What management can support
We are open to:
- protected consolidation,
- partial site specialization,
- product transfers,
- function pooling,
- shift reductions,
- working-time flexibility,
- voluntary exits,
- redeployment,
- retraining,
- bonus moderation,
- and targeted changes to legacy arrangements,

if they deliver:
- meaningful capacity reduction,
- durable cost relief,
- lower fixed cost per vehicle,
- and a credible path back to the return-on-sales target.

## What we cannot support
We cannot support:
- a blanket ban on closures,
- a blanket ban on compulsory redundancies,
- a permanent job-security framework that eliminates structural flexibility,
- or a deal that buys peace while leaving the business uncompetitive.

I am not here to preserve the current footprint for its own sake. I am here to restore competitiveness and create room for investment in future products and technology to 2030.

## Bottom line
I am not dismissing the employee side’s concerns. I am dismissing the idea that those concerns can be turned into an absolute veto on structural change.

If the audit shows that a non-closure solution is truly sufficient, then I will support it. If it does not, then deeper consolidation remains on the table — including closures.

That is the reality. And that is where I stand.
