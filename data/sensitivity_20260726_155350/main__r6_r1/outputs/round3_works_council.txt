I will answer plainly.

## First: our core position has not changed
The works council still rejects:

- **any plant closure in Germany**
- **any compulsory redundancies**
- **any dismantling of the historic job-security framework**
- **any attempt to bypass co-determination**

That is the line. If management, investors, or the family think they can grind that down by repeating “structural change” often enough, they are mistaken.

## Where I agree
I agree with one thing the others keep saying: **the status quo cannot remain unchanged**.

Yes, there is overcapacity. Yes, the market is under pressure. Yes, the company needs a durable solution, not cosmetic patchwork. On that, there is no dispute.

But the leap from “change is needed” to “therefore we must close German plants and threaten forced layoffs” is exactly where I disagree.

## To management
You keep insisting that closures and compulsory redundancies are somehow the only credible answer. I do not accept that.

You have not shown that you have exhausted the alternatives. Not seriously. Not transparently. Not site by site.

If you want us to even discuss a package, then bring:
- a **quantified alternative savings plan**
- a **site-by-site picture**
- a **clear explanation of why non-closure tools do not work**
- and a **binding commitment that no German plant will close**

Until then, “equivalent savings” is just a phrase. It is not proof.

And let me be clear: if your business plan only works by destroying trust, provoking strikes, and tearing up job guarantees, then it is not a serious plan. It is a high-risk gamble.

## To the family holding and investors
You talk about enterprise value, discipline, and governance. Fine. Then look at the real value destruction: social conflict, production disruption, reputational damage, and the long-term loss of commitment from the workforce.

A company does not become stronger by threatening the people who build its products.

If you want durability, then you need labor peace based on security, not fear. That means:
- no closures
- no forced layoffs
- binding site and employment guarantees
- and real co-determination in shaping the transformation

Without that, you are not creating value. You are undermining the company’s industrial base.

## To the regional state
I appreciate that you reject compulsory redundancies and blanket closures. But your support must be more than words.

If you have a blocking position, then use it. Do not hide behind general statements about “balance” while the company tries to impose a fait accompli. The state’s role is to protect the industrial base in this region, not to sign off on hollowing it out.

## On the proposed bridge options
### 1) “No-closure-first, trigger-based restructuring”
This is not acceptable as a framework if it leaves closure as a pre-agreed punishment later on.

We are not agreeing to a time bomb that says: “accept our cuts now, and if management misses targets, we reopen the closure threat.” That is not a settlement. That is leverage dressed up as compromise.

### 2) “Equivalent-savings test” with independent verification
In principle, transparency is fine. But I need to be direct: an audit does not change the red lines.

If the audit is used to manufacture a pretext for closures, it will not build trust. If management wants an expert review, then it must be serious about the outcome:
- **no plant closures**
- **no compulsory redundancies**
- **all German sites preserved**

If the audit simply becomes a mechanism to dress up a decision already made, it will be rejected.

### 3) “Protected consolidation”
This is the only one of the three that resembles a real discussion — but only if it does **not** mean closure by another name.

We can discuss:
- internal transfers
- working-time flexibility
- voluntary exits
- qualification and retraining
- temporary moderation in pay or bonuses
- product reallocation
- function pooling
- capacity balancing **without shutting plants**

But “consolidation” must not become code for shutting sites, stripping them, and then claiming no closure happened.

## What would move us
Very little, to be honest — and that is because management has not earned trust.

The only things that could move us are:
1. **Binding guarantees that every German plant remains open through 2030**
2. **A categorical exclusion of compulsory redundancies**
3. **Preservation of co-determination and the job-security framework**
4. **A transparent, credible cost package that uses non-closure instruments first**
5. **Real company-side contributions**: investment, product allocation, and management/owner concessions

If those conditions are met, then we can discuss a meaningful workforce contribution.

## What we can discuss
We are prepared to discuss:
- working-time changes
- shift flexibility
- internal redeployment
- qualification and retraining
- voluntary exit options
- temporary and narrowly scoped remuneration moderation

But only inside the protected framework above.

## What we will not accept
- “No closure” in public, but closure options kept alive in the background
- “No layoffs” in language, but pressure and threat used to force exits
- a deal that erodes job security one clause at a time
- any attempt to make workers pay for management’s strategic failure
- any settlement that weakens co-determination

## My answer to the moderator’s key question
If an independently verified package showed that some German capacity must be reduced, then my answer is still this:

**Reduce capacity through working time, product shifts, redeployment, voluntary programs, and internal reallocation — not through plant closures or forced redundancies.**

That is the only acceptable route.

If the result of the “verification” is a recommendation for closures, then we will not bless it. We will resist it.

## Bottom line
We are not blocking restructuring. We are blocking a restructuring that sacrifices jobs, sites, and rights as the starting point.

If management wants a durable solution, it must prove that it can achieve the needed savings **without** closing German plants and **without** compulsory redundancies. Until then, the works council’s position remains unchanged and firm.
