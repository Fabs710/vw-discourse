I will be equally direct.

## Where I agree
I agree with the repeated diagnosis that **the current situation is not sustainable**.  
We have overcapacity, pressure on margins, and a real competitiveness problem. I also agree that a cosmetic fix or a short truce would only postpone the next confrontation.

I further agree that a durable settlement is preferable to an open-ended strike spiral. The union does not want permanent conflict. We want a viable industrial future.

## Where I disagree
What I do **not** accept is the claim that the only serious route to competitiveness is:
- German plant closures,
- compulsory redundancies,
- and a one-sided erosion of the collective framework.

That is not “necessary” simply because management says so. It is a choice — and it is a choice that would shift the full burden onto the workforce.

### To management
You keep asking for “structural change,” but your framing still treats closures and forced layoffs as the default proof of seriousness. I reject that.

If you want the union to engage constructively, then you need to show that you have genuinely exhausted the alternatives:
- working-time instruments,
- internal redeployment,
- voluntary exit programs,
- qualification and retraining,
- capacity balancing through product allocation,
- temporary wage/bonus moderation,
- and a fair employee contribution tied to binding guarantees.

Right now, you are asking us to accept pain first and proof later. That is not acceptable.

## On the moderator’s bridge options

### 1) “No-closure-first, trigger-based restructuring”
I can engage with the idea of a **time-limited protected period**, but only if it is not a disguised trap.

If “trigger-based” means: *take the cuts now, and if management misses targets, reopen closures later*, then no.  
That is not security. That is deferred blackmail.

If you want serious talks, the first phase must be:
- no plant closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- real labor-cost and flexibility contributions,
- and binding company-side commitments.

A second phase can only be discussed if it is based on jointly agreed facts, not an open-ended threat.

### 2) “Equivalent-savings test” with independent verification
This is the only bridge option that has any serious value — but only if the mandate is real.

I would support an independent expert review **if** it examines:
- site-by-site economics,
- total durable savings from non-closure measures,
- realistic utilization and product allocation,
- and the cost of social conflict and execution risk.

But I will be clear: an audit cannot be used as a pretext to force through closures by another name.

If the result shows a non-closure path can achieve the required savings, then that path must be taken.  
If management wants to claim otherwise, it has to prove it with hard numbers — not rhetoric.

### 3) “Protected consolidation”
This is the only concept that can be discussed seriously, but only if “consolidation” does **not** mean plant shutdowns, forced layoffs, or the hollowing out of sites.

We can discuss:
- working-time flexibility,
- shift redesign,
- internal transfers,
- retraining,
- voluntary exits,
- temporary remuneration moderation,
- and product/function reallocation.

But we will not accept consolidation as a euphemism for closure.

## To the works council
I agree with your insistence on protecting sites and rejecting compulsory redundancies. That is a legitimate red line.

Where I would sharpen it is this: we should not allow the employer to claim that “preserving all sites” is impossible without even disclosing the real alternatives. That is why I support an expert audit and a transparent bargaining process.

But I also want to be clear: workers cannot be asked to contribute without the company, owners, and management also contributing visibly. Shared sacrifice must mean shared sacrifice.

## To the regional state
Your leverage matters, and I welcome your rejection of forced redundancies. But that has to translate into real pressure on management, not just political language.

If the state wants a socially sustainable settlement, it should back:
- binding site protections,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- investment commitments,
- and a negotiated employee contribution within a fair framework.

## To the controlling family and investors
You are right about one thing: value destruction matters.  
But you cannot define value only as cost cutting. A settlement that tears up trust, provokes lasting conflict, and undermines the workforce’s commitment will also destroy value.

If you want durability, then you need a deal that protects labor peace and gives employees a reason to stand behind the transformation.

## What would move the union
A deal could move us if it contains all of the following:

1. **No plant closures in Germany**
2. **No compulsory redundancies**
3. **A defined and time-limited employee contribution**
4. **Management and owner contributions as well**
5. **Binding investment and product commitments**
6. **Preservation of the collective wage framework**
7. **A transparent, independently verified restructuring plan**

That is the only kind of package we will regard as serious.

## What we can discuss
We are prepared to discuss:
- temporary working-time reductions,
- flexible shifts,
- internal redeployment,
- retraining and qualification,
- voluntary exits,
- bonus moderation,
- and narrowly defined cost adjustments.

We are **not** prepared to discuss any package built on:
- plant closures,
- compulsory redundancies,
- or the slow dismantling of collective protections.

## On “equivalent savings”
Let me be very clear: “equivalent in effect” cannot mean equivalent in worker pain.

If management wants comparable savings, then it must accept comparable responsibility:
- owners contribute,
- management contributes,
- the workforce contributes,
- and the solution preserves jobs and sites.

If that cannot be done, then the restructuring proposal is not balanced; it is simply imposed adjustment.

## Bottom line
The union remains open to a serious settlement. But that settlement must:
- protect jobs,
- preserve sites,
- avoid forced layoffs,
- and keep the collective wage framework intact.

If management continues to treat closures and compulsory redundancies as the starting point, then it is choosing confrontation. And if that is the choice, we will use the tools we have.
