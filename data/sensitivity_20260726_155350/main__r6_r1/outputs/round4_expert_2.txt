From an industrial-economics and restructuring standpoint, the discussion is now at the right level of specificity: the issue is not whether adjustment is needed, but whether the required savings can be achieved **without German plant closures and compulsory redundancies**.

## My assessment

### Verified from the record
- The company has a **structural cost and capacity problem**.
- Temporary or purely cosmetic measures will not be enough.
- A **durable** solution must reduce fixed cost and improve unit economics.
- On the information provided, **closures are not yet proven economically unavoidable**.

### Reasonable inference
A non-closure solution is **possible in principle**, but only if it is substantial enough to:
- reduce excess capacity,
- lower fixed cost per vehicle,
- cut indirect overhead,
- and secure multi-year labor-cost relief.

In practice, that usually means a package of **protected consolidation**, not “business as usual.”

## What a credible no-closure package would need
A package that preserves all German sites can still be economically credible if it includes most of the following:

1. **Shift and line consolidation**
   - fewer operating lines or shifts
   - higher utilization at the remaining lines
   - elimination of parallel or duplicated production steps

2. **Product reallocation**
   - transfer of models or derivatives between sites
   - specialization of sites to reduce duplication

3. **Function pooling**
   - consolidation of support functions, administration, engineering, logistics, or pre-assembly

4. **Working-time flexibility**
   - temporary or medium-term reductions in hours
   - annualized hours or more flexible shift patterns

5. **Voluntary exits and redeployment**
   - attrition, early retirement, voluntary severance, internal transfers
   - retraining into growth areas

6. **Labor-cost moderation**
   - wage/bonus moderation
   - limited changes to legacy allowances or guarantees
   - preferably time-limited but with enough duration to matter

7. **Investment and product commitments**
   - to avoid a “cut-and-run” restructuring
   - and to make the remaining footprint viable

## Economic test: what must be shown
For a no-closure deal to be credible, the audit should answer:

- What is the current **savings gap**?
- How much of that gap is due to:
  - labor cost,
  - underutilized capacity,
  - duplicated overhead,
  - product mix,
  - logistics,
  - legacy footprint?
- What savings can be achieved by non-closure measures?
- What savings are only achievable through closure?

That last comparison is decisive.

## Key point
A non-closure solution does **not** have to preserve full current capacity. It can preserve sites while still reducing:
- output,
- shifts,
- headcount,
- functions,
- and cost intensity.

So the relevant question is not “closures or nothing,” but:
**Can the company achieve the required permanent cost reduction by internal consolidation and labor-cost adjustment alone?**

## My judgment
Based on the information given:
- I **cannot** conclude that closures are unavoidable.
- I **can** conclude that a merely moderate compromise is unlikely to be enough.
- The most plausible compromise is a **protected consolidation package**:
  - all sites remain formally open,
  - but capacity is reduced materially,
  - functions are pooled,
  - labor contributes through flexibility and moderation,
  - and the company commits to investment and product allocation.
