I’ll be direct.

## Where I agree
I agree with management, the expert, and the capital side on the core diagnosis:

- the current German cost and capacity structure is not sustainable,
- temporary measures alone will not restore competitiveness,
- and a prolonged conflict would destroy value for everyone, including the workforce.

I also agree with the state and the employee side on one important point: **a purely symbolic restructuring is worthless**. If we do this, it has to be durable and operationally credible.

## Where I disagree
I do **not** accept the works council’s and union’s position that the outcome must be:
- no plant closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- no meaningful adjustment to legacy protections,
- and then somehow still deliver a structural reset.

That is not a complete solution. It is a veto on the instruments management may need to fix the business.

At the same time, I also do **not** accept management’s implication that closures are already proven unavoidable. The expert was careful, and rightly so: the record shows a serious structural problem, but **not yet proof** that German plant closures are the only viable answer.

That distinction matters. We should not turn a serious restructuring into an ideological contest over closure as an end in itself.

## My position
My position is unchanged:

- I support **durable competitiveness**.
- I support **real capacity and cost reduction**.
- I support **labor peace**, if it is based on credible economics.
- I do **not** support any solution that permanently impairs enterprise value or weakens governance control.

That means I am open to a non-closure solution **if** it is genuinely equivalent in effect. But “equivalent” means economically equivalent, not merely more comfortable politically.

## To management
You are right that the company needs meaningful capacity reduction and lower fixed cost per vehicle. But you still need to show, site by site, why the non-closure package cannot close the gap.

So I support the demand for an audit — but it must be a real one, not a procedural delay.

[SUMMON: jointly mandated independent site-by-site restructuring and industrial-economics audit with execution-risk assessment]

That audit must quantify:
- plant-by-plant cost,
- utilization,
- indirect overhead,
- product allocation,
- savings from working-time and voluntary measures,
- and the incremental savings from consolidation or closure.

If the no-closure path can deliver the required durable savings, fine. If it cannot, then we should not pretend otherwise.

## To the works council and union
I hear your insistence on job security and co-determination. That is legitimate. But your current stance still assumes you can preserve all sites and all protections while the company absorbs a structural cost reset. That is not automatically feasible.

You keep asking for proof that closures are necessary. Fair enough. Then let’s get the proof. But do not confuse the absence of proof today with a guarantee that no deeper measures will ever be needed.

What I can support is:
- working-time flexibility,
- redeployment,
- retraining,
- voluntary exits,
- function pooling,
- product reallocation,
- temporary moderation of remuneration,
- and targeted capacity reduction,

provided these measures actually deliver the savings.

What I cannot support is a package that preserves every legacy structure and then expects shareholders to accept a weaker company indefinitely.

## To the regional state
I respect your role and your concern for the regional industrial base. But I cannot support the idea that the state can block any serious restructuring simply because it is politically difficult.

If the audit shows that non-closure measures can genuinely restore competitiveness, I will support that route. If it does not, then the state cannot freeze the business in place and call that stewardship.

The company is a regional anchor, yes — but it remains an industrial business. Protecting the base means making it competitive, not insulating it from all hard choices.

## On the bridge options
### 1) Non-closure first, checkpoint later
I can support a time-limited protected period **only if** the first phase is serious:
- no closures during the review period,
- no compulsory redundancies during that period,
- meaningful employee contributions,
- and a hard, jointly agreed checkpoint.

But I will not accept a hidden promise that if management does not like the outcome, it will simply force closures later without new evidence.

### 2) Protected consolidation
This is the most realistic bridge, in my view.

It can work if it means:
- actual capacity reduction,
- internal consolidation,
- product shifts,
- function pooling,
- shift reductions,
- and lower fixed cost,

without immediate shutdowns. But it must be real consolidation, not semantic relabeling.

### 3) Employee contribution in exchange for guarantees
Yes, but the guarantees cannot be absolute forever if the economics move against them. A deal must be durable, not frozen.

If the workforce wants stronger protection, then the contribution has to be commensurate and the economics must still work.

## My non-negotiables
I will not support any outcome that:
- permanently impairs enterprise value,
- weakens control and governance,
- leaves the company structurally uncompetitive,
- or buys short-term peace at the price of long-term decline.

I also will not support a restructuring that turns into a prolonged public conflict damaging the brand and operations.

## Bottom line
I am not here to preserve the current structure.  
I am here to preserve and grow the long-term value of the family holding’s core asset.

That requires:
- real change,
- verified economics,
- and a solution that is durable enough to restore margins and competitiveness.

If the audit shows that a no-closure package can truly deliver that, I will back it seriously. If not, then deeper consolidation stays on the table.

That is my position.
