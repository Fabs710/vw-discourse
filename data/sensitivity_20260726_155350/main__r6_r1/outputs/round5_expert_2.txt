### 2) What a proper audit must show
A decision-useful independent audit should quantify, for **each German site**:

### 3) Relative economics of the main options
From an industrial-economics perspective:

#### A. Protected consolidation
This can be viable if it actually removes capacity in practice, for example through:
- shift cuts,
- line reductions,
- product transfers,
- pooling of support functions,
- and simplification of the footprint.

It can deliver real savings, but only if the volume reduction is substantial enough.

#### B. Working-time / remuneration moderation
Useful, but usually **not sufficient on its own**.  
It lowers labor cost, but it does not fully solve structural overcapacity.

#### C. Voluntary exits / redeployment
Socially and operationally preferable to forced redundancies, but often **too slow and too limited** unless paired with real capacity reduction.

#### D. Plant closures
This is usually the strongest lever for removing:
- plant-specific fixed overhead,
- duplicate functions,
- maintenance and utilities,
- logistics complexity,
- and under-absorbed fixed cost.

So in pure cost terms, closures are often the most powerful tool.  
But from the record here, they are **not yet demonstrated to be necessary**.

### 4) What can be concluded now
A technically defensible conclusion is:

- **A no-closure solution is still economically possible.**
- **It is not yet proven sufficient.**
- **Closures may be economically stronger, but they are not yet proven unavoidable.**

So the burden at this stage is not to assume closures, but to test whether a **quantified protected-consolidation package** can close the residual savings gap.

### 5) What would make a no-closure settlement credible
A non-closure package would need:
- quantified recurring savings,
- real capacity reduction in practice,
- sufficiently long labor peace to realize the savings,
- investment and product commitments to protect utilization,
- and a clear fallback if savings are missed.

If those elements are absent, the risk is that the settlement merely delays the structural decision.
