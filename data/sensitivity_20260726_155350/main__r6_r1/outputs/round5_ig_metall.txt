I will be direct.

## Where I agree
I agree with the diagnosis that the company needs a **durable restructuring**, not a cosmetic pause.

I also agree that:
- the cost base is too high,
- overcapacity is real,
- temporary measures alone are insufficient,
- and a negotiated solution is preferable to a prolonged industrial conflict.

So yes: this cannot be solved by slogans or by a short truce.

## Where I disagree
I **do not** accept that “durable restructuring” must mean:
- German plant closures,
- compulsory redundancies,
- or dismantling the collective wage framework.

That remains an assumption, not a proven necessity.

The expert has stated clearly that **closures are not yet proven unavoidable on the record provided**. That matters. If the evidence does not prove inevitability, then closures cannot be treated as the default instrument and imposed on the workforce as a foregone conclusion.

## To management
You keep arguing from general necessity:
- meaningfully lower fixed costs,
- durable capacity reduction,
- better unit economics.

We do not dispute the goal. We dispute the method.

If you want us to accept irreversible sacrifices, then bring the numbers:
- site-by-site economics,
- utilization rates,
- fixed cost per site,
- indirect overhead,
- and the actual savings from non-closure measures.

Until then, “closures remain on the table” is not a business case. It is pressure language.

And let me be explicit: I do **not** accept a “protected” phase that is really just the first step toward later closures once workers have already paid in.

## To the works council
I agree with your line on no forced redundancies, no hidden closure agenda, and preserved co-determination.

Where I would sharpen it is this: we should not allow management to present “all plants stay open” as impossible without first proving the alternatives. That is exactly why a hard audit is needed.

## To the controlling family and investors
You are right that the company cannot stay structurally weak.

But you are wrong if you think value is protected only by cutting plants and jobs. Value is also destroyed by:
- strikes,
- broken trust,
- supplier disruption,
- demoralization,
- reputational damage,
- and long-term weakening of the industrial base.

A deal that shifts all pain onto the workforce is not a durable value-preserving solution. It is short-term financial relief with long-term operational damage.

## To the regional state
I welcome your opposition to closures and your defense of the industrial base.

But words are not enough. If you mean it, use your leverage. Do not let management manufacture a fait accompli and then call it “responsible restructuring.”

## What the union can accept
We are prepared to discuss a genuine workforce contribution, but only in a balanced package.

That can include:
- temporary working-time reductions,
- shift flexibility,
- voluntary exits,
- redeployment,
- retraining,
- bonus moderation,
- and time-limited remuneration restraint.

But that must happen **with**:
- job protection,
- site protection,
- and visible contributions from management and owners.

## What we will not accept
We will not accept:
- closures as the starting point,
- compulsory redundancies,
- a permanent weakening of the collective framework,
- or a deal that makes workers pay for management’s strategic failure.

## On the bridge options
### 1) Protected consolidation with verified savings target
This is the only bridge concept with real potential.

But “protected” must mean:
- no closures,
- no forced layoffs,
- no backdoor dismantling of site guarantees.

If internal consolidation can reduce capacity and fixed cost materially without plant shutdowns, then that is the path worth testing.

### 2) No-closure-first with escalation logic
No. I do **not** support a hidden closure trigger.

If there is a protected phase, then it must be genuinely protected. Not a waiting room for future shutdowns.

### 3) Site protection in exchange for deeper internal adjustment
This is the right logic — but only if the guarantees are binding and the company-side contribution is real.

That means:
- investment,
- future product allocation,
- management restraint,
- and preservation of the collective framework.

## On the audit
Yes, we need one. And it must be real, not theater.

[SUMMON: jointly mandated independent site-by-site audit with binding access to plant economics, utilization, product allocation, indirect costs, and the savings potential of non-closure measures]

The mandate must be explicit:
- test **non-closure solutions first**
- quantify the savings gap
- compare that gap with what closures would supposedly add
- and assess whether the company can reach the required durable savings **without** closures and **without** compulsory redundancies

If the audit is framed as a closure justification exercise, we will oppose it.

## Bottom line
We are not blocking restructuring. We are blocking a restructuring that defaults to sacrificing workers, sites, and protections while management and ownership avoid meaningful contribution.

If the company can prove, with hard numbers, that a non-closure package cannot work, then it will still have to explain why workers should bear the social cost of that failure. If it cannot prove that, then closures and forced redundancies are not legitimate first choices.

Our position remains:
- **No German plant closures**
- **No compulsory redundancies**
- **Preserve the collective wage framework**
- **Preserve co-determination**
- **Negotiate a fair employee contribution only in exchange for binding site and job security**

That is the only basis for a durable settlement we can defend to members.
