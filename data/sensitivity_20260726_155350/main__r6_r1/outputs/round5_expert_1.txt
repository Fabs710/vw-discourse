From an industrial-economics perspective, the discussion has now converged on the right technical question:

## What matters now
Not “closures yes/no” in the abstract, but:

1. **How large is the structural cost gap per vehicle and per site?**
2. **How much of that gap can be closed without plant shutdowns?**
3. **What recurring savings are available from capacity, labor, logistics, and overhead measures?**
4. **What residual gap would remain if sites stay open?**
5. **Is that residual gap acceptable relative to the required margin target?**

That is the only defensible basis for deciding whether a non-closure package is economically credible.

## My assessment on the current record
Given the information provided, **closures are not proven unavoidable**.  
That does **not** mean closures are unnecessary; it means the business case for irreversible steps has not yet been demonstrated on the record here.

What can be concluded is:

- the company has a **real structural overcapacity/cost problem**;
- **temporary concessions alone will not be enough**;
- a serious solution must deliver **durable fixed-cost reduction**;
- and **non-closure measures must be quantified site by site** before anyone can say they are sufficient or insufficient.

## What a credible audit must show
For each German site, the audit should establish:

- annual output and utilization;
- direct labor cost per vehicle;
- indirect labor and overhead burden;
- logistics and internal transport cost;
- depreciation / fixed-cost absorption;
- product-mix profitability;
- transferability of volumes to other sites;
- savings potential from:
  - working-time changes,
  - bonus/pay moderation,
  - voluntary exits,
  - redeployment,
  - function pooling,
  - shift reduction,
  - line simplification,
  - and partial specialization.

Only then can one judge whether closures are economically required or whether protected consolidation can do the job.

## Likely economics of the bridge options
### 1) Protected consolidation
This is usually the strongest non-closure option economically if:
- volumes can be reduced enough to remove a full shift or a production line,
- indirect functions can be pooled,
- and the product range can be simplified.

It can produce real savings, but **not all “protected” arrangements are equal**. If output stays fragmented across too many sites, the savings often fall short.

### 2) Working-time / remuneration moderation
This helps cash cost and near-term labor cost, but by itself it usually does **not** solve a structural overcapacity problem. It is a contributor, not a full answer.

### 3) Voluntary exits / redeployment
Useful for headcount reduction and social acceptability, but it is often **too slow and too limited** to fix a major fixed-cost overhang unless combined with volume reduction.

### 4) Site closures
This is the most decisive instrument for structural cost reduction because it removes:
- fixed overhead,
- duplicate support functions,
- plant-specific logistics,
- maintenance,
- utilities,
- and under-absorbed depreciation.

So from a pure cost perspective, closures are usually the **strongest** lever. But they also carry the highest execution, labor-relations, and disruption risk.

## Practical conclusion
The economically rational path is:

- first test whether **protected consolidation plus labor contribution** can close the savings gap;
- if not, then the residual gap should be made explicit;
- only then can one justify deeper structural measures.

## What would make a non-closure deal credible
A non-closure settlement would need all of the following:

- **quantified recurring savings**, not one-off cuts;
- **capacity reduction in practice**, even if sites remain formally open;
- **long enough labor peace** to realize the savings;
- **product and investment commitments** that protect utilization of the remaining footprint;
- and a **clear mechanism for failing sites/functions** if savings are not achieved.

Without those elements, a no-closure deal risks becoming a postponement rather than a restructuring.

## Bottom line
On the facts given, the right technical answer is:

- **A no-closure solution is still economically possible, but not yet proven sufficient.**
- **Closures are a stronger cost tool, but not yet proven unavoidable.**
- The decisive issue is whether the audit can show that **protected consolidation closes the structural gap**.
