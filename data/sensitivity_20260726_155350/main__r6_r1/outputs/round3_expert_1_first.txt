## Expert assessment: can the company achieve equivalent durable savings without German plant closures and compulsory redundancies?

### Short answer
**Possibly, but only under tight conditions.**  
Based on the facts given, it is **not possible to conclude from the conversation alone** that closures are economically unavoidable. However, it is equally **not credible** to assume that working-time measures, voluntary exits, and wage moderation alone will fully close a structural cost gap if the company has **material overcapacity** and a **meaningfully higher German cost base than competitors**.

So the technically defensible position is:

- **A non-closure package may work** if the required savings are moderate, if the footprint can be consolidated internally, and if labor flexibility is expanded materially.
- **A no-closure package is unlikely to work** if the company truly needs a large, durable reduction in fixed cost and installed capacity and if the overcapacity is plant-specific rather than only process-specific.

### What can be concluded from the scenario
From the description, management appears to be arguing not just for temporary savings, but for a **structural reset**:
- shrinking European demand,
- excess domestic capacity,
- high German manufacturing cost,
- brand underperformance,
- intense price competition.

That combination usually means the problem is **fixed-cost absorption**, not only hourly labor cost. In that situation, the main levers are:
1. **capacity reduction**,
2. **product allocation changes**,
3. **headcount reduction**,
4. **labor-cost moderation**,
5. **productivity gains / automation**,
6. **network consolidation**.

A package that avoids closures can still deliver some of these, but only if it allows **real capacity removal** elsewhere:
- lines taken out,
- shifts cut,
- plant roles narrowed,
- product volumes moved,
- indirect labor reduced,
- supplier and logistics costs lowered.

If all plants remain open at roughly current structure and employment protection is very strong, then the savings likely come mostly from **temporary or partial** measures, not a durable structural reset.

## Industrial economics view: what is actually expensive?

In large automotive restructuring, the cost issue is usually a mix of:

### 1) Direct labor cost
German automotive labor is expensive in gross terms, though the gap versus other Western European countries is often **smaller than commonly portrayed** once productivity is considered. The bigger problem is frequently:
- premium pay structures,
- legacy guarantees,
- high indirect staffing,
- shift and overtime rigidity,
- limited flexibility in deployment.

### 2) Fixed factory overhead
If utilization falls, unit costs rise quickly because depreciation, maintenance, supervision, energy base load, logistics, and plant overhead are spread over fewer vehicles.

### 3) Network inefficiency
Multiple plants doing overlapping work creates:
- duplicated tooling,
- duplicate logistics streams,
- too much indirect staff,
- subscale series production,
- complexity costs.

### 4) Labor rigidity
Even if gross wages are not the sole issue, the inability to adjust:
- headcount,
- working patterns,
- shift systems,
- site roles,
can make cost reduction much harder.

### 5) Execution risk
A large restructuring can destroy output if it triggers:
- strikes,
- absenteeism,
- quality issues,
- launch delays,
- supplier disruption.

That execution risk is often underestimated by management teams and by financial stakeholders alike.

## Can alternatives to closure produce “equivalent savings”?
### Yes, in principle, if the package is broad enough
A credible non-closure restructuring would need all or most of the following:

- **shorter working time / fewer shifts**
- **voluntary exit programs**
- **redeployment across sites and functions**
- **freezes or reductions in bonuses and allowances**
- **legacy benefit changes**
- **qualification and productivity programs**
- **product reassignment**
- **function pooling and shared services**
- **capex concentration on fewer models/sites**
- **supplier and logistics redesign**

This can reduce costs meaningfully.

### But no, not if the required savings are very large
If management’s gap is primarily due to **excess installed capacity**, then the strongest savings usually come from:
- closing or repurposing a plant,
- removing a full production stream,
- permanently lowering overhead,
- exiting duplicate functions.

A no-closure solution can be **expensive to maintain** because it often preserves too much fixed cost.

## What would make a no-closure solution economically credible?

A non-closure settlement would need to meet five tests:

### 1) It must be structurally durable
Savings must persist beyond a short wage freeze or one-off severance program.

### 2) It must remove real capacity
If output demand is down, the network needs fewer production slots. Simply spreading lower volume across the same footprint is usually insufficient.

### 3) It must materially change the cost base
That means not just wage restraint, but:
- lower indirect headcount,
- less overtime premium,
- leaner shift structures,
- lower absenteeism costs,
- less subscale complexity.

### 4) It must be executable without breaking production
A deal that causes prolonged disruption may destroy more value than it saves.

### 5) It must be enforceable
In German industrial relations, a deal without clear triggers, metrics, and governance can evaporate in practice.

## My assessment of the positions in the room

### Management
Management’s argument is **plausible** if internal analysis shows a large fixed-cost gap and subscale plants. But the burden is on management to show:
- site-by-site economics,
- utilization by plant,
- contribution margins by model,
- indirect cost allocation,
- cost of a non-closure alternative.

Without that, closures are a policy preference, not a proven necessity.

### Works council and union
Their insistence on no closures and no compulsory redundancies is **understandable** as a bargaining position, but economically it may be too rigid if the company truly needs a footprint reduction. A full ban can force the remaining measures to become too costly or too weak.

### Regional state and family holding
They have a legitimate interest in avoiding industrial hollowing-out, but if they block all structural change, they may preserve employment in the short run while worsening long-run competitiveness.

### Investors
They are right to insist on durable savings and margin repair. But they should not assume that closure is always cheaper once execution risk, industrial conflict, and political friction are included.

## Likely outcome if no-closure is enforced absolutely
If the company is forced to preserve all sites and prohibit compulsory redundancies entirely, the likely consequences are:

- **smaller savings than management wants**
- **greater pressure on wages and bonuses**
- **more use of working-time flexibility**
- **more voluntary exits**
- **slower adaptation**
- **continued margin weakness if demand does not recover**
- possible underinvestment if profitability remains too low

So an absolute no-closure rule does not eliminate pain; it often shifts the burden into:
- lower pay growth,
- fewer bonuses,
- weaker investment,
- less flexibility,
- and potentially delayed adjustment later.

---

## Likely outcome if closures are pursued aggressively
If management pushes closures and layoffs, likely effects include:
- higher one-time restructuring charges,
- strike risk,
- output disruption,
- political intervention,
- brand and trust damage,
- but potentially a faster and more durable cost reset.

This can improve competitiveness if executed cleanly, but the execution risk in a co-determined German automotive setting is substantial.

## My practical conclusion
The economically sound path is **not** “closures or nothing.”  
It is a **sequenced, quantified restructuring package**:

1. **Independent site-by-site cost and utilization audit**
2. **Identify subscale operations and duplicate functions**
3. **Set a quantified savings target**
4. **Test the maximum savings from non-closure measures**
5. **If the gap remains material, negotiate targeted consolidation**
6. **Tie any labor concessions to binding investment and product commitments**
7. **Use voluntary exits and redeployment before compulsory measures**

### Bottom line
- **A non-closure deal is feasible only if it can remove enough fixed cost and capacity.**
- **If the structural gap is large, some form of consolidation is likely economically necessary.**
- **But a closure decision should be based on verified plant economics, not rhetoric.**
