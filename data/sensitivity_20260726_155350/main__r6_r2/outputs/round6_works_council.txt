I will be blunt.

## My position does not change

A deep restructuring is necessary, yes.  
But **no German plant closures** and **no compulsory redundancies**. That is the line.

And let me add the third pillar: **no weakening of co-determination or the job-security framework**. If management wants workforce consent, it must respect the workforce as a governing partner, not as a cost item to be optimized away.

## Where I agree

I agree with almost everyone on one point: **a cosmetic deal is worthless**.  
If the company only buys time, we will be back here in a year with the same crisis.

I also accept the expert’s basic message: if there is to be a closure-free solution, it has to be **deep**. It must materially reduce:
- headcount,
- capacity,
- and recurring cost.

Fine. Then let’s do that properly.

But I reject the attempt to turn “deep” into code for closure.

---

## Where I disagree — and why

### 1) Management is still trying to make closures the “reasonable” default
Management says closures remain on the table unless a closure-free package is proven.

That sounds neutral. It is not.

It is a pressure tactic that shifts the burden onto the workforce to “prove” a negative while management withholds the full bridge. You cannot demand that workers accept site destruction as the fallback when you have not even presented a transparent, site-by-site alternative.

If you want credibility, put the numbers on the table:
- exact savings gap,
- plant-by-plant effects,
- direct and indirect headcount,
- capacity by site,
- overhead by site,
- one-time costs,
- and steady-state timing.

Until then, closures are not a conclusion. They are a threat.

---

### 2) The family holding and investors are overreading the benchmark
The expert’s benchmark says a non-closure solution must be large enough to deliver **closure-like economics**.

Correct.

But that does **not** mean the only real answer is closure. It means the outcome must be economically equivalent. That can be achieved through:
- voluntary departures,
- phased retirement,
- hiring restraint,
- redeployment,
- shift reductions,
- product allocation changes,
- internal transfers,
- temporary working-time measures,
- management overhead cuts,
- procurement savings,
- and fair employee contributions.

The leap from “deep” to “close the plant” is yours, not ours.

---

### 3) “Conditional guarantees” are too vague unless they are genuine guarantees
I hear the state shareholder speaking about conditional protection and review clauses. I understand the political desire to keep everyone in the room.

But I need to be absolutely clear: a “guarantee” that can be reopened whenever management is unhappy is not a guarantee. It is a pause button.

We are prepared to discuss conditional implementation of a restructuring package.  
We are **not** prepared to accept conditional site survival in the sense that closures can simply be reintroduced later because the company wants more leverage.

If the company wants labour sacrifice, then it must offer **real site and job security to 2030**. Not a polite promise with a hidden escape hatch.

---

## On the numbers

The expert and the finance specialist both said the same thing: a closure-free package can only work if it is roughly in the range of:
- **15%–20% headcount reduction**
- **10%–25% capacity reduction**
- **2–3 years** to steady state

I am prepared to test that range as a serious restructuring target.

But let me be equally clear: those numbers do **not** justify closures.  
They justify a **serious internal restructuring**.

That means:
- voluntary exits,
- phased retirement,
- internal mobility,
- redeployment across German plants,
- shift and line reductions,
- product and volume reallocation,
- overtime and temporary staffing reductions,
- qualification programs,
- and a negotiated employee contribution only if management and owners also contribute.

That is the path that can preserve industrial capacity without destroying trust.

- no closures,

### To the Union
We are aligned on the essentials.

### To the Expert
Thank you for stating the benchmark clearly. I accept that a credible non-closure package has to be big enough to remove the same recurring burden as closure.  
What I do not accept is the suggestion that closure is therefore inevitable.

## What I will support

I will support a **deep non-closure restructuring** if it includes all of the following:
- every German plant remains open,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- binding site and job guarantees through 2030,
- meaningful management and owner contributions,
- capacity reduction through shifts, product allocation, and utilization changes,
- voluntary departures and retirement paths,
- strong co-determination in shaping implementation,
- independent monitoring of savings and milestones.

That is a serious package. It is also the only package that can keep the workforce onside.

---

## What I will not support

I will not support:
- plant closures as a fallback,
- forced redundancies as a pressure tool,
- “conditional guarantees” that are really just delayed closure options,
- or a settlement that weakens co-determination.

If management wants agreement, it must stop asking workers to underwrite the company’s failure risk while reserving the right to destroy sites anyway.
