Yes — on the facts provided, a **non-closure package can in principle deliver savings comparable to closure**, but only if it is **deep enough to replicate the recurring economics of closure**, not just reduce labor cost at the margin.

## Bottom-line assessment

A closure-free restructuring can be economically credible if it combines, at scale:

- **headcount reduction** of roughly **15%–20%**
- **capacity reduction** of roughly **10%–25%**
- **multi-year labor-cost relief** through wage restraint / concessions / working-time changes
- **lower site overhead** via network simplification, shift rationalization, and indirect labor cuts
- **product allocation changes** that reduce underutilized fixed cost
- **durable implementation** over about **2–3 years**

That is the approximate range where a non-closure package can start to look like a closure scenario in steady-state economics.

---

## Why closure is economically powerful

A plant closure typically delivers savings in four buckets:

1. **direct labor savings**  
   wages, benefits, payroll taxes, shift premiums

2. **indirect labor savings**  
   logistics, maintenance, planning, QA, internal transport, supervision

3. **fixed site overhead savings**  
   utilities, building costs, local services, security, depreciation/lease burden

4. **capacity rationalization**  
   less duplicated capability, higher utilization in remaining plants

If a non-closure package does not substantially reduce **all four**, it will usually fall short of closure-equivalent economics.

---

## What a non-closure package must achieve

To be credible, the package needs to close the gap through **durable recurring savings**, not one-off measures alone.

### 1) Headcount
A realistic closure-free target would usually require:
- **15%–20% fewer employees** in the relevant German footprint, or equivalent productivity uplift
- preferably concentrated in **indirect, overhead, and support functions first**
- with voluntary exits, hiring freezes, redeployment, retirement bridges, and internal transfers doing most of the work

If headcount reduction is materially below that range, the plan will likely need either:
- deeper wage concessions, or
- more severe capacity cuts, or
- a fallback to closure.

### 2) Capacity
A non-closure package usually needs:
- **10%–25% capacity reduction**
- by eliminating underutilized shifts, consolidating product lines, and cutting redundant line capability
- without necessarily closing buildings, but with real loss of productive throughput

If capacity falls only marginally, overhead dilution remains too high.

### 3) Wage / labor-cost contribution
A temporary wage freeze is usually not enough. For closure-like economics, you typically need one or more of:
- wage moderation or reduction
- reduction of variable pay / premiums
- longer working time for same pay, or lower pay for same time
- suspension of legacy guarantees / special protections
- tighter rules on overtime and temporary labor

This does **not** have to be permanent to matter, but it must be long enough — typically **2–3 years minimum** — to change the steady-state cost base.

### 4) Utilization
The package only works if the remaining network runs at materially higher utilization.

That means:
- fewer site-specific exceptions
- more product concentration
- less “keep everything everywhere” logic
- stronger volume allocation to the most efficient plants

If utilization stays weak, fixed cost per vehicle remains too high.

---

## What probably is not enough

A non-closure package is usually **not** closure-equivalent if it consists mainly of:
- voluntary exits only
- modest wage concessions
- short working-time measures
- small procurement savings
- no meaningful product reallocation
- no real reduction in indirect labor
- no measurable capacity loss

That kind of package may buy labor peace, but it is unlikely to close a structural cost gap at a large carmaker.

## Bounded scenario view

Without company-specific plant data, the right way to frame it is by scenario.

### Moderate non-closure scenario
- headcount reduction: **~8%–12%**
- capacity reduction: **~5%–10%**
- labor-cost relief: **temporary / partial**
- result: likely **insufficient** unless the initial gap is small

### Deep non-closure scenario
- headcount reduction: **~15%–20%**
- capacity reduction: **~10%–25%**
- recurring labor-cost relief: **material and multi-year**
- result: **potentially comparable** to closure economics if overhead is also reduced

### Closure scenario
- headcount/capacity reductions are usually deeper and faster
- savings are more immediate and structurally certain
- one-time costs are higher, and social/political cost is much higher

So the real question is not whether non-closure can save money — it can — but whether it can save **enough** and **durably**.

---

## Timing

A closure-free package generally takes longer to mature than closure:

- **0–12 months:** voluntary exits, shift changes, hiring freeze, working-time measures
- **12–24 months:** product reallocations, redeployment, indirect labor reduction
- **24–36 months:** steady-state savings fully visible

So the package must be designed to survive a **2–3 year implementation horizon**. If savings are needed immediately, closure or equivalent hard capacity cuts have an advantage.

---

## Decision rule I would use

A non-closure package is economically credible only if, by steady state, it achieves all three:

1. **cost reduction comparable to closure**
2. **capacity reduction sufficient to restore utilization**
3. **structural, recurring savings not dependent on temporary concessions**

If any one of those is missing, the package is likely postponing the problem.

## Practical conclusion

So, in expert terms:

- **Yes**, a non-closure package can be sufficient.
- **But only** if it is large enough to remove roughly **15%–20% of headcount** and **10%–25% of capacity**, with recurring labor-cost relief and network simplification.
- Anything materially smaller is more likely to be a delay tactic than a durable restructuring.
