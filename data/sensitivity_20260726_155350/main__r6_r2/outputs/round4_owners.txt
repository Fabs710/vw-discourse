I will be direct.

## Where I agree
I agree with the expert, management, and even the employee side on the core economic point: **a cosmetic compromise is worthless**. This company needs a restructuring that materially and durably lowers the cost base, improves utilization, and restores margin. That is not optional.

I also accept the benchmark now on the table: if we avoid closures, the alternative must still deliver **closure-like economics**. That means:
- meaningful headcount reduction,
- real capacity reduction,
- recurring labor-cost relief,
- and a 2- to 3-year implementation path.

That is the right standard.

## Where I disagree

### 1) “Closure-like economics” does not mean closures are the only serious answer
I do **not** accept the idea that because the package must be deep, closures are therefore the default or inevitable solution. That leap is too convenient.

The question is not whether the economics must change. They must.  
The question is whether management and the workforce can build a package that changes them **without destroying the German industrial footprint unnecessarily**.

If a deep non-closure package can truly reach the benchmark, that is preferable.  
If it cannot, then closures remain available. But they are not my first choice, and I will not treat them as a bargaining reflex.

### 2) Compulsory redundancies are not something I will endorse lightly
I am not enthusiastic about forced layoffs. They are blunt instruments and carry real cost:
- reputational damage,
- labour conflict,
- loss of know-how,
- and a weaker long-term social contract.

That said, I will **not** commit the owners to a position that rules them out absolutely before the economics are proven. If voluntary exits, redeployment, retirement paths, and working-time tools are sufficient, good. If they are not, then the company cannot be boxed into an unworkable promise.

So my position is:
- **prefer voluntary/internal tools**
- **do not pre-commit to permanent no-force guarantees**
- **require a credible fallback if the numbers do not add up**

### 3) Unconditional site guarantees through 2030 are not acceptable
I understand why the employee side wants certainty. But unconditional guarantees that freeze the footprint regardless of business performance would be value-destructive.

A guarantee must be earned by a package that actually restores competitiveness. Otherwise it is just a way to lock in structural weakness.

## On the current positions

### To the works council
You are right to reject symbolism. But I do **not** accept your implication that “no closures, no compulsory redundancies, no erosion of guarantees” can stand on its own as a solution.

You keep saying management should design a closure-free alternative. Fine — but design is not the same as wishful thinking. So far, what has been described are instruments, not yet a fully credible bridge.

You say capacity reduction can happen via shifts, product consolidation, internal allocation, downtime, redeployment. Yes.  
But the key issue is whether that package is **large enough** to remove enough fixed cost and overhead to matter. That remains unproven.

### To the union
I appreciate that you are prepared to discuss temporary, quantified contributions. That is constructive.

But temporary concessions are only meaningful if they sit inside a package that permanently improves the cost structure. If the concessions expire while the structural problem remains, we are back here again in two years.

So I need more than solidarity language. I need:
- the actual labor-cost reset,
- the actual headcount reduction,
- the actual capacity reduction,
- and the actual duration and enforcement mechanism.

### To management
You are right that we need numbers. But you also need to stop speaking as if closure is the answer before the non-closure options are fully tested.

At this point, I want the **full savings bridge** in black and white:
1. exact savings gap,
2. management overhead cuts,
3. procurement and supplier savings,
4. workforce contribution,
5. capacity reduction,
6. headcount reduction,
7. one-time restructuring cost,
8. timing to steady state.

Until then, “closures remain on the table” is not a plan. It is pressure.

### To the state shareholder
You are right that this is not just a spreadsheet exercise. The regional footprint matters, as does social stability.

But I also need you to be honest that preserving every structure by veto is not a strategy either. If a deep non-closure package can deliver the economics, I would support it. If it cannot, then the state cannot simply insist on comfort while the company loses competitiveness.

### To investors
I agree that moderate non-closure sounds too shallow. If that is all that is on offer, it is not enough.

But I do **not** accept the notion that the only serious alternative to closure is coercion. A deep non-closure package can work if it is genuinely deep, enforceable, and matched by management and owner action.

## My position, plainly

I will support a restructuring that includes:
- deep and durable cost reduction,
- substantial headcount reduction through voluntary/internal means first,
- real capacity reduction,
- management and procurement savings,
- temporary and measurable employee contributions,
- and investment commitments tied to future products and technology.

I will **not** support:
- a cosmetic compromise,
- a deal that preserves structural overcapacity,
- unconditional site guarantees that freeze flexibility,
- or a package that leaves the company uncompetitive and just postpones the problem.

## What I need next
Before taking any further position, I want the specialist exercise completed on a **deep non-closure scenario**:

That is the real test. Not slogans. Not threats. Not sentiment.

If the deep non-closure route can truly hit the benchmark, I am open to it.  
If it cannot, then we should stop pretending that a shallow compromise is enough.
