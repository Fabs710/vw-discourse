From an industrial restructuring and labour-cost modelling perspective, the answer is:

## Yes — but only a **deep** non-closure package can plausibly match closure economics
A moderate package will usually **not** be enough. To be closure-equivalent, the company would need to remove enough **fixed cost, headcount, and capacity** that the remaining footprint has materially higher utilization and lower overhead absorption.

## Practical benchmark for closure-equivalent non-closure
Based on the ranges already discussed, a credible durable package would typically need:

- **Headcount reduction:** about **15% to 20%**
- **Capacity reduction:** about **10% to 25%**
- **Implementation horizon:** about **2 to 3 years**
- **Labor-cost relief:** recurring, not one-off
- **Site overhead removal:** real reduction, not just reclassification or internal shifting

If those elements are not present, the package is likely **too shallow** to replicate closure economics.

## Scenario comparison

### 1) Closure scenario
This is the cleanest way to eliminate excess fixed cost.

**Typical effects**
- Highest removal of site overhead
- Strongest capacity reset
- Largest headcount reduction tied to the site
- Best chance of durable fixed-cost relief

**Indicative steady-state annual savings**
- Roughly **€150m to €350m per major plant-equivalent site**
- One-time restructuring cost often **€75m to €250m+**

### 2) Moderate non-closure scenario
This usually preserves too much of the cost base.

**Typical effects**
- Headcount reduction: **8% to 12%**
- Capacity reduction: **5% to 10%**
- Some labor savings, but limited overhead relief

**Indicative annual savings**
- Roughly **€70m to €180m**

**Assessment**
- Usually **not enough** if management wants closure-like economics.

### 3) Deep non-closure scenario
This is the only non-closure route that can plausibly work.

**Typical effects**
- Headcount reduction: **15% to 20%**
- Capacity reduction: **10% to 25%**
- Voluntary exits, phased retirement, redeployment, shift reduction, wage moderation, and product consolidation
- Material reduction in support and site overhead

**Indicative annual savings**
- Roughly **€120m to €300m**

**Assessment**
- Potentially closure-equivalent, but only if site overhead and underutilized fixed cost are actually removed.

## What management would need to show
A proper savings bridge should include:

1. **Starting cost base**
   - direct labour
   - indirect labour
   - site overhead
   - corporate allocations
   - fixed manufacturing cost

2. **Volume and capacity baseline**
   - output
   - shifts
   - utilization
   - plant-by-plant or cluster-by-cluster

3. **Savings by lever**
   - voluntary exits
   - retirement
   - hiring freeze
   - working-time reduction
   - wage moderation / bonus restraint
   - overhead cuts
   - procurement savings
   - product/line consolidation

4. **Transition costs**
   - severance
   - retirement bridges
   - retraining
   - transfer costs
   - write-downs

5. **Timing**
   - year 1, year 2, year 3
   - and the steady-state run-rate

## Durability matters more than headline cuts
A package can look large on paper and still fail if:

- wage concessions expire too soon,
- headcount comes out but support costs stay,
- capacity is cut without enough fixed-cost removal,
- or utilization remains too low.

For labour cost savings to be durable, the company needs both:
- **structural headcount/capacity change**, and
- **recurring labour-cost reset** for at least **2 to 3 years**, often longer if the footprint remains partly idle.

## My modelling conclusion
- **Closure**: strongest and most certain fixed-cost reset.
- **Moderate non-closure**: usually insufficient.
- **Deep non-closure**: feasible, but only if it truly reaches closure-like economics.
