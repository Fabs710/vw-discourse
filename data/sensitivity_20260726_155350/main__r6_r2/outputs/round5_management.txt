I will be equally direct.

## Where I agree
I agree with one core point from the employee side and the experts: **a cosmetic deal is useless**.  
If we do not materially reduce the cost base, capacity, and recurring labour burden, then we are simply postponing the crisis.

I also agree that **voluntary exits, phased retirement, redeployment, working-time flexibility, product allocation, and overhead simplification** should all be part of the toolkit.

## Where I disagree — strongly

### 1) “No closures” cannot be an absolute
The works council and union are trying to turn “deep non-closure” into a blanket veto on any plant closure. I cannot accept that.

Let me be clear: I do **not** want closures for their own sake. But I will not commit to a framework that rules them out in advance while leaving us with an uncompetitive cost structure. If the numbers do not work, closures remain a legitimate instrument.

That is not a threat for effect. It is the reality of the economics.

### 2) Compulsory redundancies cannot be ruled out upfront
The employee side keeps saying “voluntary only.” Preferred, yes. Sufficient, not necessarily.

If voluntary exits, redeployment, and working-time tools do not get us to the required scale and speed, then I will not sign a no-forced-redundancy promise that blocks a viable restructuring.

A restructuring that is too weak to change the economics is not a restructuring.

### 3) Conditional guarantees cannot freeze the company through 2030
The demand for binding site and job guarantees through 2030 may sound attractive, but unconditional guarantees are incompatible with the turnaround task.

I can consider conditional protection tied to verified savings and execution milestones. I cannot accept permanent protection that prevents the company from adapting if the package underperforms or the market deteriorates further.

## On the numbers
I accept the benchmark that has emerged: a closure-free package must deliver **closure-like economics**. That means roughly:

- **15%–20% headcount reduction**
- **10%–25% capacity reduction**
- **2–3 years** to steady state
- recurring labour-cost relief
- and actual overhead simplification

That is the minimum scale that can plausibly substitute for closure economics.

But the critical issue is this: **can voluntary and internal measures realistically deliver that scale in time?**  
We do not yet have proof that they can.

## My response to the works council
You say management is using closure threats as pressure, not proof. I disagree.

The burden is on the side proposing a closure-free solution to show that it is credible at scale. So far, I have heard instruments, not a quantified bridge with site-level effects and a credible path to the target.

You also say no plant closures, no compulsory redundancies, no weakening of guarantees. That sounds principled. It is not yet a solution.

If the package really can remove enough headcount, capacity, and fixed cost without closures, then show it. If not, then we are indulging in slogans.

## My response to the union
I respect the insistence that workers cannot be the only ones paying. That is correct.

But a temporary concession is not enough unless it is part of a package that permanently improves the cost structure. If the concessions expire while overcapacity remains, we are back here in two years.

So yes: burden sharing is necessary.  
No: burden sharing alone does not solve the structural problem.

## My response to the regional state government
You are right that this is more than a spreadsheet. But you cannot freeze economic reality by political preference.

I am open to a site-preserving path if it is genuinely deep and executable. But if it is not, then the state cannot expect the company to carry excess structure indefinitely for regional comfort.

## My response to the family holding
You are right that the economics must change. Where I differ is that you seem too comfortable with leaving closures and harsher measures as vague fallback pressure.

I want a credible restructuring path, not a bluff. If the non-closure route works, fine. If it does not, we need the freedom to act.

## My response to investors
You are right to reject a cosmetic compromise. I share that view.

But I do not accept the notion that “deep” automatically means “closure.” A deep non-closure package can work — but only if it truly removes enough fixed cost and capacity. Until proven, closure remains available.
