I will speak plainly.

## My position remains firm

As the regional state shareholder, I will **not** support any outcome that:
- guts employment in the state,
- closes German plants as the default response,
- or weakens our co-determination and veto role.

A durable restructuring is necessary. But a durable restructuring is **not** the same thing as a deep cut at any price.

## Where I agree

I agree with management and the experts on one key point:  
**a cosmetic compromise is worthless**.

I also agree that a closure-free solution must be **economically real**, not symbolic. If we avoid closures, then the package must still deliver:
- meaningful headcount reduction,
- real capacity reduction,
- recurring cost relief,
- and a credible 2- to 3-year implementation path.

That is the right test.

I also agree that voluntary exits, phased retirement, redeployment, working-time tools, product allocation, and overhead simplification all belong in the toolbox.

## Where I disagree

### 1) “Closure-like economics” does not automatically justify closures
Management, investors, and the family holding keep sliding from “the package must be deep” to “therefore closures must remain available.”

I do **not** accept that as the only logic.

Closures are not a normal restructuring instrument for a company like this in Germany. They are a last resort. Why?

Because a plant closure does not just remove cost. It destroys:
- jobs,
- skills,
- supplier networks,
- regional trust,
- and the company’s standing as an industrial anchor.

So no: the benchmark does **not** give you a free pass to reach for closures first.

### 2) Forced redundancies are not my preferred instrument
I am not denying the need for headcount reduction. I am saying the first path must be:
- voluntary exits,
- retirement paths,
- redeployment,
- hiring restraint,
- internal mobility,
- retraining,
- and working-time adjustments.

If those tools are not enough, I expect management to show that with numbers. But I will not endorse compulsory redundancies as the default fallback just because they are faster.

### 3) Unconditional guarantees are not acceptable — but neither is permanent insecurity
To the employee side: I understand the demand for binding protection. It is legitimate.

But to management and capital: if you insist on keeping closures and forced layoffs permanently available from day one, then you are asking workers to sign up for open-ended insecurity while contributing to the turnaround. That will not create trust, and without trust this restructuring will not be stable.

So I am not interested in blank-cheque guarantees, but I am equally not interested in blank-cheque threats.

## What I think a viable path looks like

A viable path is a **deep non-closure package** that is:
- quantified,
- time-limited,
- jointly governed,
- and monitored against milestones.

It should include:
- substantial voluntary headcount reduction,
- shift reductions and capacity cuts,
- product and volume reallocation across German sites,
- management and procurement savings,
- and a fair employee contribution.

But it must also include:
- **no plant closures as the base plan**,
- **no compulsory redundancies as the first or primary instrument**,
- **binding investment commitments in Germany**,
- and **preserved co-determination and board rights**.

That is the kind of settlement I can defend politically and economically.

## To management
You still have not given the full bridge in a way that allows us to test a closure-free solution properly.

So here is my demand:  
Put forward a **site-by-site quantified restructuring bridge** showing:
1. the exact savings gap,
2. management overhead cuts,
3. procurement/supplier savings,
4. product and volume allocation,
5. voluntary workforce reduction,
6. capacity reduction by site,
7. one-time restructuring cost,
8. and the timing to steady state.

Without that, “closures remain on the table” is pressure, not proof.

## To the works council
You are right to reject cosmetic fixes and to insist that workers cannot be the only ones paying.

But I need the employee side to be equally honest: if the benchmark is 15%–20% headcount reduction and 10%–25% capacity reduction, then the package must actually be big enough.  
That means voluntary measures may need to be **much larger** than in a normal bargaining round.

I support a closure-free route — but only if it is truly deep enough to work.

## To the union
I agree that employee contributions must be matched by management and owner contributions. That is non-negotiable.

But I also need you to accept that a temporary wage or bonus contribution is not enough unless it is tied to durable structural changes. We cannot come back in two years and discover that the problem was merely postponed.

## To the family holding
You are right that the economics must change. I agree.

Where I push back is on the way closures and harsher measures are treated as the natural fallback. That may be convenient in negotiations, but it is not the best path for a company that depends on legitimacy, workforce commitment, and regional strength.

If a deep non-closure package works, it is preferable. If it does not, then we will need to reassess. But I will not treat closure as the starting point.

## To institutional investors
You are right to reject a shallow compromise. I agree.

But I do not accept that “deep” automatically means “closure.” If the package can really remove enough headcount, capacity, and fixed cost through internal and voluntary means, then that is the smarter long-term solution.

## My bottom line

I can support a serious restructuring if it:
- protects German sites as the default,
- preserves co-determination,
- relies first on voluntary and internal measures,
- includes meaningful management and owner contributions,
- and is backed by binding investment commitments.

I will **not** support:
- plant closures as the default answer,
- compulsory redundancies as the first resort,
- or any outcome that weakens the state’s co-determination and veto role.
