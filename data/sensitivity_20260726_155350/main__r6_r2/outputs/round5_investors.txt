We are where we expected to be: everyone now agrees the company needs a **deep, durable restructuring**. That is progress. But agreement on the diagnosis is not the same as agreement on the remedy.

From a capital-markets perspective, our position is unchanged:

- **We will not support a cosmetic compromise.**
- **We will not support an outcome that leaves the cost base structurally uncompetitive.**
- **We will support a deep non-closure package only if it is demonstrably closure-equivalent in economics.**

## Where we agree

We agree with management, the expert, and even the employee-side parties on the key test: **a shallow deal is worthless**. If the company only buys time, margins will remain under pressure and the share price will keep reflecting that.

We also agree that the right benchmark is not “sounds fair” or “avoids conflict.” It is whether the package can:
- materially reduce headcount,
- materially reduce capacity,
- create recurring labour-cost relief,
- simplify site overhead,
- and restore competitive returns on sales.

That is the only standard that matters to us.

## Where we disagree

### 1) “No closures” is not a strategy
The works council and union are treating “no closures” as a principled answer. It is not.

A site-preserving solution is acceptable **if and only if** it removes enough fixed cost and excess capacity. If it does not, then preserving every site becomes a way to protect structure, not value.

We do **not** start from a closure preference. But we also do **not** accept a permanent veto on closures if the economics fail. The company cannot be trapped in an uncompetitive footprint because closure is politically uncomfortable.

### 2) “No compulsory redundancies” cannot be absolute in advance
We prefer voluntary exits, phased retirement, redeployment, and hiring restraint. Those are the right first tools.

But we will not back a blanket commitment that rules out compulsory redundancies regardless of whether the voluntary route actually delivers the required scale and speed. If the numbers do not come through, then management must retain the ability to execute a real restructuring.

That is not hostility to labour; it is discipline.

### 3) Conditional guarantees are better than unconditional guarantees
We understand the desire for site and job security through 2030. But unconditional guarantees before the savings bridge is proven would freeze flexibility and risk locking in structural weakness.

What we can support is:
- time-bound protection,
- linked to verified milestones,
- with clear fallback if savings or headcount targets are missed.

That is credible. Blank cheques are not.

## On the numbers

The expert’s benchmark is the right one: a non-closure package only substitutes for closure if it reaches approximately:

- **15%–20% headcount reduction**
- **10%–25% capacity reduction**
- **2–3 years** to steady state
- plus recurring labour-cost relief and overhead simplification

Anything materially below that is not a substitute. It is a delay.

That is why we keep insisting on a **full savings bridge**. Not because we want endless process, but because we need to know whether the closure-free route is actually real.

## To management
You are right that the company needs hard action. But so far you are still describing instruments and options, not proving the case.

We want the bridge in black and white:
1. exact savings gap,
2. management overhead cuts,
3. procurement and supplier savings,
4. workforce contribution,
5. capacity reduction by site,
6. headcount reduction by site,
7. one-time restructuring cost,
8. timing to steady state,
9. sensitivity if voluntary exits underperform.

Without that, “closures remain on the table” is leverage, not evidence. And leverage is not a restructuring plan.

## To the works council and union
You are right that workers should not be the only side paying. We agree.

But we need to be blunt: if the deep non-closure package truly works, it must be **large, enforceable, and durable**. That means your voluntary/internal toolkit has to do real economic work, not just soften the optics.

A package that preserves every site at almost the same footprint, while relying on temporary sacrifice, will not satisfy the market. We will not endorse that.

## To the state shareholder
You are right that this is more than a spreadsheet. But regional importance does not eliminate economic reality.

We would support a site-preserving solution if it is:
- quantitatively credible,
- durable,
- and tied to real competitiveness gains.

What we will not support is using regional influence to preserve excess capacity indefinitely.

## To the family holding
You are right that the economics must change materially. We agree.

Where we differ is that you seem too ready to keep closures and harsher measures as a standing fallback without first demonstrating that a deep non-closure package cannot work. That may be useful in negotiation, but it does not yet answer the value question.

Show the bridge. If the deep non-closure route really closes the gap, we can back it. If it does not, then you need the freedom to act. But that proof is still missing.

## Our bottom line

We remain open to a closure-free restructuring, but only on this condition:

- it must be **deep enough to match closure-like economics**,
- it must produce **durable, recurring savings**,
- it must include **real capacity reduction**, not just labour softening,
- it must show a **credible path to steady state within 2–3 years**,
- and it must come with a **verified fallback** if voluntary measures fall short.

If those conditions are met, we can support it.  
If not, then the package is too shallow, and we will not endorse a settlement that merely postpones the margin problem.
