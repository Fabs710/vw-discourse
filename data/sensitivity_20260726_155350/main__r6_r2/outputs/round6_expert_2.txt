## 1) Scenario comparison

### A. Closure scenario
**What it means**
- Closure of one or more German plants
- Capacity exits physically removed
- Headcount reduction concentrated and permanent
- Site overhead largely eliminated at the closed site

**Expected economics**
- **Headcount effect:** typically **20%+** in the relevant footprint, depending on scope
- **Capacity effect:** often **15%–30%+**
- **Recurring savings:** strongest and most certain
- **One-time costs:** highest

**Savings bridge**
1. Direct labor savings  
2. Indirect labor savings  
3. Fixed site overhead savings  
4. Capacity/utilization gain at remaining plants  
5. Some procurement/network simplification benefit

**Assessment**
- Best route if the company needs maximum certainty and fastest structural removal of excess cost.
- But it creates the largest transition burden and the greatest irreversibility.

---

### B. Moderate non-closure scenario
**What it means**
- No plant closure
- Voluntary exits, hiring freeze, redeployment, shift reductions
- Some wage restraint / labor contribution
- Limited capacity reduction through line and shift rationalization

**Indicative effect**
- **Headcount reduction:** about **8%–12%**
- **Capacity reduction:** about **5%–10%**
- **Recurring savings:** meaningful, but often not enough
- **One-time costs:** moderate

**Savings bridge**
1. Direct labor savings: moderate  
2. Indirect labor savings: limited  
3. Overhead savings: partial only  
4. Capacity/utilization effect: modest  
5. Labor contribution: temporary bridge, not full structural fix

**Assessment**
- Usually **below closure-equivalent economics** unless the current gap is small.
- Good for labor peace, but often not enough to eliminate structural overcapacity.

---

### C. Deep non-closure scenario
**What it means**
- No plant closure
- Large voluntary exit program
- Redeployment across the network
- Shift and line elimination
- Product allocation changes
- Significant management/owner contribution
- Multi-year labor-cost relief

**Indicative effect**
- **Headcount reduction:** about **15%–20%**
- **Capacity reduction:** about **10%–25%**
- **Implementation horizon:** **2–3 years**
- **Recurring savings:** potentially close to closure-like economics if overhead also falls materially

**Savings bridge**
1. Direct labor savings: strong  
2. Indirect labor savings: strong if support functions are centralized/reduced  
3. Fixed overhead savings: moderate to strong if sites are simplified and underused lines are removed  
4. Capacity/utilization effect: important, especially if production is concentrated into fewer shifts / fewer models  
5. Labor contribution: meaningful but time-limited, should not be the only source

**Assessment**
- This is the **only closure-free case that can plausibly substitute for closure economics**.
- It still requires disciplined execution and measurable milestones.

## 2) Closure-equivalent economics: what must be true

A non-closure package becomes closure-equivalent only if it reduces all four cost buckets enough:

### 1. Direct labor
Needs a real reduction in:
- headcount,
- wage cost,
- premiums,
- overtime,
- temporary labor.

### 2. Indirect labor
Needs cuts in:
- logistics,
- maintenance,
- QA,
- planning,
- internal transport,
- supervisory layers.

### 3. Fixed site overhead
Needs simplification of:
- site services,
- utilities,
- property / lease burden where applicable,
- security,
- local overhead functions.

### 4. Capacity dilution
Needs real removal of:
- shifts,
- product duplication,
- underutilized line segments,
- excess local capability.

If a package reduces wages but leaves the network structure intact, it will usually **not** match closure economics.
