## Executive view
A **deep non-closure package can substitute for a closure scenario only if it removes the same recurring cost base**:
- enough **headcount**
- enough **capacity**
- enough **site-level overhead**
- enough **fixed-cost drag**
- within a **2–3 year** implementation window

If it does not, it is not closure-equivalent; it is only delay.

## Decision-useful scenario frame

Because no plant-by-plant baseline has been provided, the estimates below are **bounded industrial-order-of-magnitude ranges**, not a precise valuation.

### Assumed benchmark logic
Closure economics usually eliminate:
- direct labour
- indirect labour
- site overhead
- maintenance/support burden
- underutilized capacity costs
- some future capex / support spending

A non-closure package must recover most of that through:
- voluntary exits
- shift/line reduction
- product reallocation
- working-time moderation
- management overhead cuts
- procurement / indirect savings
- lower fixed-site running cost

# 1) Closure scenario
### Description
Close one or more German plants, consolidate production, and remove the associated site overhead and capacity.

### Likely economics
**Savings at steady state:** highest of the three scenarios  
- recurring annual savings: **100% of the closure benchmark**
- one-time restructuring cost: **high**
  - severance / redundancy / transition / asset write-down / logistics / ramp-up at receiving sites

### Headcount effect
- direct and indirect headcount reduction concentrated at the closed site(s)
- full site labor exit plus some adjacent support rationalization

### Capacity effect
- **maximum capacity removal**
- most credible route to a large and immediate footprint reduction

### Timing
- decision-to-steady-state: typically **18–36 months**
- can be quicker for accounting savings, slower for operational migration

### Financial interpretation
Best for removing fixed cost and structural overcapacity quickly, but with the highest transition friction and likely the highest one-time cost.

---

# 2) Moderate non-closure scenario
### Description
No plant closure. Use voluntary exits, hiring freeze, shift removal, limited product consolidation, and temporary labour-cost moderation.

### Likely economics
**Savings at steady state:** partial  
- roughly **40%–65% of closure-equivalent savings**
- enough to improve margins, but often **not enough** to fully neutralize excess footprint

### Headcount effect
- likely **5%–10%** reduction in total German network headcount
- mostly indirect / support and voluntary exits
- limited forced structural contraction

### Capacity effect
- likely **5%–15%** reduction
- some shift cuts, lower operating days, selective line simplification

### One-time cost
- moderate
- lower than closure, but still meaningful due to:
  - severance for voluntary exits
  - redeployment
  - retraining
  - temporary inefficiency

### Timing
- **12–24 months** to stabilize
- savings ramp slower than closures

### Financial interpretation
This is usually a **bridge package**, not a final answer, unless market volumes recover or the starting cost gap is small.

---

# 3) Deep non-closure scenario
### Description
No plant closure, but a genuinely large restructuring:
- voluntary exits and phased retirement at scale
- internal redeployment
- shift elimination
- product allocation changes
- large management/overhead cuts
- working-time and wage/bonus moderation
- material capacity rationalization without formal closure

### Likely economics
**Savings at steady state:** potentially closure-equivalent, but only if executed aggressively  
- roughly **75%–100% of closure-equivalent recurring savings**
- the lower end is more common; the upper end requires strong discipline and enough fixed-cost removal

### Headcount effect
- likely **15%–20%** reduction in the affected German network
- can be achieved without compulsory redundancies only if:
  - voluntary exit uptake is high
  - hiring restraint is strict
  - redeployment outflow is real
  - retirement / age-profile supports it

### Capacity effect
- likely **10%–25%**
- achieved through:
  - shift reductions
  - product concentration
  - lower throughput at selected sites
  - partial utilization of some plants

### One-time cost
- medium to high
- lower than closure on redundancy optics, but not necessarily lower financially if:
  - incentives for voluntary exits are generous
  - multiple sites need reconfiguration
  - productivity losses occur during transition

### Timing
- **24–36 months** to steady state
- very dependent on labor agreement design and execution discipline

### Financial interpretation
This is the only non-closure path that can plausibly match closure economics.  
But it requires **real fixed-cost removal**, not just headcount softness.

## Closure-equivalent savings bridge: what must be removed

A deep non-closure package must close the gap across four buckets.

### A. Direct labour
- voluntary exits
- retirement pathways
- hiring freeze
- shift reductions

### B. Indirect labour / site overhead
- plant supervision
- maintenance support
- internal logistics
- quality/admin layers
- central support duplication

### C. Capacity and utilization
- fewer shifts
- fewer operating days
- product reallocation to higher-utilization sites
- line consolidation

### D. Recurring labour-cost relief
- bonus restraint
- wage moderation
- working-time changes
- removal/suspension of legacy guarantees

If the package only affects A and D, but not B and C, it usually fails the closure-equivalence test.

---

## My bounded estimate of the structural threshold

For a large German multi-plant carmaker with excess domestic capacity, a **credible closure-free package** generally needs about:

- **15%–20% network headcount reduction**
- **10%–25% capacity reduction**
- **2–3 years** to steady state
- and enough overhead simplification to materially reduce fixed site cost

If the package is only:
- 5%–10% headcount reduction, or
- mostly temporary wage restraint, or
- mostly voluntary exits without capacity cuts,

then it is **not** closure-equivalent.

## Practical conclusion
### If the goal is simply “some savings”
A moderate non-closure package may be enough.

### If the goal is “closure-equivalent economics”
Then the package must be **deep**, and the likely minimum order of magnitude is:
- **15%–20% headcount reduction**
- **10%–25% capacity reduction**
- **substantial overhead simplification**
- **2–3 years** to full effect

### If the package cannot remove fixed site cost
Then it will probably underperform closure economics even if the payroll line falls.
