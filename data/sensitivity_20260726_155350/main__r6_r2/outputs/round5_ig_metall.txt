## Where I agree
I agree with management, the expert, and several other stakeholders on one thing: **a cosmetic deal is worthless**.  
If this company’s restructuring does not materially reduce the cost base, the capacity overhang, and the recurring labour-cost burden, then it will just delay the next crisis.

I also agree that a closure-free solution cannot be shallow. If the benchmark is “closure-like economics,” then yes — the package has to be deep, measurable, and durable.

## Where I disagree
### 1) “Closure-like economics” does **not** justify closures as the default
Management, the family holding, and investors keep turning the benchmark into a bargaining weapon: because the package must be deep, therefore closures must stay on the table.

I reject that logic.

The benchmark tells us the **economic effect** that must be achieved. It does **not** mean the only answer is to close German plants or to use forced redundancies. That is a choice, not a necessity. And it is the wrong choice if alternatives can produce the same economics with less damage to jobs, skills, and industrial trust.

### 2) Compulsory redundancies remain unacceptable
I want to be absolutely clear: **I do not accept forced layoffs as a fallback tool**.

If the company needs headcount reduction, then it must come through:
- voluntary exits,
- phased retirement,
- redeployment,
- hiring restraint,
- internal transfer,
- qualification,
- working-time flexibility,
- and, yes, a negotiated employee contribution.

If management says that is too slow, then my answer is simple: **then management has not built a serious social restructuring plan**.

### 3) Unconditional insecurity is not a “guarantee”
To management and capital: asking the workforce to contribute while keeping closures and layoffs in reserve is not partnership. It is one-sided risk transfer.

To the state shareholder: I appreciate the desire for a conditional approach, but “conditional” must not mean a hidden backdoor for later coercion. Conditions must be **objective, transparent, and jointly governed**. Not a blank cheque for reopening the same threat later.

## On the numbers
I accept the expert’s working range as a **test**, not as a mandate for cuts:
- **15%–20% headcount reduction**
- **10%–25% capacity reduction**
- **2–3 years** to steady state

But I stress again: those reductions can be achieved through:
- shift reductions,
- product consolidation,
- volume reallocation,
- voluntary departures,
- phased retirement,
- redeployment,
- and working-time tools.

That is the route to real restructuring **without closures and without compulsory redundancies**.

You have leverage. Use it to force a real non-closure test, not to keep closure pressure alive indefinitely.

A regional anchor shareholder should insist on:
- no compulsory redundancies,
- no plant closures as a default instrument,

But I agree with you on one point: the package must be large enough. I am not defending a shallow compromise.

## To the controlling family holding

If you want a workforce that carries the restructuring, then support a package that makes participation rational:
- site security,
- fair burden sharing,

If you insist on keeping coercion in reserve from day one, then you are choosing conflict and long-term distrust.

- **No compulsory redundancies.**
- **No weakening of co-determination.**
- **No one-sided sacrifice by workers.**

I am prepared to discuss a serious, quantified restructuring **only if** it protects jobs and sites and treats employees fairly.
