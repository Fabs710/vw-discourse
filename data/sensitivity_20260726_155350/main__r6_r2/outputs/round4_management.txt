I will respond plainly.

## Where I agree

I agree with the expert, management-side observers, and even parts of the employee-side argument on one central point: **a cosmetic deal is worthless**.

If this restructuring does not materially reduce:
- fixed costs,
- excess capacity,
- and the recurring labour-cost base,

then it does not solve the problem. It only postpones it. And we do not have the luxury of postponing it.

I also agree that a closure-free solution is not automatically impossible. But the bar is high: it must deliver **closure-like economics**, not just sound humane in a press statement.

## Where I disagree

### 1) “No closures” cannot be an absolute
The works council and union keep presenting “no closures” as if it were a sufficient plan. It is not.

I respect the desire to preserve sites. But I will not accept a framework that makes German plant closures impossible in advance while leaving us with a cost base that is still structurally uncompetitive.

Let me be direct: if the non-closure package does not remove enough fixed cost and capacity, then closures remain a legitimate instrument. That is not blackmail. That is business reality.

### 2) Compulsory redundancies cannot be ruled out upfront
I hear the employee side saying “voluntary only.” That is preferred, but it is not a guarantee I can give.

If voluntary exits, phased retirement, redeployment, and working-time measures do not get us to the required scale, then we cannot pretend that the business can be rescued by goodwill alone. I will not commit the company to a no-forced-redundancy promise that blocks a viable restructuring.

### 3) Binding guarantees cannot freeze flexibility through 2030
The demand for unconditional site and job guarantees through 2030 is not compatible with the restructuring task.

A guarantee only has value if the package behind it is real. Otherwise it becomes a way to preserve the current structure regardless of economic performance. That is not a turnaround plan; that is inertia.

## On the numbers

The expert’s benchmark is useful, and I accept it as the correct decision threshold:

- **15% to 20% headcount reduction**
- **10% to 25% capacity reduction**
- **2 to 3 years** to implement
- plus recurring labour-cost relief

That is the minimum scale that can plausibly substitute for closure economics.

So the key question is not whether the employee side prefers voluntary tools. Of course it does. The question is whether those tools can realistically deliver the required scale **in time**.

If the answer is yes, then show the bridge.
If the answer is no, then we need to stop pretending that “no closures” is a serious solution.

## My position on the current employee proposals

### What is constructive
I acknowledge that the works council and union are prepared to discuss:
- voluntary exits,
- phased retirement,
- redeployment,
- working-time flexibility,
- shift reductions,
- bonus restraint,
- and temporary wage moderation.

That is constructive.

### What is not sufficient
But a list of instruments is not a savings bridge.

What I have not yet seen is a credible plan showing how these tools, alone, will remove:
- enough headcount,
- enough capacity,
- enough overhead,
- and enough recurring cost,

to restore the brand’s return on sales.

That is the gap. And until it is closed, closures and deeper measures remain on the table.

## To the works council
You say management has not proven closures are necessary. That is not the right standard.

The right standard is whether the alternative is **credible at scale**.

If you can show:
- material headcount reduction,
- real capacity reduction,
- lower support overhead,
- and durable cost relief,

then I will engage it seriously.

But if the package only works by assuming that everything stays open at roughly the same footprint while labour makes temporary sacrifices, then it is not enough. It does not meet the benchmark.

## To the union
You are right that workers cannot be the only ones contributing. I agree.

But contribution must be measured by outcome, not rhetoric. Temporary moderation of wage elements is not enough unless it is part of a package that permanently lowers fixed cost and simplifies the network.

If you want to avoid closures, then the workforce package must be deep enough to change the economics. If it is not, then it is not a solution.

## To the state shareholder
You are right to insist that this is a regional-industrial issue, not just a spreadsheet exercise. But the state cannot veto economic reality.

I will work with you on a non-closure path if — and only if — it is sufficiently deep and executable. What I will not accept is using the state’s influence to preserve structures that the market will not support.

## To the family holding
You are right that a real restructuring must materially change the economics. I agree.

Where I disagree is the implication that this can be done without hard choices on footprint and labour cost. It cannot.

If we are serious, we must be willing to shrink the cost base materially, not just talk about productivity in the abstract.

## To investors
You are right to reject a cosmetic compromise. That is exactly my position too.

But let me be equally clear: I do not share the view that closure is the default answer. It is the fallback if the deep non-closure package fails the economic test.

## My bottom line

I remain open to a closure-free settlement **only if** it delivers closure-like economics in substance, meaning:
- materially lower headcount,
- materially lower capacity,
- real and recurring labour-cost relief,
- overhead simplification,
- and a credible 2- to 3-year implementation path.

If that cannot be achieved through voluntary and internal means, then we will have to consider harsher measures. I will not sign up to a compromise that leaves the company structurally uncompetitive.
