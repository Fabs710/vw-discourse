I will be equally direct.

## Where I agree
I agree with the works council, the state shareholder, and the expert on one central point: **a cosmetic deal is worthless**.  
If this restructuring does not materially reduce the cost base and excess capacity, then it is not a solution — it is postponement.

I also agree that a closure-free package, if it is to work at all, must be **deep**, **measurable**, and **durable**. The benchmark of roughly:
- **15%–20% headcount reduction**
- **10%–25% capacity reduction**
- **2–3 years** to steady state

is a sensible test. I accept that as the level of seriousness required.

And I agree with the state shareholder that **prolonged industrial conflict is value-destructive**. We should not drift into a strike spiral if there is a credible path to a fair settlement.

## Where I disagree
### 1) “Deep” does not mean “closures are the obvious answer”
Management, the family holding, and investors keep turning the benchmark into a shortcut: because the package must be deep, closures must remain the default fallback.

I do **not** accept that logic.

The benchmark tells us what the **economic effect** must be. It does not prove that plant closures are necessary. A serious non-closure package can still achieve closure-like economics if it combines:
- voluntary exits,
- phased retirement,
- hiring restraint,
- internal redeployment,
- shift reductions,
- product and volume reallocation,
- working-time measures,
- management overhead cuts,
- procurement savings,
- and a fair employee contribution.

So no: “the numbers are tough” is not a justification for turning to closures first. It is a reason to design a real restructuring.

### 2) Compulsory redundancies remain unacceptable as a normal instrument
I need to be clear here: I am **not** accepting forced layoffs as part of the core package.

If the company needs headcount reduction, it must come through:
- voluntary departures,
- retirement bridges,
- redeployment,
- qualification and internal transfer,
- hiring freeze,
- and, where necessary, negotiated working-time adjustments.

If management says that is too slow, then my response is simple: then management has not yet built a credible social restructuring plan.

### 3) Workers must not be the only ones paying
The workforce can contribute, but only in a package where **management, owners, and capital also contribute materially**.

That means:
- management overhead reduction,
- executive restraint,
- procurement and supplier savings,
- owner-backed commitments to investment,
- and binding job and site protections.

If employees are expected to give up pay, time, and security while others keep closures and forced redundancies in reserve, that is not burden-sharing. That is unilateral risk transfer.

### 4) “Conditional guarantees” cannot be a hidden backdoor
I understand the state shareholder’s attempt to balance trust and flexibility. But a “guarantee” that can be reopened whenever management wants more leverage is not a guarantee.

If the workforce contributes, then the settlement must include:
- genuine site protection,
- genuine job security,
- and a clear horizon through 2030.

Conditions can govern implementation milestones. They cannot be a disguised license to reopen the same closure threat at any time.

## On the numbers
The expert’s ranges are useful because they force seriousness. But I want to be equally clear: those figures are a **test of depth**, not a mandate for closures.

A deep non-closure package can meet that test if it removes enough:
- direct labour,
- indirect labour,
- site overhead,
- capacity,
- and recurring labour cost.

What it cannot be is a shallow deal dressed up as reform.

## What I want from management
Management still has not done the one thing that would make this discussion concrete: present a **site-by-site quantified savings bridge**.

I want, in black and white:
1. the exact savings gap,
2. management overhead cuts,
3. procurement/supplier savings,
4. product and volume allocation by site,
5. voluntary workforce measures,
6. capacity reduction by site,
7. one-time restructuring cost,
8. and the timing to steady state.

Without that, closures are not a conclusion. They are pressure.

## To the works council
We are aligned on the core lines:
- no German plant closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- no weakening of co-determination,
- no one-sided sacrifice.

I support that. But I also want to sharpen one point: if the deep non-closure route is to work, the employee side must be prepared for **real structural changes** — not because workers caused this crisis, but because the company needs actual cost and capacity relief.

That means we should be ready to negotiate:
- shift changes,
- product allocation,
- internal transfers,
- qualification,
- and a meaningful, temporary employee contribution,
provided it is matched by management and owners and tied to binding site/job security.

## To the state shareholder
You are right to demand an economically serious solution. But I need you to be equally firm that the solution must not be built on closure threats or forced redundancy pressure.

If you want regional stability, then the state should insist on:
- no closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- serious investment commitments,
- and a jointly monitored restructuring path with clear milestones.

## To the family holding and investors
You are right that a shallow compromise is worthless. I do not dispute that.

Where I disagree is on the implied conclusion that the only serious way to get a deep result is to keep closures and forced layoffs in reserve from day one. That may be convenient as a bargaining tactic, but it is not yet proof.

Bring the bridge. If the deep non-closure path truly fails on the numbers, show it. But until then, the burden is on management and capital to prove that closure-free restructuring cannot work — not on the workforce to accept closures pre-emptively.

## My bottom line
I remain open to a serious, durable restructuring. But my conditions remain clear:

- **No plant closures.**
- **No compulsory redundancies.**
- **No weakening of co-determination.**
- **No one-sided sacrifice by workers.**
- **Any employee contribution must be matched by management and owner contributions.**
- **Any agreement must be quantified, monitored, and durable.**

If management wants a real settlement, it must stop talking in generalities and put the numbers on the table. Then we can assess whether a deep non-closure package can truly deliver closure-like economics.

Until that is done, I do not accept closures or forced layoffs as the answer.
