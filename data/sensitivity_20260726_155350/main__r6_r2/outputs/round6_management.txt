I’ll be equally direct.

## Where I agree
I agree with all sides on one essential point: **a cosmetic deal is worthless**. If we do not materially reduce the cost base and excess capacity, then we are just buying time and losing competitiveness.

I also agree that a **closure-free package is only credible if it is deep enough** to produce closure-like economics. The expert’s range is the right seriousness test:
- **15%–20% headcount reduction**
- **10%–25% capacity reduction**
- **2–3 years** to steady state

That is the minimum order of magnitude we are talking about if this company is to regain a competitive cost position.

## Where I disagree — strongly

### 1) “No closures” cannot be a blanket principle
The works council and union keep saying “no German plant closures” as an absolute. I cannot accept that.

Not because I want closures. I do **not** want closures as an end in themselves.  
I accept them only as a legitimate fallback if the deep non-closure route cannot close the gap.

But I will not tie management’s hands in advance while the company remains structurally uncompetitive. If voluntary exits, redeployment, shift changes, product reallocation, and overhead simplification are not enough, then closures must remain available.

That is not aggression. That is responsible management.

### 2) “No compulsory redundancies” cannot be pre-committed as an absolute either
Again: preferred? Yes. Sufficient by definition? No.

If the package truly delivers the required savings through voluntary measures, then fine. But I will not sign away the ability to use compulsory measures if the actual uptake falls short.

A restructuring that is too weak to change the economics is not a restructuring. It is a delay.

### 3) Conditional guarantees are necessary — unconditional guarantees are not realistic
The state shareholder and employee side are asking for site and job guarantees through 2030. I understand why. But unconditional guarantees would freeze flexibility and make the turnaround impossible.

What I can accept is **conditional protection tied to verified milestones**:
- savings delivered,
- capacity actually reduced,
- headcount actually reduced,
- fixed-site costs actually simplified.

That is a real protection framework. A blanket guarantee is not.

## On the numbers
The reason I keep insisting on a full bridge is simple: **we still do not have proof that a closure-free package can actually deliver the required economics**.

So here is the position clearly:
- I am open to a deep non-closure package.
- I am **not** willing to rule out closures or compulsory redundancies until that package is shown to be sufficient at scale.
- I will not accept a settlement that preserves structural overcapacity under the label of “site protection.”

then show it in black and white. If not, then you are asking the company to accept a weaker solution and call it victory.

## To the union

So yes, burden sharing is required.  
No, burden sharing alone is not enough.

I respect the desire to preserve regional stability. But regional importance does not cancel economics.

I can support a site-preserving route if it is credible and durable. But I cannot support a political veto on any instrument that might be needed to restore competitiveness.

The state should be pushing for a real restructuring, not for permanent insulation from hard decisions.

## To the controlling family holding
You are right about one thing: this company needs durable competitiveness. I agree.

Where I differ is that I do **not** think closures should be treated as a first resort. They are a fallback if the deep non-closure route fails. But they must remain available.

If you want long-run value creation, then you should want the best economically credible solution — not a symbolic one.

But I do not accept the idea that “deep” automatically means “close a plant.” A deep non-closure package can work — if it really removes the fixed cost and capacity burden. Until that is proven, however, I will not give up the fallback options.
