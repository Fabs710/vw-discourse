I will engage directly.

## Where I agree

I agree with the works council on the central point: **a cosmetic deal is useless**. If this company wants a durable restructuring, it must be economically meaningful, not a headline exercise.

I also agree that a **closure-free package can only be credible if it is deep enough** to produce real, recurring savings. The expert’s benchmark is the right one: if closures are avoided, then the package must still achieve something like:
- **15% to 20% headcount reduction**
- **10% to 25% capacity reduction**
- **2 to 3 years** implementation
- and durable labor-cost relief

That is not a trivial package. But it is also not an automatic mandate for closures.

## Where I disagree

### 1) Closure-equivalent economics do **not** mean closures are acceptable
Management, the family holding, and investors keep using the benchmark as a bridge to closure. I do **not** accept that leap.

The fact that a non-closure solution must be deep does **not** mean the company should start with closures or treat them as the default bargaining instrument. It means management must design a serious alternative that achieves the necessary economics **without** destroying the industrial base.

That is management’s responsibility, not a workforce obligation.

### 2) Compulsory redundancies remain unacceptable
I need to be explicit here: I do **not** accept forced layoffs as a fallback just because voluntary tools require more effort.

If the company needs headcount reduction, then it must come through:
- voluntary exits,
- phased retirement,
- redeployment,
- hiring restraint,
- internal transfer,
- qualification,
- working-time changes,
- and negotiated flexibility.

If management says those tools are too slow, then the answer is not to impose redundancies. The answer is that management has not built a sufficiently serious social restructuring plan.

### 3) “Flexibility” cannot mean permanent insecurity
The works council is right to insist that concessions must be tied to protection. I support that.

But I also want to sharpen the point: **employee contributions are only sellable if they are matched by binding commitments from management and owners**. If the company asks workers to give up pay, time, and guarantees while keeping closure threats and forced layoffs alive, then that is not a restructuring partnership. It is one-sided burden shifting.

## To management

You keep asking for the bridge. Fair. Then put the **full savings bridge** on the table, not just a list of instruments and a threat scenario.

I want, in black and white:
1. the exact savings gap,
2. management overhead cuts,
3. procurement and supplier savings,
4. productivity and complexity reductions,
5. volume allocation by plant,
6. the voluntary workforce measures,
7. the capacity reductions by site,
8. and the investment commitments attached to the package.

What I have **not** heard from you is a convincing case that closures or forced layoffs are the first or only path. You are asserting that. You have not proven it.

## To the works council

We are aligned on the essentials:
- no compulsory redundancies,
- no wage dumping,
- no one-sided sacrifice,
- and no cosmetic compromise.

Where I want to be careful is the implementation logic. If the expert’s benchmark is right, then we need to be honest that the package has to be **large**. But large does not have to mean cruel. It can mean:
- shift reductions,
- product consolidation,
- internal volume shifts,
- voluntary departures,
- phased retirement,
- and temporary contributions from employees, management, and owners alike.

That is the route I can defend to the membership.

## To the state shareholder

You are right that a regional anchor company must not be reduced to a spreadsheet. And yes, you should use your leverage.

But I need more than sympathy. I need you to back a line that says:
- **no forced redundancies**
- **no plant closures as the first instrument**
- **binding conditions on any employee contribution**
- **measurable management and owner contributions**

If you mean that, then you are part of the solution. If not, then the state is simply observing while workers are asked to absorb the shock.

## To the family holding

I understand your insistence on restoring competitiveness. That is legitimate. But you cannot ask the workforce to finance competitiveness alone while the owners preserve maximum flexibility and keep closure pressure in reserve.

If you want a workforce that carries the restructuring, you need to support a package that makes participation rational, not fearful:
- site security,
- no forced layoffs,
- fair burden sharing,
- and real investment commitments.

Without reciprocity, you will not get a durable settlement — only conflict.

## To investors

You are right that moderate non-closure is probably too shallow. I am not defending a weak package.

But you are wrong if you think the only serious alternative to closures is coercion. A **deep non-closure package** can work if it is built correctly and if management and owners contribute visibly. That means:
- recurring labor-cost relief,
- material capacity reduction,
- meaningful headcount reduction through voluntary/internal means,
- and a credible plan for future products and German investment.

If you want closure-like economics, then support the package that actually creates them without destroying labor peace and trust.

## My bottom line

I remain firm on the core line:

- **No compulsory redundancies.**
- **No plant closures as the bargaining baseline.**
- **No one-sided sacrifice by workers.**
- **Yes to a deep, quantified, durable restructuring if it protects jobs and sites and treats employees fairly.**

## What I would accept
I am prepared to discuss a package that includes:
- temporary wage moderation or restraint on selected elements,
- bonus restraint or deferral,
- working-time flexibility,
- voluntary exits,
- phased retirement,
- redeployment,
- and capacity adjustments,

**but only if** it is matched by:
- management overhead cuts,
- procurement and efficiency savings,
- product and volume commitments,
- and binding job/site protection through 2030.

## What I will not accept
I will not accept:
- closures as the default answer,
- forced layoffs as the fallback,
- or a deal where workers pay first and others pay later — if at all.

If management wants a credible solution, it should stop treating closure threats as a substitute for a real plan. Bring the full bridge. Then we can judge whether the deep non-closure route is serious. If it is, we can support it. If it isn’t, then the company is choosing confrontation, not restructuring.
