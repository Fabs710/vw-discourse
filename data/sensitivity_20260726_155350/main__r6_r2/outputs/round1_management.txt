I’ll be direct.

I agree with one point from both the works council and the union: this company does need a durable solution, not a cosmetic one. Capacity has to come down, costs have to come down, and the restructuring has to be structurally credible. On that, we are aligned.

Where I do **not** agree is on the premise that Germany can be insulated from the consequences of its own cost base while the company’s competitiveness continues to erode. That is not a viable position. German factory costs are materially above target, the European market is shrinking, and we are under pressure from lower-cost competitors on a scale we have not faced before. If we do not act decisively now, we will destroy more value over time than any restructuring would cost.

So let me be equally clear:

- **No, I will not accept a deal that leaves the cost base fundamentally unchanged.**
- **No, I will not accept a short-term arrangement that postpones the problem to 2028 or 2030.**
- **No, I will not accept a settlement that gives us “peace” but preserves structural overcapacity.**

On plant closures: I hear the red lines you are drawing, but I am not taking closures off the table as a matter of principle. First-ever closures are on the table because the current footprint is not sustainable in its present form. If the employee side wants to avoid closures, then it must help deliver savings of comparable depth and durability through other measures. That means real capacity reduction, real labour-cost reduction, and real operational flexibility — not just internal redeployment language.

On compulsory redundancies: I would prefer to minimize them, but I will not bind the company to a formula that makes execution impossible. We need mechanisms that actually reduce headcount and fixed cost at the required scale. Voluntary departures, transfers, working-time measures, and negotiated contributions may be part of that. But they must add up to meaningful structural savings.

On site and job guarantees through 2030: I cannot give a blanket guarantee if the business case does not support it. A promise that prevents necessary restructuring would be irresponsible. What I can discuss is a binding framework tied to a serious employee contribution and measurable cost relief.

So the question is not whether there will be change. The question is whether the workforce will help shape a durable restructuring, or whether it will force management into a confrontation that damages output, investment capacity, and ultimately jobs more broadly.

Here is the minimum standard for a serious negotiation from my side:

1. A **material reduction in labour cost**.
2. A **durable reduction in capacity**.
3. A package that **permanently lowers fixed costs**, not just temporarily postpones them.
4. A credible path to restore the brand’s return on sales and fund future products and technology.
5. Execution that is fast enough to matter.

If you can bring me a package that truly delivers that, I will engage on how it is achieved. If what you are offering is essentially “keep everything open, preserve everything, and management should somehow find the savings elsewhere,” then that is not a solution. That is denial.

I want a negotiated outcome, but it must restore competitiveness. Otherwise we are simply managing decline.
