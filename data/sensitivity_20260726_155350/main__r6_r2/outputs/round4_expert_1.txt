Yes — on the facts given, a **non-closure package can only substitute for closure if it removes a very large share of the same economic burden**: headcount, site fixed cost, and recurring labour cost, not just temporary concessions.

## My assessment in one line
A **credible closure-free package is possible**, but only if it is **deep**, **multi-year**, and **backed by enforceable volume/capacity reductions**. In practice, that means something like:

- **15%–20% headcount reduction**
- **10%–25% capacity reduction**
- **2–3 years to steady state**
- plus **recurring wage moderation / labour-cost relief**
- plus **management and overhead cuts**
- plus **product and volume reallocation across sites**

If the package is materially smaller than that, it will usually **delay** rather than **replace** the economics of closure.

---

## 1) What closure usually buys economically
A closure delivers savings through four channels:

1. **Direct labour cost removal**  
   Wages, benefits, shift premia, overtime, and social overhead.

2. **Site fixed-cost removal**  
   Utilities, maintenance, security, local management, plant logistics, some depreciation exposure, and support functions tied to that site.

3. **Capacity rationalisation**  
   Less excess installed capacity means better utilisation at remaining plants.

4. **Structural discipline**  
   It prevents cost creep from preserving duplicated footprint.

A non-closure package must recreate most of these effects through different means. That is why shallow concessions do not work.

---

## 2) What a non-closure package must include
To be closure-equivalent, the package needs all of the following:

### A. Headcount reduction
Likely via:
- voluntary departures
- phased retirement
- hiring freeze
- redeployment
- internal transfer
- qualification / upskilling to move people out of declining areas

**Scale:** in the range of **15%–20%** of the relevant German workforce scope is a realistic benchmark if the goal is to offset closure economics.

### B. Capacity reduction without closure
This can be achieved through:
- shift elimination
- lower shift frequency
- product consolidation
- volume transfer to other German or non-German plants
- idling specific lines or workshops
- stopping certain model variants

**Scale:** **10%–25% installed capacity reduction** is the relevant range if the goal is meaningful fixed-cost relief.

### C. Recurring labour-cost relief
Examples:
- temporary wage moderation
- bonus restraint or deferral
- working-time reductions without full pay preservation
- removal or weakening of legacy guarantees
- higher flexibility in scheduling and shift patterns

This matters because headcount reduction alone may not be enough if the remaining workforce stays on the same cost curve.

### D. Overhead and support simplification
A site-preserving package often fails because it preserves duplicated white-collar and support cost.

Needed actions:
- plant support consolidation
- engineering / admin / quality / logistics rationalisation
- centralisation of some functions
- less local duplication across plants

### E. A 2–3 year implementation horizon
Durable savings usually do **not** arrive immediately if you avoid closure.
You need time for:
- voluntary exits to accumulate
- product moves to settle
- shift schedules to be redesigned
- redeployment to happen
- staffing levels to ratchet down naturally

---

## 3) Reasonable scenario ranges

### Scenario 1: Moderate non-closure package
Likely elements:
- voluntary exits only
- limited working-time restraint
- some bonus moderation
- small shift changes
- limited product reallocation

**Likely economic effect:**
- headcount reduction: **5%–10%**
- capacity reduction: **0%–10%**
- duration: **1–2 years**
- savings: **meaningful but usually not closure-equivalent**

**Assessment:** too shallow if the company truly has structural overcapacity.

---

### Scenario 2: Deep non-closure package
Likely elements:
- large voluntary exit programme
- phased retirement
- aggressive hiring restraint
- shift elimination at selected sites
- product/volume consolidation
- recurring labour-cost concessions
- overhead simplification

**Likely economic effect:**
- headcount reduction: **15%–20%**
- capacity reduction: **10%–25%**
- duration: **2–3 years**
- savings: can approximate closure economics if execution is disciplined

**Assessment:** this is the first range that looks plausibly substitutional for closure.

---

### Scenario 3: Closure package
Likely elements:
- one or more plant closures
- headcount elimination tied to site shutdown
- fixed-cost removal from the closed site
- production moved elsewhere or phased out

**Likely economic effect:**
- headcount reduction: often **20%+** in the affected scope
- capacity reduction: often **15%–30%+**
- duration: savings start earlier at the closed site, though one-time costs are high

**Assessment:** more immediate and more certain on fixed-cost removal, but politically and socially much harder.

---

## 4) What the non-closure package must “replace” from closure
For a closure-free solution to work, it has to replace the following:

### Site fixed-cost removal
This is the hardest part. If the site remains open, you still carry:
- building maintenance
- security
- utilities
- local supervision
- some plant logistics
- part of engineering and support overhead

So a non-closure package must either:
- sharply reduce the size of the site,
- repurpose it to a much leaner footprint,
- or consolidate enough functions that the remaining overhead is materially lower.

### Capacity discipline
If all sites stay open at essentially the same installed capacity, utilisation stays too low and fixed cost is not truly reset.

That is why **capacity reduction matters as much as headcount**.

### Durability
Temporary wage moderation without permanent staffing and footprint reduction is not enough.  
If the cost base rebounds after 2–3 years, the restructuring fails.

---

## 5) Short-term vs durable savings

### Short-term
Possible through:
- wage restraint
- bonus freeze
- overtime reduction
- hiring freeze
- shift cuts

These can help immediately, but they are often **reversible**.

### Durable
Require:
- permanent headcount reduction
- lasting product and capacity reallocation
- structural overhead simplification
- sustained labour-cost reset

Durable savings are what matter for a restructuring comparison.

---

## 6) My judgement on sufficiency
If management’s economic gap is genuinely structural, then:

- a **moderate** non-closure package is unlikely to be enough;
- a **deep** non-closure package may be enough **only if** it reaches the scale above;
- anything less will probably leave the company still carrying excess fixed cost and excess capacity.

So the key test is not “closures or not.”  
It is: **does the package permanently remove enough headcount, capacity, and fixed cost to restore competitiveness?**

---

## 7) What I would ask management to quantify
To judge the package properly, management should produce a bridge showing:

1. current cost gap vs target
2. labour-cost savings from headcount reduction
3. labour-cost savings from wage/concession measures
4. savings from shift/capacity cuts
5. overhead and support-function savings
6. one-time restructuring costs
7. timing to steady state
8. sensitivity if voluntary exits come in below target

Without that bridge, no one can honestly say whether the non-closure package is sufficient.
