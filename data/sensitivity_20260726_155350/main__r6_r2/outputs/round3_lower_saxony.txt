I will be equally direct.

The expert has clarified something important: **a closure-free solution is only credible if it is economically deep enough to produce closure-like effects.** I accept that as the factual benchmark. What I do **not** accept is the conclusion that this automatically justifies closures or compulsory redundancies if the first non-closure draft is not yet complete.

As the regional state shareholder, my test is not whether management can create pressure. My test is whether the company can be put back on a competitive path **without gutting the German industrial base, without mass forced layoffs, and without sidelining the state’s co-determination role**.

## Where I agree

### With management
You are right that a cosmetic deal is worthless.  
You are right that the company needs durable savings, not temporary window dressing.  
You are right that the footprint and cost base must change.

I also agree that voluntary exits, redeployment, working-time tools, productivity gains, and targeted employee contributions can all be part of a serious package.

### With the union and works council
You are right that workers cannot be the only side carrying the burden.  
You are right that trust requires security, not just threats.  
You are right that any employee contribution must be time-limited, quantified, and matched by contributions from management and ownership.

### With the family holding and investors
You are right that symbolism is not enough.  
You are right that the economics must add up.  
You are right that a purely political compromise that leaves the company uncompetitive would fail everyone in the long run.

## Where I disagree

### 1) I do not accept closures as the default answer
Management keeps saying closures remain on the table. I understand that as a bargaining position, but I will say plainly: **I will not treat German plant closures as a normal restructuring tool.**

This company’s plants are not just cost centers. They are:
- jobs,
- skills,
- supplier networks,
- regional economic anchors,
- and part of the company’s long-term industrial capability.

If closures are used lightly, the damage is irreversible. That is why I do not accept them as the first or preferred instrument.

### 2) I do not accept compulsory redundancies as the primary method
If headcount has to come down, the first instruments must be:
- voluntary departures,
- retirement and phased retirement,
- internal redeployment,
- retraining and qualification,
- hiring freezes,
- working-time measures,
- and capacity adjustment through shifts and product allocation.

I am not prepared to endorse forced layoffs as the default solution. If management wants a social partnership settlement, then it has to earn that trust.

### 3) I do not accept blank-cheque flexibility at the expense of site security
The employee side is right to insist that concessions must be tied to protection.  
A restructuring that asks workers to pay now while leaving them exposed later is not durable; it is simply one-sided.

But I also hear the concern from the capital side: guarantees cannot be completely unconditional if the economics are not there. So the right answer is **not** “guarantees never” and it is also **not** “guarantees without any conditions.” The right answer is a **conditional, measurable framework**.

## What I think a serious package must look like

If the company wants to avoid closures, then I expect a package that is **quantified** and **balanced**. The expert’s ranges suggest what “serious” means:

- roughly **15% to 20% headcount reduction** in the affected German scope,
- roughly **10% to 25% capacity reduction**,
- meaningful recurring labor-cost relief,
- and a **2- to 3-year implementation path**.

That is the economic test. But how those numbers are delivered matters just as much as the numbers themselves.

### My preferred route
A credible non-closure package would need to combine:
- voluntary exit programs,
- phased retirement,
- internal redeployment across German sites,
- shift reductions and product consolidation,
- temporary, clearly bounded labor-cost contributions,
- management overhead cuts,
- procurement and efficiency savings,
- and binding investment commitments for future products and technologies in Germany.

That is a hard package. It is also the kind of package that can preserve the company as a regional anchor while restoring competitiveness.

## What I will not support

I will not support:
- a default closure strategy,
- compulsory redundancies as the main tool,
- a package that guts regional employment,
- a settlement that sidelines the state’s veto and co-determination role,
- or a restructuring that only postpones the problem to 2028 or 2030.

## To management

1. exact savings gap,
2. management overhead cuts,
3. procurement and productivity gains,
4. workforce contribution,
5. capacity reduction,

## To the works council and union
You are right to insist that any contribution from employees must come with real security.  
But I also need you to engage the economics honestly.

If the benchmark really is a 15% to 20% headcount reduction and 10% to 25% capacity reduction, then the question is not whether there will be change. The question is **how** that change happens:
- through voluntary and internal instruments,
- or through a confrontational path that increases risk for everyone.

If you want me to use my leverage to block closures, then I need to see a package that genuinely substitutes for them in economic effect.

## To the family holding and investors
I understand your insistence that the company must become competitive again. I agree. But long-term value is not created by stripping out the German base in a way that damages trust, skills, and political legitimacy.

A regional anchor company that loses its social foundation becomes a weaker company, not a stronger one. That is why I will back a tough restructuring only if it preserves the company’s industrial core in Germany.

## My line, clearly
I can support:
- a deep negotiated restructuring,
- real but socially managed capacity reduction,
- voluntary and internal headcount reduction,
- time-limited employee contributions,
- management cuts and productivity measures,
- and investment commitments tied to future products.

I cannot support:
- plant closures as the default instrument,
- compulsory redundancies as the main method,
- or any outcome that removes the state’s co-determination and veto role.

## Bottom line
So my position is this:

- **Yes** to a serious, quantified restructuring.
- **Yes** to closure-like economics **if** they can be achieved without closures.
- **No** to a model that makes the German footprint smaller by breaking the social and regional foundation of the company.
- **No** to treating the state shareholder as a bystander.

Bring the full, quantified non-closure package. If it truly protects jobs, sites, and long-term competitiveness, I am prepared to work with it. If it does not, then I will not pretend that a bad solution is a good one.
