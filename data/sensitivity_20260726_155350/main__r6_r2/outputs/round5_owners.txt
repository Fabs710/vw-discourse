I will be direct.

## Where I agree

I agree with management and the experts on the central economic point: **a cosmetic compromise is worthless**.  
If the company does not materially reduce fixed cost, recurring labour cost, and excess capacity, then we are only delaying the crisis and burning value.

I also agree with the employees and the state on one important point: **prolonged industrial conflict is value-destructive**. A drawn-out strike cycle would damage the business, the brand, and ultimately the workforce and owners alike.

And I accept the benchmark that has emerged: if we avoid closures, the package must still deliver **closure-like economics**:
- meaningful headcount reduction,
- real capacity reduction,
- recurring cost relief,
- and a 2–3 year path to steady state.

That is the right standard.

## Where I disagree

### 1) “Deep non-closure” is not automatically enough
The works council and union are speaking as if a closure-free package is already the answer, provided it is simply “large.”  
I do **not** accept that.

The question is not whether the package sounds fair. The question is whether it truly removes enough:
- site fixed cost,
- local overhead,
- and duplicated capacity.

If it does not, then it is not a restructuring. It is postponement.

### 2) I will not endorse an unconditional no-closure / no-force position upfront
I understand the employee side wants certainty. But I will not commit the owners to a permanent no-closure, no-compulsory-redundancy stance before the economics are proven.

That would hand the company a structural disadvantage if the voluntary/internal route falls short.

My position is:
- **prefer voluntary exits, redeployment, phased retirement, working-time tools**
- **do not pre-commit to eliminating every fallback**
- **keep closures and compulsory redundancies available as last-resort instruments if the numbers fail**

That is not aggression. It is governance.

### 3) Site/job guarantees through 2030 must be conditional
I agree that trust matters. But unconditional guarantees through 2030 would freeze the industrial footprint regardless of market reality.

That is not stewardship; that is locking in weakness.

I could support **conditional protections** tied to:
- verified savings,
- execution milestones,
- and an independently monitored restructuring bridge.

But I cannot support a blank cheque that removes flexibility while the company is still under pressure.

## What I think the real test is

The experts are right that we need a **quantified bridge**.  
Not slogans. Not principles. A bridge.

Management needs to show, in black and white:
1. the exact savings gap,
2. management overhead cuts,
3. procurement and supplier savings,
4. workforce contribution,
5. capacity reduction by site,
6. headcount reduction,
7. one-time restructuring cost,
8. timing to steady state,
9. and sensitivity if voluntary exits fall short.

Without that, nobody can honestly claim that a deep non-closure package is sufficient.

## My position on the other stakeholders

### To management
You are right to reject a cosmetic deal. I support that.

But I will not let “closures remain on the table” become an excuse to avoid designing a serious non-closure bridge.  
Show me the full package, and show me why it does or does not close the gap.

### To the works council
You are right to insist that workers cannot be the only ones paying.  
But you are not yet entitled to assume that a closure-free solution is sufficient just because it is preferable.

If the package really can deliver the scale needed through voluntary and internal means, I will consider it seriously.  
If it cannot, then insisting on no closures in advance is not a strategy; it is denial.

### To the union
I agree that employee contributions must be matched by management and owner contributions.  
But temporary wage moderation or bonus restraint is not enough unless it sits inside a package that permanently improves the economics.

### To the state shareholder
You are right that this is an industrial and regional issue, not just a spreadsheet.  
But regional importance does not cancel economic reality. If a deep non-closure package works, I will support it. If it does not, the state cannot force the owners to preserve structural overcapacity indefinitely.

### To institutional investors
You are right that a shallow compromise is useless.  
But I do not share the assumption that the only serious answer to a deep restructuring is closure. A deep non-closure package can work — but only if it is truly deep and verifiable.

## What I would support

I am open to a settlement that includes:
- substantial voluntary exits,
- phased retirement,
- redeployment,
- hiring restraint,
- shift reductions,
- product and volume reallocation,
- working-time flexibility,
- bonus restraint or temporary labour-cost moderation,
- management overhead cuts,
- procurement savings,
- and investment commitments for future products.

But only if it is:
- **quantified**
- **time-bound**
- **independently monitored**
- and **large enough to deliver closure-like economics**

## What I will not support

I will not support:
- a cosmetic compromise,
- a package that preserves structural overcapacity,
- unconditional site/job guarantees through 2030,
- or a settlement that rules out every fallback before the restructuring is proven sufficient.

## My bottom line

I remain supportive of a deep restructuring, but my support is conditional on the economics, not on the rhetoric.

If the deep non-closure route can truly deliver the benchmark, I am prepared to back it.  
If it cannot, then we should not pretend otherwise.
