We are prepared to be pragmatic, but not permissive. From a capital-markets perspective, the only acceptable outcome is one that **credibly restores margins** and **durably lowers the cost base**. A deal that preserves structural overcapacity or leaves the company dependent on repeated labor conflict is not value-preserving.

## Where we agree

We agree with management, the expert, and to a degree the employee side on one key point: **a cosmetic compromise is worthless**.

We also agree that:
- the company needs a **durable restructuring**,
- **capacity must come down**,
- the cost base must be reset materially,
- and prolonged conflict would destroy value.

The expert’s benchmark is useful because it gives us the right test: if there is no closure, then the package must still produce **closure-like economics**.

## Where we disagree

### 1) “No closures” cannot be an absolute
We do **not** support closures as a first instinct. But we also do **not** accept an unconditional “no closures ever” position if the alternative package does not actually close the gap.

The works council and union keep treating “no closures” as a sufficient moral stance. It is not. The market does not reward intentions; it rewards earnings. If the non-closure package is too shallow, then it is not a viable substitute.

### 2) “No compulsory redundancies” is not enough on its own
We prefer voluntary exits, phased retirement, redeployment, hiring restraint, and working-time flexibility. But if those tools do not deliver the required scale, then refusing to discuss any fallback is not responsible.

We are not asking for mass firings as a slogan. We are saying the company must retain the ability to execute a **real** restructuring if voluntary tools fall short.

### 3) Guarantees must be conditional
Unconditional site and job guarantees through 2030 are not compatible with a serious turnaround unless the economics are already secured.

If the employee side wants protection, it must be tied to:
- quantified savings,
- measurable capacity reduction,
- and verified implementation milestones.

Otherwise guarantees become a way to lock in uncompetitive operations and postpone the next crisis.

## What we support

We would support a **deep non-closure package** if it truly delivers closure-equivalent economics. That means, at minimum:

- **15% to 20% headcount reduction** in the affected German scope
- **10% to 25% capacity reduction**
- **2 to 3 years** to reach steady state
- recurring labor-cost relief
- real site overhead reduction, not just accounting reclassification
- management and procurement savings alongside labor measures

That is the bar.

## What we do not support

We will not support:
- a moderate package that sounds balanced but leaves the cost base intact,
- a deal that preserves every site at current scale,
- a temporary truce that merely delays the same restructuring problem,
- or a settlement that protects every guarantee while shareholders absorb the downside.

## On the current stakeholder positions

### To management
You are right that a cosmetic deal is worthless. But you still need to provide a **full savings bridge**. We want the numbers, not a threat posture.

Show us:
1. exact savings gap,
2. management overhead cuts,
3. procurement/efficiency gains,
4. labor contribution,
5. capacity reduction,
6. headcount reduction,
7. timing to steady state,
8. one-time restructuring cost.

Without that, “closures remain on the table” is still just leverage, not a plan.

### To works council and union
We hear the insistence on no closures and no forced layoffs. But you have not yet demonstrated that voluntary/internal measures alone can deliver closure-like economics at sufficient scale.

If you want capital to back a closure-free solution, it must be **large, enforceable, and fast enough**. Otherwise it is not a solution; it is a delay.

### To the state shareholder
You are right that the company is more than a spreadsheet. But regional importance cannot be used to preserve structural excess indefinitely. We will back a site-preserving solution only if it is economically credible.

### To the family holding
You are right that the economics must change. We agree. But that means you must be prepared to accept a package that is actually large enough — not just rhetorically tough.

## Bottom line

Our position is unchanged:

- **No cosmetic compromise.**
- **No outcome that leaves the cost base structurally uncompetitive.**
- **Yes to a deep non-closure package if it delivers closure-like economics.**
- **If it does not, harder measures must remain available.**

If management can show a credible, quantified deep non-closure bridge that truly resets margins, we can support it. If not, we will not endorse a settlement that simply postpones the inevitable.
