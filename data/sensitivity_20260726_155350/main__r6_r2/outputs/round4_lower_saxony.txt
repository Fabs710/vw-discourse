I will be direct.

## Where I agree
I agree with management, the expert, and the capital side on one essential point: **a cosmetic deal is worthless**.  
If the company’s cost base and capacity do not change in a durable way, then we are only postponing the crisis.

I also accept the expert’s benchmark as the right working assumption: if we avoid closures, then the package still has to be **deep enough to produce closure-like economics**. That means:
- meaningful headcount reduction,
- real capacity reduction,
- recurring cost relief,
- and a 2- to 3-year implementation path.

That is the correct standard. I do **not** dispute that the situation is serious.

## Where I disagree
### 1) Closure-like economics do **not** automatically justify closures
Management, the family holding, and investors keep moving from “the package must be deep” to “therefore closures remain available.”  
I understand that as a bargaining position. I do **not** accept it as the only path.

A non-closure package has to be judged on whether it is economically real, not on whether it sounds easy. But that does **not** mean we should start from plant closures as the default instrument.

For me, closures in Germany are not a normal restructuring lever. They are a last-resort measure, because the damage is not only financial:
- jobs are lost,
- regional supplier networks are damaged,
- skills are destroyed,
- and the company weakens as an industrial anchor.

That is why I will not treat closures as the first or preferred solution.

### 2) Compulsory redundancies are not my preferred instrument
I agree that a credible restructuring needs headcount reduction.  
I do **not** accept that the only way to achieve that is forced layoffs.

The first instruments must be:
- voluntary exits,
- phased retirement,
- redeployment,
- retraining,
- hiring restraint,
- internal transfers,
- working-time measures,
- and product/volume shifts across the German network.

If those tools do not add up, then management must explain exactly why. But I will not endorse forced redundancies as the default answer simply because they are faster on paper.

### 3) The state’s role cannot be reduced to a spectator role
I have to respond clearly to management and the family holding here: the state is not a decorative stakeholder.

We hold a blocking minority under a special statute. We have board seats. We have a legitimate public-interest mandate. And the company is deeply embedded in the regional economy.

So when you speak as if “economics” alone decides everything, that is incomplete. A sustainable solution has to be:
- economically viable,
- socially viable,
- and politically durable.

If it is not durable in the region, it will not be durable in the company.

## What I think a serious path looks like
The expert’s ranges are a useful reference, but they do not answer the governance question. So here is my position:

### I could support a deep non-closure package if it includes all of the following:
- **no German plant closures as part of the base plan**
- **no compulsory redundancies as the first or primary method**
- **substantial voluntary headcount reduction**
- **real capacity cuts through shift reduction, product consolidation, and network reallocation**
- **management overhead and complexity cuts**
- **procurement and supplier savings**
- **binding investment commitments for future products in Germany**
- **preserved co-determination and board rights**
- **time-limited and measurable employee contributions matched by management and owner contributions**

That is the kind of package that could protect the company as a regional anchor while restoring competitiveness.

### What I would not support
I will not support:
- a settlement that uses the expert benchmark as a pretext for closures,
- a package that guts regional employment,
- a plan that sidelines the state’s governance role,
- or a deal that asks workers to carry the burden while ownership and management remain largely untouched.

## On the numbers
I agree that we need numbers, not slogans. But the numbers must be presented honestly.

Management should put on the table:
1. the exact savings gap,
2. the management contribution,
3. the owner contribution,
4. the workforce contribution,
5. the expected headcount reduction,
6. the expected capacity reduction,
7. the one-time restructuring cost,
8. and the timing to steady state.

Only then can we judge whether the closure-free route is genuinely sufficient.

But I will say this clearly: **“closures remain available” is not a savings bridge.**  
It is pressure. And pressure is not yet proof that alternatives have failed.

## My response to the other parties

### To management
You are right that the company needs a hard reset.  
You are not yet entitled to conclude that closures are necessary unless the non-closure package has truly been exhausted and shown to fail.

Bring a full bridge:
- management cuts,
- procurement savings,
- overhead reduction,
- capacity reduction,
- voluntary workforce reduction,
- and investment commitments.

If that bridge works, I am prepared to work with it.  
If it does not, then you need to show why — in numbers, not in threats.

### To the works council
I support your insistence that workers cannot be the only side making sacrifices. That is correct.

But I also need you to engage the economic scale honestly. If the expert’s benchmark is right, then the package must be large.  
That means voluntary and internal tools may need to be **much larger** than in a normal bargaining round. I will support a tough package if it protects jobs and sites, but it must be credible at the scale required.

### To the union
I agree that a deep package must be fair, time-limited, and matched by management and owner contributions. That is non-negotiable.

But fairness also means being honest about the depth needed. If the workforce is asked for temporary wage moderation, flexibility, or bonus restraint, then it must be part of a package that truly protects the industrial base in Germany.

### To the family holding
You are right that the economics must change. I agree.

But I do not accept the implicit logic that the only serious way to do that is to keep closures on the table as the main threat. That risks destroying trust and legitimacy.  
A regional anchor company needs more than short-term margin repair. It needs a workforce and a state that remain committed to the company’s future.

### To investors
You are right to reject a cosmetic compromise. So do I.

But you are wrong if you think that “closure-equivalent economics” automatically means closure must be used. A deep non-closure package can work if it is designed properly and if all sides contribute. That is the route I prefer because it preserves the company’s industrial and regional strength.

## My bottom line
My position is:

- **Yes** to a serious restructuring.
- **Yes** to deep, measurable savings.
- **Yes** to substantial headcount and capacity adjustment.
- **Yes** to voluntary and internal tools first.
- **Yes** to management and owner contributions.
- **Yes** to binding investment commitments and preserved co-determination.

But also:
- **No** to treating German closures as the default answer.
- **No** to compulsory redundancies as the primary method.
- **No** to a settlement that weakens the state’s governance role.
- **No** to any outcome that guts the regional economic anchor.

I want the full quantified bridge from management, and I want it in a form that tests a **deep non-closure scenario** against the closure benchmark.
