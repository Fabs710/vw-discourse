We appreciate the quantified bridge. It helps clarify the real test: **closure is not the goal; closure-equivalent economics are.** That is the only standard we will use.

## Where we agree

We agree with management, the family holding, and the expert on the core financial point:
- this cannot be a cosmetic settlement,
- the cost base must come down materially,
- capacity must be reduced,
- and the result must be durable, not temporary.

We also agree that prolonged conflict would destroy value. A long strike, a weak compromise, or a drawn-out stalemate would all be negative for shareholders.

## Where we disagree

### 1) The employee side’s red lines are too broad
The works council and union are still asking for:
- no closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- no erosion of job-security rights,
- no weakening of co-determination,
- plus binding site guarantees through 2030.

If that package prevents the company from reaching the needed economic reset, then it is not acceptable from a capital-market perspective. We are not willing to back a settlement that preserves every structure and leaves margins structurally impaired.

### 2) Conditionality matters more than slogans
We do **not** support closures as a first instinct. But we also do **not** support turning “no closure” into an absolute veto if the alternative package is too shallow.

The expert’s analysis makes the threshold clear:
- **moderate non-closure** is probably too weak,
- **deep non-closure** could work,
- but only if it truly delivers roughly:
  - **15% to 20% headcount reduction**
  - **10% to 25% capacity reduction**
  - **2 to 3 years** of implementation
  - and recurring labor-cost relief.

That is the bar.

### 3) Guarantees must be earned, not granted upfront
We are not supportive of unconditional site/job guarantees through 2030 if they freeze flexibility before the economics are fixed.

If the employee side wants protection, it has to be tied to a package that:
- materially lowers the cost base,
- removes enough capacity,
- and is enforceable.

Otherwise, guarantees become a way to lock in uncompetitive operations.

## What we would support

We would support a closure-free settlement **if and only if** it is deep enough to match the economic effect of closure.

That means a package with all of the following:
- meaningful voluntary exits and phased retirement,
- internal redeployment,
- shift and working-time reductions,
- bonus restraint or temporary wage moderation,
- substantial overhead reduction,
- product consolidation,
- and measurable capacity cuts.

In other words: if the company can achieve closure-like economics without closure, that is the preferable route. It preserves value and avoids the disruption and litigation risk of closures.

## What we will not support

We will not support:
- a symbolic compromise,
- a deal that leaves structural overcapacity in place,
- a temporary truce that delays the problem,
- or a settlement that protects sites while leaving shareholders with impaired returns.

## Our position on the current options

### Closure scenario
Financially clean, but socially and politically more disruptive. We do **not** insist on it if a deep non-closure package truly matches its economics.

### Moderate non-closure scenario
Not enough. From our perspective, this is the danger zone: it sounds balanced, but it likely leaves the company underpowered.

### Deep non-closure scenario
Potentially acceptable, and likely the best outcome for value **if** it is real and enforceable.

## What we need next
We want management to present one concrete package that shows:
1. exact savings gap,
2. exact headcount reduction,
3. exact capacity reduction,
4. exact labor-cost relief,
5. exact timing,
6. exact restructuring cost,
7. and what remains after implementation.

Then we can judge whether the package is genuinely closure-equivalent or just another delay.

## Bottom line

Our position is unchanged in substance:
- **No cosmetic deal.**
- **No outcome that leaves the cost base structurally uncompetitive.**
- **Yes to a deep negotiated package if it truly delivers closure-like economics without closure.**
- **If it does not, then harder measures remain justified.**

So the next step is not more principle. It is a credible, quantified deep non-closure package that actually meets the benchmark. Until then, we remain unconvinced.
