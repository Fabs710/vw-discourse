I will be equally clear.

## Where I agree
I agree with management, the union, the works council, and the expert on one basic point: **a cosmetic settlement is worthless**.  
If the package does not materially reduce the cost base, excess capacity, and recurring burden, then we are only postponing the crisis.

I also agree that a closure-free solution must be **deep**, not symbolic. The expert’s benchmark is the right seriousness test:
- roughly **15%–20% headcount reduction**
- roughly **10%–25% capacity reduction**
- a **2–3 year** path to steady state
- and **durable** recurring savings, not just temporary concessions

That is the correct threshold for credibility.

## Where I disagree
### 1) “Closures remain on the table” must not become a shortcut
Management, the family holding, and investors keep repeating that closures and forced redundancies must remain available unless a deep non-closure package is proven.

I understand the logic. I reject the implication.

You still have not shown that a closure-free package fails.  
You have not put a transparent, site-by-site savings bridge on the table.  
So closures are not yet a proven necessity — they are a fallback position being used to intensify pressure.

For me, that is not acceptable as a default negotiating posture when regional employment and industrial stability are at stake.

### 2) Forced redundancies are not an acceptable first resort
I agree that voluntary exits, phased retirement, redeployment, working-time tools, and product allocation changes must all be part of the solution.

But I do **not** accept compulsory redundancies as the normal fallback just because they are faster on paper.  
If the package is well designed, the company should be able to achieve a substantial reduction through voluntary and internal measures first.

If management says that cannot be done, then the burden is on management to prove that with hard numbers — not to assume coercion from the start.

### 3) Conditional guarantees must not be a backdoor to permanent insecurity
I understand the concern about freezing flexibility. But a “guarantee” that can be reopened whenever management gets nervous is not a real guarantee.

I will not support an arrangement that gives workers up-front concessions while keeping closures and forced layoffs permanently in reserve. That is one-sided risk transfer.

If the workforce is asked to contribute, then the company must offer **real site and job protection** tied to the agreed restructuring path, not a vague promise that can be undone at the first setback.

## On the state shareholder role
I want to be direct here: the regional state shareholder should use its leverage to defend the region — not to normalize closure pressure.

A legitimate state-backed restructuring must protect:
- employment in the state,
- strategic sites,
- co-determination,
- and the company’s role as a regional economic anchor.

That does **not** mean refusing change. It means insisting that change be designed in a way that does not hollow out the region or turn the state into a spectator while closures are threatened.

## On the numbers
The experts’ ranges are useful because they define seriousness. I accept that a closure-free package must be big enough to remove the same recurring burden as closure.

But that does **not** mean closure is the only serious answer.

A deep non-closure package can, in principle, achieve closure-like economics through:
- voluntary departures
- phased retirement
- internal transfers
- hiring restraint
- shift reductions
- product and volume allocation
- working-time flexibility
- management overhead cuts
- procurement savings
- and a fair employee contribution

That is the route I support.

## My position on the other stakeholders
### To management
Bring the **full savings bridge**:
1. exact gap,
2. management cuts,
3. procurement/supplier savings,
4. product and volume allocation by site,
5. voluntary workforce measures,
6. capacity reduction by site,
7. one-time restructuring cost,
8. timing to steady state.

Until that is on the table, I do not accept the argument that closures are unavoidable.

### To the works council and union
I support your rejection of cosmetic cuts and your insistence that workers cannot be the only side paying.

But if the benchmark is closure-like economics, then the package must be genuinely large.  
That means you should be prepared for substantial restructuring measures — not closures, not forced layoffs, but real changes in shifts, allocation, internal mobility, and employee contribution.

### To the family holding and investors
You are right that the economics must change. I do not dispute that.

Where I push back is your readiness to treat closures and compulsory redundancies as the natural fallback before the closure-free route has even been tested properly. That is not proof. It is preference.

If a deep non-closure package can deliver the same economics, it is the better long-term choice for enterprise value and industrial legitimacy.

## My bottom line
I can support a serious restructuring only if it:
- protects German sites as the default,
- preserves co-determination and our veto role,
- relies first on voluntary and internal measures,
- includes real management and owner contributions,
- and is tied to binding, measurable milestones.

I will **not** support:
- plant closures as the default instrument,
- compulsory redundancies as the first fallback,
- or any package that weakens the state’s co-determination role.
