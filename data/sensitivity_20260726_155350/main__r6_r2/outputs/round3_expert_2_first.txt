Below is the closure-equivalent savings bridge I would use for decision-making. Because the discussion so far contains **ranges but no company-specific plant P&Ls**, this has to be a **bounded estimate**, not a precise forecast.

## Executive view

A **credible non-closure package can match closure economics only if it produces all three of the following**:

1. **Fixed-cost removal** at site and network level, not just variable savings  
2. **Material labor-cost reset** over multiple years  
3. **Capacity reduction large enough** to lift utilization and reduce absorption losses

In practical terms, the conversation’s own benchmark is consistent with:

- **Headcount reduction:** ~15% to 20%
- **Capacity reduction:** ~10% to 25%
- **Implementation horizon:** ~2 to 3 years

That is the right order of magnitude if the goal is to be economically comparable to closure.

## 1) Savings bridge logic

For an industrial auto operation, the annual savings bridge usually splits into:

- **Direct labor**
  - wages, salaries, social charges, overtime, bonus, shift premiums
- **Site overhead**
  - plant management, support functions, maintenance, utilities, security, logistics interfaces
- **Corporate/network overhead**
  - duplicated engineering, planning, HR, quality, production control
- **Variable cost / procurement**
  - mostly unchanged by closure unless volume is lost externally
- **Capacity utilization effect**
  - underused fixed assets and labor absorption loss if volume falls without footprint reduction
- **Transition / restructuring costs**
  - severance, retirement bridges, dismantling, transfer costs, retraining, write-offs
- **Deferred effects**
  - lower future capex, lower maintenance, lower complexity, possibly lower warranty/quality leakage if simplification is real

---

## 2) Three scenario set

### A. Closure scenario
This is the benchmark for “hard” savings.

**Mechanics**
- Full plant closure or permanent line/site shutdown
- Transfer of some volume elsewhere
- Elimination of associated headcount, overhead, and fixed site costs
- Some cost of displaced production absorbed by other plants, but with lower total overhead base

**Annual steady-state savings composition**
- **Labor cost:** high
- **Site overhead:** high
- **Corporate/network overhead:** moderate
- **Capacity utilization effect:** high
- **Variable cost:** limited or neutral
- **Transition costs:** high in years 1–2

**Typical annual steady-state gross savings**
- For a major German auto plant: **~€150m to €350m per site equivalent**, depending on size, product mix, and whether the site carries stamping, assembly, or powertrain functions.

**Restructuring cost**
- Often **€75m to €250m+ one-time**, sometimes more if there are large workforce measures, asset write-downs, and transfer expenses.

**Net present value**
- Usually strongest if the site is genuinely excess capacity and the lost fixed cost base is not rebuilt elsewhere.

---

### B. Moderate non-closure scenario
This is a “site-preserving but materially smaller” solution.

**Mechanics**
- No formal closure
- One or more lines stopped or consolidated
- Shift reductions, weekend elimination, slower pace
- Voluntary exits and retirements
- Working-time changes and labor-cost concessions
- Some production reassigned within the German network

**Likely impact**
- **Headcount reduction:** ~8% to 12%
- **Capacity reduction:** ~5% to 10%
- **Labor-cost relief:** moderate
- **Site overhead:** only partially removed
- **Utilization:** improves somewhat, but not fully

**Annual steady-state gross savings**
- Roughly **€70m to €180m** for a large plant cluster or significant site segment

**Why it often falls short**
- A large share of fixed cost remains
- Support functions are not eliminated enough
- Volume is cut, but not enough to materially reset the cost base
- You often get **temporary peace, not full economics**

**Conclusion**
- Usually **insufficient** if management needs a closure-equivalent reset

---

### C. Deep non-closure scenario
This is the only non-closure route that can plausibly approximate closure economics.

**Mechanics**
- No formal closure, but a major footprint and workforce reset
- Strong product consolidation
- Material shift elimination / line rationalization
- Voluntary exits plus phased retirement plus internal transfers
- Meaningful wage moderation or bonus restraint
- Procurement and overhead cuts
- Clear network reallocation of volumes

**Likely impact**
- **Headcount reduction:** ~15% to 20%
- **Capacity reduction:** ~10% to 25%
- **Labor-cost relief:** material and recurring
- **Site overhead:** partially or substantially removed, depending on whether an entire line or support block is exited
- **Utilization:** improves materially

**Annual steady-state gross savings**
- Roughly **€120m to €300m** for a large German auto site or comparable footprint block

**Important caveat**
- To match closure economics, the deep non-closure package must not just reduce labor. It must also remove enough **support, complexity, and underabsorbed fixed cost**.

**Conclusion**
- This is the only non-closure scenario that can reasonably be described as **closure-equivalent** in economic effect.

## 3) Illustrative savings bridge by category

Below is a normalized bridge for a large German automotive site cluster. I am using broad ranges because no base cost data were supplied.

### Closure scenario
- **Direct labor savings:** 35% to 45% of total annual gross savings
- **Site overhead savings:** 20% to 30%
- **Corporate/network overhead savings:** 10% to 15%
- **Utilization / absorption gains:** 15% to 25%
- **Transition costs:** negative in years 1–2

### Moderate non-closure
- **Direct labor savings:** 30% to 40%
- **Site overhead savings:** 10% to 15%
- **Corporate/network overhead savings:** 5% to 10%
- **Utilization / absorption gains:** 10% to 15%
- **Residual fixed-cost drag:** still large

### Deep non-closure
- **Direct labor savings:** 35% to 45%
- **Site overhead savings:** 15% to 25%
- **Corporate/network overhead savings:** 10% to 15%
- **Utilization / absorption gains:** 15% to 25%
- **Residual fixed-cost drag:** reduced but not eliminated

## 4) What the company would need to show in a proper bridge

Management should provide, per scenario:

1. **Starting cost base**
   - direct labor
   - indirect labor
   - site overhead
   - corporate allocations
   - depreciation / run-rate fixed cost
2. **Volume baseline**
   - units, shifts, capacity, utilization
3. **Headcount by function**
   - production, maintenance, logistics, engineering, admin
4. **Savings by lever**
   - exits
   - wage moderation
   - shift/working-time change
   - overhead cuts
   - procurement
   - asset rationalization
5. **Transition costs**
   - severance
   - retirement programs
   - training / transfers
   - write-downs
6. **Timing**
   - year 1, year 2, year 3 steady state
7. **Sensitivity**
   - what happens if only voluntary exits are achieved
   - what happens if wage concessions expire
   - what happens if volume underperforms

Without that, the debate is not really about economics; it is about bargaining posture.

---

## 5) My quantified judgment

### Closure scenario
- **Most certain** path to full fixed-cost reset
- Highest transition cost
- Strongest ability to remove site overhead and structural excess

### Moderate non-closure scenario
- **Usually not enough**
- Good for labor peace or partial adjustment
- Weak as a closure substitute

### Deep non-closure scenario
- **Plausibly sufficient**, but only if:
  - headcount falls by about **15%–20%**
  - capacity falls by about **10%–25%**
  - labor cost is reset for **2–3 years**
  - site overhead is actually reduced, not just shifted around

If the deep non-closure package does **not** remove enough fixed cost and support overhead, it will not be closure-equivalent.

---

## 6) Bottom line

From a restructuring finance perspective:

- **Closure** is the cleanest way to eliminate excess fixed cost.
- **Moderate non-closure** is generally too shallow.
- **Deep non-closure** can work, but only if it produces **closure-like economics**, meaning:
  - substantial headcount reduction,
  - material capacity reduction,
  - recurring labor-cost relief,
  - and real site overhead elimination.
