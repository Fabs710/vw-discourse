I will respond directly.

## First, what I agree with

I agree with all sides on one basic point: **a cosmetic deal is worthless**.  
A restructuring that does not materially reduce the cost base, simplify the footprint, and restore the brand’s return-on-sales is not a restructuring. It is delay.

I also agree with the expert’s core conclusion: **if we avoid closures, the alternative package still has to be deep enough to produce closure-like economics**. That is the right standard. I am not interested in symbolic concessions or a temporary truce that leaves us structurally uncompetitive.

## Where I disagree

### To the works council
You keep saying “no closures, no compulsory redundancies, no erosion of guarantees” as if those red lines solve the economics. They do not.

You are right that capacity reduction does not always require a legal closure. But let’s not play with words: if a site loses substantial volume, shifts, support functions, and overhead, then it is functionally being downsized. That may be acceptable. What is not acceptable is insisting on preserving the current footprint and current cost structure while also demanding that management magically close the margin gap.

You asked for a savings bridge. Fair. Here is the real issue: **unless the employee side is prepared to deliver major, durable savings, those protections become unaffordable**. Not politically unaffordable — economically unaffordable.

And I will be blunt: if voluntary exits, redeployment, working-time measures, and minor pay restraint are all that are on offer, that is **not enough**. The expert’s ranges make that obvious. We need a materially smaller headcount, materially lower labor cost, and lower network capacity. Otherwise the company remains trapped.

### To the union
I appreciate that you are prepared to discuss temporary, quantified contributions. That is constructive. But you are still trying to turn “temporary” into a substitute for structural change.

That will not work.

A time-limited wage moderation or bonus restraint is helpful only if it is part of a broader package that permanently reduces fixed costs and capacity. If you want to avoid closures, then the workforce contribution must be large enough and durable enough to matter. Otherwise we just defer the same crisis.

I also reject the suggestion that management is “weaponizing” closures. No — management is putting reality on the table. If the alternative package does not add up, then closures remain a lawful and necessary instrument. I would prefer not to use them. But I will not rule them out to preserve a comforting fiction.

### To the state shareholder
You say closures are not a normal first instrument. I agree. They are not the first instrument. But they remain a legitimate instrument if the economics demand them.

What I cannot accept is a posture that says, in effect, “keep the sites open, preserve employment, and somehow the numbers will work themselves out.” They will not.

You are right to insist on transparency and a full bridge. But transparency cannot become a veto on hard decisions. The state can influence the shape of the restructuring; it cannot repeal the competitive reality facing this business.

### To the controlling family holding
You are correct that the deal must change the economics. I agree completely. Where I disagree is the notion that the burden can be pushed mainly onto the workforce while keeping the industrial footprint effectively intact.

You talk about avoiding symbolism. Then let’s be serious: a real restructuring means accepting that some sites, lines, functions, and roles will shrink materially. If not through closure, then through deep downsizing and simplification. But the footprint must shrink. The cost base must shrink. There is no way around that.

### To investors
You are right to reject a cosmetic compromise. You are also right that we need closure-like economics if closures are avoided.

But I want to be clear: you do not get those economics by demanding everything at once and leaving management no room to negotiate. We need executable change, not just a headline outcome. If the package is to work, it must be implementable in the German co-determined structure. That means some combination of volume shifts, voluntary departures, labor-cost resets, and operational simplification — not just a demand for instant perfection.

## My position, plainly

Here is what I will support:

- **A deep restructuring**
- **Real capacity reduction**
- **Substantial headcount reduction**
- **Recurring labor-cost relief**
- **Operational flexibility**
- **Investment commitments for future products and technology**
- **A negotiated package that is durable and enforceable**

Here is what I will not support:

- A cosmetic compromise
- A package that leaves the German cost base materially uncompetitive
- A deal that preserves structural overcapacity
- A solution that postpones the margin problem to 2028 or 2030
- A settlement that asks management to fund future investment without freeing up money now

## On the numbers

The expert’s ranges are the right lens, and they tell us something important:  
If we avoid closures, then the alternative must still deliver roughly:

- **15% to 20% headcount reduction** in the affected German scope
- **10% to 25% capacity reduction**
- **durable labor-cost relief**
- and a **2- to 3-year implementation path**

That is the benchmark.

So I am asking the employee side a simple question:  
If you reject closures and compulsory redundancies, then **how exactly do you deliver those numbers?**

Not in principle. Not as a slogan. Numerically.

If the answer is only voluntary exits plus marginal wage restraint, then it does not meet the benchmark. If the answer is a true package of voluntary departures, phased retirement, redeployment, shift reductions, reduced bonuses, working-time change, product consolidation, and measurable productivity gains — then we can assess it seriously.

But I will not pretend that anything smaller is enough.

## What I want next

I want a **quantified package**, not another round of red lines.

Specifically:
1. the exact savings gap,
2. the exact management contribution,
3. the exact labor contribution,
4. the exact capacity reduction,
5. the exact headcount reduction,
6. the exact duration of concessions,
7. and the exact conditions for site/job protection.

If the employee side wants no closures, then show me a package that delivers closure-like economics without closures.  
If it cannot, then we are not discussing a viable alternative — we are discussing delay.

## Bottom line

I remain open to a closure-free solution **only if** it is deep enough to materially and durably reset the cost base.  
I will not accept a cosmetic compromise.  
I will not accept a settlement that leaves the company structurally uncompetitive.  
And I will not pretend that preserving every current site at current scale is compatible with restoring this brand’s competitiveness.

Bring the numbers. Otherwise, the discussion is still stuck in principles while the market keeps moving.
