I will be equally direct.

I agree with management, the family holding, and the investors on one point: this company needs a **durable** restructuring, not a cosmetic one. The cost base must come down, and the company must become competitive again. We are not here to defend stagnation.

But I do **not** accept the premise that the only serious route is plant closures in Germany or a restructuring that shifts the main burden onto employees and the regions. That would be economically risky, politically reckless, and unacceptable for a shareholder with a responsibility for the regional economy.

## Where I agree
- The company needs real savings.
- Capacity has to be adjusted.
- The business must regain competitiveness.
- A negotiated solution is preferable to open conflict.
- Voluntary instruments, redeployment, working-time flexibility, and targeted employee contributions can all be part of a serious package.

## Where I disagree
### On closures as a “fallback”
Management keeps saying closures remain on the table if alternatives do not add up. I understand that as a bargaining stance, but I will be clear: **I will not treat German plant closures as a normal or acceptable instrument of first resort**. This company’s German footprint is not just a cost item; it is an industrial base, a supplier network, a skills reservoir, and a regional anchor.

If you want us to consider alternatives to closure, then put real numbers on the table:
- what savings are required,
- what management itself contributes,
- what overhead reductions are possible,
- what procurement and productivity gains are achievable,
- and what the workforce contribution would be under a negotiated framework.

Do not use the threat of closures to bypass that work.

### On compulsory redundancies
I do **not** support compulsory redundancies as the main instrument. If headcount reduction is needed, the first tools must be:
- voluntary departures,
- retirement and phased retirement,
- internal redeployment,
- qualification and retraining,
- working-time solutions,
- and capacity adjustments that preserve sites.

If those instruments are genuinely exhausted, we should examine the outcome carefully. But I am not prepared to endorse forced layoffs as the default answer.

### On job and site guarantees
The works council and union are right to demand trust-building guarantees. A restructuring that asks employees for concessions while offering them no security is not a durable settlement. I can support **conditional guarantees** if they are tied to a balanced package and measurable restructuring progress. That is different from a blank cheque, but it is also different from management’s current reluctance to commit to anything at all.

What I would consider reasonable:
- site protection in Germany for a defined period,
- no compulsory redundancies as a rule,
- clear investment commitments for future products and technologies,
- and a jointly monitored restructuring path with milestones.

That is not “freezing the company.” It is creating the conditions for a workable social partnership.

## To management
You say the burden is on the employee side to prove that alternatives can add up. Fair enough. But the burden is also on management to show that it has fully explored alternatives before reaching for the most damaging instruments.

I want a package that shows:
- serious management and overhead cuts,
- realistic capacity alignment,
- productivity measures,
- supply-chain and procurement savings,
- and a genuine industrial plan for German sites.

If closures are presented before that work is done, then I will regard that as premature and unacceptable.

## To the works council
I understand your insistence on no closures and no compulsory redundancies. That position has legitimacy. But I also need to be honest: **you cannot simply say “no” to every hard instrument and expect the economics to solve themselves**. If the package you want avoids closures, then it must still deliver the necessary savings and flexibility.

So I ask you directly: if we protect the sites and avoid compulsory redundancies, what exact employee-side contribution are you prepared to consider, and for how long? If the answer is only general principles, then we do not yet have a settlement.

## To the union
You are right that any employee contribution must be time-limited, fair, and matched by contributions from management and owners. I support that principle. But we need specificity:
- Is it temporary wage moderation?
- Bonus restraint?
- Reduced overtime premiums?
- Shift flexibility?
- A solidarity contribution?

If the union is prepared to quantify a package that is limited in time and paired with binding site and investment commitments, that is a serious basis for negotiation.

## To the family holding and investors
You are right that symbolism is not enough. I agree that half-measures will not fix the margin problem. But a solution that achieves the numbers by hollowing out the German footprint is also not acceptable. Long-term value does not come from permanently damaging the company’s industrial base.

A viable restructuring must protect the company’s future **and** its regional roots. If that requires more patience, more negotiation, and more balanced burden-sharing, then that is the price of a durable solution.

## My line, clearly
I can support:
- a negotiated restructuring,
- meaningful capacity reduction,
- voluntary headcount reduction,
- time-limited employee contributions,
- working-time and flexibility measures,
- and binding investment commitments.

I cannot support:
- a default closure strategy,
- compulsory redundancies as the primary tool,
- a deal that guts regional employment,
- or any outcome that sidelines the state’s co-determination and veto role.

## Bottom line
If management wants a settlement, it must come with a package that is:
1. economically credible,
2. socially balanced,
3. reversible where possible,
4. and protective of German sites and jobs.

If that is on the table, we can work. If the proposal remains “closures first, consequences later,” then the state shareholder will not stand aside.
