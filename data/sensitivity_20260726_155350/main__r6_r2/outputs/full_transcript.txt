[Expert: Industrial restructuring / labour cost m — Round 2]
Yes — **a non-closure package can in principle deliver savings comparable to closure**, but only if it is **large, durable, and enforceable**, and only if the company is willing to combine several instruments at once.

The key point is this:

- **Closure** creates a large fixed-cost reduction quickly, because it removes plant overhead, maintenance, logistics, and some labor and utility costs.
- A **non-closure package** can match that only if it achieves:
  1. substantial headcount reduction,
  2. lower recurring labor cost per unit,
  3. lower working-capital and logistics burden from capacity consolidation,
  4. and sustained productivity/utilization gains.

If it does not do all four, it will usually be **smaller and less durable** than closure.

## 1) What “equivalent savings” usually means in plant economics

For a German automotive site or network, closure-equivalent savings are not just payroll. They normally include:

- direct labor cost reduction,
- indirect labor / support function reduction,
- plant fixed overhead elimination,
- energy, maintenance, and facilities cost reduction,
- logistics simplification,
- inventory and complexity reduction,
- and sometimes better network utilization elsewhere.

A pure labor deal without footprint reduction often misses the largest structural savings: **fixed site overhead and complexity**.

So the question is not “can workers contribute?” but “can the package remove enough recurring cost from the cost base?”

## 2) What scale is typically needed

Without company-specific cost data, I can only give bounded estimates. For a major German carmaker plant/network, a closure-equivalent non-closure package usually needs to produce roughly:

- **10% to 20% reduction in total Germany-based production-related cost base** at the affected network level, or
- **15% to 30% reduction in unit labor cost burden** if the issue is mainly labor competitiveness.

If the target is a site closure’s worth of savings, the package often has to reduce:
- **headcount by roughly 15% to 30%** in the relevant production-and-support population, and
- **effective capacity by roughly 10% to 25%**, depending on baseline utilization and how much fixed overhead can be absorbed elsewhere.

Those are broad ranges, not a promise. The closer the plants are to full utilization already, the harder it is to get closure-like savings without actual shutdown.

## 3) What a credible non-closure package would need to contain

### A. Headcount reduction
Durable savings require a meaningful shrinkage of the workforce, not just temporary restraint.

A serious package would normally combine:
- voluntary exits,
- retirement / phased retirement,
- redeployment,
- natural attrition,
- limited hiring freeze,
- and possibly targeted conversion of temporary labor into lower-cost flexibility or elimination of it.

For durable effect, I would expect:
- **at least 10% headcount reduction** network-wide in the affected German scope as a minimum,
- and more likely **15% to 20%** if no plant is closed.

If the current workforce is already heavily protected and the company relies only on attrition, the timeline becomes too slow and savings too weak.

### B. Capacity reduction without closure
If no plant closes, capacity still must come out of the system.

That usually means:
- fewer shifts,
- lower planned weekly output,
- product consolidation across sites,
- removal of duplicate lines,
- and some idle-space rationalization.

To match closure economics, you normally need:
- **one of the plants functionally downsized**, or
- multiple plants operating at lower utilization with a much simpler product mix.

Typical durable target:
- **capacity cut of 10% to 25%** in the relevant German production network.

If capacity is not reduced, then the fixed costs remain largely in place.

### C. Labor-cost contribution
This is the most politically visible part, but by itself it is rarely enough.

Possible instruments:
- temporary wage moderation,
- bonus restraint or suspension,
- shift-premium reduction,
- longer working hours without proportional pay uplift,
- reduction of special allowances,
- conversion of some pay elements into variable / contingent forms.

A credible contribution depends on the baseline. In German automotive bargaining settings, a realistic negotiated labor-cost contribution might yield:
- **3% to 8% reduction in total direct labor cost per year** during the contribution period,
- sometimes more if bonuses and supplements are significant.

But note:
- if it is **temporary**, it helps only short-term;
- if it is not tied to headcount and capacity reduction, it mostly postpones the problem.

### D. Productivity and utilization gains
A non-closure deal must include better use of the remaining footprint.

That means:
- higher line utilization,
- less complexity,
- fewer changeovers,
- higher labor productivity,
- reduced indirect staffing.

A realistic improvement range:
- **5% to 15% productivity gain** over 2 to 3 years if the organization is restructured properly.

If management assumes more than that without major simplification, the assumption is optimistic.

## 4) Can this really match closure?

### Short answer
**Yes, but only if the package is deep enough to function like a closure in economic effect, even if not in legal form.**

In practical terms, that means:
- one or more sites do not have to close, but
- they must lose enough volume, shifts, staff, and overhead that their cost structure approaches a downsized network.

If management wants to avoid closure entirely, the non-closure package typically has to be **more complex and slower** than closure, and the expected savings are often **slightly smaller unless the labor deal is very strong**.

### My assessment by scenario
#### Scenario 1: Voluntary exits + wage restraint only
- Headcount reduction: maybe **3% to 8%**
- Capacity reduction: minimal
- Durability: weak
- Verdict: **not enough**

#### Scenario 2: Voluntary exits + working-time changes + shift cuts + bonus restraint
- Headcount reduction: **8% to 15%**
- Capacity reduction: **5% to 10%**
- Durability: moderate
- Verdict: **possible partial substitute**, but still often below closure-equivalent savings

#### Scenario 3: Same as above plus product consolidation, line simplification, and a meaningful labor-cost reset
- Headcount reduction: **15% to 20%**
- Capacity reduction: **10% to 20%**
- Durable labor-cost reduction: **material**
- Verdict: **can approach closure-equivalent economics**

#### Scenario 4: Full non-closure package with binding multi-year wage moderation, deep redeployment, major utilization gains, and site rationalization
- Headcount reduction: **20%+**
- Capacity reduction: **20%+**
- Verdict: **can match closure-like savings**, but only if implementation is strict and the company truly accepts a smaller footprint in all but name

## 5) What timeline is required

Durable savings do not happen instantly.

A realistic timeline for a non-closure package is:

### 0–6 months
- agreement,
- labor peace,
- hiring freeze,
- voluntary exit launch,
- shift and overtime controls.

Savings are limited in this phase.

### 6–18 months
- most voluntary exits realized,
- shift reductions and redeployment begin,
- bonus and pay measures take effect,
- capacity starts to fall.

This is where meaningful savings start.

### 18–36 months
- utilization improvements,
- product consolidation,
- indirect headcount cuts,
- recurring fixed-cost reduction.

This is the period when the package can become durable.

If the objective is to solve a structural competitiveness problem, a package that delivers most savings only after 3 years may be too slow unless the company has enough liquidity and market stability.

## 6) Biggest risk: “no closure” but also “no real downsizing”

This is the main danger.

A package can be framed as avoiding closure, but if it includes:
- only voluntary exits,
- modest wage restraint,
- and no meaningful reduction in sites, shifts, or support functions,

then it will usually produce:
- some cash savings,
- but not a durable structural fix.

That becomes a **delay strategy**, not a restructuring.

## 7) Practical conclusion

My expert view is:

- **A non-closure deal can work only if it is economically equivalent in output, not identical in form.**
- To be credible, it likely needs:
  - **15% to 20% headcount reduction** in the affected German scope,
  - **10% to 25% capacity reduction**,
  - **3% to 8% recurring labor-cost relief per year during the concession period**, and
  - **2 to 3 years** to fully realize savings.
- If the package stays below those ranges, it is unlikely to match the savings from closure.

## Bottom line
If management wants to avoid closures, it must accept a package that still produces **real footprint shrinkage, major headcount reduction, and lasting labor-cost reset**.  
If the package is mainly a wage concession and voluntary attrition, it will **not** be equivalent to closure.

## 1) Savings bridge logic

For an industrial auto operation, the annual savings bridge usually splits into:

- **Direct labor**
  - wages, salaries, social charges, overtime, bonus, shift premiums
- **Site overhead**
  - plant management, support functions, maintenance, utilities, security, logistics interfaces
- **Corporate/network overhead**
  - duplicated engineering, planning, HR, quality, production control
- **Variable cost / procurement**
  - mostly unchanged by closure unless volume is lost externally
- **Capacity utilization effect**
  - underused fixed assets and labor absorption loss if volume falls without footprint reduction
- **Transition / restructuring costs**
  - severance, retirement bridges, dismantling, transfer costs, retraining, write-offs
- **Deferred effects**
  - lower future capex, lower maintenance, lower complexity, possibly lower warranty/quality leakage if simplification is real

---

## 2) Three scenario set

### A. Closure scenario
This is the benchmark for “hard” savings.

**Mechanics**
- Full plant closure or permanent line/site shutdown
- Transfer of some volume elsewhere
- Elimination of associated headcount, overhead, and fixed site costs
- Some cost of displaced production absorbed by other plants, but with lower total overhead base

**Annual steady-state savings composition**
- **Labor cost:** high
- **Site overhead:** high
- **Corporate/network overhead:** moderate
- **Capacity utilization effect:** high
- **Variable cost:** limited or neutral
- **Transition costs:** high in years 1–2

**Typical annual steady-state gross savings**
- For a major German auto plant: **~€150m to €350m per site equivalent**, depending on size, product mix, and whether the site carries stamping, assembly, or powertrain functions.

**Restructuring cost**
- Often **€75m to €250m+ one-time**, sometimes more if there are large workforce measures, asset write-downs, and transfer expenses.

**Net present value**
- Usually strongest if the site is genuinely excess capacity and the lost fixed cost base is not rebuilt elsewhere.

---

### B. Moderate non-closure scenario

# 3) Scenario 1 — Closure case
## Description
Close one or more sites, transfer or discontinue volume, eliminate associated headcount.

### Illustrative economics
- **Headcount reduction:** 20%–30% in affected German scope
- **Capacity reduction:** 15%–30%
- **Steady-state timing:** 12–24 months, depending on shutdown and transfer sequence
- **One-time restructuring cost:** high

### Savings bridge
For every 100 units of current cost:

- Direct labor savings: **8–12**
- Site fixed-cost savings: **4–8**
- Overhead/support savings: **1–3**
- Capacity/utilization gain: **1–3**
- Total steady-state savings: **14–26**

### One-time costs
- Severance / social plan / transfer costs
- Asset write-downs / demolition / decommissioning
- Relocation / ramp-up costs at receiving sites
- Disruption losses during transition

Illustratively: **8–15** one-time cost units, sometimes more.

### Assessment
- Highest certainty on structural fixed-cost removal
- Fastest way to remove duplicated footprint
- But highest transition cost and most severe operational disruption

- indirect labour,

- **capacity reduction** of roughly **10%–25%**
- **multi-year labor-cost relief** through wage restraint / concessions / working-time changes
- **lower site overhead** via network simplification, shift rationalization, and indirect labor cuts
- **product allocation changes** that reduce underutilized fixed cost
- **durable implementation** over about **2–3 years**

That is the approximate range where a non-closure package can start to look like a closure scenario in steady-state economics.

---

## Why closure is economically powerful

A plant closure typically delivers savings in four buckets:

1. **direct labor savings**  
   wages, benefits, payroll taxes, shift premiums

2. **indirect labor savings**  
   logistics, maintenance, planning, QA, internal transport, supervision

3. **fixed site overhead savings**  
   utilities, building costs, local services, security, depreciation/lease burden

4. **capacity rationalization**  
   less duplicated capability, higher utilization in remaining plants

If a non-closure package does not substantially reduce **all four**, it will usually fall short of closure-equivalent economics.

---

## What a non-closure package must achieve

To be credible, the package needs to close the gap through **durable recurring savings**, not one-off measures alone.

### 1) Headcount
A realistic closure-free target would usually require:
- **15%–20% fewer employees** in the relevant German footprint, or equivalent productivity uplift
- preferably concentrated in **indirect, overhead, and support functions first**
- with voluntary exits, hiring freezes, redeployment, retirement bridges, and internal transfers doing most of the work

If headcount reduction is materially below that range, the plan will likely need either:
- deeper wage concessions, or
- more severe capacity cuts, or
- a fallback to closure.

### 2) Capacity
A non-closure package usually needs:
- **10%–25% capacity reduction**
- by eliminating underutilized shifts, consolidating product lines, and cutting redundant line capability
- without necessarily closing buildings, but with real loss of productive throughput

If capacity falls only marginally, overhead dilution remains too high.

### 3) Wage / labor-cost contribution
A temporary wage freeze is usually not enough. For closure-like economics, you typically need one or more of:
- wage moderation or reduction
- reduction of variable pay / premiums
- longer working time for same pay, or lower pay for same time
- suspension of legacy guarantees / special protections
- tighter rules on overtime and temporary labor

This does **not** have to be permanent to matter, but it must be long enough — typically **2–3 years minimum** — to change the steady-state cost base.

### 4) Utilization
The package only works if the remaining network runs at materially higher utilization.

That means:
- fewer site-specific exceptions
- more product concentration
- less “keep everything everywhere” logic
- stronger volume allocation to the most efficient plants

If utilization stays weak, fixed cost per vehicle remains too high.

---

## What probably is not enough

A non-closure package is usually **not** closure-equivalent if it consists mainly of:
- voluntary exits only
- modest wage concessions
- short working-time measures
- small procurement savings
- no meaningful product reallocation
- no real reduction in indirect labor
- no measurable capacity loss

That kind of package may buy labor peace, but it is unlikely to close a structural cost gap at a large carmaker.

## Bounded scenario view

Without company-specific plant data, the right way to frame it is by scenario.

### Moderate non-closure scenario
- headcount reduction: **~8%–12%**
- capacity reduction: **~5%–10%**
- labor-cost relief: **temporary / partial**
- result: likely **insufficient** unless the initial gap is small

### Deep non-closure scenario
- headcount reduction: **~15%–20%**
- capacity reduction: **~10%–25%**
- recurring labor-cost relief: **material and multi-year**
- result: **potentially comparable** to closure economics if overhead is also reduced

### Closure scenario
- headcount/capacity reductions are usually deeper and faster
- savings are more immediate and structurally certain
- one-time costs are higher, and social/political cost is much higher

So the real question is not whether non-closure can save money — it can — but whether it can save **enough** and **durably**.

## Timing

A closure-free package generally takes longer to mature than closure:

- **0–12 months:** voluntary exits, shift changes, hiring freeze, working-time measures
- **12–24 months:** product reallocations, redeployment, indirect labor reduction
- **24–36 months:** steady-state savings fully visible

So the package must be designed to survive a **2–3 year implementation horizon**. If savings are needed immediately, closure or equivalent hard capacity cuts have an advantage.

1. **cost reduction comparable to closure**
2. **capacity reduction sufficient to restore utilization**
3. **structural, recurring savings not dependent on temporary concessions**

If any one of those is missing, the package is likely postponing the problem.
