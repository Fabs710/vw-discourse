As an independent restructuring and valuation matter, the key unresolved issue is **whether the proposed trust floor and domestic-plant protections can be financed within the company’s post-restructuring cash flow and enterprise value**.

## My neutral assessment

### 1) The trust cannot be evaluated by label alone
Economically, the retiree health trust is best analyzed as an **unsecured claim with a negotiated origin**, not as senior debt. That does **not** mean it should be treated identically to traded bond debt in all respects, but it also does not justify a materially superior recovery unless there is offsetting value to other unsecured classes.

The right test is:

- **present value of trust recovery**
- versus
- **present value of bondholder recovery**

using the **same enterprise value, same discount framework, and same insolvency risk assumptions**.

If the trust floor is large enough to consume value that would otherwise support bondholder recoveries or prevent re-leveraging, then it can undermine viability.

### 2) “Modest, capped, deferred floor” is economically plausible
From a restructuring perspective, an instrument with:

- a **small guaranteed floor**,
- **deferral**,
- **cap on upside**,
- and **performance-contingent payments**

is a standard way to preserve some value for a politically or industrially important claimant without making it functionally senior.

What matters is not the instrument’s name, but its **discounted expected value**.

A meaningful question is whether the floor is:
- low enough to be financeable,
- but high enough to be viewed as real by the union/trust.

If the floor is too high, it becomes quasi-senior and may reintroduce insolvency risk.

### 3) Bondholder “comparability” is a real issue
Bondholders are correct that they should not be left with a materially worse **economic** recovery than another unsecured constituency absent a clear offset. In restructuring analysis, “equal treatment” does not mean identical securities; it means **broadly comparable value** after adjusting for:

- timing,
- risk,
- subordination,
- liquidity,
- dilution,
- and contingent payment probability.

If the trust gets a guaranteed floor and bondholders get only speculative equity upside, the package may fail on comparability grounds unless the bondholders’ equity/warrant package is larger enough to offset that difference.

### 4) Domestic plant protections are valuable only if they are limited
Neighbouring governments’ domestic commitments can be compatible with viability if they are:

- **site-specific**,
- **time-limited**,
- **tied to investment and production minimums**,
- and limited to a **protected core**.

They become problematic if they freeze too much excess capacity. Every protected plant reduces the restructuring options available to management and can force a higher burden on creditors or retirees.

## What I would need to model this properly
To answer definitively, I would need:

1. **Current and projected EBITDA** under post-restructuring assumptions
2. **Capex and working capital needs**
3. **Plant-by-plant contribution margins**
4. **Cash cost savings from labor and footprint reductions**
5. **Liquidation value vs. going-concern value**
6. **Detailed terms of the trust instrument**
7. **Bondholder new-money / debt-for-equity terms**
8. **Equity stake and conditions attached to co-funder capital**

## Bottom line
From a valuation standpoint:

- A **modest, capped, deferred trust floor** can be financeable.
- A **large trust floor** can become economically senior in substance and jeopardize viability.
- Bondholders are entitled to insist that any trust recovery be **priced transparently** and not exceed comparable value.
- Domestic protection is acceptable only if it is **narrow enough not to destroy the footprint rationalization needed for solvency**.
