We agree on the need for a deep restructuring and on one basic point: the old balance sheet is broken. But we are not going to endorse a deal that uses “viability” as a cover for **preferential treatment of the retiree trust** or for **making dispersed unsecured bondholders absorb the worst of the pain**.

## Our position, plainly

We will support a restructuring only if it meets three conditions:

1. **Comparable economics across unsecured claims**
   - The retiree trust cannot receive materially better economic treatment than the bondholders.
   - Different instruments are fine.
   - Different labels are fine.
   - **Different economic value is not fine** unless it is justified by real offsetting consideration.

2. **Real, not cosmetic, restructuring**
   - The company must have a smaller footprint, lower fixed costs, and a capital structure it can actually carry.
   - We are not here to preserve every plant or job.
   - We are here to stop an avoidable wipe-out.

3. **No court outcome that leaves bondholders materially worse off than the trust**
   - If the out-of-court package is used to engineer a political hierarchy among unsecured creditors, we will not bless it.
   - If it goes to court, we will defend equal treatment hard.

## Where we agree

### To the Government Task Force
We agree that:
- token cuts are useless,
- the company needs finality,
- open-ended public support is unacceptable,
- and a court-supervised route is a legitimate fallback.

We also agree that the trust should not be “insulated” if that means the rest of the restructuring becomes impossible.

But your framing is still too convenient: you keep saying the trust is not sacred, which is true, but you are not applying the same skepticism to the idea that the bondholders should be the default sacrifice pool. We will not accept that either.

### To Management
We agree the company needs:
- meaningful plant rationalization,
- workforce reduction,
- brand/dealer simplification,
- and real debt relief.

What we do **not** accept is management asking creditors to underwrite a package where labour-history claims get protected more than traded unsecured claims, while telling bondholders to trust that “viability” justifies it.

### To the Union and Retiree Trust
We agree on one narrow point: the trust can be converted into a different instrument.

We do **not** agree that the trust’s history gives it a right to a better recovery than other unsecured claims. Negotiated origin does not equal economic seniority. If the trust is granted a floor, that floor must be priced honestly and must not come at the expense of bondholders’ comparable recovery.

### To the Neighbouring Governments
We agree co-funding can justify enforceable domestic commitments.

We do **not** agree that domestic commitments can become a veto over necessary closures or a reason to shift losses onto unsecured creditors. If your conditions make the plan unfinanceable, they are not rescue conditions.

## Where we disagree, sharply

### 1) The trust is not entitled to a special recovery
This is the core issue.

The union keeps saying the trust is different because it arose from concessions. We do not dispute the history. We dispute the conclusion.

This claim is still an unsecured obligation of the same distressed company. In insolvency terms, it does not magically outrank the bond debt because it was politically negotiated. If the trust receives a meaningful floor while bondholders get a materially lower present value, that is preferential treatment, full stop.

### 2) “Comparable economics” means comparable economics
The task force and management both talk about “economic equivalence,” but neither has put numbers on the table that prove it.

If the trust gets:
- a floor,
- upside participation,
- and downside protection,

then bondholders need value that is economically comparable after discounting for risk, timing, and dilution. Not “close enough.” Not “symbolically similar.” Comparable.

### 3) We will not accept being treated as the easy loss bucket
The fact that bondholders are dispersed and politically weaker does not make us the correct class to soak up everyone else’s preferred treatment.

If the deal is structured as:
- labour protected,
- trust protected,
- co-funders rewarded,
- bondholders cut hardest,

then the answer is no.

## On the experts
The experts have confirmed what we already suspected:
- the company needs a materially smaller footprint,
- a generous trust floor can reintroduce insolvency,
- and any value pool is limited.

That means the real choice is not between everyone getting what they want and nobody getting hurt. The choice is **who gets haircut first**. We are saying bondholders should not be structurally disadvantaged versus the trust.

If a trust floor is financeable, then its present value must be calibrated so the unsecured creditor package is not inferior in economic terms. If it is not financeable, then it should be reduced or eliminated — not financed by pushing bondholders deeper under water.

---

## What we would accept

We are prepared to consider:
- debt-for-equity conversion,
- warrants,
- a mix of new instruments,
- and a contingent value framework.

But the package must satisfy the following:

- **No materially better trust economics**
- **No token bondholder recovery**
- **No hidden preference through floors or valuation games**
- **No overlevered post-restructuring company**
- **No open-ended public subsidy**

A sensible starting point would be:
- bondholders and the trust both converted into equity-linked instruments,
- both subject to the same valuation framework,
- with any trust floor offset by a correspondingly stronger bondholder package if the economics differ.

---

## What we will not accept

We will not accept:
- a “durable” trust recovery that is durable because it is better than ours,
- a floor that functions like senior debt,
- a deal where bondholders are made to bear the disproportionate loss,
- or a court-supervised process used as leverage to ratify political favoritism.

If that is the direction, then we will take our chances in court rather than consent to a disguised expropriation.

---

## The concrete question that still needs answering
Before anyone talks about finality, we need actual numbers:

1. **What is the trust instrument worth in present-value terms?**
2. **What is the bondholder instrument worth in present-value terms?**
3. **What is the haircut range on each unsecured class?**
4. **What is the minimum viable footprint that supports those recoveries?**

Until those numbers are on the table, the talk of fairness is just rhetoric.
