We agree on the central point: **this company must emerge viable or it will not emerge at all**. I also agree that **management, labour, and creditors all have to share pain**. There is no credible path that preserves the old cost structure, and no one here should expect taxpayers to absorb the full loss while private claims are made whole.

That said, I need to be direct about what cannot happen from our side.

### Where we agree
- **Deep restructuring is unavoidable.**
- **Some plant, brand, and workforce rationalization is necessary.**
- **Unsecured creditors cannot expect near-par treatment.**
- **Management has already lost standing and must bear consequences.**
- **A court-supervised process is acceptable if it is the only way to get a final, viable deal.**

### Where I disagree
I do **not** accept any package that protects the retiree health obligation or the workforce at the expense of viability. The retiree trust was negotiated in a different era, but it is still a claim on a company that is now weeks from insolvency. It cannot be treated as untouchable if doing so makes the whole enterprise fail.

Likewise, I cannot support a deal that makes labour the main source of savings while leaving the balance sheet and the industrial footprint too large. We need real fixes:
- fewer brands if necessary,
- fewer plants if necessary,
- a dealer network sized to actual demand,
- and a capital structure that can survive without repeated public rescues.

### On the retiree trust
We are open to a **durable instrument**, including an equity-linked outcome, but only if it fits within a viable overall restructuring. The trust cannot be guaranteed a privileged position that blocks the balance-sheet reset. If the trust receives equity or a security, it must be part of a package that also gives the company room to live.

### On jobs and plants
I understand the concern about wholesale deindustrialization. That is not our aim. But we cannot confuse preserving every site with preserving the company. If we try to protect too much, we lose everything. The right standard is not “how much can we save unchanged,” but “what footprint allows this company to compete and survive.”

### On the process
I am not defending out-of-court agreement as a principle. I am defending **speed, certainty, and continuity of operations**. If consensus can produce that, fine. If not, then the court-supervised route must be used. What I will not support is delay that burns through the bridge financing while each stakeholder waits for someone else to blink.

### Bottom line
From management’s perspective, the non-negotiables are:
- **financing certainty,**
- **a real and final restructuring,**
- **protection of the consumer-facing franchise through the transition,**
- and **no outcome that tips this into uncontrolled liquidation.**

If the current positions are meant to hold every legacy claim close to par, then they are not consistent with survival. We need materially deeper concessions than that, from all parties.
