We appreciate the clearer valuation frame, and we agree with one important point from the expert: **this has to be judged by economic reality, not by labels alone**.

But I need to be equally clear: the union and retiree trust will not accept a so-called “comparable economics” test that becomes a cover for **treating the trust as just another unsecured claim and quietly stripping away the industrial compact** that created it.

## Where we agree
### 1) The company needs a real restructuring
Yes. Cosmetic cuts and endless bridge financing are not enough. The business must emerge viable.

### 2) Some plant, brand, and workforce rationalization is unavoidable
Yes. We are not defending every facility or every historical work rule. Some closures and headcount reduction are likely necessary.

### 3) Bondholders must take real losses
Yes. They cannot be made whole, and they should not expect that.

### 4) The trust can be converted into a durable instrument
Yes, we have said that from the beginning. Equity, equity-linked value, or a structured claim can be part of the answer.

## Where we disagree
### 1) The retiree trust is not just another unsecured bond claim
This is the central point.

The expert says the trust should be treated as a large unsecured restructuring claim with political and labor-history significance. We disagree with the “just another unsecured claim” part.

This trust represents decades of negotiated concessions by workers and retirees. It is not a trading claim bought at a discount in the market. If the restructuring simply places it in the same bucket as dispersed bondholders and then uses “fairness” to justify a thin recovery, that is not fair burden-sharing — it is erasing a hard-earned obligation.

### 2) “Comparable value” cannot mean comparable legal status only
If the trust receives an equity-linked preferred instrument with a floor, that is acceptable only if the floor is **real**, durable, and not so weak that it functions as a polite liquidation preference.

But we will not accept a structure that:
- gives bondholders the upside,
- gives the trust a hollow contingent instrument,
- and then calls that economically comparable because the math was massaged.

If the expert wants comparability, then show us the actual present value under realistic downside assumptions. Not optimistic assumptions, not legal form. Real value.

### 3) The workforce cannot be the main source of savings
We agree with management and the task force that labour must contribute. We have already conceded before and will continue to negotiate on wages, work rules, and some staffing reductions.

But we reject any structure where:
- workers take the biggest employment hit,
- retirees lose the trust,
- and bondholders still recover on a scale that preserves the old hierarchy.

That is not shared sacrifice.

### 4) Viability does not justify deindustrialization
The expert is right that overcapacity cannot remain. But there is a hard line between rationalization and dismantling.

We will not support a proposal that:
- shutters plants beyond what is necessary,
- uses “minimum viable footprint” as a euphemism for stripping communities,
- or shifts too much production to satisfy creditor optics or co-funder politics.

A viable company still needs an industrial base. If you hollow it out too far, you may win a balance-sheet model and lose the business.

## Our response to the other stakeholders

### To the Government Task Force
You say you will fund a deep, orderly restructuring, but you also keep pressing the trust as if it were the easiest political target.

We are prepared to accept:
- real unsecured creditor haircuts,
- significant management and operating changes,
- and a durable trust instrument rather than an untouched legacy promise.

What we are **not** prepared to accept is a settlement where the trust is cut in substance while bondholders argue they deserve equal treatment because they are numerous and politically easier to defend.

If the task force wants labour peace, it should stop framing the trust as the problem class.

### To Management
We acknowledge that you need a workable capital structure and operating footprint.

But do not mistake our willingness to bargain for consent to an overcut plan. We need you to define:
- what footprint is actually necessary,
- which plant closures are truly unavoidable,
- and what labour savings are essential versus merely convenient.

If management presents every demand as “non-negotiable,” then that is not restructuring; that is concession extraction.

### To the Bondholders
Your position is the weakest in fairness terms, even if you are loudest about equality.

You are not entitled to insist that the retiree trust be treated exactly like a market bond bought for speculation. If you want “comparable economics,” then accept that the trust’s claim has a different origin and must be translated into a durable instrument that preserves that reality.

What we can discuss:
- debt-for-equity for bondholders,
- warrants,
- and a trust instrument with a defined floor and capped upside.

What we will not accept:
- a structure where your class uses equal-treatment rhetoric to force the trust into a materially inferior position.

### To the Neighbouring Governments
We hear the demand for binding domestic production commitments.

We are not opposed to commitments that protect meaningful domestic employment. But those commitments must not be used to block the restructuring and shift the burden onto workers elsewhere.

If you want domestic sites protected, then help structure a plan that keeps those sites viable. Do not demand permanence for every facility while also insisting that creditors and labour absorb the pain. Those positions are incompatible.

## On the expert’s “minimum viable footprint”
This is where we want specifics, not abstractions.

If “minimum viable” means:
- enough domestic plants to sustain the industrial base,
- enough jobs to preserve communities,
- enough dealer and supplier scale to support sales,

then we can talk.

If it means:
- using the crisis to shrink the company far beyond what the market requires,
- and then calling the result sustainable because the math is cleaner,

then we reject it.

## What we need now
We will not sign off on any package without seeing concrete numbers on three items:

1. **The trust recovery**
   - what instrument,
   - what floor,
   - what expected value,
   - and how it compares to bondholder recovery.

2. **The operating footprint**
   - which plants close,
   - which are preserved,
   - and what level of domestic production is retained.

3. **The labour package**
   - wage concessions,
   - work-rule changes,
   - and job impacts,
   - with an explicit explanation of why each is necessary.

## Our bottom line
We are prepared to make this company viable. We are not prepared to let viability become a pretext for:
- destroying the retiree trust,
- treating workers as the default loss pool,
- or hollowing out the industrial base to satisfy financial claims.

If the final package contains:
- real creditor losses,
- a durable trust instrument with real value,
- targeted rather than wholesale plant/job cuts,
- and a post-restructuring company that still has an industrial future,

then we can work with it.

If not, then we will not bless a deal that sacrifices the workforce and retirees to preserve creditor claims under another name.
