We have enough clarity now to stop circling the same issues.

I’ll be blunt: **I do not accept any proposal that protects one unsecured claimant class at the expense of viability or that preserves too much footprint and debt to make the company self-supporting.** If the deal cannot clear that bar, I will push the court-supervised route.

## Where I agree
I agree with several points raised:

- **Management is right** that the company needs a real reset, not cosmetic cuts.
- **Labour is right** that workers and retirees should not be the only group taking losses.
- **Bondholders are right** that they should not be arbitrarily singled out.
- **Neighbouring governments are right** that any co-funding must buy enforceable commitments, not vague promises.

But agreement on those generalities does not settle the hard issues.

## Where I disagree

### 1) The retiree trust is not untouchable
I reject the idea that the retiree health trust must be preserved in a way that makes the rest of the restructuring less viable.

Yes, it arose from decades of concessions. That matters. But it does **not** entitle it to a protected position that leaves unsecured creditors absorbing the bulk of the pain while the underlying balance sheet remains too heavy.

So:
- I am open to an **equity-linked instrument** or other durable recovery for the trust.
- I am **not** open to a structure that makes the trust effectively senior in substance.
- I am **not** open to a “special industrial obligation” that comes at the expense of viability.

### 2) “Equal treatment” does not mean identical instruments, but it does mean comparable economics
Bondholders are correct on one point: if the trust gets a recovery that is materially better in economic terms, I will not call that fair burden-sharing.

That said, I will not accept the opposite extreme either: a bondholder recovery that is dressed up as discipline while the trust is quietly stripped to nothing.

The acceptable standard is this:
- different instruments are fine,
- **materially comparable economics** are required,
- and the total package must produce a viable company.

### 3) Domestic plant protection cannot freeze the restructuring
To the neighbouring governments: I understand why you want domestic commitments. But I will not fund a deal that hardwires an inefficient footprint.

If your co-funding is necessary, then it must support:
- a **minimum viable domestic footprint**,
- not preservation of every site,
- not a veto over necessary closures,
- and not a shift of cost onto creditors or the public purse simply to protect local optics.

You are entitled to conditions. You are not entitled to conditions that make the rescue unworkable.

## What the task force requires
Let me state the requirements plainly.

### Required
- **Deep unsecured creditor losses**
- **Meaningful labour concessions**
- **A real restructuring of the retiree trust obligation**
- **Material footprint rationalization**
- **No open-ended public subsidy**
- **A final capital structure that can stand on its own**

### Not acceptable
- Token creditor losses
- Preserving too many plants, brands, or jobs to satisfy politics
- A trust structure that is effectively insulated from sacrifice
- Co-funding tied to immovable domestic commitments that break viability
- Any deal that merely delays collapse

## On the proposed “tiered recovery” idea
The moderator’s compromise structure is not unreasonable as a drafting concept, but the economics must be real.

I would consider:
- bondholders receiving debt-for-equity plus warrants,
- the trust receiving an equity-linked preferred instrument with a floor,
- an independent valuation process,
- and a clear showing that the recoveries are economically comparable.

But I will not sign off on a valuation exercise that is used to disguise preferential treatment or inflate one constituency’s recovery while pretending symmetry.

## What I need answered now
I want actual numbers, not slogans.

### To management
Give me:
- the minimum viable footprint,
- the plant closures you say are unavoidable,
- the workforce reduction range,
- and the post-restructuring debt load the business can support.

If you cannot define that, then your “viability” argument is not serious enough.

### To bondholders
State plainly:
- what haircut range you would accept,
- whether you will accept debt-for-equity and warrants,
- and what recovery you would regard as economically comparable if the trust gets an equity-linked instrument.

If you are really prepared for a restructuring, then put a range on it.

### To the union and trust
State whether you will accept:
- a reduced but durable recovery,
- equity-linked value rather than a guaranteed senior claim,
- and plant/work-rule changes tied to viability.

If your answer is no, then say so directly. But do not then claim to support a serious restructuring.

### To neighbouring governments
State the specific commitments you need:
- minimum production levels,
- protected sites,
- and any investment commitments.

If those requirements exceed what a viable restructuring can bear, then say whether you are prepared to scale back your funding or withdraw it.

## My line in the sand
I will fund a **fast, orderly, deep restructuring** that leaves the company viable and shares pain across all private claimants.

I will **not** fund:
- a delay,
- a politically padded compromise,
- or a deal that preserves too much for any constituency and leaves taxpayers carrying the structural problem.

If the parties cannot narrow this in concrete terms, then we move toward the court-supervised process. That is not a bluff. It is the fallback already on the table.
