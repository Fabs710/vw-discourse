We agree on the central point: **the company needs a deep, immediate restructuring to survive**. We also agree that the old capital structure and operating footprint cannot be preserved intact. On those points, I think there is real common ground.

But I need to be equally clear about what I **do not** accept.

## To the Government Task Force
I agree that token cuts are useless and that the company must emerge self-supporting. I also agree that a court-supervised process is acceptable if it is the only way to get finality.

Where I disagree is with any approach that treats **viability** as interchangeable with **maximum contraction**. If the package cuts too deeply into brands, plants, dealers, and consumer confidence, then we may “complete” a restructuring and still lose the company in the market.

So my line is this:
- I support deep cuts.
- I do **not** support cuts that destroy the core franchise.
- I support an equity-linked trust solution only if it does not re-create an overlevered company.

## To the Union and Retiree Trust
I recognize the history of the trust and the concessions behind it. I do not dismiss that. But I cannot support any structure that effectively makes the trust senior in substance if that leaves the company unfinanceable.

What I can support is:
- a **durable** trust recovery,
- likely in an **equity-linked instrument**,
- with real value and a real floor,
- but not a floor so large that it crowds out the recovery needed by the rest of the business to survive.

I also agree that workers should not be the only ones paying for the rescue. But labour cannot be insulated from the operating changes the company needs:
- workforce reduction,
- work-rule reform,
- and plant rationalization where capacity is clearly excess to demand.

That is not anti-worker. It is the cost of keeping a company alive.

## To Bondholders
You are right on one crucial point: **equal treatment matters economically**. We cannot simply give the trust a meaningfully better recovery and call it fair.

I am prepared to support:
- meaningful unsecured creditor haircuts,
- debt-for-equity,
- warrants,
- and other contingent value instruments.

What I am **not** prepared to support is a deal where bondholders are cut deeply while the company is forced to preserve too much capacity and too much legacy obligation elsewhere. That would just shift insolvency forward.

The right test is not identical paper. It is **comparable economics**, measured on a realistic basis. If the trust gets a floor, then bondholders need a recovery structure that is economically coherent in light of that floor. I agree with that principle.

## To the Neighbouring Governments
I understand why you want binding domestic commitments if you are co-funding. That is reasonable. But I cannot support commitments that freeze an inefficient footprint.

If your funding is conditional on protecting every domestic site, then the rescue may fail. What we can support is:
- a **minimum viable domestic footprint**,
- protected strategic sites where they make sense,
- and enforceable production or investment commitments that are compatible with a viable enterprise.

In short: yes to commitments, no to a veto over necessary restructuring.

## Where I stand on the specialists’ findings
The experts’ assessments are broadly consistent with what we see operationally:

- We need a **materially smaller footprint**.
- We need a **meaningful workforce reset**.
- We need **real creditor losses**.
- A **large trust floor** risks reintroducing insolvency unless offset by deeper cuts or new outside money.

That last point matters. If the trust floor is too generous, then something else has to give:
- deeper plant closures,
- steeper creditor haircuts,
- or more external funding.

There is no free lunch here.

## What I can support
A workable package would have to include:

1. **A minimum viable operating footprint**
   - preserve the strongest brands and the most important plants/dealers,
   - exit or consolidate clearly excess capacity.

2. **Meaningful but bounded labour concessions**
   - staffing reductions,
   - work-rule changes,
   - and benefit restructuring tied to viability.

3. **A real restructuring of unsecured claims**
   - bondholders take major haircuts,
   - receive debt-for-equity and warrants,
   - no expectation of being made whole.

4. **A durable, reduced retiree trust instrument**
   - equity-linked,
   - with a floor,
   - but not so large that it destabilizes the balance sheet.

5. **No open-ended public subsidy**
   - only bridge financing tied to milestones and finality.

6. **Either a consensual deal or a court-supervised process**
   - whichever gets to a final, financeable outcome in time.

## What I cannot accept
I want to be explicit. I cannot support:
- an outcome that keeps too many plants and brands open to satisfy politics,
- a trust recovery that is effectively senior in all but name,
- or a creditor package that leaves the enterprise too weak to compete.

And I cannot support any result that risks an uncontrolled collapse. That is the one outcome that is fundamentally unacceptable.

## Bottom line
Management is prepared to work toward a **deep but viable** restructuring. We will not defend the old plan. But we will also not accept a settlement that destroys the franchise in the name of fairness.

If the parties want a serious next step, I think we need the following concrete items now:
- a **minimum viable footprint** proposal,
- a **trust instrument with a valuation floor and capped upside**,
- a **bondholder recovery range** in debt/equity/warrant form,
- and a clear statement on which plants, brands, and dealers are essential to preserving the going concern.

If useful, I can also help draft a **single term-sheet framework** around those points.
