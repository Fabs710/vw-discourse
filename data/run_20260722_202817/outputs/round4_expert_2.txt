Yes — on the labour/restructuring side, the key issue is whether the proposed trust floor and domestic plant protections fit inside the company’s **post-restructuring cash flow** and **enterprise value** without forcing a return to insolvency.

## Neutral assessment

### 1) Minimum viable footprint
Based on the scenario, the company does **not** need its pre-crisis footprint to survive. It needs a **smaller core network** consisting of:

- a limited number of high-output assembly plants,
- the minimum engine/powertrain or component support needed for that core,
- a rationalized dealer network,
- and a reduced brand portfolio.

In practical restructuring terms, viability usually requires:
- closing or consolidating clearly excess plants,
- eliminating duplicate capacity,
- and reducing fixed labour cost enough that the post-restructuring company can operate at stressed demand without immediate cash burn.

If too many plants are protected for political reasons, the company’s fixed cost base stays too high and the rescue becomes unstable.

### 2) Workforce footprint
The workforce reduction has to track the footprint reduction. If the plant count comes down materially, headcount must come down as well. Otherwise, the company preserves nominal capacity but not economic viability.

Typical restructuring logic is:
- operating staff reduced to match the smaller footprint,
- salaried overhead cut aggressively,
- and labour concessions used to lower cash cost, not just defer it.

A deal that protects most jobs while also requiring the trust floor and creditor recoveries is usually not financeable.

### 3) Retiree trust floor
The trust can be given a **modest, capped, deferred floor** and still be financeable.

But the hard constraint is this:

- if the trust floor is too large,
- or too certain,
- or too front-loaded,

then it becomes economically senior in substance, even if not in legal form. That would reduce the value available to bondholders and can re-trigger insolvency by making the post-restructuring capital structure too weak.

So the question is not whether a floor is possible. It is whether the floor is **small enough to fit inside the value pool** after:
- plant rationalization,
- workforce cuts,
- and debt reduction.

### 4) Comparable economics
The bondholders’ demand for comparable treatment is economically valid. Different instruments are fine, but the **present value** of the trust recovery should not be materially above the bondholder recovery unless there is an offsetting justification.

The workable structure is usually:
- bondholders: debt-for-equity plus warrants,
- trust: equity-linked preferred instrument with a capped floor,
- labour: wage/benefit/work-rule concessions,
- co-funders: fresh capital for equity.

That can work only if the trust floor is **modest enough** that the bondholder package remains broadly comparable in value.

### 5) Domestic plant protections
Protected domestic sites can be financed only if they are limited to a **small core** and paired with deeper cuts elsewhere. If domestic commitments prevent the closure of excess capacity, they shift the burden back onto creditors and labour and may make the company unfinanceable.

So the test is:

- Are the protected domestic plants part of the minimum viable footprint?
- Or are they additional capacity preserved for political reasons?

If it’s the latter, the restructuring likely breaks.

## Bottom line
From a restructuring feasibility standpoint:

- **Yes**, a modest capped trust floor can be financed.
- **No**, a large or senior-like trust floor is usually not financeable.
- **Yes**, some protected domestic plants can be retained.
- **No**, not if they prevent material footprint rationalization.
- **Yes**, the company can survive with a smaller workforce.
- **No**, not if labour cuts are too shallow to match the reduced plant base.
