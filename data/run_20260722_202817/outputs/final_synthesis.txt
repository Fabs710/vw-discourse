## 1. Decision context

A major carmaker is weeks from failure amid collapsing sales and frozen credit, with spillover risk to suppliers and the domestic industrial base. The executive task force controlling bridge financing has already rejected management’s first plan, replaced leadership, and set a sixty-day window for a far deeper restructuring. The central decision is how to allocate losses among unsecured bondholders, the retiree health trust, labour, public funders, and domestic plant stakeholders, while restoring a capital structure the business can survive.

## 2. Stakeholder assembly

- **Government Task Force (lead funder / decisive leverage):** highest standing because it controls financing and can force a court-supervised process.
- **Company Management:** newly installed leadership tasked with producing a viable plan.
- **Industrial Union & Retiree Health Trust:** high standing due to labour cooperation and the trust’s negotiated origin.
- **Unsecured Bondholders’ Committee:** large economic exposure, but weaker political standing because claims are dispersed.
- **Neighbouring Governments (co-funders):** conditional standing, significant only if they contribute fresh capital tied to domestic jobs.
- **Independent experts:** advisory, not decision-making, but influential in identifying feasibility limits.

## 3. Final positions

### Government Task Force
The task force will support a rescue only if it produces a viable post-restructuring company, material creditor losses, meaningful labour and retiree concessions, and a materially smaller footprint. It rejects any trust treatment that functions like senior debt, any footprint preservation driven mainly by politics, and any open-ended public subsidy.

### Company Management
Management supports deep restructuring, including major plant, brand, dealer, and workforce reductions, but insists the post-deal company must not be overlevered. It is willing to use an equity-linked trust instrument with a modest capped floor, but would rather go to court than accept a politically balanced yet financially fragile deal.

### Industrial Union & Retiree Health Trust
The union accepts that the company needs a real restructuring and that workers must make concessions, but refuses to let the trust be treated as a disposable unsecured claim. It will consider an equity-linked structure only if the recovery is meaningful and durable, not tokenistic, and if labour is not singled out as the main adjustment mechanism.

### Unsecured Bondholders’ Committee
Bondholders will accept major losses and debt-for-equity conversion, but only if the trust does not receive materially better economic treatment. They insist on comparable economics across unsecured claims and reject any trust floor that becomes senior in substance or leaves bondholders as the default loss bucket.

### Neighbouring Governments
The co-funders want protected domestic sites, binding production and employment commitments, and a proportionate equity stake in return for fresh capital. They will not support a deal that shifts industrial pain to their jurisdictions while protecting other plants or giving them only symbolic ownership.

### Independent experts
The experts consistently conclude that a modest, capped, deferred trust floor can be financeable, but a large floor risks reintroducing insolvency. They also find that protected domestic sites are feasible only if they are tightly limited and paired with deeper closures and workforce cuts elsewhere.

## 4. Genuine conflicts (structurally incompatible positions)

1. **Trust recovery vs. bondholder parity**
   - The union wants a real, durable trust recovery reflecting negotiated history.
   - Bondholders and the task force want the trust treated as economically comparable to other unsecured claims, not better.
   - These positions clash whenever “special history” is used to justify a higher present-value recovery.

2. **Footprint reduction vs. domestic protection**
   - Management, the task force, and experts require substantial rationalization.
   - Neighbouring governments want domestic plant protections and jobs.
   - These goals are incompatible if the protected core becomes too large to support solvency.

3. **Loss allocation vs. political legitimacy**
   - Everyone accepts losses are necessary, but each constituency seeks to avoid being the primary loss absorber.
   - The task force has the leverage to impose a harsher court route, but labour and bondholders each have meaningful consent power.
   - The deal fails if any side believes sacrifice is asymmetrical and avoidable.

## 5. Areas of convergence

- The company needs a **deep, final restructuring**, not a cosmetic fix.
- **Management must be changed and costs cut materially**.
- **Unsecured creditors will not be made whole**.
- A **court-supervised route is acceptable as a fallback** if consensus fails.
- A **different instrument** for the trust is acceptable in principle if its economics are transparent.
- A **protected domestic core** is possible if narrowly defined.
- **Fresh co-funding should buy real commitments and real equity**, not a symbolic role.
- All parties agree that **speed and finality** matter.

## 6. Key trade-offs for the decision-maker

- **Financeability vs. fairness:** a trust floor that is meaningful to labour may be too rich for bondholders and too costly for solvency.
- **Industrial preservation vs. restructuring depth:** protecting more plants preserves jobs but reduces the company’s ability to reset its fixed-cost base.
- **Consensus vs. coercion:** an out-of-court deal may preserve relationships, but the court route may be necessary to break deadlock.
- **National vs. regional interests:** neighbouring governments can provide stabilizing capital, but only if domestic commitments do not destroy the turnaround.
- **Short-term legitimacy vs. long-term viability:** a politically palatable deal can still fail if it recreates overleverage.

## 7. Integrative pathways

1. **Tiered trust instrument**
   - Give the retiree trust a modest guaranteed floor, deferred and capped, plus contingent upside tied to performance.
   - This preserves some special value without making the trust senior in substance.

2. **Parity-based unsecured value test**
   - Use one valuation framework for both the trust and bondholders.
   - Different instruments are allowed, but present-value recoveries should fall within a defined band unless offset by explicit consideration.

3. **Protected domestic core with aggressive non-core rationalization**
   - Identify a small set of strategic domestic plants for preservation.
   - Close or consolidate non-core capacity more aggressively to preserve solvency.

4. **Proportionate co-funder equity**
   - Give neighbouring governments equity tied to fresh capital, but only if they accept that their protected footprint must remain financeable.
   - This aligns political support with economic risk.

5. **Court-supervised fallback as discipline**
   - Keep the court route credible to force compromise.
   - Use the threat of a harsher imposed outcome to push both labour and bondholders toward a financeable middle ground.

## 8. Open questions

- What is the **maximum present-value recovery** the task force will tolerate for the trust?
- What **bondholder recovery range** is acceptable if the trust gets a capped floor?
- Which plants are part of the **minimum viable domestic footprint**?
- What is the **minimum workforce reduction** consistent with that footprint?
- What equity stake do co-funders require, and how much domestic commitment is enough to justify it?
- Can the trust floor be set low enough to be meaningful yet not trigger renewed insolvency?
- If consensus fails, what is the likely **court outcome** on comparative treatment?

## 9. Recommended next step

Require each stakeholder to submit a **numbered term sheet** within a fixed deadline:
- trust floor size and payment timing,
- bondholder haircut and equity/warrant package,
- plant closure/consolidation list,
- workforce reduction range,
- domestic production commitments,
- co-funder equity request,
- and an explicit “go/no-go” on the court-supervised route.

The task force should then test these numbers against a single independent viability model.

## 10. Emergent insights that only appeared through multi-turn interaction

- The dispute is no longer about whether restructuring is needed; it has narrowed to **where the value floor sits** and **who gets the first meaningful recovery**.
- “Special treatment” for the trust is politically central for labour, but economically acceptable only if it is **priced transparently** and does not become senior in substance.
- The most important feasibility constraint is not the legal label of the claim, but the **discounted present value** of the full package.
- Neighbouring governments are not just side participants; their support becomes decisive only if they accept a **protected core instead of blanket preservation**.
- The task force’s repeated push for thresholds, not principles, shows that the real bargaining unit is now **a viability-tested allocation formula**, not a narrative of fairness.
- The likely viable compromise is not equal treatment in form, but **broadly comparable economics across unsecured constituencies with different instruments**.
