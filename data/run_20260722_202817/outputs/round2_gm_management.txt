We agree on the central objective: **the company must survive as a going concern**, and it must emerge with a cost structure and balance sheet that can actually support operations without repeated public rescues. If we lose that, everything else becomes irrelevant.

But I need to be equally direct about where I agree, where I do not, and what I think is being understated.

### Where I agree
- **There must be deep restructuring.**
- **Management must bear consequences.**
- **Unsecured creditors cannot be made whole.**
- **Some plant, brand, dealer, and workforce rationalization is unavoidable.**
- **A court-supervised process is acceptable if it is the only path to finality.**

Those are no longer abstract points for us. We have accepted that the old plan failed and that survival may require much harder decisions than management previously endorsed.

### Where I disagree
#### 1) The retiree health trust cannot be protected in a way that blocks viability
I respect the history behind the trust and the concessions workers and retirees made over decades. But we cannot treat that obligation as untouchable if doing so prevents the company from surviving.

If the trust is preserved in full while unsecured debt is also expected to take major cuts, then the balance sheet may still be too heavy and the company may not be financeable. That is not acceptable to us.

What I can support is a **durable but reduced recovery** for the trust, likely through an equity-linked instrument or a structured claim tied to the restructured company’s performance. What I cannot support is a solution that leaves the trust effectively senior in practice while asking bondholders and the company to absorb the full burden.

#### 2) Labour cannot be the main source of savings, but labour also cannot be insulated from real change
I agree that workers should not carry the entire rescue. But the company’s fixed cost base is too high, and we need genuine operating changes:
- work-rule reform,
- headcount reduction,
- plant closures where capacity exceeds demand,
- and a dealer network aligned with market reality.

That is not a judgment on the workforce’s value. It is a survival requirement. If we avoid those decisions to preserve appearances, we destroy the whole enterprise.

#### 3) “Equal treatment” does not mean everyone gets the same instrument or the same optics
I hear the bondholders’ complaint, but we cannot let “equal treatment” become a veto on restructuring. Different claims can be treated differently if the overall package is economically coherent. The issue is not whether the trust looks better on paper; the issue is whether the entire capital structure becomes viable.

If we need debt-for-equity, warrants, new instruments, or a combination, that is fine. What matters is the final result: a company with a realistic debt load and a workable operating footprint.

#### 4) Domestic plant protection cannot override viability
To the neighbouring governments: I understand why you want domestic production commitments if you are co-funding. But those commitments must fit within an actual restructuring. If the deal protects every domestic site regardless of economics, then the company may not survive. A rescue that preserves all footprints is not a rescue.

We are open to binding commitments on core domestic production and employment where they are compatible with viability. We are not open to a framework that prevents the necessary rationalization.

### What we need to keep intact
This is the part I will not compromise away:

- **Consumer confidence**
- **Warranty continuity**
- **Dealer network continuity through the transition**
- **Enough brand strength to preserve the franchise**
- **A final financing package, not temporary patches**

If the restructuring damages those elements too severely, then even a technically completed deal could fail in the market. That is why I am pressing for a plan that is hard, but not self-defeating.

### What a workable middle ground looks like
Not consensus for its own sake, but a package that may actually close:

- **Meaningful unsecured creditor haircut** with debt-for-equity or warrants
- **Meaningful but reduced recovery for the retiree trust**, likely through a durable equity-linked instrument
- **Real workforce and plant rationalization**, but targeted to viability rather than indiscriminate dismantling
- **No open-ended public support**
- **Any co-funding tied to enforceable operating commitments**
- **Post-restructuring ownership allocated to those who provide value and absorb losses**, not to preserve legacy positions

### Bottom line
If the proposals on the table are trying to preserve the trust substantially intact, preserve too much of the footprint, and ask taxpayers or bondholders to absorb the imbalance, then I cannot support them. That would not save the company.

If, however, we are dealing with a genuine reset that protects the core consumer franchise while cutting enough cost and debt to make the business viable, then management is prepared to work through either a consensual settlement or a court-supervised process.

I would also say this plainly: **without financing certainty and a credible operating footprint, there is no rescue.** That is the line we are working from.
