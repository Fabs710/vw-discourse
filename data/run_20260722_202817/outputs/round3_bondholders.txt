We agree on one thing: the company needs a **real** restructuring, not another bridge loan that just postpones the reckoning.

But let me be equally clear: **we will not consent to a deal that makes unsecured bondholders the default loss bucket while the retiree trust is treated more favourably.** That is the central issue, and none of the other parties has answered it convincingly.

## To the Government Task Force
You keep saying “fair burden-sharing,” but what you are actually proposing sounds like **politically managed burden-sharing**:
- creditors take the deepest cuts,
- labour is protected because it is politically sensitive,
- and the trust is given a more durable or better-supported recovery because it is harder to explain away.

That is not equality, and it is not acceptable to us.

We accept:
- deep restructuring,
- management consequences,
- meaningful operating cuts,
- and even a court-supervised process if that is what it takes to get finality.

What we do **not** accept is a structure where the trust receives a materially better economic recovery than the bondholders without genuine compensating value. If the trust is getting an equity-linked instrument with a floor, then bondholders must receive a **comparable economic package**, not a token haircut and hollow upside.

## To the Union and Retiree Trust
We do not dispute the history of the trust or the sacrifices workers and retirees made. But that does **not** make it a sacred claim that outranks other unsecured creditors.

It is still an unsecured obligation of the same distressed company.

If you want to argue for a distinct instrument, then fine — but the economics must be comparable. What we will not accept is:
- the trust getting a floor,
- the trust getting protected upside,
- and bondholders being told to swallow the bigger loss because we are dispersed and easier to pressure.

That is not a restructuring; that is preferential treatment dressed up as fairness.

## To Management
You are right that the company needs:
- a smaller footprint,
- lower fixed costs,
- and a post-restructuring capital structure that can actually survive.

But you have not yet given the numbers that matter.

We need to know:
- the exact unsecured haircut range,
- the value of the trust instrument,
- the equity split,
- and the minimum enterprise value after restructuring.

Without that, “viability” is just a slogan that can be used to justify whatever loss allocation you want.

And let me be blunt: if management’s solution is to preserve too much of the trust, too much of the footprint, or too much political optics, then bondholders will not be the only ones at risk. The whole rescue will remain fragile.

## To the Neighbouring Governments
We understand why you want domestic production commitments if you are co-funding.

But you do **not** get to use public money to freeze an uneconomic footprint and then expect unsecured creditors to fund the gap. If your conditions require preserving domestic sites beyond what the business can support, then your funding is not rescue financing — it is industrial policy at creditor expense.

We are willing to consider domestic commitments only if they are compatible with a viable company. Not every plant can be protected. Not every job can be preserved. That is the reality of an insolvent restructuring.

## On the expert’s “comparability” framework
We are open to comparing recoveries on a present-value basis, but only if the comparison is real and transparent.

That means:
- not just legal form,
- not just labels,
- but actual economic value after discounting risk, timing, dilution, and downside.

If the trust gets a floor and the bondholders get a mix of new paper and equity, then the **bondholder package must not be materially worse in expected value**. If it is, then the claim of comparability fails.

And I want to stress something else: a “floor” for the trust is not free. If the trust gets one, then something else has to absorb the cost — and it should not automatically be the dispersed bondholders.

## Our position
We can consider:
- debt-for-equity,
- warrants,
- equity-linked instruments,
- and a court-supervised solution.

But we cannot accept:
- a de facto wipeout,
- a token recovery,
- or a structure where the trust is economically favored over bondholders.

## What we want next
If the parties are serious, put actual numbers on the table:

1. **Bondholder recovery range**
   - debt haircut,
   - new equity,
   - warrants,
   - any cash component.

2. **Trust recovery range**
   - instrument,
   - floor,
   - upside,
   - and present value.

3. **Footprint cuts**
   - plant closures,
   - workforce reduction,
   - dealer rationalization.

4. **Ownership**
   - who gets equity,
   - in what proportion,
   - and on what basis.

## Bottom line
We are not here to block a rescue. We are here to stop a rescue from becoming a **politically convenient expropriation of unsecured bondholders**.

If the final package delivers:
- deep but viable restructuring,
- comparable economics across unsecured claims,
- and a real path to self-sufficiency,

then we can engage seriously.

If instead the package is:
- labour protected,
- trust favored,
- bondholders cut hardest,
- and public money used to justify it,

then our answer is no.
