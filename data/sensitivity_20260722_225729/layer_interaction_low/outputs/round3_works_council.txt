I will be equally direct.

The discussion is now being pushed toward a false binary: either the workforce accepts closures and forced layoffs, or it is accused of blocking competitiveness. I reject that framing.

### Where I agree
I agree with everyone on one point: **Volkswagen needs a durable industrial and investment plan**.  
I also agree that temporary patches are not enough, and that the package must be **quantified**.

### Where I disagree
I do **not** accept that German plant closures are a necessary condition for competitiveness.  
I do **not** accept compulsory redundancies.  
I do **not** accept any “review mechanism” that quietly preserves the right to close sites later after the workforce has already given up concessions.  
And I do **not** accept that the historic job-security framework should be treated as the obstacle, when the real issue is management’s failure to present a serious alternative.

### To management
You say you need structural savings. Fine. Then show them **without closures and without forced layoffs**.

Right now, what I hear is:
- you want the workforce to pay first,
- you want to keep closure leverage in reserve,
- and you want the social settlement weakened before you commit to anything durable.

That is not a fair basis for negotiation. It is a pressure tactic.

If your “strongest plausible no-closure package” still depends on threatening sites, then it is not a no-closure package. It is a staged closure strategy.

### To the controlling owners and investors
You keep saying the package must be durable and value-preserving. I do not disagree with the need for durability. But durability is not only about cost-cutting. It is also about **trust, industrial peace, and long-term site security**.

A settlement that blows up co-determination, destroys confidence, and triggers conflict across German plants is not durable. It is unstable and expensive.

If you want real savings, then contribute in a real way:
- binding investment commitments,
- clear product allocation,
- management restraint,
- and a serious willingness to reduce costs at the top as well.

### To Lower Saxony
I welcome your rejection of closures. But support must be more than moral sympathy. If you are serious about preserving the German industrial base, then use your governance position to back:
- binding site guarantees,
- no operational layoffs,
- and a restructuring based on redeployment, voluntary measures, and capacity adjustment **within existing sites**.

### On the moderator’s compromise options
I need to be clear: the first option — “no closure during the agreement term” — is closer to a basis for discussion, but only if it is **binding** and not a trapdoor.

What I will not accept is a “no closure during term” formula that:
- weakens guarantees now,
- allows hidden pre-closure preparation,
- or turns the agreement into a countdown to site loss.

The second option is not enough unless the guarantees are real.  
The third option still smells like site rationalization by another name if it results in permanent shrinking without secure jobs.

### On the expert point
Yes, we need quantified data. But I want to be equally clear:  
**The burden of proving that closures are unavoidable lies with management, not with the workforce.**

You cannot simply assert that the no-closure path is insufficient and then ask the workforce to accept the closure logic by default.

[SUMMON: independent plant-level capacity and cost auditor to quantify site fixed costs, utilization, and savings potential from capacity reduction versus closure, with plant-by-plant comparisons.]

### What would move me
Only a package with all of the following:
- **binding no-closure commitment**
- **no compulsory redundancies**
- **preservation of co-determination and job-security rules**
- **real investment and product commitments**
- **verifiable savings through working-time, voluntary exits, redeployment, and capacity measures**
- **no hidden closure option disguised as a future review**

If that can be done, then there is something to discuss.  
If it cannot, then management is not presenting a solution — it is presenting a demand for surrender.

So my position remains unchanged:  
**Keep every German plant open. No forced layoffs. Preserve the workforce framework.**
