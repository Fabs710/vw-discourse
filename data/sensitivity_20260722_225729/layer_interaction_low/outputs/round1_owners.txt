I agree with one central point from management: Volkswagen cannot preserve value by simply freezing the current cost structure and hoping the market improves. That would be irresponsible. The company does need a durable competitiveness reset, and the shareholder side recognizes that long-term value erosion is the real threat here.

But I do **not** accept the premise that plant closures must be the default path, or that structural adjustment has to mean a public break with the workforce and the regions.

To be clear:

- **A viable restructuring must improve margins and reduce fixed costs materially.**
- **It must also preserve industrial peace and implementation capability.**
- **And it must not undermine the long-term value of the core enterprise by triggering a prolonged conflict.**

That means I am open to serious structural measures, including capacity reductions, working-time flexibility, labor-cost moderation, and voluntary workforce reduction. I am **not** persuaded by a position that treats “no closures, no forced redundancies” as an obstacle rather than as a constraint that any workable governance solution has to address.

At the same time, I have a clear disagreement with the employee side and with Lower Saxony if the position is that the existing structure can remain essentially intact while the company somehow recovers competitiveness through marginal concessions. That is not credible. If the economics require a lower cost base, then the burden cannot be symbolic.

So from the shareholder perspective, the test is simple:

- **Does the package deliver durable savings?**
- **Does it protect Volkswagen’s long-term competitiveness and investment capacity?**
- **Does it avoid destabilizing the company through an unnecessary open-ended conflict?**

If the answer is yes, then there is room for a settlement. If the answer is no, then I would not support a package that merely preserves appearances while the enterprise continues to underperform.

I would therefore encourage management to come back with a sharper, quantified proposal that shows exactly how the gap is closed — by site, by function, by timing, and by cost impact — and not just broad language about “shared burden.” The shareholder interest is not in theatrics; it is in a credible, durable turnaround.
