I will respond very clearly, because the situation is too serious for vague language.

### Where I agree
I agree with the Works Council on the essentials:

- **A credible industrial plan is necessary.**
- **Temporary measures alone are not enough.**
- **Investment and future products must be part of any settlement.**
- **A negotiated solution is the only viable route.**

I also agree with Lower Saxony that a destructive confrontation would damage the company and the regions badly. And I agree with the point that a cosmetic deal would be useless.

### Where I disagree — and this is the critical part
I do **not** accept a restructuring logic that keeps **plant closures** and **forced redundancies** as normal options on the table while expecting the workforce to hand over concessions first.

That is not a balanced starting point. It is a threat-based approach.

For me, the red lines remain:

- **No compulsory redundancies**
- **No German plant closures**
- **No dismantling of the collective wage framework**
- **No settlement where employees carry the main burden while management and owners remain largely untouched**

If management says the current structure is too expensive, then the answer cannot simply be: “close sites or accept layoffs.” That would violate the principle of fair burden-sharing and would trigger a conflict the company does not need.

### On management’s position
Management keeps saying “durable savings” require structural measures. On that broad point, yes — but **structural does not automatically mean closures**.

There are other structural measures that must be examined seriously first:

- working-time reductions and flexibility,
- capacity adjustments without closures,
- voluntary programs and internal redeployment,
- temporary or staged labour-cost moderation,
- limits on bonuses and other variable pay,
- negotiated contributions from the workforce **matched** by contributions from ownership and management.

What I do **not** accept is the idea that “durable” only counts if it comes from job destruction or site closures. That is too narrow, and it ignores the industrial and social damage that such a path would cause.

### On the Works Council
I appreciate the clear stance in favour of job security and site protection. But I want to say this honestly: if the position becomes “every site must stay open unchanged under all circumstances,” then we risk boxing ourselves into a slogan instead of a solution.

We need to be precise.

- I support **site security** and **no forced redundancies**.
- I support **negotiated capacity adjustment**.
- I support **fair employee contributions**.
- I do **not** support a deal that simply shifts the problem into future uncertainty or prepares closures through the back door.

So yes, there is room for adjustment — but only if it genuinely protects jobs and preserves the collective framework.

### On Lower Saxony, owners, and investors
I hear the argument that the company needs a credible margin path and that capital will not tolerate endless delay. That is understandable.

But I reject the implication that credibility requires the workforce to absorb the pain while others merely demand “discipline.” If owners and management want acceptance for a restructuring, they must show their own contribution in concrete terms:

- real investment commitments,
- a credible industrial roadmap,
- management restraint,
- and a willingness to avoid shortcuts that destroy trust.

A package that asks employees to accept wage moderation, flexibility, and job insecurity while keeping closures on the table is **not** a fair package.

### What would move us
What could move the union is a **binding, quantified, shared-sacrifice package** with these elements:

1. **No compulsory redundancies**
2. **No German plant closures**
3. **Binding site and employment security**
4. **A substantial employee contribution** only in exchange for security
5. **Material management and owner contributions**
6. **A credible investment and product plan**
7. **A clear, quantified capacity and cost adjustment plan** that avoids layoffs

That means we are open to:
- pay moderation or deferral,
- reduced bonuses,
- working-time cuts,
- internal redeployment,
- voluntary exits,
- and other negotiated measures.

But we are **not** open to a deal that makes closures or forced layoffs the price of participation.

### Bottom line
If management wants our cooperation, it needs to come back with a **real alternative to closures and compulsory redundancies** — not a pressure campaign wrapped in the language of necessity.

If that alternative is serious, quantified, and fairly shared, then we can talk.  
If not, then I will say plainly: **we will oppose a restructuring that puts the workforce in the firing line while protecting the structural privileges of management and capital.**
