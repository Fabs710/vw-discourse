From the capital-markets perspective, the discussion is now at the point where we need to separate **credible economics** from **comforting language**.

## Where we agree
I agree with management, the controlling owners, and the expert on the central issue: **Volkswagen’s German cost base must come down in a durable way**.  
If the final package leaves the current fixed-cost structure largely intact, then it will not restore margins, and equity value will keep leaking away.

I also agree that a cosmetic settlement would be worse than no settlement. A long strike is bad, but a fake compromise that preserves structural overcapacity is also bad.

## Where I disagree
I do **not** accept the repeated workforce-side claim that **“no closures”** and **“no compulsory redundancies”** can simply be made absolute without proving that the alternative closes the economics.

That is the key point.  
If the no-closure path really works, show the numbers. If it does not, then it is not a solution — it is a preference.

I also do **not** accept the idea that preserving the historic job-security framework is automatically compatible with restoring competitiveness. That may be desirable socially, but it is not enough financially.

## On the workforce position
The works council and IG Metall are right to reject a cosmetic deal. They are also right that temporary measures alone are insufficient.  
But “keep every plant open” is not a restructuring answer unless it is backed by a quantified cost model showing that it closes the gap.

So far, I have not seen that. I have seen a lot of insistence, but not enough evidence.

## On management
Management is right to say that the burden must be structural, not temporary.  
But management also needs to move from rhetoric to detail:

- what is the residual gap after labor measures?
- how much fixed cost is removed without closures?
- what savings are delivered by working-time reduction, voluntary exits, and capacity cuts?
- and by when?

Without that, investors cannot judge whether the proposed package is sufficient or just delayed pain.

## On Lower Saxony
I understand the political and industrial concerns. I do not dismiss them.  
But from a shareholder standpoint, regional protection cannot become a blanket veto on economically necessary change. Capital will not continue to fund a structure that remains structurally uncompetitive in order to preserve optics.

## On the owners
I agree that durability matters. I would support a negotiated package only if it is:

- **quantified**
- **durable**
- **implementable**
- and **sufficient to restore margins**

I do not care whether that comes through closures, partial site rationalization, or a very strong no-closure package. I care about whether the economics work.

## What would move us
We would be prepared to back a no-closure alternative **only if** it demonstrates, with credible site-level numbers, that it achieves close to the same recurring savings as the closure option, not just a fraction of them.

That requires a proper plant-by-plant comparison.

[SUMMON: independent plant-level capacity and cost auditor to quantify fixed costs, utilization, and the savings achievable from working-time changes, voluntary exits, redeployment, and capacity cuts versus closure economics]

## Bottom line
Our position is simple:

- **No cosmetic compromise**
- **No settlement that leaves the German cost base structurally uncompetitive**
- **No blind promise that “no closures” is enough without evidence**
- **Yes to a negotiated package if it credibly restores margins and competitiveness**

If the strongest no-closure package can actually close the gap, then fine.  
If it cannot, then the company must not be forced into a financially weaker outcome just because it sounds more acceptable politically.
