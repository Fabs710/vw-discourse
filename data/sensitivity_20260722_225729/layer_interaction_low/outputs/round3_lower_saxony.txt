I will be direct.

Lower Saxony cannot support a restructuring logic that starts from the premise that German plant closures are simply a normal or necessary tool. That is not a neutral technical choice for us. It would damage jobs, suppliers, training pipelines, and Volkswagen’s role as a regional economic anchor. It would also create precisely the kind of conflict that undermines implementation.

### Where I agree
I agree with management, the owners, and the investors on one point: **Volkswagen needs a durable, quantified restructuring**, not a temporary patch.  
I also agree with the expert and with the union side that **working-time changes, voluntary exits, redeployment, and capacity measures can deliver real savings**.  
And I agree that the discussion has to move beyond slogans.

### Where I disagree
I do **not** accept the repeated framing that because a no-closure package may not match closure economics one-for-one, closures therefore become the default or “legitimate” answer. That is too crude.

The company is not only a balance sheet. It is also a regional industrial system. If we destroy too much trust, site stability, and co-determination, the long-term cost will be higher than any short-term savings.

I also do **not** accept that the burden must be solved primarily through site closure or compulsory redundancies. Those are the bluntest instruments, and once used, they are very hard to reverse.

### To management
Your point that savings must be structural is valid. But you have not yet shown that structural necessarily means closures.

If you believe the current German footprint is too expensive, then show us:
- the fixed costs by site,
- the utilization levels,
- the savings from shift reductions,
- the savings from voluntary programs,
- the savings from capacity transfer within the network,
- and the residual gap after those measures.

Until that is on the table, “closures remain an option” is not a quantified restructuring case. It is leverage.

### To the Works Council and IG Metall
I support your insistence that the workforce should not be asked to pay alone. That is fair.  
I also support no compulsory redundancies as a negotiating target.

But I will say plainly: if your position hardens into “no closures, no cuts, no meaningful change,” then it will not be enough. The company does need a real cost and capacity adjustment. A solution that protects every site symbolically but leaves the business weak would eventually harm the workforce too.

So the question is not whether there must be adjustment. There must. The question is how to do it without breaking the social and regional fabric of the company.

### To the controlling owners and investors
You are right to insist on durability. But durability is not just about cutting costs; it is also about preserving the legitimacy of the restructuring.

A package that can only be imposed through prolonged conflict is not durable.  
A package that leaves the German industrial base weakened is not durable either.

If you want a credible settlement, then you must accept that:
- site protection matters,
- co-determination matters,
- and the state’s co-determination and veto role are not obstacles to be bypassed.

### What could still work
A serious package would need to combine:
- **binding investment commitments**
- **clear product allocation for German sites**
- **working-time flexibility and voluntary exits**
- **targeted capacity reductions within existing sites**
- **management and owner contributions**
- **no forced redundancies where a negotiated alternative exists**

That is the kind of arrangement we could examine.

What I am **not** prepared to support is a solution that uses “durability” as a euphemism for setting up closures later. That would not be an honest compromise.

### On the specialist input
The call for a plant-level auditor is sensible.

[SUMMON: independent plant-level capacity and cost auditor to compare site fixed costs, utilization, and the savings achievable from working-time reductions, voluntary exits, redeployment, and capacity cuts versus closure economics]

We need that to determine:
- where the cost gap really sits,
- how much can be solved without closures,
- and where the remaining gap, if any, is actually concentrated.

### My position
Lower Saxony is prepared to support a negotiated restructuring that:
- protects jobs as far as possible,
- preserves German sites,
- strengthens competitiveness,
- and keeps Volkswagen a regional anchor.

We will **not** support a restructuring that turns closure into the starting point, or that strips away the state’s legitimate role in shaping the outcome.

So my answer is:
- yes to real restructuring,
- yes to quantified savings,
- yes to shared contributions,
- **no to closures as the default solution**,
- **no to compulsory redundancies if there is a negotiated alternative**,
- and **no to any deal that weakens Volkswagen’s long-term regional role**.
