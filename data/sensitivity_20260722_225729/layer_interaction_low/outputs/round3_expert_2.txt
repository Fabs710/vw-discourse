## How to compare options
The comparison should be made on a **net present value** basis over a fixed horizon, ideally **3 to 7 years**, and it should include:

### Option A: no-closure restructuring
- recurring annual savings
- minus transition costs
- minus implementation risk
- minus any residual capacity penalty

### Option B: plant closure or site rationalization
- larger recurring savings
- minus closure and transfer costs
- minus disruption risk
- minus delay from governance or labor conflict

The economically relevant question is not whether closure saves more in theory, but whether it saves **more net of all transition costs and risks**.

## What would make a no-closure package credible
A no-closure package can substitute for closure economics only if it achieves:

1. **Material recurring savings**
2. **Sufficient speed**
3. **Durability**
4. **Low enough execution risk**

In practice, the biggest no-closure levers are usually:
- shift reduction
- capacity consolidation within sites
- voluntary exits with replacement freeze
- working-time moderation
- elimination of duplicated support functions

But these measures often plateau if the plants have:
- structurally low utilization
- duplicated fixed infrastructure
- excess minimum staffing
- weak future product allocation

## Likely outcome in industrial terms
Without site-level data, the most defensible expectation is:

- **no-closure package:** can often capture a meaningful share of the savings
- **closure:** usually delivers the highest recurring savings
- **but** closure also carries the highest one-off costs and implementation risk

So the real question is whether the no-closure package closes **most of the gap**, or **nearly all of it**. If it only closes part of the gap, then it is not a full economic substitute.
