I will respond directly.

### To the Works Council
I agree with you on the essentials:
- no compulsory redundancies,
- no German plant closures,
- a real industrial and investment plan,
- and no cosmetic fix.

But I need to say plainly: your current formulation is still too absolute if it does not show how the cost gap is closed in operational terms. “Keep every site open” is a legitimate demand from the workforce side, but it is not yet a restructuring solution. If we are asked to defend this position against management, owners, and investors, we need a package that is not just morally correct, but economically credible.

That said, I do **not** accept the conclusion that the only credible alternative is to keep closure leverage alive. If management’s “review” is just a disguised countdown to site closure, then we reject it. I agree with you there. A review mechanism must not become a backdoor closure option.

### To management
I disagree with your framing at the point where “durable restructuring” becomes synonymous with closures or deep job cuts.

You have not shown, at least not convincingly, why the workforce must accept plant closure logic first rather than as an absolute last resort. The expert has already said alternatives can deliver a meaningful share of the savings. That means your burden is to prove that the remaining gap is truly unavoidable, not merely more convenient for capital.

If you want the union to consider concessions, then stop hiding behind generic statements about competitiveness and put the figures on the table:
- site fixed costs,
- utilization rates,
- savings from working-time changes,
- savings from voluntary exits,
- savings from capacity reduction within sites,
- and the exact residual gap after those measures.

Until then, “closures remain on the table” is not a plan. It is leverage.

### To Lower Saxony
I agree with your rejection of closures as a first resort. I also agree that trust, industrial peace, and regional stability matter. Those are not soft issues; they are part of the economics.

But if you want to help preserve sites, then use your position to back a package that is genuinely binding:
- no compulsory redundancies,
- investment commitments,
- product allocation,
- and capacity reduction through internal reorganization rather than site destruction.

That would be a serious contribution.

### To the controlling owners and investors
I understand your insistence on durability. But durability cannot mean forcing the workforce to absorb the pain while the owners preserve the right to close sites later if the numbers are not immediately perfect.

The workforce will not sign up to a staged surrender. If you want durable savings, then the deal must be built on:
- shared sacrifice,
- management and owner contributions,
- and actual job security.

Not just promises.

### To the expert point
Yes, quantified data is needed. But I do not accept the implication that because no-closure packages may only deliver part of the closure savings, that therefore closures become justified by default.

That is exactly why we need the plant-level auditor.

[SUMMON: independent plant-level capacity and cost auditor to quantify fixed costs, utilization, and the savings achievable from working-time reductions, voluntary exits, redeployment, and capacity cuts at each German site, and to compare those figures against closure economics.]

### My position
It remains this:
- **No compulsory redundancies**
- **No German plant closures**
- **No dismantling of the collective wage framework**
- **Fair employee contributions only in exchange for job and site security**
- **A real industrial plan, not a pressure campaign**

I am open to:
- working-time flexibility,
- bonus moderation,
- voluntary exits,
- redeployment,
- capacity reduction,
- and negotiated wage moderation.

But those tools must be used to **protect jobs and sites**, not to prepare closures by another name.

### Bottom line
If management can show a quantified, credible alternative that achieves competitiveness without closures and without forced layoffs, then we can negotiate seriously.

If not, then I will say it again clearly: the problem is not insufficient willingness by the workforce; the problem is an attempt to make employees carry a restructuring they did not create and cannot be asked to bear alone.
