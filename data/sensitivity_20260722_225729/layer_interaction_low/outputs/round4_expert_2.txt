Yes — from a cost-modeling and restructuring-economics standpoint, the expert’s summary is directionally correct, but it needs a sharper decision framework.

## What matters economically

For Volkswagen’s German sites, the comparison should be made across **three restructuring archetypes**:

### 1) Full closure
**Economic effect**
- Highest recurring savings
- Highest one-off transition cost
- Highest implementation and disruption risk

**Typical cost components**
- severance / social plan costs
- decommissioning
- write-downs / stranded assets
- relocation of production
- temporary supply-chain disruption
- possible volume loss elsewhere due to transition friction

### 2) Partial capacity reduction without closure
Examples:
- one-shift reduction
- line consolidation
- model transfer to another site
- site footprint shrinkage

**Economic effect**
- Moderate to high recurring savings
- Lower one-off cost than closure
- Usually the most realistic “middle path” if utilization is weak but not terminal

This is often the most important option to test first, because it can remove a large share of fixed cost while avoiding the largest closure costs.

### 3) No-closure restructuring package
Examples:
- voluntary exits
- working-time cuts
- bonus moderation
- temporary labor-cost reductions
- redeployment
- management overhead cuts

**Economic effect**
- Lowest one-off cost
- Savings can be meaningful
- But often insufficient if the structural problem is deep overcapacity

This package can work only if the residual cost gap after these measures is small enough.

---

## The key test

The real question is not:

**“Can some savings be achieved without closures?”**

The answer to that is almost always yes.

The real question is:

**“Can the no-closure package deliver enough recurring savings, net of transition costs, to reach a sustainable cost base for 5–10 years?”**

That requires a **net present value** comparison.

---

## A practical decision rule

I would frame it this way:

### No-closure package is sufficient if:
- utilization can be materially improved,
- headcount can fall through voluntary means,
- labor-cost moderation is durable,
- and the remaining fixed-cost base still supports competitive unit costs.

### Partial capacity reduction is preferable if:
- the plants are underutilized,
- but still operationally viable,
- and closing them would destroy more value than it creates.

### Full closure is economically justified if:
- utilization is structurally too low,
- the site has no product pipeline,
- the fixed-cost burden cannot be offset by internal measures,
- and the closure NPV is clearly superior even after transition costs.

---

## What data would decide this

For each German site, I would want:

- installed capacity
- actual utilization
- fixed cost per unit
- variable cost per unit
- headcount by function
- labor-cost per vehicle
- one-shift reduction savings
- voluntary exit savings
- redeployment capacity
- transition costs by scenario
- capex needed to keep the site competitive
- recurring savings over 3, 5, and 10 years

Without that, no one can credibly say whether closures are necessary or avoidable.

---

## My bottom-line assessment

Based on the scenario alone:

- A **pure no-closure package** is unlikely to match the economics of closure if overcapacity is severe.
- A **mixed package** that includes one-shift reductions, voluntary exits, and internal consolidation **may** come close enough.
- The most likely economically efficient compromise is **partial capacity reduction without full closure**, if the sites are not so underloaded that only shutdown makes sense.

So the issue is not ideological. It is **how much fixed cost can be removed without eliminating the site**.
