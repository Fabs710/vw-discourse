As the independent industrial restructuring and cost-modeling expert, I can answer the core question:

**Yes, a no-closure package can in principle substitute for closure economics — but only if the site-by-site recurring savings, utilization effects, and transition costs add up to a materially comparable net present value.**  
At Volkswagen’s scale, that usually requires a combination of several measures, not any single one.

## What needs to be quantified
To determine whether non-closure restructuring can credibly replace closures, I would need, **by plant and by product line**:

1. **Current fixed cost base**
   - labor
   - energy
   - maintenance
   - logistics
   - depreciation / allocated overhead
   - plant-level support functions

2. **Current utilization**
   - actual output vs. installed capacity
   - shift pattern
   - line loading
   - model mix and margin contribution

3. **Measure-specific savings**
   - working-time reductions
   - shift consolidation / one-shift operation
   - voluntary exits
   - redeployment / transfer out of site
   - wage moderation / bonus reductions
   - management-side overhead cuts
   - procurement / logistics simplification

4. **Transition costs**
   - severance / exit packages
   - retraining
   - relocation / transfer support
   - retooling
   - temporary underutilization
   - labor conflict / disruption risk

5. **Timing**
   - when savings start
   - ramp-up path
   - one-off costs upfront vs. recurring annual savings

6. **Closure benchmark**
   - one-off closure cost
   - residual stranded cost
   - savings from eliminating the site
   - cost of displaced production elsewhere

## How the comparison should be made
The right comparison is **net present value**, not just annual savings.

A non-closure package can be economically equivalent to closure if:

\[
NPV(\text{recurring savings from non-closure measures}) - NPV(\text{transition costs}) \approx NPV(\text{closure savings}) - NPV(\text{closure costs})
\]

In practice, for a large auto plant, the closure option usually has:

- **high one-time costs**
- **high implementation risk**
- **large recurring savings**
- but also **potential hidden costs** from losing capacity flexibility, supplier inefficiencies, and disruption

A negotiated no-closure package usually has:

- **lower one-time costs than closure**
- **lower political and industrial conflict risk**
- but often **smaller recurring savings**
- and may leave some structural cost still embedded

## What measures are most economically material
In VW-like situations, the most important non-closure levers are typically:

### 1) Shift and capacity consolidation
This is often the biggest near-term lever.
- Dropping a shift can remove a substantial amount of labor, energy, and overhead cost.
- It also improves utilization of the remaining footprint.
- Savings can be large if the plant is underloaded.

### 2) Voluntary exits
- Effective if the age profile and skill redundancy permit it.
- Most valuable where headcount exceeds long-term staffing need.
- Less effective if you lose scarce technical skills or have to backfill later.

### 3) Working-time adjustment / labor-cost moderation
- Can produce meaningful recurring savings if applied across a large workforce.
- On its own, usually not enough to solve a deep structural overcapacity problem.
- Works best when paired with capacity reduction.

### 4) Redeployment and internal transfer
- Can avoid external severance and preserve know-how.
- Economically useful, but it only shifts cost unless the receiving units are also productive.
- Best when it reduces surplus in one site and avoids external hiring elsewhere.

### 5) Management-side and overhead cuts
- Necessary for credibility and burden-sharing.
- Usually smaller in absolute terms than plant-level labor and capacity measures.
- Important politically and for governance, but not sufficient alone.

## The key threshold question
The decisive issue is not whether a no-closure package creates savings. It almost certainly does.

The real question is:

**Does it close enough of the structural cost gap to make the remaining footprint competitively sustainable for 5–10 years?**

That means looking at:
- cost per vehicle
- fixed cost absorption
- utilization normalized for cycle conditions
- sensitivity to volume decline
- investment needs for EV transition
- downside risk if market demand weakens further

## My practical judgment, without site data
Based on the scenario alone, the most plausible outcome is:

- **A pure no-closure package is unlikely to fully match the economics of a hard closure option**
- **But a mixed package may get close enough** if it combines:
  - one or more shift reductions
  - meaningful voluntary exits
  - labor-cost moderation
  - internal consolidation
  - management overhead cuts
  - and product allocation / investment commitments

In other words:

- **If utilization is badly below efficient levels**, closures or equivalent footprint reduction usually win on pure economics.
- **If underutilization is moderate and there is room to compress labor and shifts**, a negotiated no-closure package can be near-equivalent in NPV, especially once closure transition costs and conflict risks are counted.

## What I would ask for next
If I were auditing this, I would request a table for each German site with:

- installed annual capacity
- current utilization %
- headcount by function
- fixed cost per unit
- cost of a one-shift reduction
- cost/savings from 10%, 15%, 20% voluntary headcount reduction
- wage moderation scenarios
- redeployment capacity
- capex required to keep the site competitive
- closure cost and annual avoided cost

Only then can we say whether:
1. **closures are economically required**,  
2. **a no-closure package is sufficient**, or  
3. **a partial site rationalization is the best middle path**.

## Bottom line
From a restructuring economics standpoint:

- **Closures are not the only way to achieve structural savings**
- **But avoiding closures usually requires deeper concessions elsewhere**
- **The comparison must be made on NPV, site utilization, and recurring savings**
- **Without plant-by-plant data, no one can credibly claim the no-closure route is either sufficient or insufficient**
