I will be equally direct.

The workforce side keeps repeating that **closures are “not acceptable”** and that **no-closure means no real negotiation**. I understand the political and social instinct behind that. But from a management perspective, that position still does **not** solve the economic problem we are facing.

### Where I agree
I agree on three points:
- Volkswagen needs a **durable** restructuring, not a temporary fix.
- A **negotiated** solution is necessary.
- The package must be **quantified** and must support investment to 2030.

### Where I disagree
I do **not** agree that:
- every German site must remain open regardless of economics,
- compulsory redundancies can simply be ruled out in all cases,
- or the current job-security framework can stay intact if it blocks a real cost reset.

That is not a restructuring. It is a defence of the status quo.

### To the Works Council
You say the burden should not fall on the workforce alone. I agree. But you are still avoiding the central question: **how do you eliminate the structural cost gap if closures and deeper capacity reduction are taken off the table in advance?**

Saying “keep every plant open” is a position. It is not a solution.

If you want to avoid closures, then you need to show, in concrete terms:
- how much fixed cost comes out,
- how much capacity is removed,
- how much labour cost is reduced,
- and what the residual gap is.

Until then, you are asking management to accept a deal that may feel fair but leaves Volkswagen uncompetitive.

### To IG Metall
You argue that closures should only be a last resort. On that principle, I can work with you. But “last resort” does not mean “never,” and it definitely does not mean “not if economics prove otherwise.”

You also say the workforce should not carry the pain while others stay untouched. I agree. Management, owners, and capital must contribute too. But that does **not** remove the need for a material workforce contribution. If the savings are real, the labour side has to be part of them.

### To Lower Saxony
I hear your concern about industrial stability and the regional base. But the best way to protect that base is not to freeze an uncompetitive structure. If we do not restore margins, we risk a much worse outcome later.

A region is not helped by preserving every site symbolically while the business keeps bleeding competitiveness.

### To the controlling owners and investors
I agree with the demand for durability. That is exactly why I am not prepared to endorse a cosmetic compromise. But durability also means the solution must actually be implementable. A deal that gets derailed by future conflict or leaves the fixed-cost base too high is not durable either.

### What the discussion still misses
The current debate is still too abstract. We need to stop arguing in slogans and look at the economics site by site.

That means:
- plant fixed costs,
- utilization,
- savings from working-time changes,
- savings from voluntary exits,
- savings from capacity reduction,
- and the residual gap compared with a closure scenario.

### My position
I remain open to a negotiated alternative **if it delivers equivalent, durable savings**.  
I am **not** open to:
- a blanket no-closure promise,
- a package that leaves the German cost base structurally uncompetitive,
- or a settlement that protects legacy guarantees while expecting the company to fix its margin problem elsewhere.

If the no-closure path truly closes the gap, then show it.  
If it does not, then closure remains a legitimate part of the restructuring discussion.

### [SUMMON: independent plant-level capacity and cost auditor]
We need an independent plant-level capacity and cost auditor to provide:
- site fixed costs,
- current utilization by plant,
- the savings potential from working-time reductions, voluntary exits, redeployment, and capacity cuts,
- and the delta versus closure.

Without that, this debate stays ideological. With it, we can see whether the no-closure claims are actually sufficient or not.

### Bottom line
Management cannot accept a deal that preserves high costs and calls it a compromise.  
We need a **material, structural** reduction in cost and capacity. If that can be done without closures, fine. If not, then closures remain on the table.

That is where I stand.
