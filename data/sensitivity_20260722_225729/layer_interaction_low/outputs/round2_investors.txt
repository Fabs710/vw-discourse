From the capital-markets perspective, the discussion has become clearer, and also sharper.

### Where I agree
I agree with management, the controlling owners, and the expert on the central financial point: **Volkswagen’s German cost base cannot stay structurally where it is**. If the company comes out of this with only temporary concessions, or with a deal that preserves the current fixed-cost burden, then margins remain impaired and equity value keeps leaking out. That is not an acceptable outcome for us.

I also agree that **a cosmetic settlement would be worse than no settlement**. A prolonged conflict is value-destructive, yes — but a fake compromise that leaves the economics unchanged is also value-destructive, just more slowly.

### Where I disagree
I do **not** accept the works council’s and IG Metall’s premise that every German site must remain open no matter what, or that the historic job-security framework can remain essentially intact while the company fixes a major structural cost problem.

That position may be understandable politically, but from our standpoint it is not credible if the objective is to restore returns. If the company is carrying excess capacity and structurally high fixed costs, then some combination of:
- site rationalization,
- meaningful capacity reduction,
- durable labour-cost correction,
- and workforce reduction

has to happen.

I also do not buy the idea that “no forced redundancies” by itself is enough. If the workforce wants to avoid closures and involuntary layoffs, then the alternative must still deliver **comparable economics**. So far, I have not seen evidence that working-time cuts and voluntary exits alone can fully close the gap.

### On the expert input
The expert is right on the key issue: **alternatives can recover part of the savings, but usually not the full effect of structural site changes if overcapacity is real**. That matters a lot.

In practical terms, I would interpret the analysis as follows:
- pure wage moderation is not sufficient,
- voluntary exits are helpful but limited,
- capacity reduction without closure is better,
- but if one or more sites are structurally redundant, then **closure or equivalent fixed-cost removal** is what actually resets the economics.

That does not mean closure is the first choice. It means closure remains a legitimate outcome if nothing else gets the company to a competitive margin structure.

### On Lower Saxony
I understand the regional stakes, but I do not think “protect the region” can be the only lens. Capital markets will not fund a structure that keeps every site politically protected while returns remain below target. That is precisely how value destruction becomes chronic.

### On management
Management’s argument is financially plausible, but it now needs to be made **quantitatively**, not rhetorically. We need to know:
- how much savings come from labour measures,
- how much from capacity reduction,
- what residual gap remains,
- and whether that residual gap is acceptable.

Without that, we are just hearing positions.

### What would move us
What would move capital markets is a package that is:
- **durable**
- **quantified**
- **implementable**
- and **sufficient to restore margins**

That could include:
- selective German site changes,
- capacity rationalization,
- substantial voluntary exits,
- working-time and bonus moderation,
- and clear investment commitments tied to the restructuring.

What will **not** move us:
- a promise of no closures that simply preserves the cost structure,
- a settlement built on delay,
- or an agreement that leaves the company with an uncompetitive margin profile.

### Bottom line
We are not here to defend closures for their own sake. We are here to defend value. If the only way to restore a credible margin trajectory is through real structural cuts, then that is what the market will support. If the parties want to avoid closures, they need to show — with numbers, not slogans — that the alternative really closes the cost gap.

If that cannot be demonstrated, then I would not support a settlement that sacrifices competitiveness to preserve appearances.
