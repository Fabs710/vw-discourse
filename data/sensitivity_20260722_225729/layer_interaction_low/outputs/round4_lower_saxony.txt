I will be clear.

## My position

Lower Saxony supports a **serious restructuring**, but not one that starts from the premise that German plant closures are the default answer. We cannot accept an approach that treats jobs, sites, and co-determination as expendable variables. Volkswagen is not just any asset; it is a regional industrial anchor with long-term obligations to employees, suppliers, and the state.

## Where I agree

I agree with management, owners, and investors on one essential point: **the current situation requires real structural action**. A cosmetic settlement would fail everyone. I also agree that the discussion must be **quantified**, plant by plant, rather than argued in slogans.

I further agree that:
- working-time flexibility,
- voluntary exits,
- redeployment,
- shift consolidation,
- and targeted capacity reductions within existing sites

are real instruments and should be on the table.

## Where I disagree

I do **not** accept that closures must remain a live bargaining tool from the outset.  
I do **not** accept that the only credible restructuring is one that preserves closure leverage and forces the workforce to negotiate under threat.  
And I do **not** accept that preserving the state’s co-determination role is somehow an obstacle to competitiveness. It is part of Volkswagen’s governance reality.

To management: you say “structural” and “durable,” but you still have not shown that non-closure measures are insufficient. That burden is yours, not the workforce’s.

To the controlling owners and investors: I understand your insistence on value and margins. But a deal that produces prolonged conflict, destroys trust, and weakens Volkswagen’s regional legitimacy is not durable value creation. It is risk accumulation.

To the works council and IG Metall: I support the red line against closures as the starting point, but you also need to remain grounded in operational reality. If the company needs real savings, then a purely defensive posture will not be enough. The question is how far a negotiated internal adjustment can go.

## What I need to see

Before anyone talks about closures as anything other than a last-resort theoretical option, we need a **credible independent plant-by-plant audit** showing:

- fixed costs by site,
- utilization by site,
- savings from working-time changes,
- savings from voluntary exits,
- savings from redeployment and shift consolidation,
- savings from management-side cost cuts,
- and the residual gap versus closure economics.

[SUMMON: independent plant-by-plant industrial accountant to quantify fixed-cost reduction potential from working-time changes, voluntary exits, shift consolidation, redeployment, and management-side cost cuts, and compare that with closure economics using Volkswagen site data]

That audit must answer a simple question:  
**Can Volkswagen achieve a durable restructuring without closures and without forced layoffs?**

If the answer is yes, then closures are unnecessary.  
If the answer is no, management must prove that with numbers, not with pressure.

## My line

Lower Saxony is prepared to support:
- binding investment commitments,
- product allocation for German sites,
- internal capacity reduction,
- labor-cost moderation in a fair package,
- and a negotiated restructuring that protects jobs as far as possible.

We are **not** prepared to support:
- closures as a starting assumption,
- compulsory redundancies as the easy fix,
- or any settlement that weakens the state’s legitimate role in Volkswagen’s governance.

So my answer is:  
**yes to real restructuring; no to closures being normalized as the primary solution; yes to quantified alternatives first.**
