I will respond plainly.

## Where I agree
I agree with management on one essential point: **Volkswagen needs a durable restructuring, not a temporary patch**. If the company’s cost base remains structurally too high, that would endanger investment, competitiveness, and ultimately jobs.

I also agree with the expert’s basic assessment that **working-time changes, voluntary exits, and capacity adjustments can deliver real savings**, but not automatically the full savings of a hard closure program.

## Where I disagree
I do **not** accept the conclusion that this necessarily justifies **German plant closures** or treating them as the default solution.

From Lower Saxony’s perspective, closing sites in Germany would be a serious mistake:
- it would weaken Volkswagen’s role as a regional economic anchor,
- it would damage supplier networks and training pipelines,
- it would create political and industrial conflict that could make implementation far harder,
- and it would undermine trust in the governance model that has protected the company’s social stability for decades.

I also do **not** accept that the only credible path is to dismantle long-standing job-security structures. That would be a fundamental breach of the social balance that has made Volkswagen strong.

## On the workforce side
I understand and share the concern that employees should not be asked to carry the whole burden. That is fair.

At the same time, I would caution the works council and IG Metall against treating **“no closures, no compulsory redundancies, no change at all”** as the only acceptable frame. If the package does not materially reduce costs and capacity, then it will not be credible. So yes, there must be a real contribution from the workforce — but it must be negotiated, proportionate, and matched by commitments from management and owners.

## What I can support
I can support a serious package if it contains:
- **no compulsory redundancies**
- **no plant closures as a first resort**
- **binding investment commitments**
- **capacity reduction through negotiated means**
- **working-time flexibility and temporary labor-cost moderation**
- **voluntary programs and redeployment**
- **preservation of co-determination and the state’s legitimate role**
- **a clear industrial plan for future products and site utilization**

That is the kind of settlement that could protect jobs and restore competitiveness at the same time.

## What I cannot support
I cannot support:
- a restructuring built around closures as the starting point,
- a package that leaves employees with the main pain while management and ownership simply demand sacrifice,
- or a solution that weakens Volkswagen’s presence in Lower Saxony in a lasting way.

## My conclusion
The discussion should now move from abstract positions to a **quantified alternative** that shows:

1. how much savings can be achieved without closures,  
2. how much capacity can realistically be reduced through negotiated measures,  
3. what employee contribution is acceptable,  
4. what management and ownership will contribute, and  
5. how jobs and sites in Germany can be preserved while restoring competitiveness.

Until I see that, I cannot accept closures as necessary or justified. The burden must be shared, and the regional industrial base must not be sacrificed lightly.
