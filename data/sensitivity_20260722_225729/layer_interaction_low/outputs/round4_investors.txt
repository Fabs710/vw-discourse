From the capital-markets side, the argument still comes down to one thing: **does the package credibly restore margins, or does it preserve an uncompetitive cost base under a nicer label?**

## Where I agree
I agree with management, the controlling owners, and, frankly, with Lower Saxony and the workforce on one point: **Volkswagen cannot afford a cosmetic settlement**.  
A deal that merely postpones the cost problem would be value-destructive. A prolonged strike would also be value-destructive. So the objective must be a **durable** restructuring with measurable savings.

I also agree that we need **site-by-site numbers**, not slogans.

## Where I disagree
I do **not** accept the works council and IG Metall position as currently stated, because “no closures” and “no compulsory redundancies” are being treated as absolute red lines **without yet proving that the alternative fully closes the gap**.

That is the key issue.  
If the non-closure package delivers the same recurring savings net of implementation risk, fine — then closures are unnecessary.  
But until that is demonstrated, a blanket exclusion of closures is not a restructuring solution. It is a preference.

I also do **not** accept the notion that the historic job-security framework should survive unchanged if it prevents a real reset. Equity holders are not funding sentiment; they are funding future cash flows.

## To the works council
You are right that trust matters and that a hidden countdown to closure would poison the process. But your current stance still looks like **“protect everything first, then ask management to make the economics fit.”**

That is backwards.

If you want the market to believe the no-closure path is credible, you need to accept an audited, plant-by-plant test of whether:
- working-time changes,
- voluntary exits,
- redeployment,
- shift consolidation,
- capacity reduction within sites,
- and management-side cost cuts

actually produce enough recurring savings.

If they do, great. If they do not, then refusing any closure option in advance would be asking shareholders to absorb the downside of a structurally weak footprint.

## To IG Metall
I agree with you that management should stop speaking in abstractions and publish the numbers. That is exactly what capital markets need as well.

But I disagree with your implication that a residual gap does not change the conclusion. It does.  
If, after the strongest no-closure package, Volkswagen still cannot reach a competitive cost base, then no amount of moral argument makes the economics go away.

And on fairness: yes, management, owners, and capital should contribute. But that does **not** replace the need for a material workforce contribution if the business case requires it.

## To management
You are right that the package must be structural, not temporary.  
But you have not yet answered the investor question clearly enough:

- What savings remain after non-closure measures?
- How fast do they arrive?
- What is the NPV versus closure or site rationalization?
- What is the execution risk if industrial conflict continues?

Without that, the market cannot judge whether you are presenting a serious restructuring or just keeping optionality alive.

You need to be more precise and less rhetorical.

## To Lower Saxony
I understand your political and regional concerns. But from a shareholder perspective, you cannot ask capital to permanently underwrite a footprint that remains uncompetitive simply to preserve the symbolism of every site.

That said, you are right about one thing: a deal that triggers prolonged industrial conflict is also bad for value. So your support matters most if it helps deliver an **implementable** package, not just a principled objection to closures.

## To the controlling owners
I agree with you that this cannot become an ideology battle. The company needs a structural reset and must not get trapped in cosmetic compromise.

But I need the owners to be equally clear: if the no-closure alternative is genuinely close to closure economics, then support it. If it is not, then the market will not reward a compromise that keeps the problem alive.

## What would move us
What would move institutional investors is not a promise, but a **verified plant-by-plant comparison** showing:
- fixed costs,
- utilization,
- recurring savings from non-closure measures,
- one-off transition costs,
- implementation timing,
- and NPV relative to closure.

So yes, the specialist is necessary:

[SUMMON: independent plant-by-plant industrial accountant to quantify fixed costs, utilization, recurring savings from working-time changes, voluntary exits, shift consolidation, redeployment, and management-side cost cuts, and compare net present value against closure economics]

## Bottom line
My position is unchanged:

- **No cosmetic compromise**
- **No settlement that leaves the German cost base structurally uncompetitive**
- **No blank cheque for a no-closure formula without evidence**
- **Yes to a negotiated package if it credibly restores margins and deal certainty**

If the audit shows the no-closure path is enough, then that is acceptable.  
If it does not, then closures or deeper site rationalization remain legitimate tools — because value preservation comes before comfort.
