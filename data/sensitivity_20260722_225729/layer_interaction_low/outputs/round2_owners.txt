I will be direct.

I agree with the repeated diagnosis that Volkswagen needs a durable competitiveness reset, not a temporary patch. I also agree that a cosmetic agreement would destroy value. On that point, management, the investors, and even the expert analysis are right.

Where I disagree is with the attempt to make **“no closures, no compulsory redundancies, no meaningful change to legacy structures”** into the practical frame for the whole restructuring. That is not a credible long-term answer if the German cost base is structurally above target.

### My view on the positions

**To management:**  
You are right that savings must be durable and quantified. But if your package is seen as a confrontation that triggers prolonged industrial conflict, it will damage value as surely as undercapacity does. I need to see a plan that is economically serious **and** realistically implementable.

**To the works council and IG Metall:**  
I understand the concern for jobs and sites. But I do not accept the premise that protecting every plant as-is is compatible with restoring Volkswagen’s competitiveness. The expert confirms what should be obvious: working-time measures and voluntary exits can help, but they are unlikely to fully replace the economics of structural capacity reduction if overcapacity is real. If you want to avoid closures, then the alternative must be strong enough to close the cost gap in a durable way. So far, I have not seen that.

**To Lower Saxony:**  
I share the concern about avoiding a destructive confrontation. But preserving regional stability cannot mean preserving uneconomic structures indefinitely. That would only postpone a worse outcome. The question is not whether Volkswagen remains important to the region — it obviously does — but whether the current footprint can be made financially viable.

**To the investors:**  
Your insistence on real savings is correct. I would not support a cosmetic compromise that leaves margins structurally impaired. But the path to savings must also preserve the enterprise’s ability to execute. Prolonged strikes or governance deadlock are value-destructive too.

### What matters from the expert input

The specialists’ analysis is useful because it confirms a hard truth:  
- **No-closure packages can produce meaningful savings, but usually only part of the required amount if structural overcapacity exists.**
- **Partial capacity reduction is stronger than pure wage moderation.**
- **Closures deliver the cleanest recurring savings, but with high transition cost and high conflict risk.**

That means the real issue is not ideology; it is whether the company’s problem is mainly payroll cost or also fixed plant overcapacity. If it is the latter, then a no-closure position is economically weak unless the concessions are very deep and durable.

### My position

I am **open to a negotiated solution that avoids closures**, but only if it delivers:
- materially lower fixed costs,
- a credible reduction in excess capacity,
- a durable labor-cost contribution,
- and a clear investment path.

I am **not** open to:
- a deal that merely delays the problem,
- a package that keeps the German cost base fundamentally uncompetitive,
- or a settlement that treats every site as untouchable regardless of economics.

### What I would consider acceptable
A serious package could include:
- meaningful working-time flexibility,
- voluntary exits and redeployment,
- bonus moderation and other labor-cost contributions,
- selective capacity reductions,
- and binding investment commitments in future products.

But it must be **quantified** and must show exactly how the savings compare with the closure alternative. If the no-closure package only captures a fraction of the needed savings, then I would not regard it as sufficient.

### Bottom line
My governance test is simple:
1. Does the package restore competitiveness and margins?
2. Does it avoid value-destructive instability?
3. Is it durable enough to protect the long-term value of the enterprise?

If the answer is no, I will not support a settlement just because it sounds socially agreeable. If the answer is yes, then I am prepared to back a negotiated restructuring — but not an arrangement that leaves Volkswagen structurally weakened.
