### Where I agree
I agree with management, the experts, and the investors on the core economic point: **Volkswagen needs a durable, structural reset in Germany**. Temporary fixes will not restore margins. A cosmetic deal would destroy value.

I also agree with Lower Saxony and the employee side on one important point: **a prolonged confrontation would be value-destructive**. The company cannot afford months of strike escalation and governance deadlock.

### Where I disagree
I do **not** accept the idea that every German plant must remain open regardless of economics, nor do I accept that no compulsory redundancies or no closures can be treated as absolute, untouchable principles in advance.

That is not a restructuring framework. It is a veto on change.

If the German footprint is structurally too expensive and underutilized, then the company must be able to remove real fixed cost. If the alternatives do not do that sufficiently, then site rationalization remains a legitimate part of the discussion.

### On the works council
You are right that the workforce should not carry the burden alone. I accept that.

I do **not** accept, however, the repeated implication that closures are merely a management threat tactic. If plant fixed costs and capacity are truly excessive, then avoiding the word “closure” does not change the economics.

What I would need from the workforce side is not a slogan of “keep every plant open,” but a **credible, quantified alternative** that closes the cost gap in a durable way. So far, I have not seen that.

### On IG Metall
You are right that working-time changes, voluntary exits, redeployment, and labor-cost moderation can deliver real savings. The expert has confirmed that.

But I disagree with the claim that this is automatically enough if closures are off the table. It may be enough in a moderate case. It is much less convincing if Volkswagen is carrying structurally redundant capacity. The burden of proof is on those saying that the no-closure path fully restores competitiveness.

### On management
I support your insistence on durability, but not an unnecessarily confrontational approach. You need to be precise and quantitative. “Closures remain on the table” is not enough by itself; we need to know what gap remains after the strongest negotiated alternative.

If management cannot show the residual gap clearly, it weakens its own case. If it can, then the governance bodies can judge whether deeper structural measures are unavoidable.

### On Lower Saxony
I understand the regional and political stakes. But protecting Volkswagen’s role in the region cannot mean preserving an uncompetitive structure indefinitely.

If we avoid hard decisions now and the company keeps losing competitiveness, the regional damage later will be worse, not better.

### What I need to see
Before I would support any final package, I want three things:

1. **A plant-by-plant quantified cost and utilization picture**
2. **A real comparison of the no-closure package versus closure economics**
3. **A binding investment and product allocation plan that supports the restructured footprint**

That requires the independent plant-level audit already requested.

### My position
I am open to a negotiated solution that avoids closures **if** it truly closes the cost gap and is durable.

I am **not** open to:
- a deal that preserves legacy structures untouched,
- a settlement that leaves the German cost base structurally uncompetitive,
- or a promise of “no closures” that simply postpones the real decision.

If the strongest no-closure package can demonstrably restore margins, I will support it. If it cannot, then the company must not be trapped by wishful thinking.

That is the standard I will use.
