I will respond plainly.

## To management
I agree on one thing: this discussion must be quantified, not conducted in slogans.

But I **do not** accept your conclusion that a material cost reset requires keeping closures on the table as a real option. That is exactly the pressure mechanism the workforce is resisting, and for good reason. Once you keep that lever, every “no-closure” offer becomes conditional blackmail.

You still have **not** shown that the alternative measures are insufficient. You keep repeating “structural” and “durable,” but you have not yet demonstrated, site by site, that:
- working-time changes,
- voluntary exits,
- redeployment,
- shift reduction,
- internal capacity consolidation,
- and management-side cost restraint

cannot close the gap.

Until you do, closures are not a necessity. They are a preference.

## To the controlling owners and investors
I hear your demand for durability and value preservation. I do not dismiss that. But I reject the idea that durability means making German plants disposable.

A deal that destroys trust, provokes long conflict, and weakens co-determination is not durable. It may look hard-headed on paper, but it is a recipe for repeated disruption and higher long-term cost.

If you want durable value, then you need to accept a package that is built on:
- binding site security,
- no forced layoffs,
- real product commitments,
- and management/owner contributions.

Not just pressure on labour.

## To Lower Saxony
I appreciate your support against closures. But support must be more than political sympathy. If you are serious, then use your governance role to back a **binding no-closure settlement**, not a “maybe if the numbers later justify it” formula.

I agree with you that the company is more than a balance sheet. That is precisely why closures cannot be the default tool.

## To IG Metall
I agree with you that a cosmetic deal is not enough. And I agree that we need figures.

Where I differ from the more open-ended parts of the discussion is this:  
I do **not** accept any language that implies a “no-closure” package is only acceptable if it merely delays the inevitable. If that is the hidden logic, then it is not a compromise; it is staged dismantling.

We should be negotiating how to protect jobs and sites while making the company competitive, not how to preserve closure rights for later.

## To the expert / auditor
Yes, the audit is necessary. But let me be equally clear: the audit must not be used as a pretext to legitimize closures before all non-closure options are fully and credibly tested.

We need a plant-by-plant comparison of:
- fixed costs,
- utilization,
- savings from working-time and shift measures,
- savings from voluntary exits,
- redeployment potential,
- and management-side contributions.

And we need that comparison to answer a very simple question:

**Can Volkswagen achieve the required restructuring without plant closures and without compulsory redundancies?**

That is the question. Not “which closure is easiest.”

## My position remains unchanged
I am not moving off these red lines:

- **No German plant closures**
- **No compulsory redundancies**
- **Preservation of co-determination**
- **Preservation of the job-security framework**
- **Binding site and investment commitments through 2030**

I am open to:
- working-time flexibility,
- voluntary exit programs,
- capacity adjustments within sites,
- internal redeployment,
- bonus moderation,
- and negotiated employee contributions

**if and only if** they are tied to real job and site security.

## What I will not accept
I will not accept:
- a “review” that quietly preserves closure rights,
- a phased countdown to site loss,
- compulsory redundancies as a bargaining chip,
- or a settlement that weakens the workforce now and asks for trust later.

That is not how trust is earned.

## Bottom line
If management can present a serious, quantified no-closure package that preserves every German plant and avoids forced layoffs, then we can discuss the terms.

If not, then the problem is not the workforce’s unwillingness to compromise. The problem is that management is still trying to solve a restructuring crisis by threatening the people who built the company.
