I appreciate the clarity from the workforce side, but I need to be equally clear: **you are still asking Volkswagen to solve a structural competitiveness problem with tools that may not be sufficient.**

## Where I agree
I agree on three points:

- **A cosmetic deal is useless.** If the outcome leaves the cost base uncompetitive, it fails.
- **Industrial peace matters.** A prolonged conflict would damage output, reputation, and trust.
- **Burden-sharing has to be real.** I am not asking employees alone to carry the burden.

## Where I disagree
### 1) “No closures” as an absolute red line
I cannot accept that as a binding premise.

If the economics require capacity to come out of the system, then we need to be honest about that. I am open to **different routes** to achieve it, but I am **not** open to a position that rules out all site-level consequences before the numbers have been solved.

A promise to keep every German plant open through 2030 may sound stabilising, but if it leaves overcapacity untouched, it is not a solution. It is a postponement.

### 2) “No compulsory redundancies” as an unconditional veto
I prefer to avoid forced layoffs, but I will not rule out measures that may become necessary if we cannot secure sufficient voluntary, negotiated, and durable reductions elsewhere.

If you want to avoid compulsory redundancies, then you need to bring a package that delivers the same structural effect through:
- capacity cuts,
- working-time changes,
- productivity gains,
- internal redeployment,
- and genuine labour-cost relief.

So far, you are describing the tools you prefer, not proving they are enough.

### 3) Capacity reduction “without closures” is not automatically enough
I hear the argument that capacity can be reduced without formal closures. That may be part of the answer. But I need more than labels.

If a plant is kept open legally while its product allocation, shifts, and utilisation are steadily hollowed out, that may preserve a nameplate site while still failing to restore competitiveness. I will not call that success just because it avoids the word “closure.”

## To the works council
You say we have not shown closures are unavoidable. Fair enough — but you also have not shown that your preferred package can generate **equivalent durable savings**.

If you want us to rule out closures and forced redundancies, then present a quantified package that closes the gap:
- how much labour cost comes out,
- how much capacity comes out,
- what the timeline is,
- and why it is durable.

“Working-time flexibility” and “qualification” are useful, but they are not, by themselves, a structural answer to excess capacity and an uncompetitive cost base.

## To IG Metall
You are right that a one-sided burden on employees is unacceptable. I agree.

But you are still not answering the key question: **what are the hard economic levers that produce the savings?**  
Temporary labour-cost contributions are only useful if they are substantial enough to matter and durable enough to change the trajectory. Otherwise, they are just delay.

If you want a no-closure path, then it has to be accompanied by:
- meaningful and verifiable labour-cost contribution,
- productivity commitments,
- and a credible reduction in the capacity overhang.

## To Lower Saxony
I respect your role under the VW framework, but your position cannot be that every German site must remain untouched regardless of competitiveness.

That would ignore the reality the company is facing. The state’s interest is not just employment preservation in the abstract; it is also a viable Volkswagen with a future. If the structure stays too expensive, the long-term regional outcome is worse, not better.

## To the controlling owners
I agree with you that value destruction through endless conflict is unacceptable. But value destruction also happens if we preserve an uncompetitive structure and keep funding it with future investment that should be going into products and technology.

So when you ask for an economically credible package, that is exactly what I am demanding too. But credibility requires more than a political commitment to preserve sites. It requires real savings.

## To institutional investors
You are right to insist on measurable improvement. I will be blunt: the brand cannot meet its return-on-sales target on sentiment. We need structural action.

That said, if the only benchmark is “maximize short-term labour reduction,” then we will end up with a fight that weakens implementation and may destroy more value than it creates. So I need you to recognize that the best solution is not necessarily the most aggressive one — it is the one that is **durable and executable**.

## What I need to see
If the employee side wants to rule out closures and forced redundancies, then bring a package that includes all of the following:

1. **Material labour-cost reduction**  
2. **Real capacity reduction**  
3. **Verified productivity gains**  
4. **Internal redeployment and qualification**  
5. **Working-time flexibility with economic impact**  
6. **A duration long enough to be structurally meaningful**  
7. **No loophole that simply freezes the problem until 2030 and then explodes**

If that can be delivered, then we can discuss the form. If it cannot, then I will not pretend that a no-closure promise solves the problem.

## My bottom line
I am not here to rubber-stamp closures for their own sake.  
I am here to secure a **durable reset** of Volkswagen’s cost and capacity base.

So let me state it plainly:

- **I agree** that a negotiated solution is preferable.
- **I agree** that burden-sharing should be broad.
- **I do not agree** that “no closures” and “no compulsory redundancies” can be treated as absolute if the savings are insufficient.
- **I will not accept** a cosmetic settlement that leaves Volkswagen structurally uncompetitive.

Bring me a package that delivers the numbers and the durability, and we have a basis to talk. If not, then the company will have to consider more drastic measures.
