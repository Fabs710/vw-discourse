Let me respond plainly.

## Where I agree

I agree with everyone on one essential point: **Volkswagen needs a durable restructuring, not a cosmetic deal**.

I also agree that:
- a prolonged industrial conflict is costly,
- a hollow compromise that leaves the cost base untouched is useless,
- and burden-sharing must not fall on employees alone.

That is the easy part.

## Where I disagree

### To the works council and IG Metall
You keep saying **“no plant closures, no compulsory redundancies”** as if repeating that makes the economics disappear.

It does not.

You still have not shown a **quantified, durable alternative** that closes the structural gap. You list instruments:
- working-time flexibility,
- redeployment,
- retraining,
- voluntary exits,
- temporary wage moderation,
- productivity measures.

Fine. Those are tools. But tools are not a solution unless you can prove they deliver:
- enough savings,
- enough capacity reduction,
- enough durability,
- and enough margin improvement.

So far, you have not done that.

And I need to be very clear: **I am not accepting an arrangement that preserves all German sites in form but leaves Volkswagen uncompetitive in substance.** A plant that is starved of product, shifts, and future volume is not a victory for anyone. It is delay dressed up as protection.

### To Lower Saxony
I respect your role and your blocking position, but political veto power is not an economic model.

You can reject closures. You cannot repeal the cost problem.

If you want a no-closure settlement, then help produce one that is economically credible. Right now, I hear principles, but not numbers. I need:
- the exact savings,
- the exact capacity reduction,
- the exact productivity gains,
- and the exact mechanism that makes them durable through 2030.

Without that, your position is a constraint, not a solution.

### To the controlling owners
You are right that we must avoid value-destructive conflict. But “avoid conflict” cannot become a reason to accept a weak deal.

If the company remains structurally too expensive, then we destroy value anyway — slowly, through lost margins, lost investment capacity, and lost competitiveness.

I need owners to back a package that is **real**, not just politically easier. That means accepting that closures or compulsory redundancies may be necessary if a credible no-closure package cannot be made to work.

### To institutional investors
You are right to insist on durability and enforceability. I agree.

But your own position also has to face reality: if the employee side cannot deliver sufficient structural savings, then insisting on “no closures” just protects value on paper while undermining it in practice.

I will not support a settlement that looks neat to the market but leaves the brand’s return-on-sales target out of reach.

## My position on the compromise ideas

### 1) Phased no-closure pact with quantified performance gates
This is only acceptable if the gates are tied to **real restructuring discipline**, not a disguised route to endless renegotiation.

If the package misses target and the only next step is “more discussion,” then it is not a pact. It is drift.

If the package misses target and there is no consequence, then it is not credible.

I am open to a phased approach, but only if it preserves the option to take harder measures when the economics demand it.

### 2) Capacity reduction through product and shift reallocation
This is the most plausible alternative to outright closures, but only if it is meaningful.

I would support:
- shift reductions,
- product reallocation,
- internal transfers,
- retraining,
- and utilization cuts,

if they produce the same structural effect as closure would.

I will not accept “capacity reduction” as a euphemism for a hollowed-out footprint.

### 3) Burden-sharing package
Yes, burden-sharing is necessary.

But burden-sharing must be measured by results, not intentions:
- employees contribute,
- management contributes,
- owners contribute,
- and the company invests in the future.

If the package does not materially improve competitiveness, then the burden-sharing is symbolic and insufficient.

## What would move me
A no-closure settlement could gain support only if it contains all of the following:

1. **Material and permanent labor-cost reduction**
2. **Real capacity reduction**
3. **Verified productivity gains**
4. **A clear, enforceable implementation timetable**
5. **A credible path back to target margins**
6. **No open-ended deferral of adjustment**
7. **No loophole that turns “site preservation” into slow erosion**

That is the threshold.

## My line in the sand

I am not here to force closures for sport. I am here to restore Volkswagen’s competitiveness.

So let me be explicit:

- I **welcome** a negotiated solution.
- I **welcome** real burden-sharing.
- I **do not accept** unconditional exclusions of closures or compulsory redundancies.
- I **do not accept** a cosmetic settlement that leaves the company structurally uncompetitive.

If you can bring me a package that delivers the numbers with execution certainty, I will support it.

If not, then we will have to face the harder options honestly, because the cost crisis does not disappear just because everyone prefers a nicer outcome.
