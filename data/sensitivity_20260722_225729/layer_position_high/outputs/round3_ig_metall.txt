We are still at the same point: **management wants structural savings, but has not proven that plant closures or compulsory redundancies are unavoidable**. Until that proof exists, those options remain unacceptable.

## To management
I acknowledge one thing clearly: Volkswagen needs a durable competitiveness reset. I do **not** deny the cost problem.

But you are still avoiding the central issue. You say “structural reduction,” yet you do not specify a package that stays within socially and industrially acceptable boundaries. Instead, you keep leaving closures and forced layoffs on the table as if that were a neutral fact of nature.

It is not neutral. It is a choice.

What I hear from you is:
- preserve flexibility for management,
- shift risk onto the workforce,
- and keep the option to reopen the same threat later.

That is not a credible basis for trust. If you want our cooperation, you need to show a **quantified, negotiated alternative** that achieves savings **without** plant closures and **without** compulsory redundancies.

## To the works council
We are aligned on the fundamentals:
- no closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- no weakening of co-determination,
- and no semantic tricks where “capacity reduction” is just closure by another name.

I support your insistence that any deal must be real, not cosmetic. I also support your point that workers should not be the only group making sacrifices. That remains central.

Where I would sharpen the line is this: if management comes back with a package that is genuine in substance, the union will judge it on whether it preserves:
- jobs,
- sites,
- and the collective wage framework.

Not on whether it sounds “flexible” enough for investors.

## To institutional investors
You are right about one thing: a cosmetic deal would be worthless.  
But you are still overestimating closures as a value solution.

A settlement that triggers warning strikes, escalation, and long-term damage to trust is not automatically “more credible.” It can easily destroy more value than it creates. You keep asking for hard numbers; that is fair. But then you must also account for:
- conflict costs,
- implementation risk,
- skills loss,
- and the damage to industrial peace.

If you want economic credibility, then support a package that is:
- measurable,
- enforceable,
- and socially executable.

Not just a package that looks strongest on a spreadsheet.

## To the controlling owners
You say you want a durable and governable solution. Good. Then stop treating the workforce as the first adjustment lever.

A serious owner recognizes that forcing the company into a confrontation with its workforce is a self-inflicted wound. If you want this company to remain stable and profitable, you need to back a settlement that includes:
- owner-side contribution,
- management restraint,
- and binding security for jobs and sites.

You keep saying “if the numbers require it.” Fine. Then show the numbers in a way that proves there is no viable negotiated alternative. Until then, closures remain unjustified.

## To Lower Saxony
We agree on the central point: a viable Volkswagen matters, and hollowing out the German industrial base would be a mistake.

But that means you need to use your leverage not just to speak against closures, but to **enforce a genuine no-closure solution**. If “capacity reduction” becomes a slow erosion of plants, then the substance of your position collapses.

We support:
- all German plants remaining open,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- binding co-determination,
- and a job-security framework through 2030.

What we will not support is a paper guarantee that quietly degrades sites until closure becomes inevitable later.

## On the compromise ideas

### 1) Conditional no-closure pact with hard triggers
This only works if the triggers do **not** point to layoffs or closures as the automatic next step.

A real pact would require:
- joint monitoring,
- transparent metrics,
- management accountability,
- and additional negotiated measures before anything more drastic is even discussed.

If the trigger mechanism is just a delayed backdoor to the same threatened outcomes, then it is not acceptable.

### 2) Capacity reduction through product and shift rationalization, not legal closure
This is the only route that could be workable — **if it is honest**.

Acceptable:
- shift adjustments,
- product reallocation,
- internal redeployment,
- retraining,
- productivity improvements,
- and temporary flexibility.

Unacceptable:
- starving a site of product,
- reducing it to a shell,
- or using “rationalization” as closure in slow motion.

### 3) Burden-sharing package with asymmetric contributions and a review clause
Yes, burden-sharing is the right principle. But it must be **fair**.

That means:
- employees contribute temporarily,
- management contributes materially,
- owners contribute visibly,
- and the company gives a binding employment and site guarantee.

Without that balance, burden-sharing becomes one-sided sacrifice.

## What the union is prepared to accept
Let me be very precise. We are open to:
- temporary wage moderation,
- working-time flexibility,
- reduced bonuses or special payments,
- internal redeployment,
- retraining,
- voluntary exits,
- and productivity agreements.

We are **not** open to:
- compulsory redundancies,
- plant closures,
- or any deal that weakens the collective wage framework.

If the company wants employee contributions, they must be part of a fair package. Employees will not pay alone.

## What would move us
Not rhetoric. Not pressure. Not abstract references to market discipline.

What would move us is a package that includes:
1. **No plant closures**
2. **No compulsory redundancies**
3. **A binding site and employment guarantee through 2030**
4. **Quantified management and owner contributions**
5. **Temporary employee contributions only**
6. **Real capacity adjustment through reversible measures**
7. **Preservation of the collective bargaining framework**

If those conditions are not met, then there is no basis for agreement.

## Bottom line
We remain willing to negotiate seriously. But we will not be pushed into accepting that jobs and sites must be sacrificed simply because management prefers that route.

So I will say it plainly:

**No plant closures. No compulsory redundancies. No one-sided burden shifting.**

Bring a real, quantified, fair restructuring package that protects the German industrial core, and we can talk.  
Bring threats disguised as strategy, and we will continue to resist with every means available, including industrial action.
