I appreciate the clarity from all sides. Let me speak plainly as a long-term controlling owner.

We agree on the core diagnosis: Volkswagen has a real competitiveness problem, and doing nothing is not an option. A weaker cost base, excess capacity, and insufficient margin performance will ultimately damage the enterprise value that all of us depend on.

Where I differ is on the framing of the solution.

### What I agree with
- **Management is right** that the company needs a durable structural improvement, not a symbolic adjustment.
- **The workforce side is right** that this crisis cannot simply be loaded onto employees while everyone else is untouched.
- **Lower Saxony is right** that Volkswagen’s industrial base, skills, and social stability matter materially to long-term value.

### Where I disagree
I do **not** support a debate that begins and ends with the idea that only plant closures and compulsory redundancies can deliver savings. That may be the easiest arithmetic on paper, but it risks creating a prolonged conflict, reputational damage, and avoidable destruction of value. A drawn-out confrontation would weaken Volkswagen further, not strengthen it.

Equally, I do **not** support the idea that all German sites and all existing guarantees can simply be frozen unchanged regardless of cost realities. If the company’s economics do not improve, then preserving every current structure by declaration is not a strategy either.

### What must be true for me to support a settlement
Any solution has to do three things:
1. **Restore competitiveness materially and durably**
2. **Preserve labour peace and implementation stability**
3. **Avoid value-destructive escalation or a governance crisis**

That means I am open to a broad package, but only if it is credible. I would expect serious consideration of:
- capacity reductions,
- internal reallocation and productivity measures,
- working-time flexibility,
- qualification and redeployment,
- labour-cost contributions that are meaningful but negotiated,
- and investment commitments tied to the restructuring.

What I am **not** prepared to back is a path that triggers a major industrial conflict, damages the brand, or undermines the company’s governance stability. Nor am I prepared to endorse a weak compromise that simply postpones the adjustment and leaves the enterprise uncompetitive.

### To management
If you want support from the controlling shareholder, you need to show that your plan is not just a cost-cutting exercise, but a credible industrial strategy with measurable savings and a realistic path to restored margins. I will not support value destruction in the name of discipline.

### To the works council and IG Metall
A categorical “no closures, no layoffs, no change” position is not sufficient if it leaves the company structurally unfit for competition. If the alternative cannot deliver equivalent savings, then it is not a real alternative. I am open to negotiated solutions, but only if they are substantial and durable.

### To Lower Saxony
I understand your red lines and your role under the VW framework. But the state’s interest is also in a viable Volkswagen, not just in preserving every current arrangement. If the company remains uncompetitive, the long-term consequences for jobs and the region will be worse.

So my position is straightforward:
- I support a serious restructuring.
- I do not support permanent value impairment through conflict or paralysis.
- I am open on the method, but not on the need for real structural improvement.

Bring forward a package that is economically credible, socially workable, and governable. If it is merely a shield against hard decisions, I will not regard it as acceptable.
