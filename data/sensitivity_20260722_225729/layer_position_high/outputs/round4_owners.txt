I will be direct.

I agree with one point across the table: Volkswagen needs a **durable, economically credible restructuring**, not a cosmetic compromise. If we settle for a paper deal that postpones the problem, that would destroy value and damage the company’s ability to invest.

Where I do **not** agree is with the attempt to make **“no plant closures” and “no compulsory redundancies” absolute principles regardless of the economics**. That is not a governance position I can support if it prevents the company from restoring competitiveness.

## To management
You are right that the current cost and capacity structure is not sustainable.

But you still have not given a disciplined, quantified bridge from the present situation to target margins. You keep asserting that “deep structural cuts” are needed, but not yet showing:
- the exact savings target,
- the exact capacity gap,
- which measures are essential,
- and what no-closure measures would be sufficient in hard numbers.

That is not good enough. If you want owners to back hard choices, you need to prove necessity, not just preference.

At the same time, I do **not** support a management approach that treats closures or forced layoffs as the default answer. A prolonged confrontation would also destroy value. If your plan cannot survive industrial resistance, it is not yet a viable plan.

## To the works council and IG Metall
I respect the refusal to accept one-sided burden shifting. That is legitimate.

I also agree that a hollowed-out “capacity reduction” is not acceptable if it is just closure by another name.

But your position still has not answered the central economic question: **what package produces enough durable savings and capacity reduction to restore competitiveness without closures?** You have listed tools:
- working-time flexibility,
- redeployment,
- retraining,
- voluntary exits,
- productivity agreements,
- temporary wage moderation.

Those are instruments, not yet a complete answer. Show me the quantified effect, the duration, and the enforcement. Without that, “no closures” remains a political demand rather than an economic solution.

And I need to be clear: I will not accept a no-closure promise that simply starves sites of product and future volume until they become unviable later. That is closure in slow motion.

## To Lower Saxony
You are right that Volkswagen is an industrial base, not just a balance sheet.

But your blocking position cannot become a veto over any meaningful adjustment. If the cost base remains structurally too high, then preserving every site in current form may delay decline rather than prevent it.

I am open to a no-closure settlement **if** it is economically credible. That means real capacity rationalization, real productivity gains, and real cost reduction. If you want the no-closure path to prevail, help make it economically hard, not just politically convenient.

## To institutional investors
You are right to insist on durability and enforceability.

But I also reject the idea that the most aggressive solution is automatically the best one. A destructive conflict can erase the value of a theoretically stronger restructuring. The real test is expected value after execution risk, strike risk, and governance damage.

So the right question is not “closures or no closures” in the abstract. The right question is: **which package actually restores margins with the highest confidence and the least long-term damage?**

## On the compromise ideas
### 1) Phased no-closure pact with quantified performance gates
This is only acceptable if the gates are not a disguised backdoor to automatic layoffs or closures.

If missed targets merely reopen the same threat, then it is not a pact; it is a delayed ultimatum.

What I could support is a phased arrangement with:
- hard metrics,
- joint monitoring,
- management accountability,
- and escalation first into more negotiation, redeployment, retraining, and additional owner/management contribution.

### 2) Capacity reduction through product and shift reallocation
This is the most plausible no-closure route, but only if it is real.

I could support:
- shift reductions,
- product reallocation,
- internal transfers,
- retraining,
- utilization changes,

if they produce measurable and durable savings.  
I will not support “capacity reduction” that leaves the business structurally unchanged.

### 3) Burden-sharing package with bounded contributions
Yes, burden-sharing is necessary.

But burden-sharing must be balanced:
- employees contribute temporarily,
- management contributes visibly,
- owners contribute materially,
- and the company invests in future products and German sites.

Without that, burden-sharing becomes a euphemism for one-sided workforce sacrifice.

## What would move me
A no-closure settlement could gain my support if it includes all of the following:
1. material and quantified labor-cost reduction,
2. real and durable capacity reduction,
3. verifiable productivity gains,
4. a credible implementation timetable,
5. a path back to target margins,
6. no open-ended deferral of adjustment,
7. no loophole for slow hollowing-out.

That is the threshold.

## My bottom line
I am not here to force closures for their own sake. I am here to protect the long-term value of the company.

So my position is unchanged:
- I support restructuring.
- I support burden-sharing.
- I do **not** support a symbolic no-closure settlement.
- I do **not** support unconditional exclusions of closures or compulsory redundancies unless a truly credible economic alternative is proven.

If you can bring a package that restores competitiveness with execution certainty, I will support it.  
If not, then we must face the harder options honestly.
