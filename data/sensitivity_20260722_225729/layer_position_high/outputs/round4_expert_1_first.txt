From a financial restructuring and industrial-planning perspective, the key question is:

**Can a no-closure package credibly deliver savings and capacity reduction comparable to plant closures, and restore margins by 2030?**

## Short answer
**Possibly in part, but only if the package includes deep, durable structural measures.**  
A no-closure solution can narrow the gap, but **it is usually unlikely to fully replicate the economics of a true plant closure** unless the company achieves:
- substantial and permanent workforce reduction,
- major shift and capacity rationalization,
- product reassignment and footprint simplification,
- lasting labor-cost concessions,
- and very high execution discipline.

If those elements are missing, a no-closure pact is more likely to **delay adjustment than eliminate it**.

---

## 1) Economic equivalence: closure vs. no-closure measures

A plant closure typically produces savings across several lines:
- direct fixed labor cost elimination,
- overhead and site-support reduction,
- maintenance and utilities savings,
- logistics simplification,
- lower complexity in planning and scheduling,
- and avoided future reinvestment in marginal assets.

A no-closure alternative can recover some of this through:
- shift reductions,
- consolidation of production lines,
- voluntary exits,
- working-time changes,
- wage moderation,
- redeployment,
- procurement and overhead cuts,
- and product concentration.

But there is an important difference:

### Closure savings are usually:
- **larger**
- **faster**
- **more durable**
- **simpler to execute**

### No-closure savings are usually:
- **smaller initially**
- **more dependent on behavior**
- **more vulnerable to backsliding**
- **harder to verify**

So the burden on the no-closure package is very high. It must do more than “soften” the problem; it must **permanently remove enough cost and capacity** to change the economics.

---

## 2) What would make a no-closure pact credible?

For it to be credible to owners and investors, the package would need to be quantified in a way that is **sufficiently close to a closure case** on net present value terms.

That means:
- a **multi-year savings bridge** showing annual and cumulative savings,
- a clear estimate of **capacity removed**,
- evidence that the savings are **structural, not temporary**,
- and a timetable showing when the margin uplift appears.

In practical terms, credibility would require at least four pillars:

### A. Permanent labor-cost reduction
This could include:
- wage moderation,
- reduction or suspension of bonuses/special payments,
- lower hours with productivity normalization,
- fewer legacy guarantees,
- and lower headcount through voluntary exits and attrition.

### B. Structural capacity reduction
Not just “less work,” but:
- fewer shifts,
- line consolidation,
- product migration,
- reduced fixed-volume commitments,
- and removal of underutilized capacity.

### C. Footprint simplification
Even if sites remain open legally, the network must be simplified:
- fewer models per plant,
- fewer duplicate functions,
- less internal transport,
- reduced complexity cost.

### D. Governance and enforcement
Without this, the deal is not financeable as credible:
- hard metrics,
- quarterly monitoring,
- escalation clauses,
- and consequences for missed targets.

---

## 3) Can it match closure economics?

### My assessment
A no-closure plan can **partially match** closure economics if the plants are severely rationalized. But **matching closure fully is difficult** unless the “no closure” language masks a very strong hidden restructuring.

Why?

Because closure removes a full cost base.  
A no-closure pact leaves behind:
- site overhead,
- residual fixed costs,
- coordination complexity,
- and the risk of future re-expansion.

Even if production volume is shifted away, the company often retains some structural cost unless the site is almost fully stripped of activity. That is why management usually sees no-closure plans as **less certain and less complete**.

---

## 4) Margin restoration by 2030: feasible, but conditional

Whether margin can return to target by 2030 depends on the **size of the gap** and the **starting point**.

A no-closure settlement can support margin recovery by 2030 if:
- savings start immediately,
- labor cost is reduced early,
- utilization improves,
- and the product mix improves at the same time.

But if:
- implementation begins late,
- labor concessions are modest,
- and capacity reductions are mostly reversible,

then the margin improvement will probably be **insufficient**.

In other words, **the timing matters as much as the amount**.  
A savings package that arrives in 2028 is much less valuable than one that is locked in during 2025–2026.

---

## 5) Execution and strike risk

This is a major issue.

A no-closure plan has higher execution risk because it relies on:
- negotiated compliance,
- internal redeployment,
- workforce acceptance,
- and stable industrial peace.

A closure plan has different risks, but typically:
- it gives clearer cost certainty,
- and fewer moving parts.

If industrial action is prolonged, the no-closure package can lose value quickly through:
- lost production,
- delayed launches,
- customer disruption,
- and management distraction.

So from a restructuring perspective, the best economic package is not just the one with the highest theoretical savings. It is the one with the best **expected value** after strike risk, timing risk, and implementation risk.

## 6) My view on the specific stakeholder claims

### To management
Your position is directionally right on the need for structural savings.  
But to make the case for closures, you still need to show:
- the quantified savings gap,
- why no-closure measures are insufficient,
- and what level of execution certainty is required.

Without that, “closure is necessary” remains a claim, not a demonstrated result.

### To labor and Lower Saxony
Your no-closure demand is understandable as a governance and employment position.  
But economically, it is only credible if it is paired with:
- genuinely large concessions,
- durable capacity reduction,
- and measurable savings.

A guarantee without hard cost reduction is not a restructuring.

### To investors and owners
Your insistence on durability is correct.  
You should focus less on the label “closure” versus “no closure” and more on:
- NPV of savings,
- certainty of implementation,
- and residual complexity.

If a no-closure deal achieves almost the same net present value as closure, it can be rational. If it doesn’t, it is value-destructive delay.

---

## 7) Bottom-line assessment

### A no-closure pact is credible only if:
- it removes a **large share** of fixed labor and overhead cost,
- permanently reduces capacity and utilization,
- is enforceable,
- and does not rely on indefinite promises.

### It is not credible if:
- it merely postpones layoffs,
- keeps excess capacity intact,
- or depends on future goodwill rather than hard commitments.

### My judgment
**A no-closure settlement is possible, but only as a strong industrial restructuring, not as a soft employment compromise.**  
If the parties cannot quantify savings and capacity reduction at a level approaching the closure case, then the no-closure option will likely fall short on 2030 margin restoration.
