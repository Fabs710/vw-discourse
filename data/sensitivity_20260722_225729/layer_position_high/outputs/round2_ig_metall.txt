We are at the point where slogans are not enough. I will respond directly.

## To the works council
I agree with your core line: **no plant closures, no compulsory redundancies, no weakening of co-determination**. That remains the union’s position as well.

Where I want to sharpen the point is this: a “capacity reduction” must not become a disguise for later closures or a slow-motion dismantling of sites. If the company is serious about an alternative, then it has to put real instruments on the table:
- reduced working time,
- internal redeployment,
- qualification,
- product allocation,
- temporary labour-cost contributions,
- and binding employment security.

That is the kind of restructuring we can negotiate. What we cannot accept is a framework where the company keeps the option of forced redundancies in its back pocket.

## To management
You still have not answered the central question: **why must the burden fall on jobs and sites first?**

You keep saying “deep structural cuts” are necessary, but you are not proving that closures or compulsory redundancies are unavoidable. That is not enough. If you want the workforce to contribute, then management and owners must also contribute in a visible way.

What is unacceptable to us:
- open-ended insecurity,
- forced redundancies,
- plant closures,
- and a one-sided wage settlement that destroys the collective framework.

If you want a deal, bring a **credible no-closure package** with measurable savings. Until then, your position remains a threat, not a solution.

## To the controlling owners
You say you want to avoid value destruction. Then support a solution that avoids industrial war.

A settlement that triggers warning strikes, escalations, and a prolonged legitimacy crisis does not protect value. And a settlement that breaks the collective wage framework and undermines trust will poison implementation.

If you want durability, then you need to back:
- site guarantees,
- job security,
- shared sacrifice,
- and owner-side responsibility.

Not just “discipline” for the workforce.

## To Lower Saxony
We appreciate that you reject closures and forced redundancies. But I need to be blunt: **that stance has to be enforced, not just stated**.

If the company comes back with a “capacity” model that hollowes out plants, we will not treat that as acceptable simply because it avoids the word “closure.” The substance matters.

So yes, we can discuss:
- temporary capacity adjustment,
- product shifts,
- flexible working time,
- and investment-linked restructuring,

but only if the outcome is a real, binding employment path through 2030.

## To institutional investors
You are right on one point: we do not support a cosmetic deal. But you are wrong if you believe closures are automatically the economically superior answer.

A negotiated settlement can be economically credible if it contains:
- durable labour-cost contributions,
- verifiable productivity gains,
- capacity adjustments without plant closures,
- and clear investment commitments.

What you have not shown is that forced redundancies and closures would create better long-term value once industrial conflict and reputational damage are priced in.

So if you want credibility, you need to stop using “efficiency” as a blanket justification for social destruction. Show the numbers, but also show the costs of conflict.

## On the moderator’s compromise options
### 1) Phased restructuring with a binding employment pact
This can be discussed — **but only with real guarantees**.  
Not a vague review clause that later opens the door to layoffs.  
Any review mechanism must be tied to:
- agreed metrics,
- joint governance,
- and no automatic path to compulsory redundancies.

### 2) Capacity reduction without formal plant closures
This is the only direction that could make sense if it stays true to substance.  
If it means shifts, product reallocations, and flexible utilization, we can talk.  
If it means starving sites until they become “uneconomic,” then we will reject it.

### 3) Burden-sharing tied to measurable investment and savings
This is the right idea, but burden-sharing must be **balanced**:
- employees contribute,
- management contributes,
- owners contribute,
- and the company commits to sites and jobs.

We are open to temporary labor-cost measures. We are not open to employees paying alone.

## My answer to the key question: what can move us?
A package that could move the union must contain all of the following:
1. **No plant closures**
2. **No compulsory redundancies**
3. **A binding employment and site pact through 2030**
4. **Temporary, negotiated employee contributions**
5. **Management and owner contributions as well**
6. **Capacity reduction through reversible measures, not site destruction**
7. **Preservation of the collective wage framework**

If those conditions are not met, then there is no basis for agreement.

## Final position
Let me be clear: we are not drifting toward consensus just because everyone says “restructuring” in a serious tone.

- We agree that Volkswagen needs change.
- We disagree that jobs and sites must be sacrificed to achieve it.
- We are prepared for a fair contribution.
- We are not prepared for employees to carry the whole burden.

If management wants a settlement, it must stop treating closures and forced redundancies as the default option and come back with a real industrial alternative. If it refuses, then industrial pressure will continue and escalate.
