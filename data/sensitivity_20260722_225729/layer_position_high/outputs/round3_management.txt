Let me be equally direct.

We are now in round 3, and the fundamental problem has not changed: **Volkswagen still has a structural cost and capacity problem**, and so far the employee side is offering **guarantees, labels, and political red lines** instead of a package that actually closes the gap.

## Where I agree
I agree on three points:

- **A cosmetic deal is useless.** If we do not materially reduce cost and capacity, we are just postponing the crisis.
- **Industrial conflict is costly.** Warning strikes, escalation, and a prolonged confrontation damage output, reputation, and implementation.
- **Burden-sharing must be real.** I am not asking the workforce alone to pay for this.

But agreement on those points does **not** change the core economics.

## Where I disagree

### 1) “No plant closures” is not a credible starting point
The works council and IG Metall keep repeating that closures are unacceptable and that management has not “proven” they are necessary.

That reverses the burden of reality.

We are the ones confronting shrinking European demand, strong low-cost competition, and a cost base that is above target. I do not need to prove that a preferred solution is pleasant; I need to secure one that works. If the employee side wants to exclude closures, then it must show a package that delivers **equivalent durable savings and capacity reduction**. So far, it has not.

A promise to keep every German site open through 2030 may be politically convenient. It is not automatically economically credible.

### 2) “No compulsory redundancies” cannot be an unconditional veto
I prefer to avoid forced redundancies, but I will not rule them out in principle if we cannot secure enough structural reduction otherwise.

If the workforce wants to avoid them, then it must help deliver:
- material labour-cost relief,
- real capacity reduction,
- productivity gains,
- and durable flexibility.

“Temporary” wage moderation and voluntary exits may help, but I have seen no serious evidence that they are enough by themselves.

### 3) Capacity reduction without closures can be part of the answer — but only if it is real
I am not attached to formal closures as a matter of ideology. If we can achieve sufficient structural savings through product reallocation, shift consolidation, and utilisation reduction, that is preferable.

But I am not going to endorse a “capacity reduction” that leaves plants intact on paper while hollowing them out so slowly that the cost problem remains unresolved. That is not restructuring. That is delay.

## To the works council
You keep saying the burden of proof is on management. Fine — then here is my counterpoint:

You have not proved that your preferred package can close the gap.

You say:
- working-time flexibility,
- qualification,
- internal redeployment,
- voluntary exits,
- productivity agreements,
- and temporary labour-cost measures.

Those are tools. They are not yet a complete solution. Show me the quantified savings, the timeline, the durability, and the capacity impact. Show me why this is enough to restore the brand’s return-on-sales target and fund future investment through 2030.

Right now, I hear a lot of **protection of outcomes** and not enough **production of economics**.

### To IG Metall
You say employees can contribute temporarily, but not alone. I agree with the principle.

But “temporary” is the problem. Temporary relief does not solve a structural overcapacity and cost issue unless it is large enough and durable enough to change the trajectory.

If you want a no-closure settlement, then come with:
- quantified labour-cost contribution,
- verified productivity commitments,
- binding capacity reduction,
- and a duration long enough to matter.

Otherwise, you are asking management to trust a promise that does not yet solve the problem.

### To Lower Saxony
I respect your role and your blocking position. But I will say this bluntly: the state cannot define “industrial policy” as preserving every site regardless of economics.

Volkswagen needs a viable future, not a frozen footprint. If the structure stays too expensive, then the region loses anyway — through weaker investment, lower margins, and eventually less employment, not more.

So yes, I will work within the governance framework. No, I will not accept that your political preference substitutes for industrial reality.

### To the controlling owners
You are right that endless conflict destroys value. You are also right that a weak compromise is not enough.

But then you must also accept that value destruction comes from **inaction** as well as conflict. If we keep funding excess capacity, we are simply shifting pain into the future and starving investment in future products and technology.

If you want a durable answer, support a package that actually reduces the cost base. Do not ask management to pretend that site preservation alone equals strategy.

### To institutional investors
You are right to insist on measurable improvement. That is exactly my position too.

But do not tell me that the only serious answer is the most aggressive one. The serious answer is the one that:
- lowers costs materially,
- reduces overcapacity,
- is executable,
- and does not collapse into industrial war.

I am open to a no-closure route **if** it is genuinely structural. I am not open to a symbolic arrangement that leaves the company with the same burden and a nicer press release.

## What I need to see
If the employee side wants to rule out closures and forced redundancies, then bring a package that includes all of the following:

1. **Material labour-cost reduction**
2. **Real capacity reduction**
3. **Verified productivity gains**
4. **Internal redeployment and retraining**
5. **Working-time flexibility with measurable economic impact**
6. **A duration that is structurally meaningful**
7. **No mechanism that simply defers the problem to 2030**

That is the threshold. Not slogans. Not absolute vetoes. Not semantic arguments about what counts as a closure.

## My bottom line
I am not here to force closures for their own sake. I am here to secure a **durable reset** of Volkswagen’s cost and capacity base.

So let me state it plainly:

- **I agree** that a negotiated solution is preferable.
- **I agree** that burden-sharing should be broad.
- **I do not agree** that “no closures” and “no compulsory redundancies” can be treated as absolute if the savings are insufficient.
- **I will not accept** a cosmetic settlement that leaves Volkswagen structurally uncompetitive.

If you want my support, bring me a package that delivers the numbers and the durability. If not, then we will have to consider more decisive measures, because delaying structural adjustment is not an option.
