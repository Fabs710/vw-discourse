From the perspective of dispersed capital-market shareholders, the central test is simple: **does the package credibly restore Volkswagen’s margins and competitiveness, or does it merely defer the problem?**

Right now, I hear three things I agree with:

1. **Management is right that the current cost base is too high.**
   If German operations remain structurally uncompetitive, then the equity case remains weak. We cannot support a settlement that leaves the brand’s return on sales below target for years.

2. **The workforce is right that a cosmetic deal is not acceptable.**
   A paper compromise that preserves all the inefficiencies, while claiming victory, would be value-destructive. We would not back that.

3. **Lower Saxony and the controlling owners are right that a drawn-out conflict destroys value.**
   Prolonged strikes, governance paralysis, and reputational damage would hurt earnings and investor confidence.

Where I differ is on the idea that **“no closures” or “no forced layoffs” can be absolute red lines regardless of economics**. From an investor standpoint, those are not the relevant red lines. The relevant red line is **structural underperformance**. If the company cannot reach a competitive cost base without substantial capacity adjustment, then preserving every site and every guarantee is not value-preserving; it is value erosion.

So my position is:

- **We support real structural restructuring.**
- **We do not support a settlement that leaves costs uncompetitive.**
- **We do not support indefinite protection of overcapacity.**
- **We do support an agreement if it delivers durable savings, implementation certainty, and a clear path back to margin improvement.**

To management: you need to show more than a threat of closures. You need a **credible, quantified plan** with:
- durable labour-cost reduction,
- capacity rationalisation,
- measurable productivity gains,
- and a realistic timeline to restore margins.

If plant closures are the only way to get there, then say so clearly and justify it. But if there are alternative measures that achieve comparable savings, we would prefer those because they reduce execution risk. What we cannot accept is delay wrapped in compromise language.

To the works council and IG Metall: your insistence on no forced redundancies and no closures is understandable, but **it is not sufficient** unless you can demonstrate that the alternative delivers equivalent economics. “Fair burden-sharing” is not enough by itself. We need to see numbers, permanence, and enforceability. If the package does not materially improve competitiveness, then it is not a settlement we can support.

To Lower Saxony and the family owners: we appreciate the focus on stability, but stability without competitiveness is not a strategy. A viable Volkswagen is the objective. If that requires difficult restructuring, then governance stakeholders should not block it simply to preserve the status quo.

Our bottom line is this:  
**We will back the path that most credibly restores risk-adjusted value.**  
At present, that means a hard-nosed restructuring with real savings, not a symbolic deal. If the eventual package is weak on cost reduction, we will view it as inadequate and will treat it accordingly in our capital-allocation and market assessment.
