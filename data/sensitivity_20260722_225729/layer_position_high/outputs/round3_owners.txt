I will be direct.

I agree with everyone on one point: **Volkswagen needs a restructuring that is real, durable, and economically credible.** A cosmetic deal that merely postpones the problem would destroy value, and I will not support that.

Where I disagree is on the increasingly rigid framing from labor and Lower Saxony that **plant closures and compulsory redundancies are off the table in principle, regardless of the economics**. That is not a viable governance position if the company’s cost base and capacity genuinely remain out of line with the market.

## To management
You are right that the status quo is not acceptable.

But you still have not given the owners and the market a sufficiently disciplined, quantified case. “Deep structural cuts” is not enough. I need to see:
- the actual savings target,
- the exact capacity gap,
- which measures are essential,
- and why the package you want is superior to a negotiated alternative.

If you cannot show that clearly, then you are asking for flexibility without proving necessity. That is not good enough.

At the same time, I do **not** support a management approach that treats closures and forced layoffs as the easiest default option. If you push the company into a prolonged conflict, you will destroy value even if the spreadsheet initially looks cleaner.

## To the works council
I respect the insistence that workers should not bear the full burden. That is fair.

I also agree that a weak “capacity reduction” can become closure by another name. That concern is legitimate.

But your position still does not answer the central economic question: **what package produces enough durable savings to restore competitiveness?**  
You keep listing acceptable tools:
- working-time flexibility,
- internal redeployment,
- retraining,
- voluntary exits,
- productivity measures,
- temporary labor-cost contributions.

Those may all be part of the answer. But by themselves, you have not yet shown that they can close the structural gap.

So I cannot accept “no closures, no compulsory redundancies” as an absolute principle unless you can demonstrate a credible, quantified alternative that actually works.

## To IG Metall
I agree that a one-sided burden on employees would be unacceptable.

I also agree that industrial peace has value. A drawn-out conflict would hurt Volkswagen badly.

But industrial peace is not a substitute for structural economics. Temporary wage moderation and flexibility are only meaningful if they are large enough and durable enough to change the trajectory of the business.

So I am open to a negotiated package. I am not open to a package that simply protects the current footprint and hopes for the best.

## To Lower Saxony
You are right that Volkswagen is not just a balance sheet. It is an industrial base and a regional anchor.

But your blocking position cannot become a veto over any meaningful adjustment. If capacity is genuinely too high, then preserving every site in its current form may simply delay decline.

I will support a no-closure outcome only if it is economically credible. That means real capacity rationalization, not symbolic adjustments.

## To institutional investors
You are right to insist on measurable improvement.

But I do not accept the idea that the only serious answer is the harshest one. A destructive conflict can easily wipe out the benefits of a blunt restructuring.

What I will support is the solution that is:
- durable,
- executable,
- and capable of restoring margins.

If that can be done without closures and compulsory redundancies, that is preferable. If it cannot, then the economics must be faced honestly.

## My position on the compromise options
### 1) Conditional no-closure pact with hard triggers
This is only acceptable if the triggers are genuinely about performance and restructuring discipline, **not** a disguised backdoor to automatic layoffs or closures.

If the trigger system merely delays the same fight, it is not a solution.

### 2) Capacity reduction through product and shift rationalization
This is the most plausible route if it is real.

I could support:
- shift reductions,
- product reallocation,
- internal transfers,
- retraining,
- and utilization changes,

if they produce measurable savings and keep the company governable.

I will not support a “capacity reduction” that preserves all labels but leaves the business structurally unchanged.

### 3) Burden-sharing package
This is necessary.

But burden-sharing must be balanced:
- employees contribute,
- management contributes,
- owners contribute,
- and the company invests in the future.

I would support a package that includes visible management restraint and credible owner backing, but only if it delivers durable improvement.

## What would move me
A no-closure settlement could gain my support if it contains all of the following:
1. material and quantified labor-cost reduction,
2. real capacity reduction,
3. verifiable productivity gains,
4. binding implementation milestones,
5. a credible path back to target margins,
6. no open-ended postponement of adjustment.

That is the bar.

## Bottom line
I am not here to force closures for their own sake. I am here to protect the long-term value of the company.

So my position remains:

- I support restructuring.
- I support burden-sharing.
- I do **not** support a symbolic compromise.
- I do **not** accept “no closures” as a principle unless it is backed by a truly credible economic alternative.

Bring a package that restores competitiveness with execution certainty, and I will support it. Bring me a political settlement that leaves Volkswagen structurally weak, and I will not.
