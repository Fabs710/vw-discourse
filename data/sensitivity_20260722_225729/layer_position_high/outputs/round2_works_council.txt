Let me be equally direct in round 2:

**We agree that Volkswagen needs a restructuring. We do not agree that restructuring requires plant closures or compulsory redundancies.**  
That is the line management, investors, and too often others keep trying to blur. We will not let that happen.

### Where I agree
- **There is a competitiveness problem.** No one on the workforce side is denying that.
- **A cosmetic solution is not acceptable.** If savings are only on paper, the company still loses.
- **A drawn-out conflict hurts everyone.** That is exactly why management should stop escalating toward closures and forced layoffs.

### Where I disagree
#### To Group & Brand Management
You keep saying “deep structural cuts” are necessary, but you are still using that as a blanket justification for hitting jobs and sites first.

That is not a plan; that is a threat.

If you want our cooperation, then show us a restructuring that does **not** destroy German plants and does **not** impose forced redundancies. You have alternatives on the table already:
- working-time reductions,
- internal redeployment,
- qualification,
- shifting product allocation,
- productivity gains,
- management and shareholder contribution,
- and temporary, negotiated labor-cost measures.

What you have **not** shown is that closures are unavoidable. Until you do, we reject them outright.

#### To Institutional Investors
You say you back the “path that most credibly restores risk-adjusted value.” Fine. Then listen carefully:  
A settlement that triggers warning strikes, a public conflict, legal battles, and governance deadlock is **not** value-preserving. It is value destruction.

So do not pretend closures are the only economically serious answer. They may be the fastest blunt instrument, but they are also the most destructive. If you want durable value, support a negotiated package that keeps the workforce onside and preserves production in Germany.

#### To the Controlling Owners
You say you do not want value destruction. Then stop backing an approach that pushes the company into a confrontation with its own workforce and co-determination bodies.

A “hard-nosed restructuring” that violates the social compact is not strength. It is short-sightedness. The company’s long-term value depends on industrial peace, skills retention, and trust. You do not restore trust by threatening sites.

#### To Lower Saxony
We appreciate your support for no closures and no forced redundancies. But let me be clear: it is not enough to say you oppose closures while accepting a weakened employment framework behind the scenes.

If there is to be any restructuring, it must preserve:
- every German plant,
- co-determination,
- the job-security framework,
- and a binding employment path through 2030.

A “capacity reduction” that simply empties plants over time is closure by another name if it leads to job destruction and site hollowing-out. We will not accept semantic tricks.

### On the moderator’s compromise options
#### 1) Phased restructuring with a binding employment pact
A time-limited pact can be discussed **only if it contains real guarantees**. Not vague intentions, not escape hatches, not review clauses that are just a doorway to layoffs later.

If the “review” is a backdoor to closures, we reject it.

#### 2) Capacity reduction without formal plant closures
This sounds less aggressive, but we will judge it by substance, not labels.  
If capacity reduction means:
- fewer shifts,
- product reallocations,
- internal transfer,
- modernization,
- and temporary flexibility,

then it can be discussed.

If it means:
- job cuts,
- site erosion,
- or making one plant “uneconomic” so closure becomes inevitable later,

then it is unacceptable.

#### 3) Burden-sharing tied to measurable investment and savings
Yes, burden-sharing matters — but it must be **fair**.

We are prepared to discuss temporary employee contributions only if:
- management gives up a meaningful share,
- owners support the company, not just demand sacrifices,
- and the result is a **binding guarantee for all German sites and jobs to 2030**.

No guarantees, no deal.

### My answer to the moderator’s pointed question
**What specific measures would we accept?**

We would accept only measures that:
1. keep every German plant open,
2. rule out compulsory redundancies,
3. preserve co-determination,
4. protect the job-security framework,
5. and deliver savings through reversible, negotiated adjustments.

That means:
- working-time flexibility,
- temporary wage moderation within a fair burden-sharing package,
- natural attrition only,
- internal redeployment,
- retraining and qualification,
- productivity agreements,
- investment commitments,
- and product allocation that secures plant utilization.

What we will **not** accept:
- closure of any German plant,
- forced layoffs,
- relocation that undermines sites,
- or a “temporary” deal that is really a staged dismantling of jobs.

### Bottom line
Management has not met the burden of proof for closures or compulsory redundancies.  
Investors should not reward confrontation.  
Owners should not hide behind “competitiveness” while dismantling industrial trust.  
Lower Saxony should use its leverage to enforce a genuine no-closure settlement.

So my position remains unchanged:

**No plant closures. No compulsory redundancies. No weakening of co-determination.**  
If Volkswagen wants a real solution, it must come back with a package that protects all German plants and all jobs through 2030. Otherwise, we will continue to resist with every means available.
