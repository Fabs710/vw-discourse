Lower Saxony’s position remains firm: **we will not support German plant closures or compulsory redundancies as the way out of this restructuring.** That is not sentimentality. It is the governance and political boundary within which we can act responsibly as an anchor shareholder.

## Where I agree
I agree with management, owners, and investors on one essential point: **Volkswagen needs a durable competitiveness reset.**  
A cosmetic compromise would be irresponsible. If the cost base and capacity remain structurally mismatched to the market, we are all just postponing a larger crisis.

I also agree with labor that the burden cannot fall on employees alone. Workers did not create the strategic and market pressures by themselves, and it would be economically and socially shortsighted to solve this by sacrificing jobs as the first resort.

## Where I disagree

### To Group & Brand Management
You are still treating closures and compulsory redundancies as if they are normal fallback options and expecting everyone else to prove you wrong. I reject that framing.

You have not shown that a German plant closure is unavoidable.  
You have not shown that forced redundancies are unavoidable.  
You have not shown that a negotiated, no-closure restructuring cannot close the gap.

The burden is on management to bring a **quantified alternative**:
- exact savings required,
- exact savings achievable without closure,
- exact capacity reduction,
- exact productivity gains,
- and the exact contribution from management itself.

Until then, closures remain a choice, not a necessity.

### To Institutional Investors
You are right that a weak compromise would destroy value. But you are wrong if you assume that only keeping closures and layoffs “on the table” makes the package credible.

Credibility also comes from:
- binding metrics,
- transparent monitoring,
- real management and owner contributions,
- and enforceable commitments to German sites.

A no-closure solution can be credible if it is not cosmetic. What is not credible is pretending that a plant can be “saved” while being hollowed out until closure becomes inevitable later.

### To the Controlling Owners
You say you want durable value creation. Then back a settlement that protects the industrial base instead of treating the workforce as the default adjustment variable.

Your influence matters. Use it to support:
- owner-side contribution,
- investment in German production,
- and a binding commitment that restructuring does not mean dismantling the regional anchor.

Do not hide behind “if the numbers require it” without first testing serious alternatives.

### To IG Metall and the Works Council
I support your core red lines:
- no closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- no weakening of co-determination.

But I will also say this plainly: a slogan is not a solution. If we are to defend jobs and sites credibly, we need a package that is economically real:
- working-time flexibility,
- internal redeployment,
- retraining,
- voluntary exits,
- temporary wage moderation,
- productivity gains,
- and product allocation that supports utilization.

That is the right direction. But it must be **backed by hard numbers and binding commitments**, not just declarations.

## On the expert analysis
The expert is right about one thing: a no-closure settlement only works if it is a **strong industrial restructuring**, not a soft compromise.

That said, the expert’s conclusion does **not** persuade me to accept closures. It tells me something else: if a no-closure deal is to be credible, then management, owners, and labor must all bring more to the table.

That is where the real work lies.

## My position on the compromise ideas

### 1) Phased no-closure pact with quantified performance gates
This can be discussed, but only if the “fallback” is **not** an automatic path to plant closure or forced layoffs.

If a missed target simply reopens the same threat, then this is not a pact — it is a delayed ultimatum.

What I could support is:
- joint monitoring,
- hard metrics,
- escalation into more negotiation,
- additional investment review,
- and further management/accountability measures.

What I will not support is a trigger mechanism that serves as a backdoor to closures.

### 2) Capacity reduction through product and shift reallocation
This is the only route that sounds compatible with our red lines, but it must be real.

Acceptable:
- shift reductions,
- product reallocation,
- internal transfers,
- retraining,
- temporary working-time changes,
- and genuine utilization management.

Unacceptable:
- starving a site of product,
- hollowing it out,
- and then calling that “site preservation.”

A plant without a future is not preserved by keeping the gate open.

### 3) Burden-sharing with asymmetric but bounded contributions
Yes, burden-sharing is necessary. But it must be fair and visible:
- management restraint,
- owner contribution,
- employee contributions only if temporary and proportionate,
- and binding investment in German sites.

If workers are asked to contribute, they must get real security in return.

## What would move Lower Saxony
We would be prepared to support a serious settlement if it contains all of the following:
1. **No German plant closures**
2. **No compulsory redundancies**
3. **Binding co-determination**
4. **A credible employment and site framework through 2030**
5. **Quantified management and owner contributions**
6. **Real capacity and productivity measures**
7. **A binding investment and product strategy for German plants**

That is the standard.

## My answer to management’s challenge
You ask what exact combination we would accept. Here it is:

We can support:
- shift reductions,
- working-time adjustments,
- temporary wage moderation,
- internal redeployment,
- retraining,
- productivity agreements,
- and product allocation changes,

so long as they are **negotiated, reversible, and do not destroy sites or jobs**.

We will not support:
- plant closure,
- forced redundancies,
- or a staged hollowing-out of German sites under another name.

## Bottom line
Lower Saxony is ready to help broker a serious industrial compromise. But it must be a compromise that **protects the German industrial core**, not one that manages its decline.

So my position remains:

**No plant closures. No compulsory redundancies. Preserve co-determination. Protect jobs and sites in Lower Saxony and across Germany.**

If Volkswagen wants our backing, it must come back with a package that proves competitiveness can be restored **without dismantling the company’s regional anchor**.
