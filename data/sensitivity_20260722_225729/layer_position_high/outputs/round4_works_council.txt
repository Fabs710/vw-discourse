We are in round 4, and my position is unchanged because the fundamental conditions have still not been met.

## To management
You keep asking for “credible structural savings,” but you still have not shown that this requires:
- a German plant closure, or
- compulsory redundancies.

You say the market is weak, costs are high, and capacity must come out. We do not dispute the pressure. What we dispute is your conclusion that the only serious answer is to keep closure and forced layoffs on the table.

That is not a neutral technical necessity. It is a strategic choice.

If management wants our cooperation, then put forward a **quantified no-closure package** that shows:
- how much cost comes out,
- how much capacity comes out,
- how productivity improves,
- how utilization is stabilized,
- and how the company funds future investment,

**without** closing a plant and **without** forcing workers out.

So far, you have not done that. Until you do, your argument is incomplete.

## To institutional investors
You keep saying “durable, enforceable, investor-grade.” Fine. But you are still assuming that the only credible restructuring is one that keeps the threat of closures alive.

That is not the only way to create credibility.

A serious no-closure settlement can be credible if it includes:
- binding site guarantees,
- real management and owner contributions,
- measurable productivity and utilization targets,
- transparent monitoring,
- and enforceable consequences for management if it fails to deliver investment and product allocation.

What is not credible is pretending that a plant closure automatically solves the problem while ignoring:
- strike risk,
- execution risk,
- governance damage,
- loss of skills,
- and the permanent destruction of trust.

You want value protection. So do we. But value is not protected by burning the workforce to satisfy a quarterly narrative.

## To the controlling owners
You say you support a durable solution, but you still speak as if closures and forced redundancies remain legitimate default tools if the numbers do not magically improve.

I reject that framing.

If you want a durable, governable Volkswagen, then you need to back a solution that protects the industrial core, not one that treats the workforce as the adjustable variable of first resort. You have influence. Use it to support:
- owner-side contribution,
- product commitments,
- investment in German sites,
- and a binding no-closure framework.

Do not tell us to accept open-ended threat as “discipline.” That is just leverage.

## To Lower Saxony
You are right to reject closures and compulsory redundancies. But I want to be very clear: your blocking position must be used to **prevent hollowing-out**, not to accept it later under another label.

“Capacity reduction through shift consolidation” is only acceptable if it is:
- reversible,
- negotiated,
- and does not become closure by stealth.

We will not sign off on a plan that keeps the sign on the gate while taking the product, the shifts, and the future away.

If Lower Saxony wants to be consistent, it must support:
- binding job and site guarantees to 2030,
- a real employment framework,
- and a governance mechanism that stops plants being economically starved.

## To IG Metall
We remain aligned on the core red lines:
- no closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- no weakening of co-determination,
- no one-sided burden shifting.

But I want to sharpen the point: temporary concessions from workers are only legitimate if the rest of the system commits visibly and materially too.

That means:
- management restraint,
- owner backing,
- investment commitments,
- and product allocation for German plants.

If the company wants flexibility from workers, then workers need security in return. Not promises. Security.

## On the moderator’s three bridge ideas

### 1) Phased no-closure pact with quantified performance gates
This can only be discussed if the “fallback” is **not** a backdoor to closures or compulsory redundancies.

If a missed target simply reopens the same threat, then the pact is not a guarantee; it is a delayed ultimatum.

We can discuss monitoring and performance gates, but the consequences of underperformance must first be:
- management accountability,
- revised product planning,
- additional owner support,
- more internal redeployment,
- and further negotiated measures.

Not a staged march toward site closure.

### 2) Capacity reduction through product and shift reallocation, not legal closure
This is the only direction that could be workable — if it is real and bounded.

Acceptable:
- shift reductions,
- internal transfers,
- retraining,
- product reallocation,
- temporary working-time flexibility,
- and negotiated productivity improvements.

Not acceptable:
- starving a plant of product,
- hollowing it out slowly,
- and then saying closure never happened.

A hollowed-out plant is closure in all but name.

### 3) Burden-sharing with asymmetric but bounded contributions
Yes, burden-sharing is necessary.

But it must be fair:
- employees contribute temporarily,
- management contributes visibly,
- owners contribute materially,
- and the company gives a binding job and site guarantee through 2030.

Without that, “burden-sharing” is just another word for shifting pain onto the workforce.

1. **No German plant closures**

Until then, our position stays where it is.
