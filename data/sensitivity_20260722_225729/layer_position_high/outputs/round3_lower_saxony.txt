Lower Saxony’s position remains clear: **we will not support German plant closures or compulsory redundancies as the solution to Volkswagen’s restructuring.** That is not a negotiating flourish; it is the boundary within which we can act as a responsible anchor shareholder and political actor.

## Where I agree

I agree with management, owners, and investors on one essential point: **Volkswagen does need a durable competitiveness reset.**  
A cosmetic compromise would be irresponsible. If the company’s cost base and capacity remain structurally mismatched to market reality, then we would all be storing up a larger crisis.

I also agree with the workforce side that **employees did not create this problem alone** and should not be the only group asked to carry it. A settlement that simply transfers the burden onto jobs would be socially unacceptable and, in the long run, economically shortsighted.

## Where I disagree

### To Group & Brand Management
You keep saying the economics may require closures or compulsory redundancies “if the numbers demand it.” That may be true in the abstract, but you still have **not shown that they are unavoidable**.

So far, what I hear is:
- a preference for maximum flexibility,
- pressure to keep all options open,
- and a refusal to commit to a no-closure route before alternatives are fully exhausted.

That is not enough.

If you want our support, you need to bring forward a **quantified alternative** that shows:
- what exact savings are required,
- what can be achieved through capacity rationalization without closures,
- what productivity gains are realistic,
- and what management itself is prepared to contribute.

Until then, closures remain unacceptable to us.

### To Institutional Investors
You are right that a weak deal would not be value-preserving. But you are wrong if you imply that the only credible answer is to leave plant closures and forced redundancies on the table from the start.

That posture ignores the governance reality of Volkswagen. It also ignores the political and industrial damage that a confrontation would create. A solution that triggers strikes, legal conflict, and a legitimacy crisis is not “discipline.” It is value destruction.

We are not here to defend inefficiency. We are here to defend a **viable industrial base**.

### To the Works Council and IG Metall
I support your core insistence on:
- no plant closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- and no weakening of co-determination.

But I also want to be equally clear: **simply saying “no change” is not enough**. If Volkswagen is to remain strong, we need real restructuring through:
- working-time flexibility,
- internal redeployment,
- retraining,
- productivity improvements,
- temporary and negotiated labour-cost contributions,
- and product allocation measures that support utilization.

What we cannot support is a frozen status quo dressed up as protection.

### To the Controlling Owners
You are right that Volkswagen cannot afford paralysis. But “discipline” cannot mean forcing through a solution that destroys trust in the co-determination model and the social compact around the company.

The VW Law and Lower Saxony’s role exist precisely to prevent decisions that would sacrifice regional stability for short-term balance sheet repair. That is not a technicality; it is part of the company’s constitutional reality.

## On the compromise options

### 1) Conditional no-closure pact with hard triggers
This is worth discussing **only if the triggers are not a backdoor to layoffs or closures**.

A real pact would need:
- joint monitoring,
- clear metrics,
- management accountability,
- and additional negotiated measures before any more drastic step is even considered.

If the trigger mechanism is just a delayed route back to the same threat, we will not accept it.

### 2) Capacity reduction through product and shift rationalization, not legal closure
This is the only route on the table that could become workable — if it is honest.

Acceptable:
- shift adjustments,
- product reallocation,
- internal transfer,
- retraining,
- and flexible utilization.

Unacceptable:
- starving a site of product,
- hollowing it out,
- and then claiming closure was never declared.

A hollowed-out plant is not a solution.

### 3) Burden-sharing package with asymmetric contributions and a review clause
Yes, burden-sharing is necessary. But it must be **balanced**.

That means:
- employees contribute temporarily,
- management contributes visibly,
- owners contribute materially,
- and the company commits to sites, jobs, and investment in Germany.

Without that balance, burden-sharing becomes one-sided sacrifice.

## What would move Lower Saxony
We would be prepared to support a serious restructuring package if it contains all of the following:

1. **No German plant closures**
2. **No compulsory redundancies**
3. **No weakening of co-determination**
4. **A binding employment and site framework through 2030**
5. **Negotiated, reversible savings measures**
6. **A credible investment and product strategy for German plants**
7. **Real management and owner-side contribution**

That is the standard. Not slogans, not threats, and not semantic rebranding of closures as “rationalization.”

## My answer to management’s challenge
You ask what exact combination we would accept. I will answer directly:

We can support:
- shift reductions,
- working-time adjustments,
- temporary wage moderation,
- internal redeployment,
- retraining,
- productivity agreements,
- and product allocation changes,
so long as they are **reversible, negotiated, and do not destroy sites or jobs**.

We will not support:
- plant closure,
- forced redundancies,
- or a staged hollowing-out of German sites under another name.

## Final position
Lower Saxony is ready to help broker a solution. But it must be a real industrial compromise, not a managed retreat from the German base.

So my position remains:

**No plant closures. No compulsory redundancies. Preserve co-determination. Protect the German industrial core.**  
If Volkswagen wants our backing, it must come back with a package that proves competitiveness can be restored **without dismantling the company’s regional anchor.**
