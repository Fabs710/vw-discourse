I’ll be direct.

## My position
Volkswagen needs a **durable competitiveness reset**. I support a restructuring that materially improves margins, fixes overcapacity, and restores investment capacity. I do **not** support a cosmetic deal that preserves the current cost structure and simply postpones the problem.

That means I am open to **non-closure alternatives** — but only if they are **quantitatively equivalent** in savings and durable in effect. If they are not, then they are not a serious substitute.

## Where I agree

### With management
You are right that:
- the status quo is not sustainable,
- delay destroys value,
- temporary measures alone are not enough,
- and any deal must be economically meaningful, not politically comfortable.

I also agree that the burden of proof sits with whoever claims a non-closure package can close the gap. That gap must be shown site by site, not argued abstractly.

### With labour representatives
You are right that the workforce should not be made to carry the whole burden. A durable settlement needs:
- burden-sharing,
- investment commitments,
- and a real industrial plan for Germany.

I also agree that a reckless closure-first approach can create lasting damage, labor conflict, and execution risk.

### With Lower Saxony
You are right that Volkswagen’s German footprint is strategically important and that the company cannot be managed as if it were a purely financial asset. Social stability and industrial depth matter.

## Where I disagree

### To the works council and IG Metall
Your “no closures, no compulsory redundancies” line is understandable, but it is **not yet a restructuring plan**. It is a red line.

If you want to rule out closures, then you must show how the same savings are achieved through:
- meaningful capacity reduction,
- redeployment at scale,
- shift and working-time changes,
- voluntary exits in sufficient volume,
- and durable labor-cost relief.

So far, I hear strong resistance, but not a fully credible alternative that closes the gap. Without that, you are asking the company to believe in a better outcome without proof.

### To Lower Saxony
Your role under the VW Law is important, but it cannot become a veto against necessary adaptation. If governance is used to block all hard choices, Volkswagen will remain uncompetitive and all stakeholders will lose. Long-term regional protection requires a viable company, not an untouchable footprint.

### To management
I support seriousness, but not closure as a reflex. If alternative measures can deliver the same financial result with less damage, they should be preferred. The burden is on management to show when non-closure measures are insufficient, not merely more difficult.

## What I will support
I could support a settlement only if it includes all of the following:

1. **Durable cost reduction**
   - Real structural improvement, not temporary optics.

2. **A material employee contribution**
   - Wage moderation, working-time flexibility, voluntary exits, redesigned guarantees, and internal mobility.

3. **A credible industrial plan for Germany**
   - Product allocation, future-tech investment, and site-specific commitments that justify the sacrifices.

4. **A stable governance outcome**
   - No prolonged conflict that destroys value, reputation, or execution capability.

## What I will not support
- A package that leaves Volkswagen structurally uncompetitive
- A cosmetic no-closure deal that merely postpones the crisis
- A one-sided burden shift onto labor
- A closure-first approach that ignores viable alternatives
- Any outcome that permanently damages the enterprise’s value or destabilizes governance

## On the expert input
The expert is right about one crucial thing: this must be decided on **numbers**, not slogans. If the problem is fixed-cost overcapacity, non-closure tools may not fully substitute for closure. That is exactly why I want a proper site-by-site model before anyone declares victory.

## Bottom line
The question is not whether the restructuring is hard. It will be.  
The question is whether it **restores competitiveness durably** without destroying industrial capability or trust.

If the employee side can present a numbers-based alternative that truly closes the cost gap, I will consider it seriously. If not, then management’s restructuring logic remains stronger than the objections to it.

I am not here to bless a compromise for its own sake. I am here to protect the long-term value of Volkswagen as a viable industrial company.
