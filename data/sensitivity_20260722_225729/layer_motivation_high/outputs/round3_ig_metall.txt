I’ll respond plainly.

## Where I agree
I agree with everyone on the core diagnosis:
- Volkswagen needs a durable restructuring.
- Delay is not an option.
- The cost base is too high.
- A cosmetic deal that preserves the full problem would fail the company.

I also agree that the workforce must contribute. IG Metall is not defending a status quo that ignores the crisis.

## Where I disagree
I do **not** accept the claim that plant closures or compulsory redundancies are the necessary price of seriousness.

That is not a proven necessity. It is a management choice, and so far I have not seen a convincing numbers-based case that says there is no other way to close the gap.

### To management
You keep saying “show the numbers.” Fair enough. But then show them properly:
- exact savings required,
- exact cost gap by site and brand,
- what non-closure measures have been tested,
- how much capacity reduction is actually needed,
- and why voluntary exits, working-time changes, redeployment, and wage contributions are insufficient.

Without that, “closures may be needed” remains an assertion, not a conclusion.

And let me be clear: if management insists on keeping closures and forced layoffs as the default answer, then it should expect resistance, not consent.

## On the works council
I support the works council’s core red lines:
- no compulsory redundancies,
- no one-sided burden shifting,
- real co-determination,
- and a serious industrial future for every German site.

But I also have to say this directly: simply repeating “keep every plant open” is not yet a complete restructuring answer either. If we want a durable settlement, we need a package that materially improves competitiveness.

That means the employee side is prepared to discuss:
- temporary wage restraint,
- working-time flexibility,
- voluntary exits,
- reduced bonuses or other internal cost relief,
- retraining and redeployment,
- and capacity reduction through internal reallocation.

But that only becomes acceptable if it is matched by:
- binding job security,
- no forced redundancies,
- no plant closures,
- and clear investment and product commitments in Germany.

## To Lower Saxony
I appreciate your support for a negotiated route and your warning against a closure-first logic. That matters.

But I agree with you only up to a point: a phase-based approach is useful only if it is not a hidden path to closures. If “review” means “we’ll keep you open until management decides otherwise,” then that is not trust-building.

Any sequencing must start from a serious commitment to preserve sites and jobs, not from a preloaded closure option.

## To the owners and investors
You are right about one thing: the settlement must be durable and economically meaningful.

You are wrong if you think that can only be achieved by forcing the workforce to absorb all the pain. That would destroy trust and provoke conflict, which is exactly what a durable solution should avoid.

If you want lasting value, then the burden has to be shared:
- owners accept lower short-term returns,
- management adjusts footprint and execution,
- labour contributes to cost relief and flexibility,
- and the company invests in the future of its German base.

## What could move the union
I want to be explicit about what can move us.

A real agreement is possible if it contains all of the following:
1. **No compulsory redundancies**
2. **No German plant closures**
3. **Binding protection for jobs and sites through 2030**
4. **Material but negotiated labour contributions**
   - temporary wage restraint
   - working-time flexibility
   - voluntary exits
   - redeployment
5. **Investment and product commitments for German plants**
6. **A durable collective agreement that protects the wage framework**

That is a fair burden-sharing package. It is not surrender.

## What I cannot accept
I cannot accept:
- closures as a “fallback”,
- forced layoffs,
- open-ended job insecurity,
- or a settlement where workers pay first and management/owners only promise later.

That would not be a partnership. It would be a transfer of risk onto the workforce.

## [SUMMON: industrial restructuring accountant / site network analyst]
I think we need a specialist to make the debate concrete rather than rhetorical:
- a **plant-network / restructuring finance analyst** who can test whether non-closure measures can quantitatively close the cost gap, site by site.

## Bottom line
IG Metall is ready for a serious deal. But the deal has to be fair:
- workers contribute,
- management contributes,
- owners contribute,
- and the German industrial base remains intact.

If management wants labor peace, it must stop treating closures and forced redundancies as the starting point. Show a credible non-closure alternative with real numbers, and we can negotiate it. If not, then the conflict will escalate, because we will not allow workers to carry all the pain for a crisis they did not create.
