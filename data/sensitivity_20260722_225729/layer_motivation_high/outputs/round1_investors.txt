From the perspective of dispersed capital-market shareholders, the central issue is not whether Volkswagen needs restructuring — it plainly does — but whether the chosen package will **actually restore competitive margins on a durable basis**. That is the standard we will use.

### Where we agree
We agree with management, the family owners, and Lower Saxony on one point: **the current cost base is too high and delay is value-destructive**. A cosmetic settlement that preserves overcapacity and leaves labour costs structurally uncompetitive would be unacceptable. It would simply defer the problem and erode shareholder value further.

We also agree with the workforce representatives that a durable solution needs more than accounting cuts. If the company hollowed out German production without a credible industrial plan, that would be a bad long-term outcome as well.

### Where we disagree
We disagree strongly with any attempt to make **“no closures”** the starting or binding condition.

To the works council and IG Metall:  
Your insistence on no plant closures and no compulsory redundancies may be understandable from a workforce-protection standpoint, but it is **not sufficient to solve the economics**. If the result is that overcapacity remains in place and the cost base stays above competitors’, then the company has not been restructured — it has merely postponed a deeper crisis. That would not be fair to shareholders, employees, or the company’s future.

To Lower Saxony:  
We respect the political and regional role, but a blocking stance against meaningful restructuring is not value-preserving. If the governance structure is used to prevent economically necessary change, that undermines competitiveness and ultimately the employment base itself. A protected status quo is not a strategy.

To management:  
We support decisive action, but not a headline-driven or symbolic closure plan. Closures are only justified if they are part of a **credible, quantitatively sufficient** package that demonstrably restores returns. If alternative measures can achieve the same durable savings with less disruption, they should be preferred. But the burden of proof lies with whoever claims the alternative is sufficient.

### What we require
We would support a settlement only if it contains all three of the following:

1. **Durable capacity and cost reduction**
   - Not temporary measures
   - Not voluntary-only solutions if they do not materially reduce fixed costs
   - A clear path back to competitive margins

2. **A credible employee contribution**
   - Wage moderation, working-time flexibility, redesigned guarantees, and voluntary exits can all be part of this
   - But the contribution must be large enough to matter financially

3. **A real industrial plan for Germany**
   - Product allocation
   - Investment commitments
   - Technology roadmap
   - Enough certainty to justify the near-term pain

### What we will not support
- A deal that leaves the cost base uncompetitive
- A settlement that merely defers restructuring
- A “socially balanced” package in name only, with no real savings
- Prolonged industrial conflict that destroys value while sides posture

### Bottom line
We are **not** interested in consensus for its own sake. We are interested in **risk-adjusted shareholder value**. If the final package credibly restores margins, we can support it — even if it includes painful concessions. But if the outcome is a protected but still uncompetitive structure, then we will not regard that as acceptable, and we would expect capital markets to penalise it accordingly.
