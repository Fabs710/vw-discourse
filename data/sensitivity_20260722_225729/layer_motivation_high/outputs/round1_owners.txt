I appreciate the clarity from all sides. My position is equally clear: **Volkswagen needs a durable competitiveness reset, but it must not be achieved in a way that permanently damages the company’s value or destabilizes its governance and social fabric.**

Let me respond directly.

To **management**:  
I agree with the diagnosis that the current cost base is too high and that structural change is unavoidable. A mere postponement would destroy value. However, I do **not** think the answer should be a blunt closure-led strategy if there are credible alternatives that achieve comparable savings with less long-term damage. Plant closures may sound decisive, but they also carry major execution risk, reputational damage, labor conflict, and industrial loss. If there is a path to materially lower fixed costs, reduce overcapacity, and restore margins without permanently impairing core capabilities in Germany, that is preferable from an ownership perspective.

To be explicit: **I support a tough restructuring, not a symbolic one**. But I will not back a package that produces a short-term accounting improvement while creating a long-term weaker Volkswagen.

To the **works council and IG Metall**:  
I understand and respect the insistence that the workforce should not carry the whole burden. That is fair. But “no closures, no compulsory redundancies, no change in guarantees” is not a complete answer either. If the economics do not work, preserving every structure unchanged is not a viable stewardship position. The company’s long-term survival matters to employees as much as to owners.

So I would challenge you on one point: if you reject closures categorically, then you need to be prepared to deliver something that is **economically equivalent** in effect — not just aspirational language. That means real productivity gains, real labor-cost relief, and durable capacity adjustment. If the package is too weak, then it is simply delaying the problem and will return in a worse form.

To **Lower Saxony**:  
I support your concern for regional stability and the company’s industrial base. But I would caution against turning that into an absolute veto over all meaningful restructuring. Volkswagen cannot remain competitive by preserving every site regardless of economics. A governance model that blocks necessary adaptation would itself erode value and ultimately threaten the employment base you are trying to protect.

Your role as anchor shareholder is important, but it should be exercised as a steward of long-term value, not as a guarantee that no hard choices are ever made.

So where does that leave me?

I could support a settlement if it meets three conditions:

1. **Credible and durable cost reduction**  
   Not temporary optics. Real structural improvement in labor and capacity costs.

2. **A negotiated labor contribution**  
   This can include working-time flexibility, wage moderation, voluntary exits, and redesigned guarantees — but it must be substantial enough to matter.

3. **A long-term industrial plan for Germany**  
   Investment, product allocation, and future technologies in the German base must be part of the deal. The workforce should not be asked to sacrifice for a hollowed-out company.

What I will **not** support is:
- a pure closure strategy that destroys industrial capacity unnecessarily,
- a status quo defense that avoids hard choices,
- or a package that shifts all burden onto labor while protecting every existing entitlement unchanged.

In short: **the company needs reform, but reform must be value-preserving and durable.** If there is a serious negotiated route to that outcome, I will consider it. If not, then management and labor are heading toward a destructive conflict that would harm all shareholders, employees, and the wider industrial base.
