We are still missing the decisive piece: **a concrete, quantified alternative to plant closures that actually closes the cost gap**. Repeating “structural change” does not make closure-led restructuring the only option, and it does not answer the question of **who pays**.

Let me be equally direct.

## Where I agree
I agree with the works council on the essentials:
- Volkswagen needs a durable solution, not a temporary patch.
- The cost base must come down.
- The company needs investment, product allocation, and a credible industrial future in Germany.
- Burden-sharing is possible only if it is fair and negotiated.

I also agree with Lower Saxony and the owners on one point:
- **delay is not an option**.
A cosmetic package that leaves the company uncompetitive would fail everyone.

## Where I disagree
I do **not** accept the conclusion that this means plant closures or compulsory redundancies are unavoidable.

Management is treating closures as if they were the benchmark for seriousness. That is a false premise. A serious restructuring can be achieved through:
- capacity reductions,
- shift adjustments,
- working-time reductions,
- voluntary exits,
- redeployment,
- temporary wage restraint,
- and targeted contribution from labor.

What is **not** acceptable is using the restructuring to destroy job security and force workers to carry all the risk.

## On the works council’s position
You say no closures, no compulsory redundancies, and guarantees through 2030. That is a legitimate starting line, and we support it. But I also need to be frank: if management is going to accept that, then it must be because the employee side delivers a **real, measurable contribution** that is economically meaningful.

So I am not here to defend a status quo that does nothing. I am here to defend a **fair settlement**:
- workers contribute,
- management contributes,
- owners contribute,
- and the company invests in the German base.

## What would move the union
A labor-side contribution is possible, but only if it is tied to hard commitments. For example:
- temporary wage moderation or deferred compensation,
- tighter working-time flexibility,
- voluntary exit programs,
- reduced bonuses or other internal cost relief,
- capacity optimization across plants,
- and possibly a negotiated reduction in certain guarantees.

But this only becomes acceptable if there is:
- **no forced redundancy**
- **no plant closure**
- **binding employment and site security**
- **real investment and product commitments in Germany**
- **a durable collective agreement that protects the wage framework**

That is the line.

## To management
If you want labor to make a contribution, then stop talking as if closure threats are the natural starting point. They are not. They are escalation. If you insist on keeping closures and forced layoffs “on the table,” then you should expect the workforce to mobilize accordingly — and that is exactly what the end of the peace obligation means.

I also want the numbers, not slogans:
- What is the exact savings target?
- How much comes from labor cost versus capacity?
- Which non-closure measures have been genuinely tested?
- What savings are required to avoid closures?
- What investment is being committed in return?

If you cannot answer that, then you are asking workers to accept pain without proof.

## To the capital-side stakeholders
To the owners and institutional investors: I hear your insistence on durable savings. Fine. Then do not demand that labor alone produces them.

If you want competitiveness, then accept that:
- management must change its footprint and execution,
- owners must tolerate lower short-term returns,
- and labor must not be turned into the only adjustment variable.

A “value-preserving” deal is not one where workers surrender protections while others keep full flexibility. That is not durable; it is conflict dressed up as realism.

## Bottom line
The union’s position remains clear:

- **No plant closures**
- **No compulsory redundancies**
- **No one-sided burden shifting onto employees**
- **Yes to negotiated capacity adjustment**
- **Yes to working-time and flexibility solutions**
- **Yes to temporary labor-cost contributions if matched by binding job, site, and investment guarantees**
- **Yes to a durable collective settlement that preserves the wage framework**

If management comes with a real package that meets those conditions, we can negotiate seriously. If it keeps insisting that closures are the only credible path, then it is choosing confrontation over settlement.
