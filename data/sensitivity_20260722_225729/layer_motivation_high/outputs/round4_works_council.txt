## My position remains unchanged

Volkswagen needs restructuring. I accept that.  
Volkswagen needs durable savings. I accept that too.  
But I **do not accept** that this must be done through plant closures or compulsory redundancies.

And I reject the growing attempt to present that as the only “serious” option.

## Where I agree

I agree with the expert, and I agree with parts of management’s and the owners’ argument, on one point only: **this must be decided with real site-by-site numbers, not abstract rhetoric**.

If the company wants to claim that keeping all German plants open is impossible, then prove it:
- show the fixed-cost burden by site,
- show the utilization rates,
- show what non-closure measures actually save,
- and show why those measures are supposedly insufficient.

That is a reasonable demand.

I also agree that temporary optics are useless.  
A deal that merely postpones the problem would fail the company and the workforce.

## Where I disagree

### To management
You say closures are not the first preference, only a consequence if the numbers require them. But in practice you are still treating closure as the benchmark and asking labour to justify its own survival.

That is backwards.

The burden is on management to prove that every non-closure instrument has been exhausted and is still insufficient. So far, you have not done that.

And let me be clear: “closures may be necessary” is not proof. It is a threat.  
The workforce is not going to accept a restructuring built on threats.

### To the controlling owners and investors
You keep repeating “durable competitiveness” as if it were automatically incompatible with keeping German plants open. It is not.

What destroys value is not protecting the industrial base. What destroys value is:
- destroying trust,
- provoking prolonged conflict,
- weakening co-determination,
- and breaking the job-security architecture that has stabilised this company for years.

A company cannot be made stronger by treating its own workforce as expendable.

### To Lower Saxony
I appreciate your refusal to make closures the starting point. On that, we are aligned.

But I will also say this: “site-specific specialization” must not become a polite label for sorting plants into survivors and sacrifice zones. If that is the plan, then it is closure by another name.

So yes, I support a numbers-based review. But no, I do not accept a process designed to smuggle in closures through the back door.

## What I accept as a real restructuring route

A serious route exists, but it must start from **no plant closures and no compulsory redundancies**.

That means:

1. **Capacity reduction through internal measures**
   - shift changes
   - working-time adjustments
   - product reallocation
   - redeployment
   - retraining
   - voluntary exits only

2. **Material labour contribution**
   - temporary wage restraint
   - bonus moderation
   - flexibility arrangements
   - negotiated productivity measures

3. **Binding security**
   - site security
   - job security
   - and protection of co-determination through 2030

4. **Investment and product commitments**
   - not vague promises
   - binding allocation of future products to German sites
   - real investment in EV, software, and next-generation production

That is a restructuring package.  
Plant closures are not required to define one.

## What I do not accept

I do **not** accept:
- a “closure-last” rule if closure is still a live fallback,
- conditional job security that can be withdrawn when management changes the metrics,
- “protected core sites” if that means other German sites become disposable,
- or a governance process where workers are expected to concede first and defend themselves later.

Those are not compromises. Those are staged concessions.

## On the moderator’s proposals

### 1) Site-by-site closure-last test with hard triggers
No. I will not accept a framework where the trigger is merely waiting for management to decide that a site failed.

If a site is kept open only until some future review says otherwise, then the workforce is already living under a closure clock. That is not trust-building, and it is not acceptable.

### 2) Binding employment and investment pact, conditional on quantified labour contribution
This is closer to a serious discussion, but only if the conditions are not one-sided and not reversible at management discretion.

If “conditional” means management can later declare the savings insufficient and tear up security, then it is not a pact. It is a trap.

### 3) Modular plant-network restructuring with protected core sites
I am skeptical. In theory, specialization may reduce duplication. In practice, it often becomes a hierarchy of “favoured” and “disposable” sites.

The works council’s position is simple: **every German plant must remain open**.  
Not every plant must do the same thing, but every plant must have a future.

A solution that marginally improves near-term metrics while detonating industrial peace is not equivalent. It is inferior.
