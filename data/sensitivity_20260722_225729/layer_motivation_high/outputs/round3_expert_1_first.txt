From a restructuring-accounting and plant-network perspective, the key question is **not** whether VW has a crisis. It does. The question is whether a **non-closure package can generate economically equivalent fixed-cost relief and capacity reduction** versus closing one or more plants.

## Short answer
**A “no closures, no compulsory redundancies” package can work only if the company has enough excess capacity, enough voluntary attrition, and enough wage/flexibility relief to remove a very large amount of fixed cost.**  
If the structural gap is driven mainly by **severe plant underutilization**, then non-closure tools usually do **not** fully substitute for a closure, because they reduce cost only partially while the plant’s fixed overhead remains.

## How to assess this properly
You need a site-by-site model with at least these elements:

1. **Current and forecast capacity utilization by plant**
   - installed capacity
   - actual output
   - expected volume under base, downside, and EV-transition scenarios

2. **Fixed cost structure by site**
   - labor fixed and semi-fixed costs
   - depreciation / lease / utilities / maintenance
   - plant supervision and indirect overhead
   - logistics and internal transfer costs

3. **Variable cost impact of alternative measures**
   - wage moderation
   - reduced hours / short-time work
   - shift changes
   - bonus removal
   - voluntary exits and redeployment

4. **Closure economics**
   - one-time severance, decommissioning, environmental, and relocation costs
   - stranded depreciation / impairment
   - whether savings are truly durable after closure

5. **Non-closure savings potential**
   - how much of labor cost is actually avoidable
   - how much capacity can be removed without a site closure
   - whether idle capacity still carries most of its fixed cost

## Standard analytical conclusion
In industrial restructuring, there is a common rule:

- **If the problem is mainly variable cost**, labor concessions and working-time tools can often close much of the gap.
- **If the problem is mainly fixed cost and excess footprint**, then only capacity removal—often including closure or major line consolidation—fully restores economics.

That is because:
- reduced wages help,
- reduced shifts help,
- voluntary exits help,
- but an open plant still carries overhead, maintenance, security, utilities, depreciation, and management cost.

So the burden of proof for management is to show:
1. how much of the gap is fixed versus variable,
2. what savings the non-closure package delivers,
3. and whether the remaining gap is acceptable relative to target margins.

## What would make a non-closure alternative credible
A non-closure deal can be credible if it includes all of the following:

- substantial and verified **headcount reduction through voluntary attrition**
- **permanent** rather than temporary labor-cost relief
- meaningful **working-time and shift flexibility**
- **capacity rationalization** across lines and plants
- product allocation that raises utilization at retained sites
- clear investment commitments that improve future load factors

If those measures still leave a large underutilized footprint, then the economics usually still favor closure or major consolidation.
