## What would be needed to assess “non-closure equivalence”
To determine whether a no-closure package can substitute for closures, you would need, at minimum, for **each German site**:

1. **Current and projected volume/utilization**
   - installed capacity
   - current output
   - forecast volume by model and year
   - utilization rate by line/site

2. **Site-level cost structure**
   - direct labor cost
   - indirect labor / overhead
   - fixed manufacturing overhead
   - depreciation and lease/rental costs
   - energy, maintenance, logistics, and local support costs

3. **Scenario-specific savings**
   - voluntary exits
   - working-time reductions or flexibility
   - wage restraint / bonus moderation
   - redeployment and retraining
   - product reallocation
   - line consolidation
   - procurement / yield / scrap improvements

4. **Closure / consolidation economics**
   - one-time closure costs
   - severance and pension impacts
   - decommissioning / remediation
   - asset impairment
   - transfer costs
   - stranded fixed cost elsewhere in the network

5. **Residual viability metrics**
   - EBITDA or contribution margin after restructuring
   - fixed-cost absorption
   - break-even volume
   - margin sensitivity to volume loss

## How the analysis should be structured
The correct comparison is not “closure vs. no closure” in the abstract. It is:

- **Scenario A:** keep site open, apply negotiated labor and capacity measures  
- **Scenario B:** consolidate production or close site  
- **Scenario C:** hybrid / phased approach with trigger-based review

For each scenario, calculate:
- annual recurring savings
- one-time restructuring cost
- payback period
- net present value over 3–5 years
- impact on group capacity and utilization
- implementation risk

## Key accounting point
A non-closure package is only economically equivalent to closure if it produces:
- **durable recurring savings**
- that are **close enough to the closure-led savings**
- after adjusting for **one-time closure costs** and **execution risk**

In many industrial cases, closure appears superior on pure fixed-cost math because it removes stranded capacity more decisively. But that advantage must be tested against:
- severance/remediation charges,
- asset impairments,
- disruption costs,
- labor conflict risk,
- and loss of flexibility.
