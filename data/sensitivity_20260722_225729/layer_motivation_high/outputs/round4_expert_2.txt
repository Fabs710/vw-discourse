Yes. Based on the record so far, the decision still turns on whether Volkswagen’s German network problem is mainly:

1. a **labor-cost gap**, or  
2. a **fixed-cost / underutilization problem**, or  
3. a mix of both.

At this stage, the evidence provided is insufficient to prove that a **no-closure** package is economically equivalent to a **closure / consolidation** package. But the right way to test that is clear.

## What I would require

For **each German site**, I would need:

- installed capacity
- current utilization
- projected volume by model and year
- direct labor cost
- indirect labor / overhead
- fixed manufacturing overhead
- energy, maintenance, logistics, and local support costs
- depreciation and lease costs
- one-time closure / consolidation costs
- severance, pension, and remediation costs
- asset impairment
- transfer / relocation costs
- savings from:
  - voluntary exits
  - working-time changes
  - wage restraint / bonus moderation
  - redeployment / retraining
  - product reallocation
  - line consolidation
  - productivity gains

## The decision rule

A non-closure package is only a credible substitute if, **site by site**, it produces:

- durable recurring savings
- sufficient fixed-cost relief
- a realistic utilization path
- acceptable break-even volume
- and a payback / NPV profile that is close enough to the closure option

In practice, this means comparing at least three scenarios:

### Scenario A: retain site, apply non-closure measures
- voluntary exits
- shift / working-time changes
- labor-cost moderation
- product reallocation
- efficiency measures

### Scenario B: consolidate or close site
- one-time restructuring costs
- removal of stranded fixed cost
- lower recurring overhead

### Scenario C: hybrid / phased approach
- keep site open initially
- set measurable savings and utilization triggers
- review later for consolidation if thresholds are missed

## Key analytical point

If the plants have **structural underutilization**, then labor concessions alone are usually not enough.  
In that case, the fixed-cost burden remains too high unless there is:

- capacity reduction,
- network rationalization,
- or a product / volume transfer that materially raises utilization.

If, by contrast, the plants are broadly viable and the main issue is labor cost, then a negotiated package could work without closures.

## What cannot be concluded yet

From the information given, I cannot verify:

- which plants are structurally uneconomic,
- whether all German sites can remain open and still meet margin targets,
- or whether closures are economically unavoidable.

So the correct conclusion is:

**The non-closure option is possible in principle, but not yet proven.**  
It needs a site-by-site fixed-cost and utilization model before anyone can claim equivalence.
