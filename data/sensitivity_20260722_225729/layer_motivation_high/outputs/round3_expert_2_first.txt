## Expert assessment: can a non-closure package substitute for plant closures?

### Bottom line
From a plant-network economics perspective, **a non-closure package can only substitute for closures if the company’s problem is mostly labor cost and temporary underutilization, not structural fixed-cost overcapacity**.

If Volkswagen’s German network has:
- persistent low utilization,
- duplicated fixed overhead across too many sites,
- and plant-specific fixed costs that cannot be materially reduced without shutting a site or line,

then **non-closure tools will usually fall short of a true closure in economic effect**.

So the core answer is:

- **Yes, in principle** if the savings gap is modest and can be closed by labor concessions, working-time flexibility, voluntary exits, and product reallocation.
- **No, in most severe overcapacity cases** if the problem is mainly stranded fixed cost and permanently excess footprint.

---

## What must be true for “no closures” to work

A no-closure solution must generate enough **durable annual savings** to offset the structural burden of keeping all sites open.

That requires some combination of:

1. **Headcount reduction through voluntary attrition**
   - meaningful enough to reduce labor cost permanently
   - not just a one-time accounting effect

2. **Working-time and shift reductions**
   - fewer shifts or shorter hours can help if demand is lower
   - but this only lowers cost if it actually removes paid hours and some indirect staffing

3. **Wage moderation / labor-cost contribution**
   - can reduce cash cost materially
   - but it does not eliminate plant overhead

4. **Capacity rationalization without closure**
   - line consolidation, product transfers, and better load balancing
   - this can raise utilization at retained sites, which is important

5. **Investment and product allocation**
   - if more volume is assigned to fewer sites, utilization improves
   - this is often the single most important non-closure lever

If these measures still leave sites at weak utilization, the residual fixed-cost burden usually remains too high.

---

## Why closures are economically different

A plant closure removes more than labor cost. It removes:

- site supervision
- maintenance and security
- utilities and facility overhead
- some logistics complexity
- duplicated indirect functions
- and, most importantly, underutilized fixed capacity

That is why closures often produce a step-change in cost rather than a gradual reduction.

By contrast, a retained but underloaded plant still carries much of its:
- building cost,
- infrastructure cost,
- indirect labor,
- and depreciation/stranded asset burden.

So the economic gap between “open but smaller” and “closed” can be large.

---

## The key analytical test

The decision should be made site by site using a simple test:

### Annual economic comparison
**Keep site open if:**
\[
\text{Annual savings from closure} - \text{annual cash cost of closure} > \text{annual savings from non-closure package}
\]

Where:

### Closure savings include
- avoided labor and indirect labor
- avoided overhead
- avoided maintenance and utilities
- avoided logistics complexity
- improved network utilization elsewhere

### Closure costs include
- severance
- decommissioning
- environmental remediation
- relocation and transfer costs
- stranded depreciation / impairment
- transition disruption

### Non-closure savings include
- labor concessions
- headcount reduction via voluntary exits
- shift/working-time changes
- productivity measures
- model reallocation
- procurement and indirect cost savings

If the non-closure package cannot match the **durable annual net savings** of closure, then it is not an equivalent substitute.

## Practical conclusion

### A non-closure package is credible only if it includes:
- substantial voluntary headcount reduction
- real reduction in paid labor hours
- durable labor-cost relief
- product and volume reallocation to lift utilization
- measurable overhead reduction
- and a residual footprint that is economically viable

### If the company still has materially excess fixed capacity after that:
- the economics usually favor closure or major consolidation,
- because open plants retain too much fixed cost.
