## IG Metall’s position

We agree on the diagnosis:
- Volkswagen needs a durable restructuring.
- The current cost base is too high.
- Delay is not an option.
- The company needs future investment, product commitments, and a credible industrial plan.

We also agree that the workforce must contribute.  
IG Metall is not defending a status quo that ignores the crisis.

## Where I disagree — and why

### 1) Plant closures are not the necessary condition for seriousness
I do **not** accept the claim that closures and compulsory redundancies are the only credible path to competitiveness.

That is still an assertion, not a proven necessity.  
If management and the owners want to make that claim, then they must show the site-by-site numbers:
- fixed-cost burden,
- utilization,
- savings from labor measures,
- savings from working-time flexibility,
- savings from product reallocation,
- and the actual gap that remains.

Until then, closures remain a choice, not a fact.

### 2) A “closure-last” rule is not acceptable if closure stays on the table as an active fallback
I have to be clear here: I do **not** support a model where the workforce is asked to concede first while closure remains the standing threat in the background.

That is not a partnership. It is bargaining under a gun.

If management wants trust, then it must stop treating closure as the default benchmark. The burden of proof is on management to show why non-closure measures are insufficient.

### 3) Conditional guarantees can easily become empty guarantees
I understand the pressure to link commitments to measurable outcomes. But job security cannot be turned into a device that management can later withdraw by redefining the target.

If the guarantee is “secure as long as management likes the savings,” then it is not security. It is a revocable permission slip.

So yes, I am open to negotiated flexibility and contributions.  
No, I am not open to a framework where workers give concessions up front and get uncertainty in return.

## What I agree with the works council on
I support the core red lines:
- no compulsory redundancies,
- no one-sided burden shifting,
- no industrial plan built on threats,
- and real co-determination.

I also agree that workers must not be asked to finance management’s structural failures alone.

## Where I differ from the works council
I need to say this carefully: saying “keep every German plant open” is a legitimate industrial demand, but it is not yet a complete restructuring answer by itself.

If we want a durable settlement, we need a package that is not just defensive but also materially improves competitiveness.

That means:
- real labor-cost relief,
- working-time flexibility,
- voluntary exits,
- redeployment and retraining,
- capacity reduction through internal measures,
- and binding investment and product allocation in Germany.

But all of that only becomes acceptable if it is matched by:
- **no compulsory redundancies**
- **no German plant closures**
- **binding job and site security through 2030**
- **protection of the collective wage framework**

That is the minimum for a fair deal.

## To management and capital-side stakeholders
Let me be explicit: if you want worker contributions, then you need to stop presenting them as prepayment for closures.

If the workforce is to contribute fairly, then management and owners must also contribute:
- through investment,
- through acceptance of lower short-term returns,
- through a genuine industrial plan,
- and through written commitments on jobs and sites.

A deal where labor pays, but management keeps closure rights and investors keep all the upside, is not durable. It will blow up.

## To Lower Saxony
I appreciate your support for negotiated restructuring and your rejection of a closure-first approach. That matters.

But I agree with the works council on one point: a review mechanism must not become a staged path to closures. If “review” means “we’ll wait and then decide who gets cut,” then the trust basis is already broken.

Any sequencing must start from the principle that all German sites remain open and viable, with capacity adjustment done through internal measures.

- voluntary exits,

## What would move us
A serious agreement is possible if it contains all of the following:

1. **No compulsory redundancies**
2. **No German plant closures**
3. **Binding job and site security through 2030**
4. **Material but negotiated employee contribution**
   - temporary wage restraint
   - working-time flexibility
   - voluntary exits
   - redeployment
   - retraining
5. **Investment and product commitments for German sites**
6. **Protection of the collective wage framework**
7. **Real co-determination in implementation**

That is a fair burden-sharing package.  
It is not surrender. It is a responsible restructuring.

## What I cannot accept
I cannot accept:
- closures as a fallback,
- forced layoffs,
- open-ended insecurity,
- or a settlement where workers carry the pain while management and owners retain maximum flexibility.

That would not be a durable compromise. It would be a transfer of risk onto the workforce.

## Bottom line
IG Metall is ready to contribute to a serious deal. But the deal has to be fair:
- workers contribute,
- management contributes,
- owners contribute,
- and the German industrial base remains intact.

If management wants labor peace, it must stop treating closures and compulsory redundancies as the starting point. Show a credible non-closure alternative with real numbers, and we can negotiate it. If not, then the conflict will escalate, because we will not allow workers to bear all the pain for a crisis they did not create.
