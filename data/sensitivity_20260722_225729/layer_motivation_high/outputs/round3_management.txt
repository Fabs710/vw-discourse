I’ll respond directly.

## First: what I agree with

I agree with the expert on one critical point: **this must be decided on numbers, not slogans**.  
And I agree with all sides that Volkswagen needs a durable restructuring, not a cosmetic patch.

I also agree that:
- temporary measures alone are not enough,
- the company needs investment in future products and technology,
- and a deal that simply postpones the crisis would be value-destructive.

So yes: the crisis is real, and delay is not an option.

## Where I disagree — and strongly

What I do **not** accept is the attempt to convert “no closures” and “no compulsory redundancies” into a permanent principle that management must simply work around.

That is not a restructuring solution. It is a constraint that may or may not be compatible with the economics. If the numbers show that the footprint remains too large, then refusing to adjust that footprint is not protection — it is denial.

## To the Works Council

You keep saying that closures are “the most destructive option.” They may be disruptive, yes. But disruption is not the same as destruction.  
A plant that is structurally underutilized and too costly to carry is already destructive to the enterprise if we keep funding it indefinitely.

I agree with you on one point: **management must prove the necessity of any closure**.  
That is fair. And that is exactly why I want the site-by-site analysis the expert describes.

But until we have that analysis, your position still has the same gap: you say non-closure restructuring can work, yet you have not shown how it closes the fixed-cost problem on a durable basis.

So I reject your claim that “no closures” is already a complete industrial solution. It isn’t.

## To IG Metall

I appreciate that you acknowledge workers must contribute. That is important. But your position still assumes that a negotiated labour contribution, working-time flexibility, and voluntary exits will somehow be enough without proving it.

Maybe it will. Maybe it won’t.  
That is precisely why we need the numbers.

And I need to be blunt: **temporary wage restraint is not enough** if the structural cost base remains too high.  
If the package does not materially reduce fixed cost and capacity, then we will be back here again, only weaker and with less credibility.

So I disagree with any approach that treats labour concessions as a substitute for structural footprint adjustment unless the analysis proves equivalence.

## To Lower Saxony

I understand your political and regional responsibilities. I respect them. But I reject the idea that the VW Law can be used as a shield against necessary restructuring.

A blocking position does not change the economics. It only changes who is forced to confront them later.

If you want to protect jobs in the long run, then you need a viable company first. That means accepting hard choices if the data show they are required.

So I support a negotiated route — but not a veto over reality.

## To the controlling owners

You are right to insist on durability and value preservation.  
But I also need to say this clearly: a refusal to consider hard restructuring because it is politically uncomfortable would also destroy value.

So I agree with you only if you remain open to the conclusion that a closure or major consolidation may be necessary where a site cannot be made economically viable.

## To institutional investors

You are right that the package must restore competitive margins on a durable basis.  
I agree. But then do not confuse “no closures” with prudence. If the economics do not work, then a no-closure deal is not prudence — it is deferred impairment.

## What the expert’s intervention changes

The expert has said the key thing: **if the problem is fixed-cost overcapacity, then non-closure tools may not fully substitute for closure**.

That is exactly my point.

I am not saying closures are the first preference. I am saying they remain necessary unless someone can prove, plant by plant, that:
- the cost gap closes,
- capacity is sufficiently reduced,
- and the remaining footprint is viable.

That proof has not been provided.

## What management requires

We need a package that does all of the following:

1. **Quantified capacity reduction**
   - Not just internal reshuffling. Real removal of excess capacity.

2. **Material labour-cost relief**
   - Wage moderation, yes.
   - Flexibility, yes.
   - But substantial enough to affect the cost base.

3. **Durable impact**
   - Not a one-off measure that fades in 18 months.

4. **A credible investment path to 2030**
   - So we can fund the next generation of products and technology.

We need the site-by-site economics now:
- current utilization,

## Bottom line

I do **not** accept:
- unconditional guarantees through 2030,
- a blanket exclusion of plant closures,
- or a deal that preserves excess fixed cost under a softer label.

I **do** accept:
- a rigorous, site-by-site comparison of closure versus non-closure options,
- negotiated labour contributions if they are material,
- investment commitments in Germany,
- and a serious effort to preserve a viable industrial base.

But let me be crystal clear: if the numbers show that non-closure measures do not close the gap, then management will not sign up to a cosmetic deal just to preserve appearances. We are here to restore competitiveness, not to freeze the cost problem in place.
