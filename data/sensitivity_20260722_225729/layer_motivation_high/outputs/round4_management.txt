## Management’s position

Volkswagen needs a **durable structural reset**.  
Not a symbolic adjustment, not a temporary truce, and not a package that leaves the cost base fundamentally uncompetitive.

We need:
- lower fixed costs,
- lower labour costs,
- real capacity reduction,
- and a footprint that can actually earn its cost of capital and fund the 2030 product and technology agenda.

If we do not achieve that, we will simply postpone the problem and weaken the company further.

## Where I agree

### With the expert
I agree completely that this is a **numbers question**, not a slogan contest.  
We need a site-by-site view of:
- capacity utilization,
- fixed cost burden,
- savings from voluntary exits and flexibility,
- and the economics of closure versus retention.

That analysis is not optional. It is the only serious basis for decision-making.

### With IG Metall and the works council on one point

A durable settlement needs labour contribution, investment commitment, and industrial clarity.

### With Lower Saxony and the owners
I agree that Volkswagen’s German industrial base matters, and that a reckless move would damage long-term value.  
I am not arguing for disruption for its own sake. I am arguing for a viable structure.

## Where I disagree — and strongly

### 1) “No closures” cannot be a starting principle
I have to say this plainly:  
**“No German plant closures” is not a restructuring strategy. It is a red line.**

If the numbers show that a site is structurally underutilized and too expensive to carry, then refusing closure in advance is not protecting Volkswagen — it is protecting an uncompetitive cost base.

The works council keeps saying that closures are a choice, not a necessity. That may be true — but only if a non-closure package closes the gap. So far, that proof has not been provided.

### 2) “No compulsory redundancies” is also not a free-standing solution
I understand the political and social appeal of that demand.  
But if we cannot reduce headcount materially and permanently, then we do not solve the cost problem. We just rename it.

Voluntary exits, redeployment, and natural attrition are useful tools. But they are unlikely to be sufficient on their own if the network is carrying too much fixed capacity.

### 3) Binding guarantees through 2030 are not compatible with an unconditional restructuring need
I cannot accept unconditional job and site guarantees through 2030 if they prevent the company from making the hard adjustments required by the economics.

A guarantee that forbids the company from responding to structural overcapacity is not stability. It is a freeze on necessary change.

## To the works council

You are asking management to accept a framework where:
- no closures,

If the non-closure package leaves us with excess fixed cost, then it is not a solution.

They remain on the table because the business may require them, not because they are politically attractive.

## What the expert’s intervention means

The expert has made the key point: **if the issue is fixed-cost overcapacity, non-closure measures may not fully substitute for closure**.

That is exactly why we need the site-by-site model now.

And let me be clear: if that model shows that a plant can be made viable through a serious mix of:
- voluntary exits,
- working-time changes,
- productivity gains,
- product reallocation,
- and labour-cost relief,

then I am open to that.

But if it shows persistent underutilization and unrecoverable fixed cost, then we cannot pretend that internal reshuffling equals structural reform.
