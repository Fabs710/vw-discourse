Lower Saxony’s position remains consistent: we want Volkswagen to emerge from this crisis stronger, not smaller for the sake of being smaller.

I agree with management, the owners, and the investors on one central point: **delay is not a strategy**. The cost base has to come down, and the restructuring must be durable. I also agree that temporary measures alone are not enough if they do not materially improve competitiveness.

But I disagree sharply with the idea that the only credible path is plant closures or the threat of compulsory redundancies.

## Where I agree
- **The crisis is real.**
- **Volkswagen needs structural change.**
- **The German operations must become more competitive.**
- **The company needs a serious industrial plan, not a cosmetic deal.**
- **The workforce can and should contribute, if the burden is shared fairly.**

## Where I disagree
Management and the capital side keep presenting closures as if they were the natural benchmark of seriousness. They are not. They are one possible instrument, and a very blunt one at that.

For Lower Saxony, plant closures in Germany are not an acceptable starting point, because they carry three major risks:
1. they damage regional employment and supplier networks,
2. they weaken Volkswagen’s industrial base in Germany,
3. they create a confrontation that could destroy trust and slow the company down for years.

That is not “value-preserving” in any meaningful long-term sense.

## On the works council and IG Metall
I support your core line that the workforce must not be made to pay alone. That said, if you want to keep every site open, then you cannot stop at principles. You need to show a **credible alternative package** that actually closes the cost gap.

So I will be direct: I do **not** see “no closures, no compulsory redundancies” as a complete restructuring plan by itself. It is a red line, not the full solution.

What I could support is a negotiated package built on:
- capacity adjustments without closures,
- shift and working-time changes,
- voluntary exits and redeployment,
- temporary wage restraint or deferred compensation,
- retraining and internal mobility,
- and binding future product and investment commitments for German plants.

That is a serious route. It is also the only route I see that preserves social stability while still delivering reform.

## On management’s argument
Management is right that a package must be quantified. So quantify it.

If you argue that closures are necessary, then show:
- the exact savings gap,
- the exact contribution expected from labor,
- what non-closure measures have been exhausted,
- and why internal rebalancing cannot achieve the same effect.

So far, I hear a lot of “closures may be needed” but not enough proof that they are unavoidable. In a company like Volkswagen, that matters. A decision of this magnitude must be justified, not simply asserted.

## On the capital side
To the owners and investors: I hear your insistence that the company must restore margins. I do not dismiss that. But long-term value is not protected by treating the German footprint as disposable.

Volkswagen’s strength is not only a balance sheet; it is also its industrial depth, skilled workforce, and regional anchoring. If restructuring destroys that base, the company may look leaner temporarily but weaker strategically.

So no, I will not support a package that hollow outs Volkswagen in Germany and calls that competitiveness.

## What Lower Saxony would support
We are prepared to support a socially balanced restructuring if it includes:
- **real cost reduction**,
- **real capacity adjustment**,
- **a negotiated labour contribution**,
- **investment in future products and technologies in Germany**,
- and **binding protection for employment and sites wherever possible**.

But I am not prepared to give a blank cheque for plant closures or forced layoffs. And let me be clear again: **Lower Saxony’s role under the VW Law is not decorative**. Our co-determination and veto rights are part of the governance structure, and we will use them to protect legitimate regional and industrial interests.

## Bottom line
I do not accept a “closure-first” restructuring logic.

I do accept:
- a tough restructuring,
- a fair employee contribution,
- capacity reduction through negotiated means,
- and a durable industrial plan that keeps Volkswagen anchored in Germany.

If management and the capital side want a stable settlement, they need to stop framing the choice as either closure or collapse. There is space for a serious negotiated solution — but only if it protects jobs, preserves sites, and keeps Volkswagen’s German industrial base intact.
