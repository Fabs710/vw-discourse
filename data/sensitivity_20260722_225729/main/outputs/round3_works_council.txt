Let me be equally clear in round 3: **the employee side will not accept a restructuring built on German plant closures or compulsory redundancies.** That line has not moved, and vague pressure from management, owners, or investors does not change it.

## To management
You have now had two rounds to explain why closures and forced layoffs are supposedly unavoidable. What you have offered is still not a convincing case — it is an assertion.

Yes, Volkswagen needs durable savings. Yes, the company must remain investable. Yes, a cosmetic fix is worthless. We agree on that.

But you still have not shown:
- why the structural gap cannot be closed by **capacity reduction without closure**,
- why workforce reduction cannot be achieved through **attrition, voluntary exits, redeployment, and working-time tools**,
- why the first response must be to threaten sites and break trust.

The expert made one thing very clear: a no-closure package is possible **in principle** if it is broad, durable, and quantified. That is exactly where the real work should begin. Not with closure threats.

So if you want a serious negotiation, then put forward a package that preserves the German plants and the job-security framework and shows, line by line, how the savings are achieved:
- shift reductions,
- line consolidation,
- internal transfers,
- retraining,
- productivity gains,
- voluntary departures,
- and a limited, time-bound employee contribution.

What you cannot do is demand that the workforce accept irreversible damage first and trust that management will somehow make it work later.

## To the controlling owners
You say you want a durable reset and not a symbolic truce. Fine. Then stop treating closures as a convenient test of seriousness.

A plant closure is not “discipline.” It is a blunt instrument with enormous social and industrial costs. It would tear at trust, trigger conflict, and damage the co-determination framework that has kept this company stable.

If you truly want value preservation, then you should support a solution that is:
- economically credible,
- socially sustainable,
- and compatible with industrial peace.

That means **no plant closures in Germany** and **no compulsory redundancies**.

You have the leverage to force a worse outcome, but that does not mean it is the better one.

## To Lower Saxony
I appreciate that you continue to reject closures and compulsory redundancies. But let me be frank: political statements are not enough if management is still free to posture toward closures as the default option.

We need a common blocking line in practice, not just in speeches. Otherwise the workforce is left to carry the fight alone while others hedge.

So I expect Lower Saxony to do more than say “no” in principle. I expect it to help secure:
- binding site guarantees,
- binding investment commitments,
- and a real alternative restructuring path.

## To IG Metall
We are aligned on the essentials, but I want to sharpen the point: employee contribution is only discussable if it is **strictly limited, reversible, and conditional** on hard guarantees.

That means:
- no open-ended wage sacrifice,
- no permanent erosion of the collective framework,
- no backdoor weakening of job security,
- no deal that hands management flexibility while workers carry the risk.

If there is to be any concession, it must be tied to:
- **no closures**,
- **no compulsory redundancies**,
- **site security through 2030**,
- and full respect for co-determination in the restructuring design.

Without that, there is nothing to negotiate.

## On the expert comparison
The experts have helped frame the issue, but let’s not misuse their answer.

They said a no-closure package can work **only if** it is unusually broad and durable. Exactly. That means the burden is on management to come forward with a real package that meets that standard.

What we have heard so far is:
- closures would create strong savings,
- no-closure packages are harder,
- hybrid options may be plausible.

That does **not** mean closures are acceptable. It means management must do the harder work of building an alternative.

And I will say this plainly: if the company’s restructuring only “works” by destroying plants and forcing people out, then the model is socially reckless even if someone can make it look neat on a spreadsheet.

## Our position remains
We will support a serious restructuring only if it is based on:
1. **No German plant closures**
2. **No compulsory redundancies**
3. **Binding job and site guarantees through 2030**
4. **Real co-determination in the restructuring plan**
5. **A negotiated package of alternative savings measures**

Those alternative measures can include:
- working-time flexibility,
- temporary and limited employee contributions,
- internal redeployment,
- qualification and retraining,
- productivity improvements,
- and capacity reduction **without closure**.

What they cannot include is a disguised closure strategy or forced headcount cuts dressed up as inevitability.

## What I reject explicitly
- A fait accompli.
- Any plan that starts from closures and then asks us to negotiate the details of our own weakening.
- Any attempt to use investor impatience as leverage to break the workforce settlement.
- Any “durable solution” that is durable only because it destroys the social contract inside Volkswagen.

## Final point
We are not here to protect bad management decisions. We are here to protect jobs, sites, and the co-determination framework that gives this company its stability.

So let me be plain:  
**If management wants a settlement, it must come to the table with a quantified, no-closure, no-forced-layoff restructuring path.**  
If it cannot do that, then it should not expect the workforce to endorse the destruction of German plants and livelihoods.
