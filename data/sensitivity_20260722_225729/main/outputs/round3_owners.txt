I will be direct.

## Where I agree
I agree with management, investors, and Lower Saxony on the core diagnosis:
- Volkswagen needs **durable structural savings**.
- Temporary fixes are not enough.
- A cosmetic compromise would be value-destructive.
- A prolonged strike or governance breakdown would also destroy value.

I also agree with the works council and IG Metall on one crucial point:
- **industrial peace matters**.
A restructuring that wins on paper but triggers a long conflict can easily become worse for the company than a harder negotiated outcome.

## Where I disagree
I do **not** accept the employee-side attempt to make
- **no closures**,
- **no compulsory redundancies**,
- and **full guarantees to 2030**
the starting point for everything else.

That is not a restructuring framework. It is a veto on structural adjustment.

At the same time, I also do **not** accept management’s implied premise that plant closures and forced redundancies are automatically the best or only serious answer. They may be part of the solution, but only if the economics and execution case truly justify them.

## What the expert input changes
The expert has made the key point clearly:
- a **no-closure package can work in principle**,
- but only if it is **broad, durable, and quantified**,
- and only if it delivers **recurring** savings, not just temporary relief.

That is exactly the right standard.

So the real issue is not “closures versus no closures” as slogans. It is:
- what recurring cost reduction is needed,
- what combination of measures achieves it,
- and which package is actually executable without destroying value.

## To the works council and IG Metall
You are right that alternatives must be tested seriously. But you have not yet produced a package that is economically equivalent to the structural relief management is asking for.

What you have listed are instruments:
- working-time flexibility,
- retraining,
- redeployment,
- voluntary exits,
- productivity measures.

Those are useful. They are not, by themselves, a complete answer to structural overcapacity.

If you want to rule out closures and compulsory redundancies, then show the numbers:
- how much capacity comes out,
- how much fixed cost comes out,
- how much labour cost comes out,
- and how durable those savings are through 2030.

Without that, “no closures” is a political position, not a credible restructuring plan.

## To management
You are right not to dilute the objective. The company needs a real cost reset.

But be careful not to overplay the closure card. If you push too hard on closures before a credible alternative is tested, you risk creating exactly the kind of industrial and political resistance that makes the restructuring slower, costlier, and less effective.

So yes, keep structural savings as the objective. But do not confuse that with insisting on the most confrontational instrument first.

## To Lower Saxony
Your rejection of closures and compulsory redundancies is understandable, but it cannot become a blanket refusal to accept real structural change.

If you want to block closures, then you must also be serious about the alternative:
- actual capacity rationalization,
- real productivity gains,
- voluntary workforce reduction,
- and a contribution from labour that is limited but meaningful.

If that package does not add up, then your red line alone is not enough to make the business sustainable.

## To institutional investors
You are right to demand a solution that restores margins and investor confidence. But the market will not be well served by a deal that looks decisive and then becomes unworkable on the ground.

Value creation here depends on both:
- economic correctness,
- and implementation credibility.

## My position
From the controlling owners’ perspective, the priority is straightforward:
1. **restore Volkswagen’s competitiveness and margins**,  
2. **preserve long-term ownership value**,  
3. **avoid value-destructive instability**,  
4. **and keep governance control intact**.

That means:
- no symbolic truce,
- no endless delay,
- no fake compromise,
- and no settlement that leaves the company structurally uncompetitive.

It also means I am open to a **real hybrid**, but only if it is genuinely real:
- meaningful capacity reduction,
- meaningful labor-cost contribution,
- workforce adjustment mainly through voluntary and natural mechanisms where possible,
- and hard numbers that show the residual gap is closed.

## What I need next
I support the requested scenario stress-test, but I want it to answer one central question:
- Can a no-closure or hybrid package deliver **durable recurring savings** close enough to a closure-led restructuring to preserve competitiveness by 2030?

If the answer is yes, I will evaluate it seriously. If the answer is no, then management’s case for sharper structural action remains credible.

I will support **real restructuring**, not symbolic compromise.
I will not support **permanent value impairment**.
