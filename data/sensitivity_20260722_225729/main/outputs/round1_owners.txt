I appreciate the clarity from all sides. From the perspective of the controlling owners, we are looking at this through one lens above all: **Volkswagen must regain durable competitiveness without destroying the long-term value of the enterprise or provoking a permanent governance and industrial crisis**.

So let me be equally direct.

### Where I agree
I agree with management on the core diagnosis:  
the current cost base is too high, the market environment is tougher, and Volkswagen cannot protect future investment if the underlying structure remains uncompetitive.

I also agree with the works council, IG Metall, and Lower Saxony on one important point:  
**a purely confrontational approach would be value-destructive**. A prolonged strike, reputational damage, and open warfare between the company and its workforce would harm the business and the owners alike.

### Where I disagree
I do **not** accept the idea that every German site must remain untouched no matter what the economics are.

At the same time, I do **not** accept a restructuring plan that jumps too quickly to plant closures and mass compulsory redundancies as the first or only serious option. That would be a blunt instrument, and once used, it is difficult to reverse. It would also risk long-term damage to Volkswagen’s workforce capability, public legitimacy, and political stability.

So I am not endorsing either extreme:
- **not** a status quo that preserves every structure regardless of cost,
- **not** a shock-therapy plan that relies primarily on closures and forced cuts.

### What I expect instead
If management is serious about restoring margins, then it needs to present a package that produces **real, measurable, durable savings** without permanently impairing the company’s industrial base.

That means:
- capacity reduction, yes;
- productivity improvement, yes;
- labour-cost contribution, yes;
- internal redeployment and qualification, yes;
- working-time flexibility, yes;
- but also a credible investment path into future products and technologies.

What I will not support is a plan that asks the workforce for sacrifice while leaving execution vague or while undermining the company’s future capacity to innovate.

### To the works council and IG Metall
Your rejection of compulsory redundancies and indiscriminate closures is understandable, but it cannot amount to a veto on all structural change.

If there are alternative measures that achieve the same economic effect, show them in hard numbers:
- How much cost comes out?
- How durable is it?
- What capacity comes out?
- What investment is protected?
- What is the timetable?

General appeals to fairness are not enough if the economics do not close.

### To management
You need to be careful not to overplay your hand.

If you push a solution that is seen as punitive or destabilizing, you may win a formal argument and lose the company’s ability to implement anything at all. Given the governance reality here, that is not a serious strategy.

The owners will not back a path that:
- permanently damages Volkswagen’s value,
- triggers avoidable governance conflict,
- or undermines labour peace without a credible replacement.

### To Lower Saxony
I hear the concern about regional footprint and the value of reversibility. That is legitimate. But a blanket refusal of closures is only credible if there is a realistic alternative that delivers the same structural correction.

We should not confuse protecting an industrial presence with protecting every existing configuration. Competitiveness still matters, and if it is not restored, then the burden on the entire group — including workers, regions, and shareholders — becomes worse over time.

### Bottom line
The controlling owners’ position is this:

- We support a **durable competitiveness reset**.
- We do **not** support a value-destructive escalation.
- We do **not** support a cosmetic compromise that postpones the problem.
- We are open to a negotiated package, but it must produce real structural savings and preserve Volkswagen’s long-term earning power.

If the employee side and Lower Saxony want to rule out closures and forced redundancies entirely, then the burden is on you to present an alternative that is just as effective and durable. Otherwise, management’s case for more forceful restructuring remains credible.

In short:  
**competitiveness must be restored, but not by wrecking the enterprise’s stability or governance balance.**
