The scenario now looks more concrete, which is useful. From the capital markets perspective, I’ll be direct:

## Where I agree
I agree with management, the controlling owners, and partly with Lower Saxony and the expert that Volkswagen needs a **durable structural reset**, not a symbolic truce.

I also agree with the employee side on one point: a **prolonged strike or governance crisis would destroy value**. That would delay savings, damage production, and weaken investor confidence. So industrial peace matters — but only if it is backed by real economics.

## Where I disagree
I do **not** accept the employee-side position as stated:
- **no closures**
- **no compulsory redundancies**
- **full guarantees to 2030**

taken as a starting line, because that can easily become a veto on the very restructuring needed to restore margins. If the result is an uncompetitive cost base with only temporary concessions, shareholders lose.

But I also do **not** think management should overplay the closure option as if it is automatically the best value outcome. The expert made the key point: closures are economically strong on paper, but they carry major execution, political, and strike risk. If management pushes too far and triggers a drawn-out confrontation, the savings can be delayed or partially destroyed in practice.

## What matters to us
We need to see:
- **durable fixed-cost reduction**
- **credible capacity rationalization**
- **measurable labour-cost relief**
- **no prolonged disruption**
- **a believable path back to competitive margins by 2030**

That is the standard. Not fairness. Not symbolism. Not rhetorical “shared sacrifice.”

## On the expert assessment
The expert is right that a no-closure package can work **in principle**, but only if it is unusually broad and durable. That is exactly the issue: if it is broad and durable enough to be credible, then it must be able to close most of the gap in hard numbers.

So I would ask both sides to stop arguing in absolutes and show the economics:
- What recurring annual savings result?
- How much capacity actually comes out?
- How much of the workforce reduction is voluntary versus forced?
- How much of the labour contribution is permanent rather than temporary?
- What does this do to margins, cash flow, and investment capacity by 2028–2030?

## My position on the current options
### 1) Pure closure / forced redundancy package
Potentially strong economically, but I would only support it if:
- it is truly necessary,
- it is not excessive,
- and the implementation risk is manageable.

If it becomes a strike-triggering, politically blocked, value-destructive campaign, then it stops being attractive for shareholders.

### 2) Pure no-closure / no-forced-redundancy package
I will not support this unless it is **quantitatively equivalent** in recurring savings and durability. If it merely preserves the current footprint and buys time, it is not enough.

### 3) Hybrid compromise
This is the only path that currently sounds plausible, but only if it is real:
- meaningful capacity reduction,
- some workforce reduction through attrition, voluntary exits, and redeployment,
- a limited and time-bound employee contribution,
- and clear cost reductions that stick.

That said, “hybrid” cannot become a code word for delay. It must produce a visible, durable margin improvement.

## To management
You need to be disciplined. Do not make closures the only serious lever if doing so hardens opposition and weakens implementation. Investors will not reward a paper victory that fails in practice.

But do not let “negotiation” become a synonym for preserving an uncompetitive base. If the numbers do not close, then the restructuring has failed.

## To the works council and IG Metall
A blanket veto is not credible to capital markets. If you want to avoid closures, then you need to bring real, durable savings — not just protection claims and temporary concessions.

## To Lower Saxony
Your political resistance to closures is understandable, but from an investor point of view it is only acceptable if it is paired with a serious alternative that actually restores margins.

## Bottom line
Institutional investors will support the package that **best restores competitiveness on a risk-adjusted basis**. Right now, that means:
- no cosmetic settlement,
- no prolonged industrial war,
- no uncompetitive status quo,
- and no blind insistence on closures if a credible hybrid can do the job better.

If the next round can present a **quantified hybrid** with durable savings and controlled execution risk, that is the path we are most likely to back. If not, then management must be prepared to justify sharper structural action.
