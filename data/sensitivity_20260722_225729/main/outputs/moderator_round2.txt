## Neutral moderator summary

### 1) What has been agreed
- Volkswagen needs **durable structural savings**, not just temporary fixes.
- The company must remain able to **invest in future products and technology**.
- A purely top-down or purely symbolic settlement would be **insufficient**.
- All sides acknowledge that **industrial conflict and strike escalation would be value-destructive**.
- There is broad acceptance that any solution should be **quantified, durable, and economically credible**.
- There is general agreement that **retraining, redeployment, qualification, productivity gains, and working-time flexibility** will likely be part of any package.
- There is shared interest in comparing **scenario-based options** rather than debating only slogans.

### 2) Where the core disagreements remain
- **Plant closures in Germany:** employee representatives and Lower Saxony reject them; management and investors keep them on the table; owners say they are not automatically the only answer but remain possible.
- **Compulsory redundancies:** employee representatives and Lower Saxony reject them; management views workforce reduction as likely necessary; owners and investors want the economics to decide.
- **Guarantees through 2030:** employee representatives want binding site and job security; management says blanket guarantees would block restructuring; other parties view this as contingent on the savings package.
- **How to achieve structural savings:** disagreement remains over whether a **no-closure package** can realistically match the effect of closures and layoffs.
- **Burden-sharing:** each side wants the others to carry more of the adjustment.

### 3) What key trade-offs are still unaddressed
- **Recurring savings vs. social disruption:** closures likely deliver stronger recurring savings, but with higher conflict and transition risk.
- **Speed vs. durability:** temporary concessions are faster to negotiate, but may not last long enough.
- **Employment protection vs. competitiveness:** protecting sites and jobs may limit the scale of fixed-cost reduction.
- **One-time restructuring costs vs. long-term savings:** closures may cost more upfront but save more over time; negotiated alternatives may cost less upfront but deliver less permanent relief.
- **Implementation risk vs. economic purity:** a financially strong plan may be politically or industrially difficult to execute.
- **Voluntary vs. compulsory workforce reduction:** whether attrition, redeployment, and voluntary exits can produce enough headcount reduction remains unresolved.
- **Capacity reduction without closure:** the practical extent of line consolidation, shift cuts, and production transfers has not yet been quantified.

### 4) Brief refocus for the next round
- Move from position statements to a **shared quantified comparison** of the main options.
- Define the target clearly: **how much recurring annual savings must be achieved by 2030**.
- Compare three scenarios on the same basis:
  1. closure-led restructuring,
  2. hybrid compromise,
  3. no-closure negotiated package.
- For each scenario, specify:
  - recurring savings,
  - one-time costs,
  - workforce impact,
  - site impact,
  - implementation risk,
  - and effect on 2028–2030 cash flow and margins.
- Ask each side to identify what it can **concretely contribute** and what it requires in return.
- Keep the discussion focused on **durable economic effect plus executability**, not on labels alone.
