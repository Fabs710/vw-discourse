I’ll respond directly.

We all agree on the diagnosis: Volkswagen has a real competitiveness problem, and a cosmetic solution will fail. But I need to be equally clear that the employee side’s current line — “no closures, no compulsory redundancies, full site guarantees to 2030” — does **not** solve the structural problem we face.

### Where I agree
I agree that:
- this must be a negotiated settlement, not a pure command-and-control exercise;
- temporary measures alone are insufficient;
- we need investment in future products and technology;
- retraining, redeployment, qualification, and working-time flexibility are part of any serious package.

### Where I disagree
I do **not** accept that we can restore competitiveness while ruling out the instruments that actually remove structural overcapacity.

You are asking management to guarantee every site, every job-security framework, and every legacy protection first, and only then to “find savings” elsewhere. That is backwards. It would leave the cost base fundamentally uncompetitive and would simply postpone the crisis.

Let me say it plainly:
- **No closures, no compulsory redundancies, no meaningful reduction in fixed costs** is not a restructuring plan.
- It is a wish list.
- And it would fail the brand’s return-on-sales target and the 2030 investment agenda.

### To the works council and IG Metall
You keep demanding “hard numbers.” Fair enough. Here are the hard realities:

- German factory costs are materially above target.
- Demand in Europe is shrinking.
- The company cannot carry excess capacity and still fund the product offensive we need.
- Structural savings must be large enough to change the cost base permanently, not just defer it for a year or two.

If you want to avoid closures, then bring an alternative that is **economically equivalent** in hard numbers:
- How much capacity comes out?
- How much fixed cost comes out?
- How much labour cost comes out?
- What is the timeline?
- What is binding and durable through 2030?

Temporary working-time reductions, internal transfers, and qualification measures can help, but they do **not** by themselves solve overcapacity. If that is all that is on offer, then we are not talking about a durable solution.

### To Lower Saxony
I understand your concern about irreversible damage. But a blanket refusal of closures is not a responsible position if no equivalent structural alternative is provided.

If the state wants to protect the regional industrial base, then it should support a solution that preserves competitiveness, not one that freezes the company in an uncompetitive structure. The greater threat to the workforce and the region is not adjustment — it is prolonged underperformance, lost investment capacity, and continued margin erosion.

### To the controlling owners and institutional investors
I agree with both of you on one central point: cosmetic compromise is unacceptable.

But I want to be equally clear that the market will not reward a deal that preserves the current cost structure and merely wraps it in language about “shared sacrifice.” The company needs:
- real fixed-cost reduction,
- real capacity reduction,
- and a real labour-cost contribution.

Anything less will not restore investor confidence or free capital for the technology transition.

### My position
So management’s position remains:
- We are prepared to negotiate.
- We are open on the exact mix of measures.
- But we will not accept a settlement that leaves the cost base structurally uncompetitive.
- We will not accept a package that protects all sites and all legacy guarantees while asking the business to fund the future with one hand tied behind its back.

If the employee side wants to rule out closures and forced redundancies entirely, then the burden is on you to produce a credible alternative with the same economic effect. Until that happens, plant closures and deeper workforce reductions remain on the table.

### What we need next
If this discussion is to move forward, I need one of two things:
1. a quantified employee-side alternative that closes the structural gap; or
2. acceptance that some combination of closures, capacity cuts, and workforce reductions will be part of the final package.

Without one of those, we are not negotiating a solution — we are negotiating the delay of a solution.
