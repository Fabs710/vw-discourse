We have heard the same pattern for four rounds now: management keeps asking the workforce to prove a negative, while the employee side is being asked to surrender sites and job security upfront and trust that the rest will somehow work out.

That is not a serious basis for bargaining.

## To management
I agree on the diagnosis:
- Volkswagen needs durable structural savings.
- Temporary fixes are not enough.
- A cosmetic agreement would fail.
- Industrial conflict would be damaging.

Where I **disagree** is your attempt to turn that diagnosis into a one-way threat:
- closures,
- compulsory redundancies,
- and then perhaps “some” concessions from workers.

No. If you want a durable settlement, the burden is on you to show why the structural gap cannot be closed without breaking the red lines that matter to the workforce.

The expert was clear:
- a **no-closure package is possible in principle**
- if it is broad, durable, and quantified.

That means the alternative is not fantasy. It exists. So stop treating closures as the default answer and put the actual numbers on the table:
- savings by measure,
- timing,
- permanence,
- and what happens if voluntary exits underdeliver.

## To the controlling owners
I agree that cosmetic compromise is worthless.  
I also agree that Volkswagen must remain competitive.

But I strongly disagree with the implication that value preservation requires plant closures as a first resort.

A closure may produce neat headlines on paper. In reality, it risks:
- strike escalation,
- political blockage,
- trust destruction,
- and a weaker restructuring outcome overall.

That is not “discipline.” That is gambling with industrial stability.

If you want value preservation, support a package that:
- keeps German plants open,
- avoids compulsory redundancies,
- and shares the burden fairly.

## To Lower Saxony
Your red line against closures and compulsory redundancies is right.  
But it has to be more than a statement.

If you are serious, then use your blocking position to support a real no-closure framework:
- binding site security,
- binding investment commitments,
- and a quantified restructuring path that does not rely on breaking the workforce settlement.

## To the works council
We are aligned on the core line:
- no closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- no dismantling of the collective framework.

I do want to sharpen one point: employee contribution is only acceptable if it is **strictly limited, reversible, and conditional**. It cannot become a permanent wage sacrifice or a backdoor weakening of standards.

A contribution can be discussed only if it is tied to:
- site security through 2030,
- job security through 2030,
- co-determination in the restructuring,
- and a genuine no-closure solution.

## What I am prepared to discuss
I am not rejecting restructuring. I am rejecting a restructuring that puts the workforce last and calls that realism.

A fair package could include:
- working-time flexibility,
- temporary and limited wage restraint,
- internal transfers,
- retraining and qualification,
- productivity improvements,
- shift reduction,
- line consolidation,
- voluntary exits,
- and capacity reduction without closure.

What it cannot include is:
- compulsory redundancies,
- plant closures,
- or an open-ended erosion of the wage framework.

## What I reject explicitly
- A closure-first approach.
- Forced layoffs dressed up as inevitability.
- A deal where workers pay first and others contribute later, if at all.
- Any “durable” solution that destroys trust and industrial peace.

## Bottom line
We are not the obstacle to restructuring.  
We are the obstacle to **bad restructuring**.

If management wants a settlement, it must bring a **quantified no-closure package** that protects jobs, sites, and the collective wage framework while delivering real savings.

If it refuses to do that, then it is management — not the workforce — choosing conflict.
