Management keeps asking for “structural savings” as if that phrase automatically justifies plant closures and forced redundancies. It does not.

Let me be very clear in round 2:

- **We agree** that Volkswagen needs a durable competitiveness solution.
- **We agree** that temporary measures alone are not enough.
- **We agree** that the company needs a credible investment path into future products.
- **We do not agree** that closing German plants or imposing compulsory redundancies is an acceptable starting point.

### To management
You say the cost base is too high and capacity too large. Fine — then show us the **full hard numbers** for the gap you claim, and show us why it cannot be closed without destroying sites and jobs.

So far, what we have heard is:
- “closures may be necessary,”
- “job reductions may be necessary,”
- “legacy protections must go,”

but not a serious demonstration that all alternatives have been exhausted.

That is not a restructuring plan. That is a threat.

If management wants credibility, then present a package built on:
- capacity adjustment through **non-closure** measures,
- internal redeployment,
- working-time flexibility,
- qualification and transformation programmes,
- targeted efficiency improvements,
- and a **limited, negotiated workforce contribution**.

What we will not accept is a plan that starts from the assumption that the workforce must pay by losing plants and jobs.

### To the controlling owners
You say you do not support a value-destructive escalation. On that point, we agree.

But let’s be honest: a plant closure and compulsory redundancies would be exactly that. It would not just destroy jobs. It would damage trust, fuel industrial conflict, and undermine the very co-determination structure that has made Volkswagen stable for decades.

You also say the burden is on us to show equivalent savings. That is fair in principle. But then management must stop hiding behind vague claims and put the real figures on the table:
- Which sites?
- How many jobs?
- What savings?
- Over what horizon?
- What is the impact on future production and logistics?
- What is the cost of strike escalation and lost output?

Without that, “structural savings” is just a slogan.

### To Lower Saxony
Your position is important, and I appreciate that you reject closures and mass compulsory redundancies. But that has to translate into an actual blocking stance, not merely a political warning.

We need a clear common line:  
**no German plant closure, no compulsory redundancies, binding site guarantees through 2030, and full respect for co-determination in any transformation plan.**

If those points are not secured, then the workforce has every reason to assume that management is preparing a fait accompli.

### To IG Metall
We are aligned on the essentials. I want to be explicit: any employee contribution must remain **limited, time-bound, and conditional** on binding guarantees. No open-ended concessions. No permanent erosion of the job-security framework. No deals that create “flexibility” for management while leaving workers exposed.

### Our position for this round
If the company wants serious negotiations, then the only credible framework is:

1. **No plant closures in Germany.**  
2. **No compulsory redundancies.**  
3. **Binding job and site guarantees to 2030.**  
4. **A genuine co-determination role in shaping restructuring.**  
5. **A negotiated package of alternative savings measures**, including:
   - temporary working-time reductions,
   - internal transfers,
   - retraining and qualification,
   - productivity improvements,
   - and a fair, limited workforce contribution.

That is the floor. Not the ceiling.

### What is not acceptable
- Turning overcapacity into closure plans.
- Calling forced job cuts “unavoidable” before alternatives are exhausted.
- Using shareholder pressure to break the historic protections of the workforce.
- Demanding concessions without binding guarantees in return.

If management wants to argue that no other route exists, then it must prove it with numbers, not slogans. Until then, our position stands: **the German plants stay open, the jobs stay protected, and co-determination remains real.**
