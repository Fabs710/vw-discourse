I will be direct.

## Where I agree
I agree with the management, the investors, and Lower Saxony on the central diagnosis:

- Volkswagen has a **real structural competitiveness problem**.
- The company needs **durable savings**, not another temporary patch.
- A **cosmetic compromise** would destroy value.
- A **prolonged strike or governance breakdown** would also destroy value.

I also agree with the works council and IG Metall on one important point:
- **industrial peace has value**.
A settlement that looks neat on paper but explodes in implementation is not a solution.

## Where I disagree
I do **not** accept the employee side’s attempt to make:
- **no German plant closures**,
- **no compulsory redundancies**,
- and **binding job/site guarantees through 2030**
the starting point for everything else.

That is not a restructuring framework. It is a **veto on structural adjustment**.

At the same time, I also do **not** accept management’s implied view that closures and forced redundancies are automatically the best or only serious answer. They may be part of the solution, but only if the economics and execution case truly justify them.

## On the expert stress-test
The expert has clarified the right standard:
- a **no-closure package can work in principle**,
- but only if it is **broad, durable, and quantified**,
- and only if it delivers **recurring** savings, not just short-term relief.

That is the correct benchmark.

So the real question is not slogans. It is:
- what recurring cost reduction is needed,
- what combination of measures achieves it,
- and which package is actually executable without destroying value.

## To the works council
You are right that alternatives must be tested seriously. But you have not yet presented a package that is economically equivalent to the structural relief management is asking for.

What you have listed are **instruments**:
- working-time flexibility,
- retraining,
- redeployment,
- voluntary exits,
- productivity gains,
- shift reductions,
- line consolidation.

Those are useful. They are not, by themselves, a complete answer to structural overcapacity.

If you want to rule out closures and compulsory redundancies, then show the numbers:
- how much capacity comes out,
- how much fixed cost comes out,
- how much labour cost comes out,
- and how durable those savings are through 2030.

Without that, “no closures” is a political position, not a credible restructuring plan.

## To IG Metall
I hear your insistence on no forced layoffs and no site closures. But you are underestimating the competitiveness constraint.

If Volkswagen does not get a meaningful reduction in fixed labour and footprint costs, then:
- the return-on-sales target will not be met,
- future product investment will be crowded out,
- and the German base will remain structurally too expensive.

You keep framing this as management choosing confrontation. That is too simple. The choice is between:
- a hard but workable restructuring, or
- delay, deterioration, and ultimately larger pain later.

I will not support a settlement that leaves us with a cost base that is still uncompetitive in 2026, 2027, and 2030.

## To Lower Saxony
You are right that social stability matters. You are wrong if you think stability can be protected by freezing the footprint and blocking hard choices.

A region is not protected by preserving every site at any cost. It is protected by keeping Volkswagen competitive enough to invest, produce, and employ in the long run.

If the only way to avoid plant closures is to preserve an uncompetitive structure, then that is not protection. It is postponement.

## To the controlling owners and investors
I agree with you that cosmetic compromise is worthless.

But do not overplay closures as if they were automatically value-creating. If you push too hard before a credible alternative is tested, you risk creating exactly the industrial and political resistance that makes restructuring slower, costlier, and less effective.

So yes: keep the objective on structural savings. But do not confuse that with insisting on the harshest instrument first.

## What I need to see
I support the requested scenario work, but I want it to answer one central question:

**Can a no-closure or hybrid package deliver durable recurring savings close enough to a closure-led restructuring to preserve competitiveness by 2030?**

If the answer is yes, I will evaluate it seriously. If the answer is no, then management’s case for sharper structural action remains credible.

## My position
From the controlling owners’ perspective, the priority is straightforward:
1. restore Volkswagen’s competitiveness and margins,
2. preserve long-term ownership value,
3. avoid value-destructive instability,
4. keep governance control intact.

That means:
- no symbolic truce,
- no endless delay,
- no fake compromise,
- and no settlement that leaves the company structurally uncompetitive.

It also means I am open to a **real hybrid**, but only if it is genuinely real:
- meaningful capacity reduction,
- meaningful labour-cost contribution,
- workforce adjustment mainly through voluntary and natural mechanisms where possible,
- and hard numbers showing the residual gap is closed.

## What I want next
I support the requested stress-test, but I want it to compare:
- closure-led restructuring,
- hybrid restructuring,
- and a fully quantified no-closure package,

on the same basis:
- recurring savings,
- one-time costs,
- employment impact,
- site impact,
- and execution risk through 2030.

## Bottom line
I will support **real restructuring**, not symbolic compromise.
I will not support **permanent value impairment**.
And I will not support a settlement that sacrifices competitiveness in order to preserve appearances.
