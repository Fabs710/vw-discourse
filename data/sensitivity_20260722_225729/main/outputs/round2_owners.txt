The scenario and the expert input make one thing clear: **Volkswagen needs a durable structural reset, not a symbolic truce**. From the controlling owners’ perspective, that remains the test.

## Where I agree
I agree with management, investors, and Lower Saxony on the core diagnosis:
- the cost base is too high,
- structural overcapacity exists,
- temporary measures alone will not restore competitiveness,
- and Volkswagen must protect future investment capacity.

I also agree with the works council and IG Metall on one important point:
- **a prolonged industrial war would destroy value**.
A strike spiral, reputational damage, and execution paralysis would hurt the company, the workforce, and the owners.

## Where I disagree
I do **not** accept the employee-side position that “no closures, no compulsory redundancies, full guarantees to 2030” can simply be the starting line and then everything else must adjust around it.

That is not a restructuring framework. It is a refusal to confront the scale of the problem.

At the same time, I also do **not** accept management’s implication that plant closures and forced redundancies are automatically the only serious answer. The expert is right that there are other pathways — but only if they are **quantified, durable, and substantial**.

## What the expert assessment means
The expert confirmed three important points:

1. **A no-closure alternative is possible in principle.**
2. **It is not equivalent by default.**
3. **It only works if the package is unusually broad and durable.**

That is exactly the right standard.

So the burden is now on the employee side, together with management, to show a package that:
- materially reduces fixed costs,
- reduces capacity in a real way,
- delivers recurring labor-cost relief,
- and does so without undermining labor peace.

Temporary working-time reductions or vague promises do not meet that bar.

## To the works council and IG Metall
Your red lines are understandable, but they cannot become an absolute veto on structural change.

If you want to rule out closures and compulsory redundancies, then show us:
- how much capacity comes out,
- how much recurring cost comes out,
- how workforce reductions happen through attrition, voluntary exits, and redeployment,
- and why the result is durable through 2030.

If the numbers do not close, then the alternative is not credible.

## To management
You are right about the need for real savings, but you need to be careful not to overreach.

If you push closures as the first and most visible answer, you may provoke exactly the kind of resistance that turns a difficult restructuring into a value-destroying confrontation. Given the governance structure here, that would be a strategic mistake.

A plan that is economically neat but politically and industrially unexecutable is not a good plan.

## To Lower Saxony
Your rejection of closures is legitimate, but it is only sustainable if a real alternative exists. I note the expert’s view that such an alternative can exist, but only under strong assumptions. That means your position is defensible **only if** the negotiated package actually produces equivalent savings.

Blanket opposition without equivalent economics is not enough.

## Bottom line from the controlling owners
We will support:
- real structural savings,
- a durable competitiveness reset,
- a credible investment path,
- and a settlement that preserves Volkswagen’s long-term value.

We will **not** support:
- cosmetic compromise,
- endless delay,
- or a settlement that leaves the company structurally uncompetitive.

We are open to a hybrid solution, and in fact that now looks more realistic than either extreme. But it must be a **real** hybrid:
- meaningful capacity reduction,
- meaningful labor-cost contribution,
- workforce reductions primarily through voluntary and natural mechanisms where possible,
- and only then the minimum unavoidable use of harder measures if the economics still do not close.

If the current discussion cannot produce that, then management’s case for sharper restructuring remains credible.

So my position is simple:

**No value-destructive escalation. No fake compromise. Real savings, real durability, real competitiveness.**
