We have been clear from the start, and I will not dilute it now:

**No German plant closures. No compulsory redundancies. No deal that makes the workforce carry the full burden while management and owners keep their distance.**

That is our line.

## To management
You keep repeating “structural savings” as if the words themselves justify closures and forced layoffs. They do not.

What you have still not shown is:
- that every alternative has been exhausted,
- that capacity reduction cannot happen without closure,
- that workforce adjustment cannot be done through attrition, voluntary exits, redeployment, and working-time tools,
- and that your preferred route is economically superior once strike risk, trust destruction, and execution disruption are included.

The expert did not say closures are necessary. The expert said a **no-closure package is possible in principle** if it is broad, durable, and quantified. That is the real benchmark.

So if you want a serious negotiation, then stop using closures as your opening move and put numbers on the alternative:
- how much capacity comes out,
- how much labor cost comes out,
- how much fixed cost comes out,
- what is temporary and what is permanent,
- and how the plan protects the future of the German plants.

We are prepared to discuss a fair employee contribution. We are **not** prepared to discuss destroying sites and throwing people out as the price of “competitiveness.”

## To the controlling owners
You are right about one thing: cosmetic compromise is worthless.

But do not confuse that with “therefore closures are fine.” They are not.

A settlement that breaks trust, provokes strikes, and undermines co-determination is not durable. It is reckless. The company’s social stability is not an add-on; it is part of its industrial strength.

If you want durable value protection, then support a package that includes:
- binding site security,
- no forced redundancies,
- real investment commitments,
- and a shared-sacrifice arrangement.

If you insist that employees must first accept closure threats and then negotiate, you are not seeking a solution. You are trying to dictate one.

## To Lower Saxony
Your political stance matters, but it must be more than a statement.

If you truly reject closures and compulsory redundancies, then use your blocking position accordingly. The workforce should not have to infer your red lines from press comments.

We need a common line in practice:
- no German plant closure,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- no hollowing out of collective protections,
- and a real alternative restructuring path.

If Lower Saxony is serious, it should help force management toward that framework.

## To the expert findings
The expert analysis is useful because it confirms something management does not want to admit:

- A **no-closure** restructuring can work **in principle**.
- It is not automatically easy.
- It requires durable capacity reduction, recurring labor-cost relief, and real workforce adjustment through non-compulsory means.

Exactly. That means the next step is not to reopen closure threats. It is to build the quantified alternative properly.

If management claims the gap is too large, then show the math. If the gap can only be closed through closures, then that claim has to survive scrutiny on:
- industrial execution,
- labor conflict,
- political blockage,
- and long-term brand damage.

A spreadsheet that ignores those factors is not an honest basis for restructuring.

## Our position on concessions
We remain prepared to discuss a **limited, time-bound, and conditional employee contribution**, but only under these conditions:
- **no closures**
- **no compulsory redundancies**
- **binding site and job security through 2030**
- **protection of the collective wage framework**
- **full co-determination in the restructuring design**

That contribution can include:
- temporary working-time flexibility,
- limited and reversible wage restraint,
- internal redeployment,
- retraining and qualification,
- productivity improvements,
- and capacity reduction without closing plants.

What it cannot include is an open-ended erosion of standards or a one-sided rescue plan where workers pay, and management merely administers the cuts.

## What we reject
- Forced layoffs dressed up as inevitability.
- Plant closures presented as the only “serious” option.
- Any plan that uses investor impatience to break the workforce settlement.
- Any solution that preserves management flexibility by stripping workers of security.

## Bottom line
We are not the obstacle to a solution. We are the obstacle to a bad solution.

If Volkswagen wants a durable settlement, then the company must come forward with a **quantified no-closure restructuring path** that protects jobs and sites while delivering real savings.

If it cannot or will not do that, then it is management — not the workforce — that is choosing conflict.
