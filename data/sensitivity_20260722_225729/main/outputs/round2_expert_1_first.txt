The specific question raised by management is whether there exists a **quantified employee-side alternative** that can close the structural gap **without German plant closures or compulsory redundancies**, and whether such an alternative could plausibly achieve an economically comparable effect by 2030.

My assessment, as an industrial restructuring and financial modeling matter, is:

## Short answer
**Yes, in principle—but only if the alternative is unusually broad, durable, and heavily quantified.**  
A package built only on temporary concessions, working-time cuts, or vague “efficiency” commitments is **not** economically equivalent to closures and forced layoffs. To be credible, it would need to combine:

- **meaningful capacity reduction**
- **permanent fixed-cost reduction**
- **labor-cost relief with multi-year duration**
- **significant productivity improvement**
- **capex discipline and product-mix improvement**
- **and likely some workforce reduction through attrition, transfers, and voluntary separation**

If the company’s structural gap is large, then avoiding closures and compulsory redundancies usually means the burden must be shifted into:
1. **voluntary exits / early retirement / natural attrition**,  
2. **working-time and pay measures**,  
3. **higher plant utilization through consolidation without closure**, and  
4. **a credible commitment to product allocation and investment**.

That can sometimes substitute for closures, but only if the savings are real, durable, and sufficiently large.

## Core analytical point
A restructuring of this type should be judged on **cash flow impact** and **permanent EBITDA uplift**, not on labels like “closure” versus “no closure.”

A plant closure usually delivers savings from:
- avoided labor cost,
- avoided overhead,
- lower maintenance and utilities,
- reduced logistics complexity,
- lower depreciation on underutilized assets over time,
- and better network efficiency.

An alternative package must replicate most of that economic effect. In practice, the main challenge is that **working-time reductions and temporary wage restraint are not equivalent** to permanent capacity removal, because they:
- often expire,
- depend on continued cooperation,
- and do not eliminate fixed asset and overhead burdens.

## What would make an alternative credible
A non-closure package would need to contain measurable components such as:

### 1) Capacity rationalization without site closure
This can include:
- line consolidation,
- removal of shifts,
- product concentration at fewer lines,
- shared modules across plants,
- and production transfers between sites.

This is economically meaningful only if it produces **permanent utilization gains**. If it simply moves inefficiency around the network, the savings will be limited.

### 2) Workforce reduction primarily through voluntary means
If compulsory redundancies are excluded, then the reduction has to come from:
- retirement programs,
- severance packages,
- internal redeployment to growth areas,
- hiring freezes,
- and attrition.

That can work, but it is slower and often costlier upfront than layoffs. The trade-off is:
- **lower social conflict**
- in exchange for **higher near-term cash cost** and **less certainty**.

### 3) Labor-cost contribution
This can include:
- temporary pay restraint,
- reduction or suspension of bonuses,
- longer working hours for the same pay in selected areas,
- conversion of variable pay to deferred forms,
- and reduced benefit generosity.

For modeling purposes, the key issue is whether the concession is:
- **temporary**
- or **structural**.

Only structural concessions count as true substitutes for closures.

### 4) Productivity and process improvements
These are often overclaimed. They must be modeled conservatively:
- reduced scrap,
- shorter changeover times,
- lower indirect headcount,
- procurement savings,
- and automation.

These are real, but they usually do not close a major overcapacity gap by themselves.

## Financial modeling view
If management says the company needs “durable structural savings,” the question is usually: **how large is the recurring annual EBIT or cash-flow gap?**

A workable model should quantify:
- annual fixed-cost reduction,
- annual variable-cost reduction,
- one-time restructuring charges,
- working-capital effects,
- severance and retraining cost,
- and capex needed to support the new footprint.

### Typical economic comparison
A plant closure tends to have:
- **high one-time restructuring charges**
- but **large recurring annual savings**

A negotiated alternative without closures tends to have:
- **lower one-time charges**
- but also **lower recurring savings**
- unless it includes serious workforce and capacity measures

So the key test is:

> Do the recurring savings from the alternative, net of its costs, reach a level close enough to the closure case to preserve competitiveness and fund future investment?

If not, then management will be right that the alternative is not equivalent.

## The main limitation of a no-closure package
The biggest problem is **fixed-cost absorption**.

Even if labor costs are reduced, a plant network with too much capacity still carries:
- maintenance,
- utilities,
- site overhead,
- logistics inefficiency,
- and management complexity.

So if the company truly has structural overcapacity, then avoiding closures generally requires:
- either a meaningful reduction in output footprint,
- or a strong product allocation shift that raises utilization elsewhere.

Without that, savings tend to be partial and fragile.

## My judgment on feasibility
### Feasible if:
- the needed savings are moderate rather than extreme,
- the company can sharply improve utilization,
- several thousand job reductions can be achieved through attrition/voluntary exits,
- labor gives up enough recurring cost,
- and management accepts less immediate flexibility in exchange for social peace.

### Not feasible if:
- the overcapacity is severe,
- multiple plants are structurally underutilized,
- the brand needs a large margin step-up quickly,
- and the required savings must be fully locked in by 2030.

In that case, a no-closure, no-compulsory-redundancy package is usually **not fully economically equivalent**, unless the employee side is willing to deliver a much larger and more durable contribution than is typically assumed in public bargaining.

## Practical conclusion for the negotiation
The discussion should not be framed as:
- “closures versus no closures”

but as:
- “What recurring annual savings, by line item, are needed?”
- “Which combination of measures gets closest?”
- “What is the residual gap if closures are excluded?”
- “Who bears the one-time versus recurring cost?”
- “How much certainty is required for investor confidence?”

## Bottom line
My expert view is:

- A **quantified employee-side alternative is possible in principle**, but only if it contains **durable capacity reduction, meaningful workforce reduction through non-compulsory means, and recurring labor-cost relief**.
- **Temporary working-time measures and nonbinding promises are not enough.**
- If the restructuring gap is large, then a no-closure package will likely require **more burden on employees up front** than is politically comfortable, and may still fall short of the full economic effect of closures.
