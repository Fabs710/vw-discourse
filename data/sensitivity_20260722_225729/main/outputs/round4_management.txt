I’ll be equally direct.

## Where I agree
I agree with everyone on the **diagnosis**:
- Volkswagen has a structural cost problem.
- Temporary fixes are not enough.
- A cosmetic deal would be a mistake.
- Industrial conflict would hurt the company.

I also accept the expert’s point: a **no-closure package can exist in principle** if it is broad, durable, and quantified.

## Where I disagree
What I do **not** accept is the attempt to turn that principle into a **blanket veto** on the tools that may actually be required to restore competitiveness.

The employee side keeps saying:
- no German plant closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- binding job and site guarantees through 2030,
- and then expects management to somehow close the entire cost gap.

That is not a restructuring plan. That is a red line set before the economics are solved.

## To the Works Council
You say management has not proven closures are unavoidable. Fair enough — but you still have not produced a credible alternative that closes the gap with equal durability.

What you list is a set of instruments:
- working-time flexibility,
- retraining,
- redeployment,
- productivity gains,
- voluntary exits,
- shift reductions,
- line consolidation.

I do not dismiss those. But they are **components**, not yet a complete package.

If you want to rule out closures and compulsory redundancies, then I need a **quantified answer**:
- how much recurring annual savings by 2030,
- how much capacity is actually removed,
- how much labour cost comes out permanently,
- and what happens if voluntary exits fall short.

Until that is on the table, “no closures” is a position, not a solution.

## To IG Metall
I hear the insistence on no forced layoffs and no permanent erosion of standards. But you are ignoring the scale of the competitiveness problem.

If Volkswagen does not materially reduce fixed labour and footprint costs, then:
- the return-on-sales target will remain out of reach,
- investment capacity for future products will be crowded out,
- and the German base will stay structurally too expensive.

A limited, time-bound employee contribution may be part of the answer. But it cannot be the only answer, and it cannot substitute for real structural savings.

## To Lower Saxony
I understand your political and regional concerns. But a region is not protected by freezing an uncompetitive structure.

If the only way to avoid plant closures is to preserve too much excess capacity and too much cost, then that is not a protection strategy. It is a postponement strategy.

I am open to a negotiated solution. I am not open to a solution that leaves the company unable to compete.

## To the controlling owners
You are right to reject a fake compromise. I agree. But you should also be careful not to treat closures as automatically superior.

Closures may produce savings on paper, but they also raise:
- strike risk,
- political blockage,
- trust destruction,
- and execution risk.

That is why I am not saying closures are the goal. I am saying **durable structural savings** are the goal.

If the employee side can deliver equivalent, recurring savings without closures, I will evaluate that seriously. But they have not done so yet.

## To institutional investors
You want margin restoration and credibility. I want the same. But credibility does not come from the harshest headline; it comes from the package that actually works.

So the real test is simple:
- does the proposal reduce the cost base enough?
- does it do so durably?
- can it be implemented?

If yes, it is worth considering. If not, then it is not enough.

## My position
I will not sign up to a settlement that:
- preserves the current cost structure,
- leaves overcapacity intact,
- and calls that a compromise.

What I need is one of two things:
1. a **fully quantified no-closure package** that genuinely closes the gap, or
2. acceptance that **some combination of closures, capacity cuts, and workforce reductions** remains necessary.

I am not moving off the core objective:
- lower fixed costs,
- less overcapacity,
- a more competitive German footprint,
- and funding for product and technology investment through 2030.

## What I want next
Bring me a package with the numbers:
- recurring savings by measure,
- one-time costs,
- timing through 2028–2030,
- and implementation risk.

If the no-closure route is truly equivalent, prove it.  
If it is not, then we should stop pretending that a red line alone can solve Volkswagen’s competitiveness problem.
