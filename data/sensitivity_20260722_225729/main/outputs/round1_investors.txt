From the perspective of dispersed capital-market shareholders, the key issue is not who “wins” the bargaining, but whether Volkswagen gets back to a **credible, durable, investment-grade path to competitive margins**. On that basis, I can say:

### Where I agree
I agree with management, and partly with the controlling owners, on the diagnosis:
- the cost base is too high,
- German capacity is too heavy for the market outlook,
- and the current return-on-sales profile is not good enough.

I also agree that a purely cosmetic settlement would be unacceptable. If this ends with vague promises, temporary measures, and no structural change, then the equity story remains broken and capital will continue to be assigned elsewhere.

### Where I disagree
I do **not** think management should assume that plant closures and mass forced redundancies are the only credible answer.

That may be one route to structural savings, but it is not automatically the best one if it triggers a prolonged strike, political blocking, execution risk, and long damage to operating stability. Value destruction from a drawn-out confrontation can easily offset the intended savings, at least in the near and medium term.

At the same time, I do **not** accept the employee-side position that “no closures, no compulsory redundancies” can simply stand as a veto while leaving the economics unchanged. If the cost base remains structurally uncompetitive, then the equity holders bear the downside through lower margins, weaker cash generation, and a permanently lower valuation.

### What would move us
We would support a package only if it is visibly and credibly designed to restore competitiveness, with:
- **durable** fixed-cost reduction,
- a **clear capacity adjustment**,
- measurable labour-cost relief,
- no prolonged industrial disruption,
- and a believable investment path into future products.

That can be done through a mix of instruments. But the mix has to be economically real, not politically symbolic.

### My position on the current lines
- To management: do not treat closures as the only serious lever. If you push too hard, you may end up with a weaker deal, delayed implementation, and lower shareholder value.
- To the works council and IG Metall: a blanket refusal of closures and forced redundancies is not credible unless you can show equivalent savings in hard numbers.
- To Lower Saxony and the controlling owners: legitimacy matters, but so does capital discipline. Protecting the industrial footprint is not a substitute for a competitive cost base.

### Bottom line
Dispersed institutional investors will back **whatever clearly restores margins and cash generation**, but not a settlement that merely postpones the problem or preserves an uncompetitive structure. We are prepared to tolerate difficult restructuring if it is real, durable, and executable. We are **not** prepared to support an outcome that leaves Volkswagen structurally impaired and stuck in repeated bargaining cycles.

So the question for the table is simple:

**Show us a credible path to competitive margins with durable savings. If you cannot, then the market will assume the restructuring has failed, regardless of the rhetoric.**
