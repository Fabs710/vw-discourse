The expert assessment is useful, and it confirms something important: **a no-closure solution is possible in principle, but only if it is genuinely durable, quantified, and socially balanced**. That is the right standard. It also means management cannot simply assert that closures are the only serious option.

### Where I agree
I agree with management, the owners, and the investor side on the diagnosis:
- Volkswagen needs durable structural savings.
- Temporary fixes alone will not do.
- The company must restore competitiveness and protect future investment capacity.

I also agree with the expert that a settlement must be judged on **recurring economic effect**, not on rhetoric.

### Where I disagree
I do **not** accept the leap from “durable savings are needed” to “German plant closures and forced redundancies must therefore remain on the table as the default answer.”

That is not the only serious path, and it is not the one Lower Saxony can support. We have been explicit from the start: **we will not back closures in Germany or mass compulsory redundancies**. The expert has now shown that alternatives can be modeled. That is precisely why management must stop treating closures as the first resort.

### To management
You asked for hard numbers. Fair enough. The burden is now on management to put forward, or work with the employee side to produce, a **quantified alternative package** that achieves the same recurring savings as closely as possible **without closing German sites**.

But let me be equally clear: if the only way your model “works” is by closing plants and breaking the workforce settlement, then that is not a clean managerial victory. It is a high-risk political and industrial outcome that would damage Volkswagen’s legitimacy and, very likely, its execution.

A restructuring that triggers prolonged conflict is not automatically value-creating. It may look neat on a spreadsheet and fail in practice.

### To the works council and IG Metall
I appreciate the clarity that no one is simply defending the status quo. But a hard “no” to every structural adjustment also will not be enough. If closures are off the table, then the employee side has to help deliver:
- real capacity reduction,
- meaningful productivity gains,
- and a serious, limited workforce contribution.

The expert is right that temporary working-time measures are not enough by themselves. If we want to protect sites, then the alternative has to do real work economically.

### To the controlling owners and investors
You are right that cosmetic compromise is worthless. But you should also recognize that a blunt closure strategy can easily destroy more value than it creates if it undermines labor peace, public legitimacy, and the company’s operational continuity.

In other words: **structural savings matter, but so does the method by which they are achieved**. A solution that is economically “clean” but industrially explosive is not a good solution.

### My position
Lower Saxony’s position remains:
- **no German plant closures**,
- **no compulsory redundancies**,
- **preservation of co-determination**,
- **binding site and investment security**,
- and a negotiated restructuring that delivers real savings through other means.

That does **not** mean unlimited guarantees with no adjustment. It means the adjustment must come from:
- capacity rationalization without closures,
- internal reallocation,
- voluntary departures and attrition,
- working-time flexibility,
- productivity improvements,
- and a fair, time-bound employee contribution.

### What I need next
I support the expert’s suggestion that we move to a scenario-based comparison. But I would want that exercise to test not only the closure case, but also a **genuinely negotiated no-closure package** that preserves the regional industrial base.

So yes, let us quantify it. But no, that does not mean Lower Saxony is moving off its red lines.
