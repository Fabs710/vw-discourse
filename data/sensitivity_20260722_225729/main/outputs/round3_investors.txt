The capital markets position is now pretty clear:

## Where we agree
We agree with management, the controlling owners, and even Lower Saxony on the diagnosis:
- Volkswagen has a real structural cost problem.
- Temporary fixes are not enough.
- The company needs durable savings, not a cosmetic settlement.
- A prolonged strike or governance breakdown would destroy value.
- Any solution must preserve investment capacity for future products and technology.

We also accept the expert’s key point: a no-closure path is possible **in principle**, but only if it is **broad, durable, and quantified**.

## Where we disagree
We do **not** accept the employee-side position as currently framed:
- no plant closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- full guarantees to 2030,

if that becomes a de facto veto on real restructuring.

From a shareholder perspective, that would risk leaving Volkswagen with an uncompetitive cost base and structurally impaired margins. That is unacceptable.

At the same time, we also do **not** support management using closures as a default threat if the same economics can be achieved through a less destructive route. A closure-led plan may look strong on paper, but if it triggers prolonged industrial conflict, delayed implementation, or political blockage, the value case weakens fast.

## What we need to see
We need a package that delivers:
- **durable fixed-cost reduction**
- **real capacity rationalization**
- **measurable labor-cost relief**
- **controlled execution risk**
- **a credible path back to competitive margins by 2030**

That is the standard. Not symbolism. Not fairness language. Economics.

## On the current three paths
### 1) Closure-led restructuring
Potentially strongest on recurring savings, but only acceptable if:
- it is truly necessary,
- the savings are clearly superior,
- and the industrial/political risk is manageable.

If it leads to a strike spiral or implementation failure, it stops being value-creating.

### 2) Pure no-closure package
Not acceptable unless it is **quantitatively equivalent** in recurring savings and durability. Temporary working-time measures, vague redeployment promises, and short-lived wage restraint are not enough.

### 3) Hybrid compromise
This is currently the most plausible route, but only if it is real:
- meaningful capacity reduction,
- workforce reduction mostly through attrition, voluntary exits, and redeployment,
- some limited and time-bound employee contribution,
- and enough permanent savings to move margins materially.

But “hybrid” cannot mean delay or cosmetic balance. It must close the gap in hard numbers.

## Our message to management
Do not assume that the market will reward the harshest plan. It will reward the plan that restores competitiveness **with the highest risk-adjusted value**.

So:
- do not overplay closures if a credible hybrid exists;
- but do not accept an arrangement that preserves an uncompetitive structure and calls it compromise.

## Our message to the workforce side
A blanket veto is not credible to capital markets.

If closures and forced redundancies are to be ruled out, then you must put forward a package that actually closes the structural gap:
- real capacity reduction,
- real fixed-cost reduction,
- real workforce adjustment,
- and durable concessions, not temporary gestures.

## Our message to Lower Saxony
Your opposition to closures is understandable, but it remains acceptable only if there is a serious alternative that restores margins. Political resistance alone does not preserve value.

## Bottom line
Institutional investors will back the package that:
1. restores competitiveness,
2. protects margins and cash flow,
3. keeps disruption contained,
4. and is credible enough to execute through 2030.

Right now, that points most strongly to a **quantified hybrid restructuring**.  
If no one can show that such a package closes the gap, then management’s sharper restructuring case regains force.
