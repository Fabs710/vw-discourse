We are now at the point where the debate has to become concrete, not rhetorical.

I will respond directly to each side.

## To the Works Council and IG Metall

I hear your red lines. I also hear your refusal to engage with the scale of the structural problem.

You keep repeating:
- no German plant closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- no changes to legacy guarantees,
- no erosion of the collective framework.

But you still have not shown how that combination delivers the required permanent cost reset. Saying “there are alternatives” is not the same as proving them.

I agree with you on one thing: a cosmetic deal would be worse than no deal.  
I also agree that management, owners, and capital must contribute.  
But I do **not** agree that the answer is simply to preserve the existing footprint and then ask everyone else to absorb the mismatch through goodwill.

That does not restore competitiveness.

What I need from the employee side is not another statement of principle, but a **credible package with quantified permanent savings**:
- by site,
- by measure,
- by timing,
- and with proof that it closes the gap without closures.

Until then, “no closures” is not a solution. It is a constraint.

## To Lower Saxony

I respect the governance reality, but I reject the idea that your blocking position can be used to rule out hard choices in advance.

You say no closures and no forced redundancies. That may be politically understandable. It is not, by itself, economically sufficient.

If you want to prevent closures, then help produce a package that makes them unnecessary. That means:
- genuine capacity reduction,
- real labour-cost contribution,
- management and overhead cuts,
- and binding implementation milestones.

If you instead insist that every German site must remain untouched, then you are not protecting Volkswagen. You are protecting inertia.

## To the controlling owners

I agree with you more than with the employee side on the central issue: Volkswagen needs a durable structural reset, not a delay.

You are right that temporary measures alone are insufficient.  
You are right that the cost base must come down.  
You are right that we need measurable, enforceable savings.

Where I would press you harder is this: if you want value protection, then you must back the restructuring model that actually produces it. That may require supporting footprint rationalization if no equivalent package is delivered.

I do not accept a governance stance that says “change must happen” but then blocks the instruments required to achieve it.

## To the institutional investors

You are right to insist on credibility and implementation certainty.  
You are also right that wage restraint, bonus restraint, and productivity gains have to be part of the answer.

But I do not accept the suggestion that management should simply wait for an independent alternative to appear while the current structure continues to erode margins.

The company is under time pressure. Market pressure is real. The European volume outlook is weak. The cost problem is not hypothetical.

So yes, we should test alternatives rigorously. But they must be tested against a hard standard:
**Do they permanently reduce the cost base and capacity enough to restore returns?**

If not, they are not sufficient.

## On the auditor / expert point

The expert advice is useful, and I support it.

[SUMMON: independent plant-by-plant industrial and financial auditor to verify site-level utilization, fixed cost, capex, and savings from closure vs. non-closure measures]

We need this because we are no longer debating abstractions. We need plant-by-plant evidence on:
- utilization,
- unit cost,
- required capex,
- volume outlook,
- and the permanent savings from each option.

That is the only basis on which we can distinguish between:
- a genuinely equivalent non-closure package, and
- a politically convenient delay mechanism.

## My position, plainly stated

I am **not** seeking a social demolition plan.  
I am seeking a **durable industrial reset**.

That means:
- real capacity reduction,
- real labour-cost contribution,
- management and overhead cuts,
- and a structure that restores the brand to competitiveness by 2030.

I am open to achieving that **without** closures if, and only if, the alternative is genuinely equivalent in permanent savings and capacity reduction.

But I will not support a package that:
- freezes the footprint,
- protects every legacy arrangement,
- and leaves the cost base structurally uncompetitive.

That would be a cosmetic compromise, and I will not pretend otherwise.

## What would move me

Three things only:

1. **Plant-by-plant quantified savings**
   - not slogans, not generalities

2. **Binding labour contribution**
   - durable, measurable, and not just temporary restraint

3. **A restructuring package that closes the fixed-cost gap**
   - through closures, or through an alternative that is truly equivalent

If the other sides are willing to engage on that basis, we can negotiate.  
If not, then the responsibility for a prolonged conflict and continued margin erosion will not lie with management alone.
