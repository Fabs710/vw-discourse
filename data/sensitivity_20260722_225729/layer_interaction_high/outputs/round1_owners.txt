I will be direct.

I agree with one core point from management: Volkswagen cannot solve this by simply preserving the current cost structure and hoping the market improves. That would be value-destructive, and as long-term controlling owners we cannot support a path that leaves the group uncompetitive.

But I also need to be equally clear on the other side: I do **not** support a restructuring that destroys the company’s industrial base, triggers prolonged conflict, or undermines the governance and social stability that have made Volkswagen resilient over time.

### To management
Your diagnosis that cost and capacity must come down is credible enough to merit serious consideration. What is not yet credible is any assumption that plant closures or blunt layoffs are the only path to structural improvement.

If you want support from the controlling shareholders, you need to show a plan that does three things at once:

- restores margins and cash generation,
- preserves labour peace over the medium term,
- and does not permanently weaken the group’s long-term productive capacity.

A merely aggressive plan is not enough. A durable plan is required.

### To the Works Council and IG Metall
I understand the red lines you are drawing: no forced redundancies, no casual dismantling of protections, no treating the workforce as the sole adjustment variable. That concern is legitimate.

But I will also say plainly: a position that rules out closures, rules out compulsory redundancies, and rules out meaningful structural change all at once may become economically self-defeating if it leaves no room to fix the underlying overcapacity problem. We cannot endorse a solution that protects every existing structure regardless of market reality.

If you want to avoid closures, then the alternative must be more than slogans. It must deliver real, measurable, durable savings. That means serious discussion of:
- capacity reduction,
- working-time flexibility,
- redeployment,
- productivity improvements,
- cost contributions from labour,
- and possibly changes to legacy guarantees.

### To Lower Saxony
I respect the role of the state and the VW Law constraints. But I do not accept the implication that “no closures” is a sufficient strategy in itself. A blanket refusal to consider plant rationalisation risks converting a competitiveness problem into a long-term erosion problem.

The state’s role should be to help secure a durable industrial future, not freeze the footprint indefinitely. If the company can achieve equivalent savings through other measures, that is preferable. But if those alternatives do not add up, then governance cannot be used simply to block adjustment forever.

### Where I stand
Our position is this:

- We support a **serious restructuring**.
- We are open to **solutions that avoid closures**, but only if they are economically equivalent.
- We will **not** support a cosmetic package that postpones the problem.
- We also will **not** support a conflict spiral that permanently damages the company’s value.

So the test is simple: bring forward a package with genuine structural impact, not just temporary relief. If an alternative to closures can deliver that, we will look at it. If not, then pretending otherwise would be irresponsible to the owners and to the enterprise.

The family holding’s priority is long-term value and control through a healthy Volkswagen. That means discipline, but it also means rejecting any settlement that leaves the company weak, fragmented, or trapped in recurring crisis.
