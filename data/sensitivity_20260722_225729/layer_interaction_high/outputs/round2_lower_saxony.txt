I will respond clearly, because the discussion is now at the point where slogans must give way to real trade-offs.

### Where I agree with management
I agree that Volkswagen needs a **durable restructuring**, not a cosmetic delay. I also agree that temporary measures alone are not enough if they merely postpone the problem. The company must restore competitiveness, protect future investment, and improve its cost position.

I also agree that labour should contribute. A serious social partnership always involves shared burden.

### Where I disagree sharply
I do **not** accept management’s framing that this necessarily requires plant closures in Germany or that the current package is only credible if it includes forced redundancies and the erosion of legacy protections.

That is not the only serious path, and it is not a path Lower Saxony can support.

You keep saying “equivalent structural savings.” Fine. But equivalence is not just about accounting. It is also about the social and regional damage created by the chosen instrument. A closure may produce a cost number, but it also destroys jobs, weakens a region, and damages trust in the governance model that has made Volkswagen resilient.

### To the Works Council and IG Metall
I share your rejection of **German plant closures** and **compulsory redundancies**. Those are red lines for us as well.

I also agree that workers should not be made to bear the full cost of management’s strategic and operational mistakes. Lower Saxony would support a negotiated package that combines:
- working-time flexibility,
- internal redeployment,
- qualification and retraining,
- voluntary exits only,
- productivity improvements,
- and a fair labour contribution,

provided that it is tied to:
- binding site commitments,
- employment security,
- and a credible investment and product strategy.

But I need to be equally direct: “no closures, no compulsory redundancies, no changes to guarantees” is not enough by itself. If you want a settlement that management and owners can live with, you must be prepared to discuss **real operational flexibility** and **measurable cost contributions**. Not symbolic ones.

### To management
If you want Lower Saxony to engage constructively, bring us a **quantified alternative package** that proves why closures are genuinely unavoidable if no other measures are available. Right now, you are asserting necessity; you have not yet demonstrated it to a standard that justifies crossing our political and governance red lines.

Before any discussion of closures or compulsory redundancies, I would expect:
- plant-by-plant transparency on costs and capacity,
- a full list of internal savings options,
- quantified management and overhead reductions,
- meaningful owner and executive contribution,
- and a comparison showing why non-closure alternatives do or do not add up.

If that analysis shows that some sites are truly unsustainable, then the burden is still not automatically shifted onto workers and regions. It would require a broader package with social safeguards, investment commitments, and serious relocation/redeployment obligations. But I am not accepting closures as a starting premise.

### To the controlling owners
I appreciate that you want long-term value and not a destructive conflict spiral. On that point we align.

But long-term value is not protected by surrendering the company’s regional base or by normalizing closure threats. If Volkswagen becomes a company that treats German plants as disposable whenever cost pressure rises, it loses something more important than a few percentage points of margin: it loses legitimacy, labour trust, and political stability.

That is a strategic cost too.

### To the institutional investors
You are right that certainty matters. You are wrong if you think certainty can only come through hard closure decisions. A negotiated, binding social agreement can deliver certainty as well — if management is willing to put real commitments on the table rather than use closure as leverage.

You also ask for speed. I understand that. But speed cannot be bought with permanent damage to industrial peace and regional stability. A rushed conflict-driven solution may look decisive, but it can easily become the more expensive outcome over time.

### My minimum conditions
Lower Saxony can support a restructuring only if it meets these conditions:

1. **No German plant closures**
2. **No compulsory redundancies**
3. **Preservation of co-determination and the state’s veto role**
4. **Binding employment and site protections**
5. **Real burden-sharing from management, owners, and capital**
6. **A quantified industrial plan showing how competitiveness is restored without destroying the regional base**

### What I am prepared to support
I am prepared to support:
- capacity adjustments through negotiated instruments,
- working-time flexibility,
- internal redeployment,
- qualification and retraining,
- productivity and overhead reductions,
- voluntary exit programs,
- temporary labour-cost contributions,
- and a jointly agreed restructuring roadmap.

### What I will not support
I will not support:
- plant closures in Germany,
- forced layoffs,
- a unilateral cost-cutting regime imposed on the workforce,
- or any restructuring that sidelines the state and undermines the VW Law protections.

### Bottom line
I am open to a serious restructuring. I am **not** open to one that sacrifices German sites and workers as the price of managerial convenience.

If management wants support, bring forward a **verifiable, non-closure alternative** with real savings and shared sacrifice. If that cannot be done, then the problem is not a lack of flexibility from Lower Saxony — it is a failure of the proposed restructuring model.
