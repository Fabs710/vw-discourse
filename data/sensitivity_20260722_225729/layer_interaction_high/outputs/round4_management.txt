I’ll be equally direct.

## To the Works Council and IG Metall

I acknowledge one important point: **a cosmetic deal is not acceptable**. On that, we agree.  
I also agree that **temporary measures alone will not fix Volkswagen’s structural problem**.

Where I disagree is your attempt to turn **“no closures” and “no compulsory redundancies” into a conclusion before the economics are proven**. That is not a restructuring plan; it is a restriction on the available tools.

You keep saying the burden of proof is on management. Fair enough. It is — and I accept that. But you are **not** exempt from evidence either. If you insist that a no-closure package can fully close the gap, then you need to show:
- where the permanent savings come from,
- how much capacity is actually removed,
- what the recurring labour-cost reduction is,
- and why that package is durable enough to restore margins to target.

So far, I have heard principles. I have not heard a quantified alternative that matches the scale of the problem.

## To Lower Saxony

I respect your governance role and your concern for industrial stability. But I do **not** accept the argument that your blocking position can be used to rule out hard choices in advance.

You say you support restructuring without closures or forced layoffs. That is acceptable only if it delivers **equivalent structural savings**. If it does not, then you are not solving the crisis — you are postponing it while the cost base remains uncompetitive.

I am willing to work within a non-closure path **if** it is real, measurable, and durable. I am not willing to pretend that a political preference is the same thing as an industrial solution.

## To the controlling owners

I agree with you on the central point: Volkswagen needs a **durable structural reset**, not another temporary patch.  
I also agree that management, owners, and capital must not demand that workers alone carry the burden.

Where I would push you harder is this: if you want to protect value, then support the restructuring instruments that actually create value. That includes footprint rationalization if the alternative package does not close the gap.

I do not want closures for their own sake. I want **permanent fixed-cost reduction**. If a credible alternative can do that, fine. If not, then closing off closures in advance is not value protection — it is value destruction.

## To institutional investors

You are right to demand:
- measurable savings,
- a lower break-even volume,
- implementation certainty,
- and durability.

That is exactly why I cannot accept a settlement that merely preserves the current structure and calls it compromise. But I also reject the idea that the only credible route is to force closures immediately without fully testing the alternatives.

What I need from the discussion is not more general pressure for “credibility,” but a **comparative, site-by-site assessment** of:
- closure,
- capacity reduction,
- line consolidation,
- product reallocation,
- overhead cuts,
- voluntary exits,
- working-time changes,
- and labour-cost contributions.

If non-closure measures can deliver the same recurring savings, I am open to them. If they cannot, then I will not sign up to a cosmetic compromise.

## On the audit issue

I support an independent, plant-by-plant verification. But let me be clear about the purpose.

This is **not** to prove that closures are desirable.  
It is to prove whether **equivalent structural savings** can be achieved without them.

[SUMMON: Independent plant-by-plant industrial and financial auditor with access to site-level cost, utilization, capex, product allocation, and recurring savings data, mandated to compare closure, consolidation, and non-closure restructuring options]

That review must answer:
- which sites are structurally viable,
- which sites need material capacity reduction,
- what permanent savings each alternative delivers,
- and what the investment implications are through 2030.

Without that, everyone is just repeating positions.

## My position, plainly

I am **not** retreating from the need for deep restructuring.  
Volkswagen needs:
- lower fixed costs,
- less overcapacity,
- a lower labour-cost base,
- and enough freed-up capital to invest in future products and technology.

I am open to achieving that **without closures**, but only if the alternative is genuinely equivalent and enforceable.

I am **not** open to:
- a settlement that preserves an uncompetitive cost base,
- temporary concessions dressed up as structural reform,
- or a plan that blocks the company from restoring margins.

## What would move me

Three things, and only three:

1. **Plant-by-plant hard numbers**
   - current cost, volume, capex, and utilization
   - quantified savings from each option

2. **A durable labour-cost contribution**
   - not symbolic, not temporary
   - real recurring savings

3. **A package that restores competitiveness by 2030**
   - meaning lower break-even volume and structural cost reduction

If the employee side can bring me a package that meets those conditions, I will back it.  
If not, then I will continue to insist that closures remain available as a necessary instrument.

## Bottom line

We all agree Volkswagen needs a durable restructuring.  
We do **not** agree that a no-closure settlement is automatically sufficient.

So let’s stop debating slogans and put the numbers on the table. If a non-closure solution is truly equivalent, I will support it. If it isn’t, then pretending otherwise would be irresponsible to the company, the brand, and the future investment base.
