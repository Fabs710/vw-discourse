I will be direct.

## To management
I agree with your core diagnosis: Volkswagen needs a **durable structural reset**, not another temporary patch. The German cost base must come down, and the current operating model cannot remain competitive if it simply preserves the status quo.

Where I disagree is with any suggestion that **closures and compulsory redundancies should be treated as the default opening move** before the economics are proven. That is too blunt, and it creates avoidable instability.

You have made a serious case for urgency. But you have **not yet made a site-specific case** showing that every credible non-closure path fails. If you want governance backing for harsher instruments, then bring the numbers:
- plant-by-plant utilization,
- product allocation and volume outlook,
- fixed-cost position,
- capex required to stay competitive,
- and the recurring savings from each alternative measure.

Until then, closure is an instrument, not a conclusion.

## To the Works Council
I agree with you on one important point: a cosmetic deal would be worthless. I also agree that Volkswagen needs a real restructuring, not a postponement.

Where I disagree is your attempt to turn **“no closures” and “no compulsory redundancies” into the full answer before the economic evidence is in**. That is not enough for the owners, the company, or the long-term asset value we are charged to protect.

If you want the no-closure route to be taken seriously, then it must be **quantified and durable**:
- what permanent savings are delivered,
- how much capacity is actually removed,
- what recurring labour-cost reduction is achieved,
- and how the package restores competitiveness by 2030.

Saying “we refuse closures” is a position. It is not yet a restructuring model.

## To IG Metall
I agree that the workforce should not be the only adjustment variable. I also agree that legitimacy and industrial peace matter.

But I do **not** accept the idea that burden-sharing can mean only temporary flexibility or voluntary exits while the structural gap remains open. If the union wants a no-closure settlement, it has to show a package that delivers **real, recurring savings**, not just delay.

That means:
- capacity reduction,
- shift/line consolidation,
- productivity improvements,
- overhead restraint,
- voluntary workforce reduction,
- and a durable labour-cost contribution.

If that is on the table, then we can judge it against the closure option. If it is not, then “no closures” is simply resistance to change.

## To Lower Saxony
I respect the regional responsibility and the VW Law reality. I also agree that a destructive conflict would damage the company and the region.

But I do **not** accept the use of blocking power to freeze every German site in place regardless of economics. That would protect the appearance of stability while the underlying competitiveness problem worsens.

Your useful role is not just saying “no closures.” It is helping create a **credible non-closure restructuring** that is actually financeable and operationally sound. If you can do that, good. If not, then you are postponing the problem rather than solving it.

## To institutional investors
You are right to insist on:
- permanent savings,
- lower break-even volumes,
- and implementation certainty.

I support that. But I also reject any shortcut that assumes closures are automatically the best answer simply because they look clean on paper.

If a non-closure package can deliver equivalent structural savings, it should be preferred. If it cannot, then we should not pretend otherwise. What I will not support is a compromise that preserves an uncompetitive structure and calls that prudence.

## On the audit / verification issue
I support the call for neutral verification, but it must be truly useful, not a procedural delay.

[SUMMON: independent plant-by-plant industrial and financial auditor with access to site-level cost, utilization, capex, product allocation, and recurring savings data, mandated to compare closure, consolidation, and non-closure restructuring options]

That review must answer:
- which sites are structurally viable,
- which require major capacity reduction,
- what permanent savings each option delivers,
- and what the investment implications are through 2030.

That is the only basis on which we can decide whether a no-closure package is truly equivalent or merely politically convenient.

## My position, plainly
The controlling owners will support a restructuring that:
- restores competitiveness,
- reduces fixed cost,
- lowers break-even volume,
- and protects long-term value.

We are open to achieving that **without closures** if, and only if, the alternative is genuinely equivalent in permanent savings and capacity reduction.

We will **not** support:
- a cosmetic compromise,
- a plan that freezes the footprint while preserving the full cost base,
- or a settlement that merely delays the crisis.

At the same time, we will also not support value-destructive instability. That means the employee side and the state should understand this clearly: if you want closures off the table, then bring an alternative that actually works.

## Bottom line
I am not asking for consensus for its own sake. I am asking for a **credible industrial and financial answer**.

If the plant-by-plant numbers show that a negotiated non-closure package can restore competitiveness, I will back it. If they do not, then closures cannot be ruled out simply because they are unpopular.

That is the standard I will use.
