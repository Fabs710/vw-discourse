I agree with the Works Council on the fundamentals: **no plant closures in Germany, no compulsory redundancies, and no dismantling of core job-security protections**.

Let me be equally clear from the union side: workers are not prepared to absorb the full cost of management’s strategic and operational failures. If Volkswagen wants a durable restructuring, then **owners, management, and capital must also contribute**. That means shared sacrifice, not one-sided austerity.

Where I am open:
- **temporary working-time reductions**
- **capacity adjustments through natural attrition**
- **internal redeployment and qualification**
- **a negotiated employee contribution**
- **possibly foregone bonuses or other labour-cost measures**

But this is only acceptable if it is tied to:
- **binding site guarantees**
- **guaranteed employment security**
- **a credible investment and product commitment**
- **no forced layoffs**
- **no plant closures**
- **protection of the collective wage framework**

What I reject:
- using the crisis as a pretext for open-ended job insecurity;
- forcing workers to pay while shareholders and top management are shielded;
- presenting closure threats as the starting point of talks.

The company has a competitiveness problem, yes. But that does **not** justify making the workforce carry the burden alone. If management wants labour peace, it needs to stop talking about unilateral cuts and start negotiating a fair package that protects jobs, sites, and the long-term industrial base.

So our message is the same: **we are ready to negotiate, but only on the basis of binding guarantees and fair burden-sharing.** If that is not on the table, then industrial action remains a realistic option.
