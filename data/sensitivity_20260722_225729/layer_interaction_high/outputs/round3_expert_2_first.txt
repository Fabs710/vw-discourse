From an industrial-audit perspective, the central question is not whether Volkswagen has a cost problem — that appears well supported — but whether the company can close the savings gap **without** German plant closures or compulsory redundancies, using measures that are **permanent, measurable, and site-specific**.

## Bottom line
Based on the information provided, **there is not yet enough evidence to conclude that closures are strictly required**. But there is also **not enough evidence to conclude the opposite**.

What is missing is a **plant-by-plant bridge from current economics to target economics**:
- current fixed cost by site,
- utilization by line,
- product allocation and volume outlook,
- headcount by function,
- wage and overhead burden,
- required capex to keep each site competitive,
- and the savings from each alternative measure.

Without that, “no closures” remains a **policy position**, not a verified industrial conclusion.

## What can be assessed now

### 1) A structurally high-cost base is plausible
Given:
- a weak European auto market,
- EV transition costs,
- pressure from lower-cost competitors,
- and management’s stated margin target miss,

it is credible that Volkswagen’s German passenger-car operations have **excess fixed cost relative to expected volume**. In plant economics, when volume falls below a site’s efficient scale, the cost per unit rises sharply.

### 2) Closure is not automatically the only way to remove fixed cost
In manufacturing audits, equivalent structural savings can sometimes be achieved through:
- line consolidation,
- shift elimination,
- product transfer to fewer sites,
- insourcing/outsourcing changes,
- permanent headcount reduction via voluntary exits and attrition,
- working-time redesign,
- simplification of logistics and indirect functions,
- and overhead cuts.

These can be structurally durable **if** they permanently reduce capacity or recurring labor/overhead spend.

### 3) But “non-closure” savings often have limits
A no-closure package may fail if:
- a site is materially underutilized,
- the product assigned to it has poor future volume,
- the required capex to keep it viable is uneconomic,
- or the savings from internal measures are mostly one-off or temporary.

In that case, management may still have a valid industrial case for footprint reduction, even if not necessarily for immediate full closure.

## What would need to be verified site by site
To answer the question rigorously, each German site should be tested on these items:

1. **Current capacity utilization**
   - installed capacity vs. current and forecast volume
   - shift pattern
   - bottlenecks and loss hours

2. **Unit cost position**
   - direct labor cost per vehicle
   - energy, logistics, maintenance, and depreciation burden
   - indirect labor allocation
   - scrap/rework and quality losses

3. **Product and volume outlook**
   - assigned models and platform life cycle
   - forecast volume by year
   - probability of successor allocation

4. **Capex requirement**
   - maintenance capex to keep running
   - transition capex for EV/platform changes
   - tooling and automation needs
   - payback period

5. **Restructuring options and savings**
   - headcount reduction potential
   - shift reduction impact
   - consolidation savings
   - procurement/overhead savings
   - one-time restructuring charges versus recurring savings

6. **Implementation risk**
   - industrial relations stability
   - supplier disruption
   - ramp-up risk if volumes are moved
   - cost of transition downtime

## Preliminary audit conclusion
### Verified
- A durable restructuring is necessary.
- The German cost base is likely above target.
- Temporary measures alone are unlikely to solve the issue.

### Reasonable estimate
- Some mix of capacity reduction, headcount reduction, and overhead simplification is likely required.
- Non-closure measures may deliver meaningful savings, but probably not equally at every site.

### Not yet verifiable from the record
- That plant closures are economically unavoidable.
- That every alternative measure has been quantified and exhausted.
- That non-closure measures cannot bridge the gap.

## Financial consequence logic
If a site is closed, the company usually gets:
- recurring fixed-cost removal,
- reduced depreciation/maintenance burden over time,
- lower working capital tied to that site,
- but also one-time closure costs:
  - severance,
  - decommissioning,
  - contract termination,
  - asset write-downs,
  - logistics reconfiguration,
  - and potential supply disruption.

If a site is preserved through internal restructuring, the company may avoid large one-time closure costs, but it must still prove:
- sufficient recurring savings,
- acceptable future capex burden,
- and sustainable utilization.

So the real audit issue is not “closure or no closure” in the abstract. It is:
**Which site, at what utilization, with what future product, can generate the required return after restructuring?**

## My neutral assessment
At this point, the strongest evidence-based statement is:

> Volkswagen likely needs permanent structural savings, but the record provided does not yet prove that German plant closures are the only way to achieve them.

To make that determination, I would require:
- site-level P&Ls,
- capacity and volume forecasts,
- capex plans,
- and quantified savings from each alternative measure.

If those data are provided, I can assess each plant individually and state where a no-closure solution is financially credible, where it is borderline, and where closure or major footprint reduction becomes the economically stronger case.
