I will be equally direct.

### To the Works Council
I agree with your core red lines:
- **no German plant closures**
- **no compulsory redundancies**
- **no dismantling of the collective wage framework**
- **no forcing the workforce to carry the entire burden**

That remains the starting point from IG Metall’s side as well.

Where I want to sharpen the position is this: if management wants a durable deal, then it must stop using “equivalent structural savings” as a code word for pushing through closures by another name. We are prepared to discuss real restructuring, but it has to be **socially balanced and contractually binding**.

That means I support your call for:
- working-time adjustments,
- qualification and retraining,
- internal redeployment,
- voluntary pathways only,
- and a fair labour contribution,

but only if they come with:
- **site guarantees**
- **employment security**
- **investment commitments**
- **no forced layoffs**
- **preservation of the collective bargaining framework**

### To management
Your argument is that natural attrition and temporary working-time cuts are not enough. Fine — but that still does not justify forcing the workforce to absorb the adjustment through closures or layoffs.

If you want a real solution, then show us a package that includes **management and owner contributions**, not just worker concessions. That means:
- executive restraint,
- overhead reduction at the top,
- dividend discipline,
- and a credible industrial plan that protects the future of the sites.

What I do **not** accept is this logic:
1. declare overcapacity,
2. threaten plant closures,
3. demand wage concessions,
4. and call that the only realistic path.

That is not a negotiation. That is pressure.

### To Lower Saxony
I appreciate your clear line against closures and forced redundancies. But let me be clear from the union side: political support alone is not enough if it remains abstract. If the company is serious, then the governance side must help secure a binding framework that prevents unilateral escalation.

So yes, we need:
- no closures,
- no compulsory layoffs,
- no erosion of job security.

But we also need a **practical industrial compromise** that gives the company room to adjust without destroying trust. That means negotiated capacity reduction, not destruction; fair labour contribution, not one-sided sacrifice.

### To the controlling owners
You say a blanket refusal of all structural change may be economically self-defeating. I agree with the second half: we do need structural change. But your conclusion is too broad.

Structural change does **not** automatically mean closures or dismissals. It can mean:
- capacity reduction through natural fluctuation,
- shifts in working time,
- product and investment reallocation,
- productivity improvements,
- internal transfers,
- and a negotiated contribution from labour.

If the owners want long-term value, then they should support a settlement that protects the workforce and keeps the company socially stable. A conflict spiral, plant closure debate, and attack on labour standards will not strengthen Volkswagen in the medium term.

### To the institutional investors
You keep asking for speed and certainty. I understand that. But certainty achieved through social rupture is a false economy. Long strikes, damaged trust, and a broken collective framework create their own risk premium.

If you want durable margin improvement, then the right path is not to force the workforce into defensive action. The right path is a negotiated package that:
- protects jobs,
- avoids closures,
- reduces costs structurally,
- and creates industrial peace.

You also say workers should contribute. I agree. But contribution must be **fair and shared**. That means:
- no full wage freeze imposed unilaterally,
- no hollowing out of guarantees,
- and no “workers pay, everyone else waits” model.

### Our position — more concrete
IG Metall is prepared to discuss a package with:
- temporary working-time reductions,
- a negotiated employee contribution,
- bonus restraint or similar labour-cost measures,
- voluntary exit instruments only,
- redeployment and qualification,
- productivity agreements,
- and a jointly monitored capacity adjustment.

But only if the following are binding:
1. **No plant closures in Germany**
2. **No compulsory redundancies**
3. **Employment and site security**
4. **Protection of the collective wage framework**
5. **Real contributions from management, owners, and capital**
6. **A long-term investment and product commitment**

### What would move us
Not rhetoric about competitiveness, but:
- a quantified savings package that does not rely on closures,
- binding guarantees,
- and visible contributions from the top.

### What will not move us
- “closures remain on the table” as leverage,
- shifting all pain onto employees,
- and treating co-determination as a nuisance rather than a governing reality.

So my message is simple: **we are ready to negotiate a serious restructuring, but not a social demolition plan.** If management insists on plant closures or forced layoffs, it will face resistance — not consent.
