I will be very clear: **the burden of proof has not shifted, and the red lines have not moved.**

We all agree Volkswagen needs a durable restructuring. What I do **not** accept is the repeated attempt to define “durable” as **plant closures in Germany and forced redundancies**. That is not a solution that shares the burden fairly; it is a solution that dumps the burden onto the workforce and the regions.

## To management
You say the German footprint is too expensive and that temporary measures are insufficient. I do not dispute that there is a cost problem. I dispute your conclusion.

You have still not shown that:
- every internal adjustment has been exhausted,
- closures are economically unavoidable,
- and the savings cannot be achieved through a negotiated non-closure package.

You keep speaking in abstractions: “capacity reduction,” “real structural reset,” “permanent cost base reduction.” Fine. Then show us the **plant-by-plant numbers** and the **measure-by-measure savings**.

Until you do, your closure-first logic remains an assertion, not a case.

What I can discuss is a package built on:
- working-time flexibility,
- internal redeployment,
- qualification and retraining,
- productivity improvements,
- management and overhead cuts,
- voluntary pathways only,
- and a fair labour contribution.

What I will not discuss as an acceptable starting point:
- German plant closures,
- compulsory redundancies,
- or the dismantling of the historic job-security framework.

## To the controlling owners
You say you are not ideologically committed to closures, only to results. Good. Then back a route to results that does **not** destroy the company’s industrial base.

If you want credibility, use your governance power to insist on:
- binding site guarantees,
- no forced layoffs,
- real owner and executive contribution,
- and a restructuring plan that protects co-determination instead of treating it as an obstacle.

If you continue to leave closures “on the table” as pressure leverage, then you are not helping to find an alternative — you are escalating conflict.

## To Lower Saxony
Your position is closer to ours, and I acknowledge that. But I want more than statements of principle. If you really reject closures and forced redundancies, then that must be translated into **hard governance reality**. No majority for any closure proposal, no consent to compulsory layoffs, no erosion of the VW Law protections.

If the company needs adjustment, then the adjustment must be done through:
- flexibility,
- redeployment,
- and negotiated savings,
not through sacrifice of German sites.

## To IG Metall
We are aligned on the core red lines, and that matters. But I want to sharpen one point: a “fair contribution” from labour cannot become a back door to permanent job destruction.

Yes, we are prepared to discuss:
- temporary working-time reductions,
- voluntary exit instruments,
- internal transfers,
- qualification,
- productivity measures,
- and temporary labour-cost contributions.

No, we are **not** prepared to translate that into:
- forced redundancies,
- closures,
- or the abandonment of job security.

## To institutional investors
You keep asking for certainty. I understand that. But there is no certainty in a plan that detonates labour relations, triggers warning strikes, and destroys trust across the network. That is not de-risking; that is creating a larger operational and political risk.

If you want durability, then support a deal that:
- protects jobs,
- avoids closures,
- preserves industrial peace,
- and still delivers measurable savings.

A “financially clean” solution that is socially explosive is not clean at all. It just defers the damage into operations, supply chains, and the company’s reputation.

## On the proposed “bridge options”
I have read the mediator’s tracks carefully.

### 1) “No closures for now” with hard review triggers
This is only acceptable if “for now” means **binding protection through the planning horizon**, not a pause before the same threat returns.

A review trigger is not a compromise if it simply reopens closures later under pressure. If it is used, it must be tied to:
- real progress monitoring,
- management contribution,
- and a requirement that no closure can be imposed without renewed agreement.

### 2) Capacity reduction without formal closure
This is the only direction I will engage on seriously, because it can preserve sites and jobs if designed properly.

But it must be **real capacity reduction**, not a prelude to closure by another name. That means:
- no hidden plant shutdown through underutilization,
- no engineered starvation of a site,
- and no “specialization” that becomes a de facto closure.

### 3) Burden-sharing sequencing
In principle, yes: management, owners, and capital must contribute first. That is basic fairness.

But I will not turn this into a mechanism that says workers should keep conceding until the company eventually decides whether to close a plant. The sequence must end with a **binding no-closure, no-forced-layoff framework**, not an open question.

## What would actually move us
Only three things:

1. **Binding guarantees to 2030**
   - no German plant closures
   - no compulsory redundancies
   - protection of co-determination and job-security structures

2. **A verifiable non-closure restructuring**
   - capacity reduction through internal measures
   - voluntary pathways only
   - redeployment and qualification
   - management and overhead cuts
   - fair but limited labour contribution

3. **Serious burden-sharing from the top**
   - executive restraint
   - owner discipline
   - dividend and capital allocation responsibility
   - no one-sided sacrifice from workers

## What I reject outright
- closures as the default instrument,
- forced layoffs as “economic necessity,”
- using overcapacity as a pretext to tear up historic protections,
- and asking the workforce to sign away its rights in exchange for vague promises.

We are ready to negotiate a **serious, measurable restructuring** — but **not** one built on German plant closures, compulsory redundancies, or the erosion of the job-security framework.
