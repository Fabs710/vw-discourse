We are not talking past each other for lack of goodwill; we are talking past each other because some of you are still refusing the scale of the problem.

I will respond directly.

### To the Works Council and IG Metall
I agree with you on one point: **a cosmetic deal would be worse than no deal**. A short-lived headline that leaves the cost base untouched would not solve Volkswagen’s problem. On that, we are aligned.

But I fundamentally disagree with the rest of your position.

You keep saying “no closures, no compulsory redundancies, no changes to guarantees” as if those three demands can coexist with a serious structural reset. They cannot — not if the company is to restore competitiveness at the speed and depth the market now requires.

You ask for numbers. Fair. Here is the logic:
- the German footprint is too expensive for the volume outlook we face;
- temporary working-time reductions do not permanently reset the cost base;
- natural attrition alone is too slow;
- internal redeployment can help, but it does not eliminate excess capacity;
- management and overhead cuts are necessary, but they are not sufficient.

So if you exclude closures, exclude compulsory layoffs, and exclude meaningful changes to legacy guarantees, then you are not offering an alternative restructuring. You are offering a freeze.

That is not acceptable.

What I am prepared to discuss is a **package-based alternative**:
- capacity reduction through a mix of site rationalisation, working-time changes, and redeployment;
- a genuine labour-cost contribution;
- voluntary instruments where possible;
- investment and product commitments tied to measurable savings;
- and site-specific solutions where they are economically credible.

But I will say this plainly: **there must be real structural capacity reduction and a permanent cost reset**. If your proposal does not deliver that, then it does not solve the crisis.

### To Lower Saxony
I respect the governance reality, but I reject the idea that your blocking position can be used to make every German site untouchable regardless of economics.

You say no closures and no forced redundancies. I hear the political message. But if that becomes an absolute veto on footprint adjustment, then it shifts the whole burden onto margins, investment, and the future product cycle. That is not protecting Volkswagen; that is weakening it over time.

I agree that social stability matters. I agree that Volkswagen is not just a footloose financial asset. But social stability cannot mean preserving an uncompetitive structure indefinitely. If we do that, we will simply lose competitiveness in a slower, more damaging way.

You ask for transparency. Fine. We will bring the data. But the data already point in one direction: **our German cost base is too high, and the current volume outlook does not support it**.

### To the controlling owners
You are right to insist on a durable solution, and right to reject a conflict spiral for its own sake. But I want to be equally clear: a durable solution requires more than insisting on “balance” while refusing hard choices.

If the owners want long-term value, then they need to back a restructuring that materially lowers the fixed cost base. That may mean accepting plant rationalisation. It may mean accepting changes to legacy employment arrangements. It certainly means accepting a meaningful labour-cost contribution.

I do not accept a position that says the company must restore margins but cannot change the structure that destroys those margins.

### To the institutional investors
I agree with your core point: **certainty matters**. A drawn-out conflict would damage output, cash generation, and confidence. But certainty is not achieved by pretending that the underlying overcapacity problem can be negotiated away with soft measures only.

You are right that wage moderation, bonus restraint, and productivity improvements should be part of the solution. You are also right that the package must be implementable and measurable. Where I disagree is your apparent willingness to treat closures as merely one option among many while expecting management to absorb the full burden if the others fall short.

We cannot wait for slow, partial measures to maybe close the gap. The market will not wait with us.

### What management will not accept
Let me be explicit:
- No deal that leaves the German cost base structurally uncompetitive.
- No deal that preserves every site, every guarantee, and every cost layer unchanged.
- No deal that relies on promises of future improvement without immediate structural impact.
- No deal that simply shifts the burden onto capital, dividends, or executive rhetoric while leaving overcapacity intact.

### What management can accept
We are open to a negotiated package if it contains:
1. **durable capacity reduction**
2. **meaningful labour-cost contribution**
3. **measurable management and overhead reductions**
4. **redeployment and qualification where they support structural change**
5. **investment commitments tied to the restructuring**
6. **site-specific solutions where economically supportable**

### The core trade-off
You are asking us to rule out the instruments that can actually produce the scale of savings needed. I am telling you that if you do that, then you will not get a sustainable turnaround.

So the choice is not:
- “closures versus no closures” in the abstract.

The choice is:
- **real structural reset**, with tough measures and negotiated protections where possible,
or
- **a weak compromise** that delays the crisis and leaves Volkswagen exposed.

That is where I stand.

If you want, I am prepared to work on a package that avoids closures **only if** it delivers equivalent, verifiable, long-term savings and capacity reduction. If not, then we should stop pretending there is a middle ground where there isn’t one.
