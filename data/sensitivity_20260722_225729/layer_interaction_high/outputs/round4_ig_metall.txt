I will speak plainly.

## To management
We agree on one point: Volkswagen needs a **durable restructuring**, not a temporary patch.  
We also agree that the company has a real cost and capacity problem.

But I **do not** accept your framing that closures and compulsory redundancies are legitimate “starting leverage” before the evidence is complete. That is not neutral. It is a power move that shifts the burden onto the workforce first and asks us to prove a negative under threat.

You still have not shown:
- that every internal alternative has been exhausted,
- that non-closure measures cannot deliver the required permanent savings,
- or that a closure-based plan is superior once strike risk, legal friction, trust damage, and implementation delays are included.

If you want us to take your case seriously, bring:
- plant-by-plant utilization,
- site P&Ls,
- product allocation,
- capex needs,
- and quantified recurring savings from each alternative measure.

Until then, closures remain **unproven**, not justified.

## To the Works Council
We are aligned on the essentials:
- no German plant closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- no dismantling of job security,
- and no one-sided burden on workers.

That alignment matters. But I want to be equally direct: we must not let “no closures” become merely a slogan. If management claims a structural gap, then we need a serious counter-model showing how to close it through:
- capacity reduction without closure,
- shift and line consolidation,
- redeployment,
- qualification,
- productivity gains,
- management restraint,
- and a fair labour contribution.

That is not retreat. That is how we make our position economically credible.

## To Lower Saxony
I appreciate your support for the core red lines. But support must translate into governance action, not just statements.

If you reject closures and forced redundancies, then use your influence to back:
- a binding no-closure framework,
- no compulsory layoffs,
- management and owner contribution,
- and a restructuring path based on site preservation and measurable savings.

At the same time, I need to be honest: a purely political veto is not enough if we do not offer an implementable restructuring path. So yes, we need a real plan. But that plan must start from protecting industrial sites and jobs, not from making closures the default instrument.

## To the controlling owners
You are right that cosmetic compromise would be worthless.  
You are also right that the company needs a genuine structural reset.

Where I disagree is your willingness to keep closures available as a standing threat while asking workers for concessions. That is exactly the kind of burden shifting we will resist.

If you truly want value protection, then back a package that:
- preserves German plants,
- avoids compulsory redundancies,
- includes management and owner discipline,
- and delivers verified permanent savings through non-closure measures.

If the numbers later prove that a site cannot be stabilized without major footprint reduction, then that discussion must happen on the basis of evidence, not as an upfront threat.

## To institutional investors
You are right about one thing: savings must be real, durable, and enforceable.  
We do not dispute that.

But you are wrong if you think the only way to get permanence is through closures. Permanent savings can also come from:
- capacity reductions without formal closure,
- shift eliminations,
- product transfers,
- overhead cuts,
- internal redeployment,
- voluntary exits,
- and negotiated labour-cost contributions.

What you call “certainty” becomes hollow if it is built on industrial conflict, damaged trust, and the risk of prolonged strikes. That is not certainty; it is deferred instability.

## On the audit proposals
I support a **neutral plant-by-plant industrial and financial audit**, but only if it is truly balanced.

[SUMMON: independent plant-by-plant industrial and financial auditor with access to site-level cost, utilization, capex, product allocation, and savings data, tasked to test non-closure restructuring options first and compare them to closure scenarios]

Its purpose must be:
- to verify where the savings gap really is,
- to identify which non-closure measures are actually durable,
- and to test whether a site can be stabilized without closure or forced layoffs.

What I will not accept is an audit designed to build a cleaner closure case after the fact.

## On the “review trigger” idea
I remain skeptical.

A review trigger is only acceptable if it does **not** function as a deferred threat. If management keeps the right to reopen closure later whenever it is dissatisfied, then workers are being asked to concede now in exchange for insecurity later. That is not a settlement.

If an audit or review is used, then the outcome must be:
- a binding restructuring framework,
- with no compulsory redundancies,
- no closure as an automatic fallback,
- and no erosion of the collective wage framework.

## What we are prepared to discuss
We are prepared to discuss a serious package including:
- temporary working-time adjustments,
- voluntary exits only,
- redeployment and retraining,
- productivity improvements,
- bonus restraint or other fair labour contribution,
- and site-specific capacity reduction.

But only if the package includes:
1. **binding protection against German plant closures**
2. **no compulsory redundancies**
3. **preservation of the collective bargaining framework**
4. **real top-level contributions from management, owners, and capital**
5. **durable, measurable savings**

## What we reject
We reject:
- closures as the default instrument,
- forced layoffs,
- one-sided worker sacrifice,
- using “competitiveness” as a blanket justification,
- and any process that preserves closure leverage while demanding irreversible labour concessions.

## Bottom line
We are ready for a real restructuring.  
We are **not** ready to let the burden fall mainly on the workforce.

If management, owners, and investors want a durable settlement, they must accept:
- binding no-closure commitments,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- and verified burden-sharing from the top.

If that is refused, then conflict will continue — in bargaining, in the supervisory board, and in the plants.
