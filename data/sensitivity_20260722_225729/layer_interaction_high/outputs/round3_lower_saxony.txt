I will be direct.

## To management
I agree that Volkswagen needs a **durable restructuring** and that temporary fixes alone will not do. I also agree that the company must restore competitiveness and lower its cost base.

But I **do not** accept your repeated implication that this necessarily requires **German plant closures** or **compulsory redundancies**. You still have not demonstrated that all internal alternatives have been exhausted or that a non-closure package cannot close the gap. Until you do, closures are a **threat**, not a proven necessity.

What I can support is a restructuring that:
- reduces capacity through negotiated, site-preserving measures,
- includes working-time flexibility,
- redeployment and qualification,
- voluntary exit pathways only,
- management and overhead restraint,
- and a real labour contribution,

**provided** it is backed by binding employment and site commitments.

What I will not support:
- a closure-first strategy,
- forced layoffs,
- or using co-determination and the VW Law as something to be bypassed once pressure rises.

If you want my support, bring **plant-by-plant numbers** and show why a negotiated non-closure package fails. Otherwise, your case remains incomplete.

## To the Works Council
I agree with your core red lines:
- no German plant closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- no hollowing out of job security,
- no one-sided burden on the workforce.

That is also my line.

Where I would press you is this: a refusal of closures alone is not enough if it is not paired with a credible restructuring path. We need a package that is more than a political veto. We need a plan that preserves sites and jobs **and** restores competitiveness.

That means I am prepared to back:
- capacity reduction through internal reallocation,
- shift and working-time adjustments,
- qualification,
- productivity measures,
- and voluntary instruments,

but only in a **binding social partnership framework** with no closure and no forced layoffs.

## To IG Metall
I agree that certainty achieved through social rupture is a false economy. A strike spiral, a broken collective framework, and damaged trust would harm Volkswagen’s long-term health. That is exactly why I support a negotiated solution rather than a coercive one.

But I am not prepared to endorse a settlement where labour says “no closures, no layoffs” and management says “therefore closures are inevitable” before the evidence is tested. That is not a negotiation; it is a power contest.

I would support a deal if labour contributes through:
- temporary working-time measures,
- moderated wage growth or bonuses,
- voluntary exit options,
- productivity agreements,

while receiving in return:
- site guarantees,
- employment security,
- and preservation of co-determination rights.

## To the controlling owners
I agree with you that Volkswagen needs a real structural reset and not a cosmetic compromise. I also agree that a purely one-sided adjustment would be bad governance.

But I do **not** accept the idea that “real structural reset” is a synonym for keeping closures permanently on the table as leverage. That is not neutral stewardship; it is pressure politics. It also creates exactly the conflict risk that could damage Volkswagen’s role as a regional anchor.

If you want long-term value, you should support a package that proves restructuring can happen **without sacrificing German sites** unless and until that is proven impossible. That means backing negotiations for:
- binding site commitments,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- management restraint,
- and measurable contribution from owners and capital as well.

## To the institutional investors
I understand your focus on credibility, margins, and implementation certainty. I do not dismiss that.

But from a governance and political perspective, a solution that is “financially clean” while socially explosive is not a good solution. It may create a temporary accounting improvement and a long-term industrial problem.

If you want durable value, support a package that:
- protects employment and sites,
- avoids closures where possible,
- delivers verifiable savings,
- and preserves industrial peace.

You are right that savings must be real. I agree. But “real” does not automatically mean “closure.” That is precisely the question that must be tested, not assumed.

## On the auditor / expert
I support the call for a neutral, plant-by-plant assessment.

[SUMMON: independent plant-by-plant industrial and financial auditor to verify site-level utilization, fixed costs, capex needs, product outlook, and the savings from closure versus non-closure measures]

That review must answer:
- which sites are actually underutilized,
- what permanent savings each measure delivers,
- what capex is required to keep each site viable,
- and whether the non-closure package truly matches the economics.

Until that work is done, no one should pretend the closure case is settled.

## My position, plainly
Lower Saxony will support a restructuring that:
- protects jobs and plants in the state,
- preserves Volkswagen as a regional economic anchor,
- maintains co-determination and our veto role,
- and restores competitiveness through a negotiated, durable solution.

We will **not** support:
- plant closures in Germany as the starting point,
- compulsory redundancies,
- or any restructuring that sidelines the state and weakens the VW Law framework.

If management can demonstrate, with hard evidence, that a non-closure package cannot deliver the needed structural savings, then the discussion becomes more difficult. But that proof has not been delivered yet. Until it is, the burden is on those proposing closures, not on those defending the company’s regional base.

So my message is simple:
**Bring a verified non-closure restructuring with real savings and binding commitments, or accept that your case for closures remains unproven and will face resistance.**
