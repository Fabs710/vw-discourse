I will respond plainly.

## Where I agree

### With the auditor / expert
I agree with the central methodological point: **we need plant-by-plant evidence, not slogans**.  
If management claims closures are unavoidable, then it must show:
- site-level utilization,
- fixed-cost position,
- future product allocation,
- required capex,
- and the savings from each alternative.

That is exactly the kind of evidence that matters in a serious restructuring.

### With Lower Saxony and the Works Council
I agree that **workers must not carry the entire burden**, and that a one-sided adjustment would be socially and industrially destructive.  
I also agree that a conflict spiral with strikes and shattered trust is a real risk.

### With the point that a durable restructuring is necessary
Yes: temporary measures alone are not enough.  
We are not defending the status quo. We are prepared to negotiate a real restructuring.

## Where I disagree

### With management
Your position still reads like this:
1. declare overcapacity,
2. keep closures on the table,
3. demand that labour accept the pain,
4. and call that “equivalent structural savings.”

I reject that logic.

You have **not** shown that closures are the only credible route.  
And even if some sites are under pressure, that does **not** justify compulsory redundancies or the erosion of the collective wage framework as a first move.

You say the German cost base is too high. That may be true.  
But the burden of proving that no non-closure package can close the gap is still on you. Until then, “closure” is not a conclusion; it is a threat.

### With institutional investors
You are right that savings must be durable and measurable.  
You are wrong if you think that automatically means plant closures or open-ended concessions from labour.

A package can be durable only if it is also **legitimate and implementable**.  
A settlement that provokes warning strikes, destroys trust, and makes the workforce feel sacrificed is not durable; it is unstable.

### With the controlling owners
I appreciate the statement that you are not ideologically committed to closures. But leaving closures “available” as pressure while demanding wage restraint from workers is not neutral. It shifts bargaining power against the workforce while pretending to be pragmatic.

If you want a real alternative, you must use your governance weight to back:
- no German plant closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- and a fair burden-sharing package.

### With management’s “real structural reset” language
We are not opposed to structural change. We are opposed to **social demolition disguised as restructuring**.

Structural change can mean:
- capacity reduction through internal reallocation,
- shift and line changes,
- product consolidation,
- qualification,
- redeployment,
- voluntary exits,
- management and overhead cuts,
- and negotiated labour contributions.

It does **not** automatically mean closures and forced layoffs.

## What we are prepared to accept

IG Metall remains prepared to discuss a package that includes:

- temporary working-time reductions,
- internal transfers and redeployment,
- qualification and retraining,
- productivity improvements,
- voluntary exit instruments only,
- bonus restraint or similar labour-cost measures,
- and a negotiated employee contribution.

But only if the following are binding:

1. **No German plant closures**
2. **No compulsory redundancies**
3. **Protection of the collective bargaining framework**
4. **Binding employment and site security**
5. **Real contributions from management, owners, and capital**
6. **A durable industrial and investment commitment**

## What would move us
Not rhetoric. Not pressure. Not “competitiveness” as a magic word.

What would move us is:
- a quantified savings package,
- plant-by-plant transparency,
- verifiable permanent savings outside closures,
- and visible burden-sharing from the top.

If management wants labour flexibility, then management must first show what it contributes itself:
- executive restraint,
- overhead reduction,
- dividend discipline,
- and a serious industrial plan.

## What will not move us
These will not move us:
- threats that closures remain on the table,
- trying to force acceptance by time pressure,
- turning legacy guarantees into a unilateral bargaining chip,
- or asking the workforce to pay for management’s failure to plan correctly.

## On the mediator’s bridge options

### 1) “No closures for now” with hard review triggers
This is only acceptable if “for now” means a **binding protection period**, not a pause before the same threat returns.

A review trigger cannot be a disguised backdoor to closure.  
If it is used, then:
- management must first implement its own contributions,
- the independent monitor must verify savings,
- and no closure can be imposed without renewed agreement.

### 2) Capacity reduction without formal closure
This is the only route I see as potentially workable, because it preserves sites while still reducing structural costs.

But it must be genuine.  
We will not accept:
- underfunding a plant until it collapses,
- starving a site of future allocation,
- or calling a de facto shutdown “specialization.”

### 3) Burden-sharing sequencing
Yes, management, owners, and capital must contribute first. That is basic fairness.

But sequencing alone is not enough. It must end in a **binding non-closure settlement**, not in another round of closure threats later.

## What is needed now
I support the auditor’s call for a serious independent assessment.

[SUMMON: independent plant-by-plant industrial and financial auditor with access to site-level cost, utilization, capex, and volume data]

This is necessary to test:
- whether the claimed savings gap is real at each site,
- what non-closure measures actually deliver,
- and whether any closure case is truly unavoidable.

## My bottom line
We are ready to negotiate a **serious, measurable restructuring**.

We are **not** ready to accept:
- German plant closures,
- compulsory redundancies,
- or dismantling the collective wage framework.

If management wants cooperation, it must come with numbers, burden-sharing, and binding guarantees.  
If it insists on treating closures as the starting point, then it should expect resistance — in bargaining and in the plants.
