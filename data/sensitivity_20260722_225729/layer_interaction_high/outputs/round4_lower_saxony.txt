I will be very clear.

## My position
Lower Saxony supports a **durable restructuring** of Volkswagen. We do **not** support a restructuring that is built on:
- German plant closures as the starting point,
- compulsory redundancies,
- or weakening the state’s co-determination and blocking role under the VW Law.

That is not a red line I am using as a bargaining tactic. It reflects our responsibility to the regional economy, employment, and the long-term stability of Volkswagen as an industrial anchor.

## To management
I agree with you on one fundamental point: **temporary measures alone are not enough**. VW needs a durable reset of costs and capacity.

Where I strongly disagree is your repeated claim that closures must remain the default leverage before the facts are established. You still have **not** demonstrated that:
- every internal alternative has been exhausted,
- non-closure measures cannot produce equivalent permanent savings,
- or that a closure-based plan would be superior once industrial conflict, legal friction, and implementation risk are properly counted.

So no: I do not accept “closures remain available if needed” as a neutral statement. In this context, it is pressure politics.

What I can support is a package based on:
- capacity reduction through internal reallocation and specialization,
- shift and working-time adjustments,
- qualification and redeployment,
- voluntary exit pathways only,
- management and overhead restraint,
- and a fair labour contribution,

**provided** it is backed by binding employment and site commitments.

If you want my support for any more painful measures, then bring **plant-by-plant numbers** and show why the non-closure route fails. Until then, your closure case is unproven.

## To the Works Council
I agree with your core line: no German plant closures, no compulsory redundancies, no hollowing out of job security, and no one-sided burden on the workforce. That is also our position.

Where I want to sharpen the discussion is this: defending sites is necessary, but not sufficient. We also need a restructuring path that is economically credible. That means:
- real capacity reduction,
- productivity improvements,
- redeployment and retraining,
- voluntary pathways,
- and management contribution.

I do not see this as a contradiction. I see it as the only way to preserve Volkswagen’s industrial base without sacrificing its future.

## To IG Metall
I agree that social peace is not a soft issue; it is part of industrial competitiveness. A strike spiral would damage Volkswagen and the region.

But I also need to be frank: if the union’s position is simply “no closures, no layoffs” without a serious alternative package, then management will continue to use the gap as justification for harsher measures. So the task is to make the no-closure route **economically credible**, not just morally correct.

That means a binding package with:
- temporary working-time flexibility,
- voluntary exits,
- internal transfers,
- qualification,
- productivity measures,
- and fair labour-cost contributions,

in exchange for:
- binding site and employment security,
- and preservation of co-determination structures.

## To the controlling owners
I agree that Volkswagen needs a real structural reset, and I agree that a cosmetic compromise would be a mistake.

But I do **not** accept the notion that “real reset” means keeping closures on the table as a standing threat while expecting the workforce to concede first. That is not value discipline; it is conflict escalation.

If you want long-term value, then support a package that proves restructuring can be done **without sacrificing German sites**, unless and until a site-specific analysis proves that impossible. That means backing:
- binding site commitments,
- no forced redundancies,
- management restraint,
- and measurable contributions from owners and capital.

## To institutional investors
You are right to insist on:
- measurable savings,
- lower break-even volumes,
- implementation certainty,
- and durability.

I do not dispute that at all.

What I dispute is the assumption that only closures can deliver that. Permanent savings can also come from:
- capacity reduction without formal closure,
- line and shift consolidation,
- product transfer,
- overhead cuts,
- internal redeployment,
- voluntary exits,
- and negotiated labour-cost contributions.

What I will not support is a “financially clean” plan that is socially explosive and creates major strike and execution risk. That is not certainty; it is deferred instability.

## On the audit / verification issue
I support the call for an independent, plant-by-plant assessment — but it must be used to test **non-closure viability first**, not to build a cleaner closure case.

[SUMMON: independent plant-by-plant industrial and financial auditor, tasked to verify site-level utilization, cost position, capex needs, product outlook, and the savings from non-closure restructuring measures compared with closure scenarios]

That review must answer:
- which sites are actually underutilized,
- what permanent savings each measure delivers,
- what capex is needed to keep each site viable,
- and whether a non-closure package truly matches the economics.

Until that work is done, no one should pretend the closure case is settled.

## What would move Lower Saxony
Three things only:

1. **Binding guarantees**
   - no German plant closures
   - no compulsory redundancies
   - protection of co-determination and job-security structures

2. **A verifiable non-closure restructuring**
   - capacity reduction through internal measures
   - voluntary pathways only
   - redeployment and qualification
   - management and overhead cuts
   - fair but limited labour contribution

3. **Real burden-sharing from the top**
   - executive restraint
   - owner discipline
   - dividend and capital allocation responsibility
   - no one-sided sacrifice from workers

## What I reject
- closures as the default instrument,
- forced layoffs as “economic necessity,”
- treating overcapacity as a pretext to tear up historic protections,
- and asking the workforce to sign away rights in exchange for vague promises.

## Bottom line
Lower Saxony is prepared to back a serious, measurable restructuring that preserves Volkswagen as a regional economic anchor and supports its long-term health.

We are **not** prepared to support:
- plant closures in Germany as the starting point,
- compulsory redundancies,
- or any restructuring that sidelines the state and weakens the VW Law framework.

Bring us a verified non-closure package with hard numbers and binding commitments, and we will engage seriously. If not, then the burden of proof remains where it belongs: on those proposing closures, not on those defending the company’s regional base.
