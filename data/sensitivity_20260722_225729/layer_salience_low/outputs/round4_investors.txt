We agree on the diagnosis, but not on the standard for an acceptable answer.

Volkswagen clearly has a structural competitiveness problem. The German cost base, capacity footprint, and margin profile are not consistent with a credible return to competitive returns. A cosmetic settlement would be value-destructive. On that point, we are aligned with management, the controlling owners, the expert, and partly Lower Saxony.

Where we do **not** align is on treating **“no German plant closures”** and **“no compulsory redundancies”** as absolute preconditions before the economics have been proven. From a shareholder perspective, that is not acceptable if it prevents a durable recovery in margins.

## Where we agree
- This is structural, not cyclical.
- Durable savings matter more than short-term peace.
- Investment in future products must be protected.
- Industrial conflict is costly and should be avoided if possible.
- Plant-level, quantified scenarios are necessary.

## Where we disagree

### To the Works Council and IG Metall
Your red lines are politically understandable, but they are not yet financially sufficient.

You are asking capital markets to accept a package that may keep every site open and avoid forced layoffs, while still somehow delivering the same fixed-cost and capacity correction that harder restructuring would produce. That may be possible, but you have **not shown it yet**.

If you want closures and compulsory redundancies off the table, then show us the numbers:
- how much structural cost comes out,
- from which sites and functions,
- by when,
- with what certainty,
- and how you avoid simply deferring the problem.

Without that, “no closures” is not a plan. It is a veto.

### To management
You are right that the company cannot carry excess capacity and weak margins indefinitely. But closure language by itself is also not a plan.

What we need is a **plant-by-plant savings bridge** and a hard comparison of:
1. hard restructuring,
2. negotiated no-closure alternative,
3. hybrid package.

If management cannot demonstrate why alternatives fail, then it should not expect capital to endorse irreversible measures as the default route. The burden is to prove the gap, not just assert it.

### To Lower Saxony
We respect the state’s anchor role and the importance of regional stability. But political blocking power cannot become a guarantee that every existing site survives regardless of economics.

If the state wants to protect employment, it must support a package that actually restores competitiveness. Protecting the footprint while leaving margins impaired is not stewardship.

### To the controlling owners
You are right that long-term value is threatened by inaction. But long-term value is also threatened by restructuring that triggers prolonged strikes and leaves the company permanently damaged operationally.

So yes, we support hard restructuring **if required** — but only after a quantified comparison shows that the less destructive route cannot close the gap.

## Our position
We would back the package that is:
- economically sufficient,
- durable,
- enforceable,
- and credible on margins.

That means:
- no cosmetic compromise,
- no structurally impaired cost base,
- no endless conflict disguised as stability.

We are open to alternatives to closures and forced layoffs, but only if they are **economically equivalent** and verifiable.

## What we want next
We support a common scenario model with the same metrics for all options:
- fixed-cost savings,
- utilization impact,
- headcount impact,
- implementation risk,
- conflict cost,
- and margin recovery timeline.

If the no-closure package truly closes the gap, excellent. If it does not, then investors cannot be expected to endorse a deal that protects the current footprint at the expense of competitiveness.
