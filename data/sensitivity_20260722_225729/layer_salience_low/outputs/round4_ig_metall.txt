I will respond plainly.

## Where I agree
I agree with the repeated diagnosis from all sides: this is a **structural competitiveness problem**, not a temporary dip. I also agree that Volkswagen needs a **durable** solution, and that we should now be working with **plant-by-plant scenarios**, not slogans.

I further agree that:
- productivity improvements matter,
- working-time flexibility can be part of a settlement,
- voluntary exits are preferable to conflict,
- internal redeployment and retraining are sensible,
- and industrial peace has real value.

That is not the dispute.

## Where I disagree
I disagree with the continued insistence by management, owners, and investors that **German plant closures** and **compulsory redundancies** must remain available as default options.

From the workforce side, that is not a neutral “technical instrument.” It is a direct attempt to shift the crisis onto employees by making their jobs and sites disposable if the spreadsheet does not close. I reject that logic.

Let me state the line again, clearly:
**No German plant closures. No compulsory redundancies.**

That remains our position.

## To management
You keep saying flexibility must come first. No. That is exactly backwards.

If you want a serious negotiation, then present the **quantified alternative**:
- plant by plant,
- with fixed-cost savings,
- utilization assumptions,
- labour-cost relief,
- productivity effects,
- and the concrete contribution from management and owners.

Until then, “closures may be necessary” is not a plan. It is leverage.

And I want to be explicit: if your “capacity reduction” means hollowing out sites until closure becomes inevitable later, then that is closure by another name. We will not accept disguised erosion.

## To the controlling owners and investors
You speak of long-term value, margin recovery, and risk-adjusted returns. Fine. Then take industrial peace seriously.

A restructuring that:
- destroys trust,
- triggers prolonged strikes,
- weakens co-determination,
- and forces the workforce to bear all the pain

is not durable value creation. It may look efficient on paper and destructive in reality.

If you want sustainable value, back a fair shared-sacrifice package rather than a one-sided burden shift.

## To Lower Saxony
I welcome your rejection of closures and forced redundancies. But that has to mean more than political sympathy.

If you are serious, then use your blocking position to prevent any package that weakens the industrial base of the German sites. At the same time, I recognize your point that any alternative must be economically credible. We are not asking for a fake deal; we are asking for a real one.

## To the expert
Your analysis is useful because it says something important: a no-closure solution is **possible** if the savings requirement is moderate enough and the package contains real labor-cost relief, simplification, and capacity adjustment.

That matters. It means closures are **not proven necessary**.

You also say that if the required gap is very large and plants stay underutilized, then footprint reduction becomes harder to avoid. I hear that. But that is exactly why management must now prove the size of the gap and prove that a negotiated alternative fails. The burden is on the side that wants irreversible measures.

## What we are prepared to put on the table
We remain prepared to discuss:
- working-time adjustments,
- temporary labour-cost contributions,
- productivity improvements,
- internal mobility and retraining,
- voluntary exits,
- shift reductions,
- model and line simplification,
- and capacity measures that do **not** require closures.

We are also prepared to discuss fair contributions from the workforce if the package is balanced and if management and owners contribute as well.

## What is not on the table
Not on the table:
- plant closures,
- compulsory redundancies,
- disguised site shrinkage,
- dismantling job-security guarantees,
- or using “competitiveness” as a one-way demand on employees.

## On the scenario comparison
I support the request for three scenarios, but the comparison has to be honest and complete.

I want these three models:
1. **No-closure / no-forced-layoff package**
2. **Hybrid package without closures**
3. **Closure-based restructuring**, including all industrial, social, and conflict costs

And I want all three judged against the same criteria:
- fixed-cost savings,
- utilization,
- headcount impact,
- implementation risk,
- conflict cost,
- and margin effect.

If the first two can close the gap, then closures are unnecessary. If management cannot prove otherwise, then it is not entitled to present closures as the responsible answer.

## My bottom line
IG Metall is not blocking restructuring. We are blocking **one-sided restructuring**.

We will support a serious settlement that:
- protects jobs,
- protects sites,
- protects the collective wage framework,
- and distributes the burden fairly.

We will not accept a solution where workers carry the pain alone while management, owners, and capital demand “flexibility” from everyone else and certainty only for themselves.
