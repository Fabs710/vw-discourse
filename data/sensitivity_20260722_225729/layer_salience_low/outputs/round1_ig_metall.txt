We are aligned on the core point that the workforce cannot be asked to carry the crisis alone.

Let me be equally clear: **plant closures in Germany and compulsory redundancies are not acceptable starting points**. Those would destroy trust, undermine the collective bargaining framework, and shift the burden entirely onto employees. That is not a fair restructuring, and it will meet resistance.

Where we do agree:
- The company needs a durable solution, not cosmetic savings.
- Capacity reduction may be necessary.
- A workforce contribution can be part of the answer.
- Any settlement must be tied to credible investment and a future industrial plan.

Where we do **not** agree is on the idea that management can simply impose pain first and negotiations later. If workers are to contribute, then **management, owners, and capital must also contribute visibly and materially**. That means:
- no forced layoffs,
- no closure of sites as the default route,
- protection of the collective wage framework,
- and binding guarantees that jobs and locations are secured in return for concessions.

We have already shown that we are willing to talk about alternatives: working-time measures, capacity adjustments, internal efficiency, and a fair contribution from employees. But if the restructuring logic remains “jobs and sites on the chopping block while everyone else is protected,” then there is no basis for agreement.

So our position is:
1. **No compulsory redundancies.**
2. **No German plant closures.**
3. **Shared sacrifice, not one-sided cuts.**
4. **A negotiated package with binding job, site, and investment commitments.**

If management wants cooperation, it must abandon the threat-based approach and come back with a credible plan that protects employment and spreads the burden fairly. Otherwise, industrial action will remain on the table.
