We agree on the core diagnosis: Volkswagen has a structural competitiveness problem, not a temporary one. The German cost base is too high, capacity is misaligned with demand, and a cosmetic deal would destroy value. That part is clear.

But I want to be equally clear about what matters to capital markets: **we do not care whether the solution is “pleasant”; we care whether it is economically sufficient, durable, and enforceable.**

### Where we agree
- With management: the status quo is not acceptable.
- With owners: long-term value requires real restructuring, not window dressing.
- With Lower Saxony and labor: one-sided pain is unstable and expensive if it triggers prolonged conflict.
- With the workforce: industrial peace has real value, and forced disruption is not free.

### Where we disagree
I disagree with the employee side insofar as it treats **“no closures” and “no compulsory redundancies” as absolute starting points**. If those red lines prevent a package from fully restoring competitiveness, then they are not a responsible position for shareholders to support.

I also disagree with management if closures are being used as a negotiating posture without a fully tested alternative. A threat is not a plan. Capital markets need quantified evidence, not just hard language.

And I disagree with any attempt to preserve the current footprint merely to avoid conflict. If that leaves the cost base structurally impaired, it is a value-destroying compromise.

### What would move us
We would support the package that can **prove**, with numbers:
1. how much structural cost comes out,
2. how much capacity is removed or made flexible,
3. when margins return to a competitive level,
4. what the implementation risk is,
5. and how industrial peace is secured without sacrificing the economics.

That means we need a **fully quantified comparison** of scenarios:
- a hard restructuring with closures/job reductions,
- a negotiated alternative with capacity reduction, working-time changes, and labor-cost relief,
- and a hybrid package, if it truly closes the gap.

### Our minimum acceptable standard
We will not support an outcome that:
- leaves the cost base uncompetitive,
- preserves excess capacity under a political label,
- or delays the problem while burning cash and credibility.

We are open to alternatives to closures and forced layoffs, but only if they are **economically equivalent** and durable. If they are not, then the company should stop pretending they are a real substitute.

### To management
Bring the numbers. Not just the diagnosis, but the bridge to competitive margins:
- savings by line item,
- timing,
- certainty,
- and execution path.

### To labor and Lower Saxony
If you want closures and forced redundancies off the table, then show a package that fully closes the competitiveness gap. Otherwise, you are asking shareholders to accept structural underperformance in exchange for temporary peace, and we will not back that.

### Bottom line
We support a negotiated settlement only if it restores competitive returns.  
No cosmetic compromise.  
No structurally impaired margin profile.  
No endless conflict masquerading as stability.
