I will respond directly.

### Where I agree
I agree with the central diagnosis from management, the owners, the investors, and the expert: Volkswagen has a **structural competitiveness problem**, not a temporary dip. The company needs a durable solution, and it needs one that restores margins and funds future investment. I also agree that a purely cosmetic compromise would be irresponsible.

I also agree with the employee side on one important point: **the burden cannot fall on the workforce alone**. If there is a serious restructuring, then management, owners, and the company itself must contribute as well. Industrial peace has value, and Volkswagen is not just any asset — it is a regional economic anchor.

### Where I disagree
I do **not** accept the repeated framing that German plant closures and compulsory redundancies must remain the default “economically admissible” option.

For Lower Saxony, plant closures are not a neutral technical instrument. They carry lasting consequences for:
- employment,
- supplier networks,
- regional value creation,
- and trust in the co-determination model.

Likewise, compulsory redundancies are not my preferred route and should not be treated as the first instrument. If alternatives can achieve comparable durability, they must be seriously preferred.

That said, I also do **not** accept the employee-side position as complete, because simply saying “no closures” and “no forced layoffs” is not yet a restructuring plan. It is a red line. Red lines matter, but they do not by themselves close the cost gap.

### My position
Lower Saxony remains open to a **negotiated restructuring** that:
- protects core German sites,
- avoids compulsory redundancies wherever possible,
- delivers real and durable savings,
- reduces capacity in a way that does not hollow out the industrial base,
- and includes credible investment commitments for future products and technologies.

But I will be very clear: I will **not** support any outcome that:
- guts regional employment,
- destroys the company’s role as an anchor of the state economy,
- or sidelines co-determination and the state’s blocking role.

That is not a bargaining tactic; it is a line that follows from our responsibility.

### On the arguments from management
Management is right that flexibility is needed. But flexibility is not the same as a license to force closures before alternatives have been tested. If you want support for a major restructuring, then show the numbers in a way that proves why a no-closure package is insufficient.

So far, I have heard a strong diagnosis, but not yet a fully convincing demonstration that only closures can solve it. That burden of proof still matters.

### On the arguments from the works council and IG Metall
I hear your demand for binding site and job guarantees. I take it seriously. But a guarantee without an economically credible foundation is not durable. If you want to exclude closures and compulsory redundancies, then the alternative package must be substantial enough to restore competitiveness.

What I need to see is not another general statement, but a **concrete and quantified package**:
- what capacity is reduced,
- what productivity gain is delivered,
- what labour-cost contribution is made,
- what investment is committed,
- and how the package remains enforceable over time.

### On the arguments from the controlling owners and investors
You are right that value destruction through inaction is unacceptable. But you should also recognise that value is not protected by treating German sites as disposable cost items. A settlement that damages the industrial base and triggers prolonged conflict may look tidy on paper and prove expensive in reality.

So I am not aligned with any logic that says “if the model prefers closures, then closures are automatically the answer.” The governance and regional consequences are too important for that.

### On the expert analysis
The expert makes the key point correctly: a no-closure solution may be possible **if** the savings requirement is within reach through labor-cost relief, simplification, and capacity reduction. That is exactly why we should now move from principle to **tested scenarios**.

But the expert also makes clear that if the required fixed-cost removal is large and the plants remain underutilized, then a no-closure package may not be enough. That is why I am not prepared to rule in or rule out any instrument before seeing the plant-by-plant evidence.

### What I want next
I support the call for **fully quantified scenarios**. Not slogans, not threat language, not political theater.

I would like to see three comparable packages:
1. **Hard restructuring**
   - including what the social and regional costs would be

2. **Negotiated no-closure package**
   - with quantified labor contribution, working-time changes, productivity gains, and capacity measures

3. **Hybrid conditional package**
   - with protections for sites, clear milestones, and review mechanisms

But I want one condition made explicit: any scenario must be tested against the objective of **preserving Volkswagen as a regional economic anchor** and maintaining co-determination credibility. Those are not optional side issues for Lower Saxony.

### My bottom line
I am open to hard restructuring, but **not** to a restructuring that sacrifices German plants and jobs as a shortcut.

I am open to a fair workforce contribution, but **not** to a one-sided transfer of crisis costs onto employees.

I am open to a negotiated solution, but **only** if it preserves viable sites, protects core employment, and secures the long-term health of the company.
