## Independent assessment: can a no-closure package close the gap?

Yes — **in principle**, a no-closure package can sometimes close a meaningful portion of the gap through:
- labor-cost relief,
- working-time flexibility,
- voluntary exits,
- productivity gains,
- line/model simplification,
- and better utilization.

But for a large, high-cost manufacturer with structurally underused German plants, there is a **practical threshold** beyond which a no-closure package becomes financially unlikely to be sufficient unless concessions are unusually deep and durable.

### My high-level answer
For Volkswagen, a **credible no-closure scenario** would usually need to deliver all of the following:
1. **material fixed-cost reduction**
2. **durable labor-cost relief**
3. **real capacity release**
4. **measurable productivity improvement**
5. **low implementation risk**
6. **acceptable margin recovery within a reasonable horizon**

If it cannot do that, then **some form of footprint reduction becomes financially hard to avoid**.

---

## What I would regard as the threshold for viability

Because I do not have VW’s internal plant-level model, I can only give a disciplined analytical threshold, not a verified company-specific number.

A no-closure package is usually only viable if it can close roughly **70–100% of the identified structural cost gap** through measures that are:
- recurring, not one-off,
- enforceable, not aspirational,
- and large enough to compensate for continued fixed-cost burden.

If the package only closes, say, **30–50%** of the gap, then it is generally not a restructuring solution — it is a delay mechanism.

### In practice, the package would need combinations such as:
- meaningful wage moderation or temporary wage freeze
- working-time reductions or shift reductions
- substantial headcount reduction via attrition/voluntary exits
- productivity gains from process simplification and automation
- internal redeployment and fewer layers
- reduction in planned output/capacity at specific sites, even if sites remain open

If those measures cannot get close to the required savings, a no-closure path is weak.

---

## Where the line typically is

The line is crossed when:
- plants remain **structurally underutilized**
- fixed costs remain too high relative to output
- labor savings are **temporary or reversible**
- and the company still cannot restore a credible operating margin

At that point, **keeping every site open can become economically equivalent to preserving excess capacity**.

So the key question is not “Can we avoid closures politically?”  
It is: **Can we reduce the economic burden of those sites enough without closing them?**

## My judgment on the three scenarios

### 1) Hard restructuring with closures
**Most likely to maximize certainty of cost removal.**  
Pros:
- highest probability of durable fixed-cost reduction
- strongest margin recovery potential
- clearer capacity alignment

Cons:
- high conflict cost
- high one-time restructuring cost
- industrial peace risk
- possible execution delays and reputational damage

### 2) Negotiated no-closure package
**Possible only if concessions are large enough.**  
Pros:
- lower immediate conflict
- preserves sites and political acceptability
- potentially faster to agree if trust exists

Cons:
- may not remove enough fixed cost
- may preserve excess capacity
- savings may be deferred, partial, or reversible

### 3) Hybrid conditional package
**Often the most realistic compromise.**  
Pros:
- immediate no-closure commitment
- binding milestones
- preserves optionality
- creates a path to later footprint adjustment if needed

Cons:
- may still not be sufficient if milestones are missed
- can become a delay tactic unless triggers are real and enforceable

## What would need to be shown to prove no-closure viability

To determine whether a no-closure package is genuinely sufficient, I would need:
- plant-by-plant fixed-cost base
- capacity utilization by site and line
- labor cost per vehicle and per hour, by location
- headcount by function and skill group
- one-time restructuring costs
- expected savings by year
- implementation assumptions and risk sensitivities
- effect on operating margin and free cash flow

Without that, no one can honestly say whether closures are avoidable.

---

## Bottom line

### My answer in one sentence:
A no-closure package is financially viable **only if it can deliver very deep, durable, and enforceable structural savings — close to the full gap — while also reducing effective capacity enough to restore margins**.

### Practical conclusion:
- If the gap is **moderate**, a negotiated no-closure or hybrid package may work.
- If the gap is **large and persistent**, and underutilization remains high, then **some footprint reduction becomes financially difficult to avoid**, even if not all sites close.
