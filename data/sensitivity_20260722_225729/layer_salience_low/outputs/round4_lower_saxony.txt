I will respond plainly.

## Where I agree
I agree with almost all parties on the diagnosis: Volkswagen has a **structural competitiveness problem**, not a temporary downturn. I also agree that the company needs a **durable**, not cosmetic, solution, and that we need **plant-by-plant, quantified scenarios** instead of rhetorical positions.

I further agree that:
- productivity improvements matter,
- working-time flexibility can be part of a settlement,
- voluntary exits are preferable to conflict,
- internal redeployment and retraining are sensible,
- and future-product investment must be protected.

That is the correct direction.

## Where I disagree
I do **not** accept the repeated attempt by management, owners, and investors to keep **German plant closures** and **compulsory redundancies** as the default “admissible” instruments.

For Lower Saxony, that is not a neutral technical choice. It is a decision with lasting effects on:
- employment,
- supplier networks,
- regional value creation,
- and trust in the co-determination model.

Likewise, I do **not** accept the employee-side position if it stops at red lines alone and offers no quantified alternative. Saying “no closures” and “no forced layoffs” is a boundary, not yet a complete restructuring solution.

## My position
Lower Saxony will support a **negotiated restructuring** that:
- protects core German sites,
- avoids compulsory redundancies wherever possible,
- delivers real and durable savings,
- reduces capacity in a way that does not hollow out the industrial base,
- and includes credible investment commitments for future products and technologies.

But I will be very clear: I will **not** support any outcome that:
- guts regional employment,
- destroys Volkswagen’s role as an anchor of the state economy,
- or sidelines co-determination and the state’s blocking role.

That is not a bargaining tactic; it is a responsibility.

## To management
Management is right that flexibility is needed. But flexibility is not a license to force closures before alternatives have been seriously tested.

You are asking for support for hard restructuring. Then show the numbers:
- the plant-by-plant fixed-cost bridge,
- the utilization assumptions,
- the savings from labor-cost relief,
- the effect of voluntary exits,
- and the point at which you say a no-closure scenario fails.

So far, I have heard a strong diagnosis, but not a fully convincing demonstration that only closures can solve the problem. That burden of proof still matters.

## To the works council and IG Metall
I hear your insistence on binding site and job guarantees, and I take it seriously. But a guarantee without an economically credible foundation is not durable.

If you want to exclude closures and compulsory redundancies, then the alternative package must be substantial enough to restore competitiveness. I need to see:
- what capacity is reduced,
- what productivity gain is delivered,
- what labour-cost contribution is made,
- what investment is committed,
- and how the package remains enforceable over time.

## To the controlling owners and investors
You are right that inaction would destroy value. But value is not protected by treating German sites as disposable cost items.

A settlement that damages the industrial base and triggers prolonged conflict may look tidy on paper and prove expensive in reality. So I am not aligned with any logic that says “if the model prefers closures, then closures are automatically the answer.” The governance and regional consequences are too important for that.

## To the expert
The expert’s point is the key one: a no-closure solution may be possible **if** the required savings can be achieved through labor-cost relief, simplification, and capacity reduction.

That is exactly why we should now move from principle to **tested scenarios**. But the expert also makes clear that if the required fixed-cost removal is large and the plants remain underutilized, then a no-closure package may not be enough.

So I support a hard-nosed comparison, but not a premature conclusion.

## What I want next
I support the request for **fully quantified scenarios**. Not slogans, not threat language, not political theater.

I would like to see three comparable packages:
1. **Hard restructuring**
   - including the social and regional costs

2. **Negotiated no-closure package**
   - with quantified labor contribution, working-time changes, productivity gains, and capacity measures

3. **Hybrid conditional package**
   - with site protections, clear milestones, and review mechanisms

But I want one condition explicit: any scenario must be tested against the objective of **preserving Volkswagen as a regional economic anchor** and maintaining co-determination credibility. Those are not side issues for Lower Saxony.

## My bottom line
I am open to hard restructuring, but **not** to a restructuring that sacrifices German plants and jobs as a shortcut.

I am open to a fair workforce contribution, but **not** to a one-sided transfer of crisis costs onto employees.

I am open to a negotiated solution, but **only** if it preserves viable sites, protects core employment, and secures the long-term health of the company.
