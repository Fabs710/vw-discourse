I will respond plainly.

### Where I agree
I agree with management, investors, and Lower Saxony on the central diagnosis: Volkswagen cannot keep carrying a structurally uncompetitive cost base and excess capacity in Germany. If we pretend that the current footprint can simply be preserved without consequence, we will erode margins, weaken the brand, and destroy long-term value. That is unacceptable.

I also agree with the workforce side on one important point: a one-sided solution would be unstable and therefore value-destructive. Industrial peace matters. A restructuring that creates prolonged conflict, repeated strikes, and reputational damage is not a good outcome for the company or for the owners.

### Where I disagree
I do **not** agree that “no closures” and “no compulsory redundancies” can be treated as absolute conditions before the economics are shown.

If the employee side wants to rule those tools out entirely, then it must present a credible alternative that closes the full competitiveness gap with the same durability. So far, I have heard principles and red lines, but not a fully quantified substitute that matches the scale of the problem.

I also do **not** accept the idea that protecting every existing structure is automatically synonymous with protecting long-term employment. Sometimes refusing change faster just means a larger value destruction later. That would hurt the workforce, the owners, and the company alike.

### To the Works Council and IG Metall
You are right to insist that employees cannot carry the burden alone. I support that. But your current position still reads as a defensive wall rather than a restructuring answer.

If you want to avoid closures and forced redundancies, then bring forward a package that is:
- quantitatively credible,
- durable,
- enforceable,
- and large enough to restore competitiveness.

That means real measures, not just appeals to fairness:
- meaningful labour-cost relief,
- productivity improvements,
- working-time flexibility,
- capacity reductions with economic effect,
- and a contribution from the workforce that is matched by management, owners, and the company.

Binding guarantees through 2030 are not meaningless to me, but they cannot be unconditional if the business case does not work. Guarantees without viability are just postponed conflict.

### To Lower Saxony
I appreciate the seriousness with which you are approaching this. Your insistence that industrial legitimacy matters is correct. But I would caution against turning political blocking power into a de facto guarantee that no hard choices will ever be made.

If we over-protect the current footprint, we may protect appearances while undermining the actual enterprise. That is not stewardship.

### To management
I am aligned with you on the need for substantial structural change, but I want discipline here as well: do not use closures as a rhetorical threat without a fully tested alternative analysis.

If you want support for deep restructuring, then the package must be fully quantified:
- how much cost comes out,
- from where,
- by when,
- with what certainty,
- and what the employment and site consequences are.

A good governance process should compare complete scenarios, not slogans.

### My position
The controlling owners will support a settlement only if it delivers all of the following:
1. a **durable and material improvement in competitiveness**,
2. a **real reduction in structural cost and capacity**,
3. a **workforce contribution that is meaningful but shared fairly**,
4. **industrial peace** or at least a credible path to it,
5. and **no permanent damage to enterprise value**.

That means I am open to alternatives to closures and forced layoffs — but only if they are economically equivalent and enforceable. If they are not, then I will not support a cosmetic compromise that preserves overcapacity and weak margins.

### On the next step
I support moving to fully specified scenarios. But let them be real scenarios, not just wishful protection statements.

I would like to see at least these compared:
- a **hard restructuring** scenario with closures and job reductions,
- a **negotiated alternative** with capacity reduction, working-time changes, and labour-cost relief,
- and a **hybrid package** with conditional site protections tied to quantified savings and investment commitments.

Only one test matters: which package restores Volkswagen’s competitiveness and long-term value without creating avoidable instability.

If none of the alternatives deliver equivalent effect, then we should stop pretending closures can be ruled out in advance.
