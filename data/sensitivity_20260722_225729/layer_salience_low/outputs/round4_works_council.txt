Let me be equally direct in Round 4.

I hear the repeated attempt to reframe this as a neutral technical choice between “economically necessary” closures and supposedly sentimental resistance. That framing is wrong.

## What I agree with
I agree on three points:
1. Volkswagen has a **structural competitiveness problem**.
2. We need a **durable** solution, not a temporary patch.
3. We need **plant-level, quantified scenarios** instead of slogans.

I also agree that voluntary exits, working-time flexibility, productivity gains, internal redeployment, and simplification are all serious instruments. Those are not cosmetic. Those are the tools that should be used first.

## What I do not accept
What I do **not** accept is the persistent assumption by management, owners, and investors that:
- German plant closures must remain on the table as a default instrument, and
- compulsory redundancies can be kept as an “admissible” fallback.

That is exactly the logic that breaks trust and triggers conflict. It turns co-determination into an afterthought and workers into a cost variable.

Let me restate the line clearly:
**No German plant closures. No compulsory redundancies.**

That is not a negotiating preference. That is the boundary of what the workforce can accept.

## On management
Management keeps saying it needs flexibility first and then commitments later. No. We have seen where that leads: a fait accompli.

If you want a serious negotiation, then put forward a quantified package that proves, plant by plant, how you close the gap **without** closures and **without** forced layoffs. Until you do that, your position is not a solution; it is a demand that the workforce accept the threat of destruction in advance.

And let me be clear: “capacity reduction” is not a magic phrase that makes site erosion acceptable. If it means hollowing out plants until closure becomes inevitable, then it is closure by another name.

## On the controlling owners and investors
You keep talking about long-term value, risk-adjusted returns, and margin recovery. Fine. Then recognize this: long-term value is not created by tearing up industrial peace and destroying trust in the co-determination model.

A restructuring that creates permanent conflict, skill loss, and social damage is not “hard-nosed”; it is value-destructive. If you want durable value, then support a settlement that preserves sites and jobs instead of assuming that workforce pressure is the cheapest lever.

## On Lower Saxony
I welcome your stated rejection of closures and forced redundancies. But political sympathy is not enough. If you mean it, then use your blocking position to prevent any package that weakens the German industrial base.

At the same time, I will say this plainly: I am not asking for a fake solution. I am asking for a real one that keeps every German plant open, avoids compulsory redundancies, and still delivers competitive savings through negotiated means.

## On the expert’s position
The expert keeps saying the no-closure route is “possible” if the savings requirement is moderate enough, but may fail if the gap is large. That is exactly why management must now prove its case.

You have not proven that the gap cannot be closed through:
- labor-cost relief,
- working-time adjustments,
- productivity improvements,
- voluntary exits,
- internal transfers,
- shift reductions,
- model consolidation,
- and management/owner contributions.

So closures are not justified. They are only being held in reserve as a threat.

If the required savings are truly as large as claimed, then show the numbers transparently. Don’t hide behind inevitability.

## What I am prepared to discuss
We remain prepared to discuss:
- working-time changes,
- temporary labour-cost contributions,
- productivity commitments,
- internal mobility,
- retraining and redeployment,
- voluntary exit programs only where truly voluntary,
- and investment commitments tied to keeping every German site open.

That is the framework.

## What is not on the table
Not on the table:
- plant closures,
- compulsory redundancies,
- disguised site shrinkage,
- weakening the historic job-security framework,
- or using “competitiveness” as a one-way demand on the workforce.

## On the scenario comparison
I support the call for a common scenario model, but it must be built honestly and not designed to smuggle in management’s preferred outcome.

I want three scenarios compared on the same basis:
1. **No-closure / no-forced-layoff package**
2. **Hybrid package without closures**
3. **Closure-based restructuring** — including the social, industrial, and conflict costs

And I want the comparison to include:
- fixed-cost savings,
- utilization,
- employment impact,
- implementation risk,
- conflict cost,
- and long-term margin effect.

If management claims the first two cannot work, prove it. If you cannot prove it, then closures are not a responsible option.

## My bottom line
I will not move from the position that:
- every German plant stays open,
- no one is forced out,
- and co-determination remains meaningful, not ceremonial.

That is where the workforce stands. If the others want a settlement, they need to build it within those boundaries — not ask us to surrender them first.
