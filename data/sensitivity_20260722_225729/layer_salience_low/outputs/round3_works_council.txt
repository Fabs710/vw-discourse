### First, what I agree with
I agree with the expert and with parts of management’s diagnosis on one point: this is a **structural** problem, not a temporary blip. We do need durable savings, and we do need investment in future products and technologies.

### What I do **not** accept
I do **not** accept the notion that plant closures and compulsory redundancies are “economically admissible tools” that somehow must remain available as the default answer.

That framing is exactly the problem. It treats workers’ livelihoods and entire sites as variables to be sacrificed if management cannot otherwise balance the spreadsheet. That is not restructuring; that is a unilateral transfer of risk onto the workforce.

And let me be clear:  
**No German plant closures. No compulsory redundancies.**  
That is still my line.

### On the expert’s argument
The expert says closures are not proven to be strictly necessary, but may become necessary if the gap is large. That is a convenient way of leaving the most destructive option sitting on the table while demanding concessions first from employees.

My response is simple:
- If management believes alternatives are insufficient, **show it in numbers**.
- If it cannot prove that a no-closure package fails, then closures are **not justified**.
- And if the company is serious about a negotiated solution, then it must stop using closure threats as leverage.

The expert is right about one thing: the burden of proof is on the side that wants to destroy sites and jobs. That burden has not been met.

If you want a serious negotiation, then present:
- a plant-by-plant savings bridge,

### On the “compromise” proposals
I am going to be very clear that I do **not** see the suggested compromise packages as acceptable in their current form.

#### 1) Conditional site-security package
A “no immediate closures” package with review milestones sounds harmless, but in practice it is just a delayed closure mechanism. You freeze the sites now, extract concessions now, and then reopen the closure threat later if targets are missed — which, given the way these exercises are usually designed, management can always claim.

That is not job security. That is conditional insecurity.

#### 2) Hybrid restructuring with targeted footprint adjustment
“Partial line consolidation,” “shift reductions,” and “capped headcount reduction through attrition” are often just euphemisms for getting to closures by another name. If the result is that sites are hollowed out, that still damages the plant base and the workforce.

If the line is: every German plant stays open, no compulsory redundancies, no forced layoffs — then we can discuss productivity, working time, and negotiated contributions. But not disguised site erosion.

#### 3) Scenario contest with arbitration trigger
I have no objection to transparent numbers. But I do object to an arbitration structure being used to legitimize outcomes that violate our red lines.

We are not entering a process where the “independent” answer is used to force through closures as the presumed rational choice. Co-determination is not a decorative institution. It is a real veto framework for a reason.

### What we are actually prepared to discuss
We are prepared to discuss:

- productivity improvements,
- internal mobility,

- voluntary exit options only where truly voluntary,

That is the framework.

### What must be excluded
Anything that includes:
- plant closures,
- compulsory redundancies,
- disguised site shrinkage designed to force closures later,
- dismantling job-security guarantees,
- or using “competitiveness” as a one-way ratchet against workers.
