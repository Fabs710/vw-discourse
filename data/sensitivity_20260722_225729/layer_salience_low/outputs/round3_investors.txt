We agree on the diagnosis, but not on the standard for the answer.

Volkswagen has a structural competitiveness problem. The German cost base is too high, capacity is misaligned, and a cosmetic settlement would destroy value. On that point, we are aligned with management, the controlling owners, the expert, and — in part — Lower Saxony.

Where we part company is on the insistence that **“no German plant closures” and “no compulsory redundancies”** can be treated as absolute red lines before the economics are proven. From a capital-markets perspective, that is not acceptable if it blocks a credible return to competitive margins.

### Where we agree
- This is structural, not cyclical.
- Durable savings matter more than temporary peace.
- Investment in future products must be protected.
- Industrial conflict has real cost and should be avoided if possible.
- We need quantified scenario analysis, not slogans.

### Where we disagree
#### To the Works Council and IG Metall
Your position is understandable politically, but it is not yet financially sufficient.

You are asking shareholders to accept a package that may preserve every site and every guarantee, while still somehow delivering the same fixed-cost and capacity correction that a harder restructuring would produce. That may be possible, but you have not shown it.

If you want closures and forced redundancies off the table, then show us the numbers:
- how much structural cost comes out,
- from which sites and functions,
- by when,
- with what certainty,
- and how you avoid simply deferring the problem.

Without that, “no closures” is not a plan. It is a veto.

#### To management
You are right that the company cannot preserve excess capacity and weak margins indefinitely. But closure language alone is also not a plan.

What we need is a **plant-by-plant savings bridge** and a clear comparison of:
1. hard restructuring,
2. negotiated no-closure alternative,
3. hybrid package.

If management cannot demonstrate why alternatives fail, then it should not expect capital to support irreversible measures as the default. The burden is to prove the gap, not just assert it.

#### To Lower Saxony
We respect the state’s role as an anchor shareholder and the importance of regional stability. But political blocking power cannot become a guarantee that every existing site survives regardless of economics.

If the state wants to protect employment, it must support a package that actually restores competitiveness. Protecting the footprint while leaving margins impaired is not stewardship.

#### To the controlling owners
You are right that long-term value is threatened by inaction. But long-term value is also threatened by a restructuring that triggers prolonged strikes and leaves the company permanently damaged operationally.

So yes, we support hard restructuring **if required** — but only after a quantified comparison shows that the less destructive route cannot close the gap.

### Our position
We would back the package that is:
- economically sufficient,
- durable,
- enforceable,
- and credible on margins.

That means:
- no cosmetic compromise,
- no structurally impaired cost base,
- no endless conflict disguised as stability.

We are open to alternatives to closures and forced layoffs, but only if they are **economically equivalent** and verifiable.

### What we want next
We support the request for a common scenario model. It should include:
- fixed-cost savings,
- utilization impact,
- employment and site effects,
- implementation risk,
- conflict cost,
- and margin recovery timeline.

If the no-closure package truly closes the gap, great. If it does not, then investors cannot be expected to endorse a deal that protects the current footprint at the expense of competitiveness.
