Let me be direct.

I agree with everyone on **one** point: Volkswagen has a structural problem, and a cosmetic compromise will not solve it. That is exactly why I am not prepared to sign up to a deal that preserves the current cost base and merely renames it “restructuring.”

But I do **not** accept the employee-side attempt to make **“no plant closures” and “no compulsory redundancies”** absolute preconditions. Those may be understandable red lines from a labour perspective, but they are not a restructuring answer. If they prevent the company from closing the competitiveness gap, then they are simply a way of postponing the problem.

## Where I agree
I agree that:
- the solution must be durable, not temporary;
- industrial peace has value;
- investment in future products and technologies matters;
- numbers must replace slogans;
- and if alternatives to closures can truly deliver equivalent savings, they should be considered.

I also agree with the expert’s core point: the real issue is the **fixed-cost base and capacity utilization**. That is exactly why I keep saying we need deep structural change, not marginal adjustments.

## Where I disagree
I disagree with the claim that the burden of proof somehow sits entirely with management before employee-side red lines are even tested.

The burden is on **all of us** to produce a package that works. But let’s be honest: if the workforce insists in advance that:
- no German plant can close,
- no compulsory redundancies can happen,
- and job guarantees must run through 2030,

then it is the employee side that must explain how a materially lower cost base is still achieved. So far, I have not heard that explanation in a quantified way.

“Working-time adjustments,” “voluntary exits,” “internal mobility,” and “productivity improvements” are all useful instruments. They are **not**, on their own, proof that the required savings can be achieved at the scale and speed we need.

## On the Works Council and IG Metall
You say closures and forced redundancies are a threat. I say they are a consequence we must be willing to consider if the economics require it.

You keep asking for a plant-by-plant savings bridge. Fine — I support that, and I would welcome an independent model. But I want to be equally clear: unless that model shows that a no-closure package can deliver the same durable structural effect, then the answer cannot be “therefore no closures ever.” That would be an evasion, not a solution.

Your proposed no-closure packages may reduce pain, but if they leave us with:
- excess capacity,
- a still-uncompetitive labour cost base,
- and weak margins,

then they are not enough. We cannot keep paying for underutilization and call it social responsibility.

## On Lower Saxony
I respect that you want to defend the industrial base in the region. But blocking power is not a substitute for viability.

If the state uses its position to rule out necessary restructuring, then it is not protecting jobs — it is protecting a status quo that may become unsustainable. That would eventually hurt the region more, not less.

What I can support is a package that preserves viable German production **because** it delivers real savings and restores competitiveness — not because it avoids hard decisions.

## On the controlling owners and investors
I agree with the owners and capital markets on the core principle: we need material and durable competitiveness gains. I also agree that a one-sided deal would be unstable and value-destructive.

Where I push back is this: we must not confuse “industrial peace” with “industrial inertia.” A deal that avoids conflict by leaving the cost base structurally impaired is not peace; it is delayed deterioration.

I also agree that alternatives to closures deserve a fair test. But that test has to be hard-nosed. If the alternative package does not close the gap, then it is not an equivalent substitute.

## On the expert assessment
The expert was right to say a no-closure solution is **possible only if the savings requirement is moderate enough and the package includes real labour-cost relief, simplification, and capacity reduction**.

That is the key phrase: **capacity reduction**.

If the employee side means by “capacity adjustment” that we reduce some shifts, move some volume around, and hope for the best while every site remains intact, then that will not be sufficient. If the market is shrinking and the cost base is too high, then we need a more decisive structural correction.

And yes, the expert is also right that voluntary exits are preferable to compulsory redundancies **if** they are sufficient. But if they are not sufficient, then refusing to discuss harder measures in advance is not responsible management.

## What I am prepared to do
I am prepared to move immediately to a **fully quantified comparison of scenarios**. That is the right next step.

But let me be equally clear about the scenarios I want compared:

1. **Hard restructuring scenario**
   - plant closure(s) if required
   - deeper job reductions
   - stronger structural cost removal
   - highest certainty of closing the gap

2. **Negotiated alternative**
   - no immediate closures
   - capacity reduction
   - working-time changes
   - labour-cost relief
   - voluntary exits
   - productivity improvements

3. **Hybrid scenario**
   - conditional site protections
   - binding savings milestones
   - targeted footprint adjustment
   - investment commitments tied to real delivery

That comparison must include:
- fixed-cost savings,
- utilization impact,
- headcount effects,
- implementation risk,
- and the cost of conflict if industrial peace breaks down.

## My minimum requirement
I will not accept a deal that:
- leaves the company structurally uncompetitive,
- preserves excess capacity under a political label,
- or kicks the margin problem down the road to 2030.

A real settlement must deliver:
1. a **substantial and durable** reduction in structural cost,
2. a **meaningful reduction in capacity**,
3. a **material labour-cost contribution**,
4. and a credible path back to competitive returns.

If the no-closure package can do that, I will examine it seriously. If it cannot, then closures and stronger workforce reductions remain on the table because the company’s future comes first.

## My position, plainly
I am not here to agree to a compromise for its own sake. I am here to restore Volkswagen’s competitiveness.

That means I will not accept a cosmetic deal, and I will not accept a package that protects every existing structure while expecting the company to fund its future out of weak margins.

So yes — bring the plant-level model. Bring the numbers. Bring the full scenario comparison.

But let’s stop pretending that “no closures” is a plan unless it can prove, in hard economic terms, that it fixes the problem as deeply and durably as the company needs. If it cannot, then it is not an answer.
