We agree on the diagnosis: Volkswagen has a structural problem, not a temporary one. We also agree that a durable solution must protect the company’s future and its ability to invest. That is not where the dispute lies.

The dispute is whether the burden is to be solved by **destroying sites and forcing redundancies**, or by a **fair restructuring that shares the pain**.

Let me be equally clear: **IG Metall does not accept German plant closures or compulsory redundancies.** Not as a starting point, not as a bargaining chip, and not as a “technical option” that can be activated later if management runs out of imagination. Those measures shift the entire crisis onto the workforce and would damage trust, industrial peace, and the collective bargaining framework.

## Where I agree
I agree with the expert and with parts of management on several points:
- the company needs real, durable savings;
- the cost base and capacity situation must change;
- labour can and should contribute if the package is fair;
- product and investment commitments matter;
- and numbers have to replace slogans.

I also agree with the moderator that we need **fully specified scenarios**. That is the right next step.

## Where I disagree
I disagree with the repeated implication that closures and forced layoffs are the “economically admissible” fallback that must remain open by default.

The expert says a no-closure solution is “possibly” viable if the required savings are moderate and the package includes labor-cost relief, simplification, and capacity reduction. That is precisely the point: **you have not shown that those conditions are impossible**. So closures are not proven necessary. They are being kept on the table as pressure.

That is not a neutral position. That is a threat.

I also disagree with management’s framing that employee red lines are somehow unrealistic because they come first. No — the burden of proof lies with the side that wants irreversible measures. If management claims alternatives are insufficient, then management must prove that with a transparent cost bridge. Until then, “closures may be needed” is not evidence.

## On the expert’s plant-level argument
The expert’s plant-level analysis is useful, but it still does not justify closures as inevitable.

What it does show is:
- the real issue is fixed-cost base and utilization;
- labor-cost relief, simplification, and capacity reduction can materially help;
- voluntary exits are slower but less disruptive;
- compulsory redundancies are faster but carry high conflict and skill-risk;
- and the decisive variable is the size of the gap.

That means the question is not “closures or nothing.” The question is whether management is willing to pursue a serious package of:
- working-time flexibility,
- temporary wage restraint,
- internal mobility,
- voluntary exits,
- shift reductions,
- line consolidation where needed **without closing sites**,
- and product allocation changes.

That is where the real bargaining begins.

## On management
Management says it wants flexibility first. We say no: **present the quantified alternative first**.

If you want to claim that all German sites must remain open only at the cost of unacceptably high savings shortfalls, then show:
- the plant-by-plant fixed-cost bridge,
- the capacity-utilization assumptions,
- the savings from labor-cost relief,
- the effect of voluntary exits,
- and the precise point at which you say the no-closure scenario fails.

Without that, you are not negotiating; you are asking the workforce to accept the threat of closures in advance.

And let me be clear: if management presents a credible package that keeps all German sites open, excludes compulsory redundancies, and delivers fair savings with management and owner participation, we will negotiate seriously. But we will not negotiate under the gun of plant shutdowns.

## On the controlling owners and investors
You keep talking about value, risk-adjusted NPV, and competitiveness. Fine. Then understand this: **prolonged industrial conflict is not value-neutral**.

A deal that:
- breaks trust,
- triggers strikes,
- destroys co-determination legitimacy,
- and forces employees to bear all the pain

is not a stable value solution. It may look efficient on paper and expensive in reality.

If you want durable value, back a shared-sacrifice package instead of assuming that workforce pressure is the cheapest path.

## On Lower Saxony
I welcome your rejection of closures and compulsory redundancies. But that cannot remain a political statement only. If the state is serious, it must use its position to prevent a one-sided solution and insist on a genuine negotiated package.

That said, I also hear your point that the economics must be sustainable. We are not asking for a fake deal. We are asking for a fair one.

## What we are prepared to put on the table
We remain prepared to discuss:
- working-time adjustments,
- temporary labor-cost contributions,
- productivity improvements,
- voluntary exits,
- internal transfers and retraining,
- and capacity reductions that do **not** require plant closures.

We can discuss wage moderation and other collective concessions if, and only if, they are paired with:
1. **No compulsory redundancies**
2. **No German plant closures**
3. **Binding site and job protection**
4. **Management and owner contributions**
5. **Credible investment commitments**

That is the framework for a durable settlement.

## What is not on the table
Not on the table:
- forced layoffs,
- closures disguised as “footprint reduction,”
- hollowing out sites until closure becomes a foregone conclusion,
- or using “competitiveness” as a one-way demand on employees.

## On the next step
We support the moderator’s call for **three fully costed scenarios**. But they must be built on a common template and not designed to smuggle in management’s preferred outcome.

I would propose the following structure:

1. **No-closure / no-forced-layoff package**  
   - all German plants remain open  
   - voluntary exits only  
   - working-time flexibility  
   - wage and cost contribution from labor  
   - management/owner contribution  
   - investment commitments  
   - quantified savings and utilization assumptions

2. **Hybrid package without closures**  
   - site-preserving capacity reductions  
   - line reallocation and simplification  
   - no compulsory redundancies  
   - stronger management productivity commitments  
   - binding review only if jointly agreed targets fail

3. **Management closure scenario**  
   - must be fully quantified, including social, industrial, and conflict costs  
   - not treated as the default, but as the benchmark management wants to justify

If management believes the first two cannot work, then it should be able to prove it. If it cannot prove it, then closures are not a responsible basis for settlement.

## My bottom line
IG Metall is not blocking restructuring. We are blocking **one-sided restructuring**.

We will support a serious, shared-sacrifice settlement that:
- protects jobs,
- protects sites,
- protects the collective wage framework,
- and contributes fairly to competitiveness.

We will not support a solution that saves the balance sheet by making the workforce absorb the crisis alone.
