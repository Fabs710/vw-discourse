Let me be equally direct.

I agree with one central point from everyone here: Volkswagen’s problem is structural, and a cosmetic deal will not fix it. I also agree that we need quantified scenarios, not slogans, and that industrial peace has value. But I do **not** accept the employee-side attempt to turn “no plant closures” and “no compulsory redundancies” into absolute preconditions. If those preconditions prevent the company from reaching a materially lower cost base, then they are not a solution. They are a veto.

## Where I agree
I agree that:
- this is not a cyclical dip;
- we need durable savings, not a temporary patch;
- voluntary exits, working-time flexibility, productivity gains, and simplification all matter;
- investment in future products and technology must be protected;
- and any serious discussion must be plant-by-plant and scenario-based.

That is all correct.

## Where I disagree
I disagree with the claim from the Works Council and IG Metall that the burden of proof lies entirely with management before closures or compulsory redundancies can even remain on the table.

The burden is on **all of us** to produce a package that actually works. But if the employee side insists in advance that:
- no German plant may close,
- no compulsory redundancies may occur,
- and job guarantees must run through 2030,

then it is the employee side that must show how a structurally uncompetitive cost base is still brought into line. So far, I have not seen that proof.

“Working-time adjustments,” “internal mobility,” “voluntary exits,” and “productivity commitments” are useful instruments. They are **not** proof that the required savings can be achieved at the scale and speed this company needs.

## On the Works Council and IG Metall
You say closures and forced redundancies are threats. I say they are instruments that must remain available if the economics require them. That is not posturing. It is basic restructuring reality.

You keep demanding a plant-by-plant savings bridge. I support that. In fact, I insist on it. But let me be clear: unless that analysis shows that a no-closure package can deliver a **durable** structural effect, then the answer cannot be “therefore no closures ever.” That would be evasion, not responsibility.

Your proposed packages may reduce pain, but if they leave:
- excess capacity,
- an still-uncompetitive labour cost base,
- weak margins,
- and underutilized plants,

then they are not sufficient. They simply delay the reckoning.

And one more point: “capacity reduction” is not a harmless phrase if it means hollowing out sites until closure becomes inevitable later. If a plant cannot sustain itself economically, preserving the shell is not a victory.

## On Lower Saxony
I respect your role as an anchor shareholder and your concern for the regional industrial base. But political blocking power is not a substitute for economic viability.

If the state uses its influence to rule out necessary restructuring, then it is not protecting jobs — it is protecting a status quo that may become more damaging over time. That would hurt the region, the supplier network, and the workforce eventually.

What I can support is a package that keeps viable German production because it delivers real savings and restores competitiveness — not because it avoids hard decisions.

## On the controlling owners and investors
I agree with the owners and investors on the core principle: Volkswagen needs material and durable competitiveness gains. A one-sided compromise that preserves the current footprint and leaves margins impaired would destroy value.

But I also caution against thinking that aggressive restructuring is automatically value-maximizing if it triggers prolonged industrial conflict. Value is not created by force alone. Still, the answer to that is not to freeze the footprint. It is to build a package robust enough to withstand conflict and still deliver the economics.

## On the expert analysis
The expert’s point is the right one: a no-closure solution is **possible only if** the savings requirement is moderate enough and the package includes real labor-cost relief, simplification, and capacity reduction.

That is exactly the issue: **capacity reduction**.

If “no closures” simply means shifting some volume around, trimming some shifts, and preserving every site regardless of utilization, that will not be enough if the market remains weak. If the company needs a deep reset of German cost and capacity, then keeping every site open may be economically impossible unless there are unusually large concessions or a strong demand recovery.

## What I want to see next
I support the call for a common scenario model. But I want it built honestly, not as a vehicle to smuggle in preferred conclusions.

We should compare three scenarios on the same basis:
1. **Hard restructuring**
   - including possible closures where required
   - full structural cost removal
   - highest certainty of closing the gap

2. **Negotiated no-closure package**
   - all sites remain open
   - working-time changes
   - labour-cost relief
   - voluntary exits
   - productivity and simplification measures

3. **Hybrid conditional package**
   - strong site protections initially
   - binding savings milestones
   - targeted footprint adjustment
   - review mechanism if targets are missed

And each scenario must show:
- fixed-cost savings,
- utilization impact,
- headcount effects,
- one-time restructuring costs,
- implementation risk,
- conflict cost,
- and margin recovery timeline.

## My minimum requirement
I will not accept a deal that:
- leaves the company structurally uncompetitive,
- preserves excess capacity under a political label,
- or kicks the margin problem down the road to 2030.

A real settlement must deliver:
1. a substantial and durable reduction in structural cost,
2. a meaningful reduction in capacity,
3. a material labour-cost contribution,
4. and a credible path back to competitive returns.

If the no-closure package can do that, I will examine it seriously. If it cannot, then closures and stronger workforce reductions remain on the table because the company’s future comes first.

## Bottom line
I am not here to preserve the status quo. I am here to restore Volkswagen’s competitiveness.

So I will not sign up to a cosmetic deal, and I will not pretend that “no closures” is a plan unless it can prove, in hard economic terms, that it solves the problem as deeply and durably as the company requires.
