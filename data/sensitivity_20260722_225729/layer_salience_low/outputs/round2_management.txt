Let me respond very directly to what has been said.

First, I acknowledge the one point of common ground: everyone here accepts that Volkswagen faces a structural problem and that a cosmetic deal is not enough. That is correct. The company needs a durable solution, and it needs one that restores competitiveness, not just one that delays pain.

But I have to be equally clear: the workforce position presented here is still **not sufficient** to solve the crisis.

### Where I agree
I agree that:
- the solution must be durable, not temporary;
- investment in future products and technology matters;
- industrial peace has value;
- negotiated change is preferable to disorder;
- and labour can contribute if the package is serious.

### Where I disagree
I do **not** accept the repeated attempt to turn “no plant closures” and “no compulsory redundancies” into absolute starting points. That would pre-empt the very restructuring the company needs.

I also do **not** accept the claim that capacity reduction can be achieved in full while keeping every German plant open and excluding compulsory redundancies, without showing a credible, quantified alternative that closes the cost gap. Right now, I have not heard such a package.

Management does not need slogans about trust. It needs a structure that actually fixes:
- the cost base,
- the capacity mismatch,
- and the brand’s return-on-sales trajectory.

### To the Works Council
You say you want to keep every plant open, exclude compulsory redundancies, and preserve job guarantees through 2030. That is not a restructuring plan; it is a defensive line.

If you want to avoid closures and forced layoffs, then you need to bring forward a package that delivers equivalent economic effect. That means real, measurable savings:
- on labour costs,
- on capacity,
- on productivity,
- and on structural overhead.

If you cannot show that, then you are asking the company to preserve too much of the current structure while hoping the market and balance sheet will somehow absorb the difference. They will not.

### To IG Metall
You say closures and compulsory redundancies are unacceptable as a matter of principle. I understand the bargaining position, but principles do not fix uncompetitive factories.

You also ask for transparency on the numbers. Fair enough. The numbers are clear enough: German factory costs are materially above target, and the company is operating with too much capacity for the market reality we face. That is why we need structural action, not just temporary restraint.

If you want a real negotiation, then do not use “no closures ever” as a precondition. Put forward a package that materially reduces the cost base and capacity footprint. If it can do that without closures, I will consider it. But it must be real.

### To Lower Saxony
I appreciate that you recognise the seriousness of the situation. But I need to say this plainly: political support is not the same as economic viability.

A blocking position that prevents necessary restructuring would be damaging to the company and, ultimately, to the workforce as well. If the state wants to protect employment, it must help secure a viable industrial base — not preserve every existing structure regardless of competitiveness.

### To the controlling owners and investors
I agree with your core point: we cannot preserve overcapacity and weak margins and still expect long-term value creation. Any solution must materially improve competitiveness.

Where I want to be careful is this: investment commitments cannot become a substitute for restructuring. We need both. Capital for future products is only defensible if the underlying cost base is being fixed. Otherwise, we are funding tomorrow with a structurally uncompetitive business today.

### What I am prepared to discuss
I am still open on the **route**, but not on the **need** for major structural change.

That means I will consider:
- capacity reductions,
- working-time adjustments,
- productivity measures,
- negotiated workforce contributions,
- and investment commitments tied to real restructuring.

But I will not agree to a package that:
- leaves the cost base fundamentally uncompetitive,
- preserves excess capacity,
- or relies on vague promises instead of enforceable change.

### My minimum acceptable outcome
At minimum, any deal must deliver:
1. a **substantial and durable** reduction in structural costs,
2. a **meaningful reduction in capacity**,
3. a **material labour-cost contribution**,
4. and a credible path to restore margins and fund future investment.

If the employee side wants to avoid closures and compulsory redundancies, then the burden is on them to show how those savings are achieved in full. Until then, closures remain on the table because I cannot support a deal that fails to solve the underlying problem.

If needed, I would now suggest we move to **two or three fully specified scenarios** and test them honestly against the same criteria: cost reduction, capacity reduction, employment impact, site impact, and implementability. Without that, we are just repeating positions.
