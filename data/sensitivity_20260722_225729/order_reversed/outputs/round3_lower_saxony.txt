We are now at the point where the debate must become concrete, and I will be equally concrete.

## Where I agree

I agree with management, the controlling owners, and investors on one central point: **Volkswagen needs a durable restructuring, not a cosmetic compromise**. The expert has confirmed what we already knew: a serious no-closure package can create meaningful savings, but only if it is deep, enforceable, and sustained.

I also agree that:
- the current German cost base is too high,
- capacity must come down,
- and a deal that merely postpones the problem would be unacceptable.

So I am **not** defending the status quo.

## Where I disagree

I do **not** accept the implication that plant closures are the natural or preferred answer.

To management and investors: you are right to demand numbers, but wrong if you treat closures as the default benchmark before the alternative has truly been tested. The expert has shown that a serious no-closure package can plausibly reach the **€2.5bn to €4bn** range annually. That is not trivial. That is a real restructuring if it is done properly.

To the controlling owners: I share your concern about value destruction through confrontation. But closure-heavy restructuring can also destroy value through strikes, political backlash, loss of trust, and a weakened industrial base. You cannot protect long-term value by breaking the social settlement that makes Volkswagen work.

## My bottom line on the options

### 1) No-closure first, with automatic review trigger
This is the only route I see as potentially credible at this stage — but only if it is truly binding.

I would support:
- a **time-limited first phase**,
- clearly quantified annual savings targets,
- real labor-cost contributions,
- working-time and flexibility changes,
- productivity measures,
- management overhead cuts,
- and a fixed review point.

But I need to be blunt: the fallback cannot be a vague threat. If targets are missed, there must be a **credible, pre-agreed second phase**. That does not mean automatic closures tomorrow, but it does mean the company cannot endlessly revisit the same discussion.

### 2) No compulsory redundancies plus voluntary footprint adjustment
This is acceptable in principle, and it is the most realistic path to keep the social peace.

I can support:
- voluntary exits,
- early retirement,
- redeployment,
- internal transfers,
- and negotiated site-specific capacity reductions.

But I will not support a plan that turns “voluntary” into a slow-motion shutdown by attrition without a real industrial future for the sites in Lower Saxony. If capacity is reduced, the remaining sites need products, investment, and a future.

### 3) Shared-burden package
Yes, burden-sharing must include management and owners. I support:
- overhead discipline,
- limits on executive excess,
- and a clear commitment that the workforce is not the only one paying.

But let us be honest: symbolic restraint at the top does not solve a structural production problem. Management and owner contributions are necessary for fairness and legitimacy, but they do not replace operational restructuring.

## What I can support

I can support a package if it contains all of the following:
- **quantified annual savings**
- **durable labor-cost contributions**
- **real capacity reduction without automatic closures**
- **headcount reduction through attrition, redeployment, and voluntary exits**
- **management overhead cuts**
- **binding investment commitments for German sites**
- **a fixed review point with a credible fallback**

That is a serious compromise. It is also the minimum consistent with protecting Volkswagen as a regional anchor.

## What I will not support

I will not support:
- a closure-first strategy presented as inevitability,
- a package that guts employment in Lower Saxony,
- a settlement that weakens co-determination or the state’s blocking role,
- or a deal that leaves the company structurally uncompetitive while claiming victory.

Those outcomes would be unacceptable.

## On the red lines of the employee side

I understand the insistence on avoiding forced redundancies and preserving sites. Those concerns are legitimate. But they must be matched by real concessions that are durable enough to close the cost gap.

If the employee side wants a site-preserving solution, then it must be willing to accept:
- substantial productivity gains,
- meaningful labor-cost moderation,
- flexibility changes,
- and capacity reductions that are economically real, not cosmetic.

That is the price of preserving the industrial base.

## My political and governance position

Lower Saxony will use its rights responsibly, but not passively. I will not allow the state’s role to be sidestepped, and I will not accept a restructuring that treats co-determination as an obstacle to be managed around.

Volkswagen’s legitimacy depends on being both:
- competitive, and
- socially anchored.

If either side is sacrificed, the company weakens.

## Final position

I am prepared to back a **serious no-closure-first restructuring** if it is:
- quantified,
- enforceable,
- large enough,
- and tied to a real review mechanism.

I am **not** prepared to endorse closures as the starting point, and I am certainly not prepared to accept mass job losses in Lower Saxony as the price of a quick fix.

The burden is now on management and investors to prove that any alternative to closures is insufficient. Until then, I will continue to push for a restructuring that protects jobs and sites while restoring competitiveness.
