I will be direct.

Lower Saxony is **not** prepared to endorse a package that makes plant closures the default answer, nor a settlement that turns “restructuring” into a code word for gutting employment in Germany. I also will not support any approach that sidelines the state’s co-determination role or weakens the governance balance that is part of Volkswagen’s legitimacy.

## Where I agree with the others

### With management
You are right about one thing: Volkswagen needs a **real, durable restructuring**.  
A cosmetic deal, or a short-lived labor truce, would simply postpone the problem.

### With investors and the controlling owners
You are right that the package must be **quantified, enforceable, and durable**.  
And you are right that a prolonged conflict would destroy value.

### With the expert
The expert’s assessment is important: a no-closure package is not automatically weak. It can be economically meaningful if it is deep enough and properly locked in. That matters.

## Where I disagree

### 1) On closures as the benchmark of seriousness
I do **not** accept that plant closures should be treated as the standard against which all other options are measured.

The evidence discussed here supports a different conclusion: a serious no-closure package can plausibly deliver **multi-billion-euro annual savings**. That is not symbolic. That is restructuring.

So when management or investors imply that closures are the “real” solution and everything else is second-best by definition, I disagree. That is too simplistic and too risky.

### 2) On compulsory redundancies
I do **not** accept forced layoffs as a necessary starting point.

Volkswagen has other levers:
- working-time and shift changes,
- voluntary exits,
- early retirement,
- internal transfers,
- redeployment,
- productivity gains,
- management overhead cuts,
- and complexity reduction.

Those must be exhausted seriously before anyone claims there is no alternative.

### 3) On the fallback logic
I also do **not** accept a “review” mechanism that is really just a hidden path to closures.

If there is to be a review, it must be:
- date-certain,
- metric-driven,
- transparent,
- and genuinely tied to delivery.

But the fallback cannot be “we did not fully get our way, so now we close plants.” That would make the first phase a trap, not a settlement.

## My position on the core question

The question is not whether Volkswagen needs savings. It does.  
The question is whether those savings can be achieved **without damaging the regional industrial base and without breaking the social partnership model** that has sustained the company.

My answer is: **yes, they can — but only if the package is serious and balanced.**

That means:
- real labor-cost contribution,
- real capacity reduction,
- real headcount adjustment through voluntary and internal measures,
- real management contribution,
- and binding investment commitments for German sites.

## What I can support

I can support a **time-limited no-closure-first settlement** if it includes all of the following:

- a **quantified annual savings target**
- a **binding cap on compulsory redundancies**
- **site protection**
- **capacity reduction without plant closures**
- **management overhead cuts**
- **worker flexibility and productivity measures**
- **binding investment commitments**
- **a strict review point**

That is the kind of package that protects Volkswagen as a regional anchor while still restoring competitiveness.

## What I will not support

I will not support:
- a closure-led restructuring as the starting point,
- a package that weakens co-determination,
- a settlement that offloads the adjustment mainly onto workers,
- or a “solution” that creates long-term political and economic damage in Lower Saxony.

## Final position

So let me be clear: Lower Saxony will continue to use its governance rights to defend a **site-preserving, no-forced-layoff solution**.

That is not a refusal to restructure. It is a refusal to sacrifice the company’s social and regional foundation for the illusion of a quick fix.

If management, investors, and the controlling owners want my support, they must show that the no-closure path is not just politically easier, but economically robust enough to secure Volkswagen’s future. Until then, I remain opposed to closures as the default route.
