We are now at the point where rhetoric has to give way to a decision.

## Where I agree

I agree with management and the investors on the core economic point: **Volkswagen needs a restructuring that is durable, measurable, and large enough to matter**. I also agree with Lower Saxony and the employee side that **an open-ended conflict would destroy value**. None of us benefits from a prolonged strike, reputational damage, or a breakdown in governance.

I also accept the expert’s conclusion that **a no-closure package can be economically meaningful**. That matters. It means closures are **not** the only conceivable path to improvement.

## Where I disagree

### To IG Metall and the Works Council
Your red lines are still too absolute.

A package with:
- no closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- no erosion of guarantees,
- and preserved site structure,

**cannot automatically be presumed sufficient** just because it is politically more comfortable. If those constraints prevent Volkswagen from restoring margins, then they are not a solution; they are a delay.

I do not accept a position that says: “we will only support restructuring if it leaves the structure largely untouched.” That would risk permanent value erosion.

### To Lower Saxony
I respect the need to preserve legitimacy and the regional industrial base. But legitimacy is not preserved by defending every site regardless of economics. If the company remains structurally uncompetitive, the damage will come back through weaker investment, lower margins, and eventually a weaker footprint anyway.

So I will not support a package that protects political comfort at the expense of enterprise value.

### To management
I support firmness, but not empty maximalism. If you want closure authority to be credible, you must show that the non-closure path has been fully tested and found insufficient. Otherwise, closure talk becomes leverage theater. That weakens governance and hardens resistance.

## My position on the compromise options

### 1) No-closure first, with automatic review trigger
This is the only option I see as potentially workable — but only if the trigger is real.

I would support a **time-limited, quantified first phase** with:
- specific annual savings targets,
- capacity reduction,
- labor-cost adjustments,
- and a fixed review point.

But I want to be very clear: if the targets are missed, the fallback cannot be a new round of talking. It must be a **credible footprint reduction pathway**. Automatic review must mean something.

### 2) No compulsory redundancies plus voluntary footprint adjustment
This is acceptable **only if it delivers actual structural savings**. Voluntary exits, early retirement, redeployment, and negotiated downsizing can be part of a serious package. They are not enough if they simply soften the optics while the economic problem remains.

### 3) Shared-burden package
Yes, management and owners should contribute through overhead discipline and governance restraint. That is fair.

But I will say plainly: **symbolic burden-sharing does not substitute for operational restructuring**. Wage restraint, working-time flexibility, and productivity gains must be real and durable. Management and owner restraint is supportive, not decisive.

## What I would support

I would support a negotiated package if it contains all of the following:

- **Quantified annual savings**
- **Durable labor-cost contribution**
- **Real capacity reduction**
- **Headcount reduction through attrition, redeployment, and voluntary exits**
- **Management overhead cuts**
- **A binding review point**
- **A credible fallback to footprint reduction if targets are missed**

That is the minimum standard for a responsible settlement.

## What I will not support

I will not support:
- a hollow no-closure agreement that preserves excess structure,
- a package whose savings are temporary or reversible,
- or a settlement that leaves Volkswagen structurally uncompetitive in the name of avoiding difficult choices.

## My bottom line

I am still open to a **serious no-closure-first settlement**, but only if it is:
- large enough,
- durable enough,
- and enforceable enough.

If the employee side insists that closures and compulsory redundancies are impossible under any circumstances, then it is asking the owners to accept whatever economic outcome follows from that stance. I am not prepared to do that.

So the burden now is on those opposing closures to show, in numbers, that their alternative is **at least close enough to the economics of footprint reduction**. If they cannot, then the fallback has to remain on the table.
