Yes — on the economics, the clearest answer is:

**A no-closure package is only sufficient if it achieves a very large, durable, and enforceable fixed-cost reduction.**  
If it does not, then footprint reduction becomes economically hard to avoid.

## Practical threshold

For a company like Volkswagen, I would treat a no-closure settlement as potentially credible if it delivers:

- **annualized savings in the multi-billion-euro range**
- **material headcount reduction**
- **real capacity/utilization improvement**
- **permanent labor-cost relief**
- **overhead and complexity cuts**
- **high confidence of delivery, not just aspiration**

In round terms, the discussion needs to be around **roughly €3bn per year, or at least the upper end of a €2.5bn–€4bn-type range**, if the alternative is closure-led restructuring.

## Why that threshold matters

A plant-closure scenario typically produces savings from several sources at once:

- labor cost removal
- plant fixed overhead removal
- logistics simplification
- utilization gains at the remaining footprint
- management and maintenance simplification

A no-closure package can substitute for some of that, but only if it includes:

- substantial workforce reduction through attrition / voluntary exits / retirement
- shift and working-time reductions
- lower guarantees / wage moderation / allowance reform
- production complexity reduction
- overhead cuts outside the plants

If it relies mainly on temporary wage restraint, it will fall short.

## When no-closure stops being enough

Economically, the no-closure route stops being sufficient when one or more of these is true:

1. **Savings are temporary rather than structural**
2. **Headcount reduction is too small to change the cost base**
3. **Capacity remains too underutilized**
4. **Overhead is not materially reduced**
5. **Concessions are easily reversible**
6. **There is no credible enforcement mechanism**

At that point, management may still prefer the no-closure deal for industrial-relations reasons, but from a restructuring-finance standpoint it is not equivalent to footprint reduction.

## Closest rigorous test

The right test is not “closures vs no closures” in the abstract. It is:

**Does the no-closure package deliver enough permanent fixed-cost removal, plus enough operational simplification, to match the net economics of a closure-led plan after accounting for restructuring charges and execution risk?**

If yes, it can be justified.  
If no, footprint reduction is the more credible economic solution.

## What data would be needed to prove it

To answer with precision, I would want plant-by-plant and function-by-function data on:

- current fixed and variable cost base
- utilization by site
- labor productivity by site
- headcount by function
- overhead allocation
- severance / restructuring charges
- logistics impacts
- duration and reversibility of concessions

Without that, any exact figure is still an estimate.

## Bottom line

**Economically, no-closure can work — but only at the upper end of the savings debate and only if it creates real structural change.**  
If the package comes in materially below that, it is not a true substitute for footprint reduction.
