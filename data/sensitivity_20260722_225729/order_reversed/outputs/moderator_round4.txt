### Core impasse
The dispute is now concentrated on one central issue:

**Whether Volkswagen can achieve a sufficiently large, durable, and enforceable cost reset without plant closures or compulsory redundancies.**

The fracture is not over the need for restructuring; nearly everyone accepts that. The impasse is over **what counts as enough** and **what form of adjustment is still compatible with the red lines being asserted**.

More specifically, the stalemate sits at the intersection of:
- **employee-side red lines**: no closures, no compulsory redundancies, no erosion of guarantees;
- **management / investor demands**: credible, structural, multi-billion-euro annual savings with real capacity and overhead reduction;
- **Lower Saxony’s governance priority**: site preservation and regional stability;
- **owners’ preference**: avoid a conflict that destroys value, but do not accept a cosmetic settlement.

The unresolved question is not whether savings are needed, but **whether the no-closure route can be made economically equivalent enough to a footprint-reduction scenario**.

---

### Possible compromise positions
Below are three facilitator-style compromise structures that could bridge the most conflicting positions:

#### 1) Time-limited no-closure first phase with hard trigger metrics
A first phase could preserve all sites and avoid compulsory redundancies for a fixed period, but only if it includes:
- date-certain review,
- plant-by-plant and function-by-function savings targets,
- headcount and capacity milestones,
- binding transparency on delivery,
- and a pre-agreed fallback if targets are missed.

This is the most direct bridge between the employee red lines and the investor/management demand for enforceability.

#### 2) No compulsory redundancies, but with deeper structural adjustment
This would preserve the no-layoff principle while still requiring material change through:
- voluntary exits,
- early retirement,
- redeployment,
- shift reductions,
- utilization increases,
- overhead cuts,
- and product / complexity simplification.

The key condition is that the package must be large enough to be financially meaningful, not merely symbolic.

#### 3) Shared-burden package with binding management and owner contributions
A broader settlement could combine labor-side flexibility with:
- executive and overhead restraint,
- procurement / complexity cuts,
- investment commitments for German sites,
- and governance assurances that the burden is not placed only on workers.

This would not replace operational restructuring, but it could make a no-closure deal more politically and socially durable.

### Pointed question to each stakeholder

#### Institutional Investors
**What minimum annualized, recurring savings threshold would you regard as sufficient to support a no-closure settlement, even if the package falls short of full footprint reduction?**

#### Expert
**What specific evidence set would you require to conclude that a no-closure package is close enough to a closure-led scenario to be financially credible?**

#### Controlling Owners
**What governance commitment are you prepared to make to support a no-closure-first settlement if management and labor reach quantified, enforceable targets?**

#### Lower Saxony
**Would you support a time-limited no-closure package if it included measurable capacity reduction, binding investment commitments, and a credible fallback mechanism?**

#### Group & Brand Management
**What exact combination of labor, overhead, and capacity measures would you accept as sufficient to rule out closures as necessary?**

#### IG Metall
**What quantified employee-side contribution would you be prepared to lock in to prove that a no-closure package is structurally strong, not just politically defensive?**

#### Works Council
**What specific site-level adjustment measures would you accept if they preserved plant continuity but still materially reduced fixed costs and complexity?**

#### Expert 2
**At what point do voluntary exits, redeployment, and working-time changes become large enough to substitute for footprint reduction in a Volkswagen-scale restructuring?**
