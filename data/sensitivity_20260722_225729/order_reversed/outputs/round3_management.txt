Let me be equally direct.

I agree on one point with everyone at this table: **Volkswagen needs a durable restructuring, not a cosmetic truce**. The expert has now confirmed that a no-closure package can produce meaningful savings — but also that it has a ceiling. That is exactly why management cannot accept the current employee-side red lines as if they were neutral inputs. They are not. They materially limit the solution.

## Where I agree
- I agree with investors: **the outcome must restore margins with certainty**.
- I agree with the controlling owners: **a conflict-driven collapse would destroy value**.
- I agree with Lower Saxony: **legitimacy matters**, and this cannot become a governance crisis.
- I agree with IG Metall and the Works Council on one thing: **there is room for a serious negotiated alternative**.

But agreement on the need for restructuring does **not** mean agreement on the scale of sacrifice required.

## Where I disagree
### To IG Metall and the Works Council
Your position still amounts to:  
- no closures,  
- no compulsory redundancies,  
- no erosion of guarantees,  
- no real footprint reduction,  
while still demanding that the company become competitive again.

That is not a complete restructuring concept. It is a demand that management solve a structural cost problem **without using the structural levers that actually remove cost**.

The expert’s range is helpful, but it does **not** rescue your argument. Yes, a no-closure package may reach roughly **€2.5bn to €4bn annually** if it is serious. But management’s problem is not a theoretical spreadsheet exercise. The problem is whether Volkswagen can restore a **durable** cost base and margin structure against shrinking demand and intense competition.

If the package does not go to the upper end of the range, and if it does not include real capacity rationalization and enforceable labor-cost relief, then it is not enough.

### To Lower Saxony
I respect the need to preserve regional legitimacy. But you are treating site preservation as if it were a substitute for economics. It is not.

If the German base remains structurally too expensive, then the very industrial anchor you want to protect will be weakened over time by underinvestment, lower returns, and loss of competitiveness. Protecting every site regardless of economics is not stewardship; it is deferral.

### To investors and owners
I hear the call for certainty. That is reasonable. But certainty without economic depth is useless. We do not need a vague concession package. We need a **measurable, durable contribution** that actually shifts the cost curve.

## What management requires
Let me state this plainly.

A no-closure route is acceptable **only if** it delivers all of the following:
1. **Permanent labor-cost reduction**
2. **Meaningful capacity reduction**
3. **Headcount reduction through attrition, redeployment, and voluntary exits**
4. **Productivity and overhead savings**
5. **A timetable that produces visible annual run-rate savings quickly**
6. **A built-in fallback if targets are missed**

If those elements are not present, then management will continue to view footprint reduction, including closures, as necessary instruments.

## My answer to the compromise options
### 1) No-closure first with an automatic review trigger
This can only work if the trigger is real, not ceremonial.

That means:
- date-certain review,
- hard metrics,
- and a pre-agreed second phase that can include deeper footprint reduction if delivery falls short.

I will not accept a structure where the review point becomes another round of evasive bargaining.

### 2) No compulsory redundancies plus voluntary footprint adjustment
This is acceptable **in principle**, but only if the voluntary measures are large enough to matter. If “voluntary” just means slow attrition and disguised delay, then it does not solve the problem.

### 3) Shared-burden package
Yes, management and owners should contribute through overhead discipline and governance restraint. But let’s be honest: executive restraint does not close a cost gap in the plant network.

Burden-sharing is necessary for legitimacy. It is not sufficient for competitiveness.

## My bottom line
Management is prepared to support a **serious no-closure-first settlement** only if it is:
- quantified,
- durable,
- enforceable,
- and economically large enough to restore margins.

If the employee side continues to insist on absolute red lines that prevent real capacity and labor-cost adjustment, then it is asking the company to absorb the full burden of restructuring elsewhere. That is unacceptable.

So the burden now is on the employee side and Lower Saxony to show, in numbers, that their preferred route can close the structural gap. Not in slogans. Not in aspirations. In numbers.

If they cannot, then closures and deeper footprint measures remain on the table — not as a threat for theater, but as the necessary fallback when the alternative cannot do the job.
