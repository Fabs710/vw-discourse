I will be blunt.

## Where I agree
I agree with everyone on one point: **Volkswagen needs a real restructuring, not a cosmetic one**.  
I also agree that:
- the current German cost base is too high,
- a prolonged conflict would damage the company,
- and any solution must be **durable, quantified, and enforceable**.

So yes, there must be savings. There must be changes. And there must be implementation discipline.

## Where I fundamentally disagree
I **do not** accept the logic that this automatically leads to plant closures or compulsory redundancies.

That is where management, investors, and the controlling owners are still trying to force the debate into a false choice:
- either accept closures and layoffs,
- or accept failure.

That is not the only choice. It is simply the choice that is most convenient for those who want to shift the burden onto the workforce.

## To management
You keep saying the no-closure route only works if it reaches the upper end of the expert range. Fine. Then engage the no-closure route properly instead of treating closures as your default threat.

But let me be clear:  
**A no-closure package is not “insufficient” just because it does not include plant shutdowns.**

The expert has confirmed that a serious package can deliver **€2.5bn to €4bn annually**. That is not small. That is not symbolic. That is a serious restructuring if you actually implement it.

So if you want to argue that the no-closure route fails, you need to show:
- where the savings gap really is,
- what management has already cut,
- what overhead has been removed,
- what product and capacity decisions have been taken,
- and why worker-side measures plus company-side measures are supposedly not enough.

Do not hide behind vague “competitiveness” language while still refusing to use management’s own levers.

## To investors
You are right to insist on durability and certainty.  
But you are wrong if you think only closures can create that.

You keep treating “closure-led” restructuring as the benchmark of seriousness. That is a political reflex, not an economic law. A negotiated no-closure settlement can be durable if it includes:
- binding labor-cost contributions,
- capacity reduction through shifts and redeployment,
- productivity gains,
- management overhead cuts,
- and a strict review mechanism.

What you are still not acknowledging is the cost of destruction:
- strikes,
- loss of trust,
- loss of know-how,
- and the damage done when a company breaks its social contract.

That is real economic damage. It is not a side issue.

## To the controlling owners
You say you want value preserved. Then stop backing a strategy that openly threatens the workforce with closures and forced layoffs.

If you want a stable, valuable Volkswagen, then support a solution that preserves the company’s industrial legitimacy:
- no plant closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- no weakening of co-determination,
- no dismantling of the job-security framework.

That is how you preserve long-term value. Not by provoking a confrontation and then calling the result “discipline.”

## To Lower Saxony
I agree with you more than with the others on the principle that legitimacy matters.  
But I need to be clear: legitimacy is not preserved by polite statements alone.

If you truly believe Volkswagen should remain an industrial anchor, then use your blocking position to defend that. Not in words. In governance.

Do not let “review mechanisms” become hidden routes to closures. Do not let “footprint adjustment” become a slow-motion shutdown. If the state wants to stand for industrial stability, then it must stand against plant closures and forced layoffs.

## On the compromise options
### 1) Time-limited no-closure first phase with hard metrics
This is only acceptable if it is a **true no-closure first phase**.

That means:
- a clear savings target,
- a fixed review date,
- transparent metrics,
- and a package that is genuinely designed to work without closures.

What I will not accept is a trapdoor arrangement where the so-called “review” is really just a delayed closure decision.

If targets are met, the no-closure agreement stands.  
If management claims they are not, then management must prove that transparently and with full disclosure of its own contribution.

### 2) No compulsory redundancies plus structural adjustment
This is the only realistic staffing principle.

We are prepared to discuss:
- voluntary exits,
- early retirement,
- redeployment,
- internal transfers,
- working-time changes,
- and productivity measures.

But let me repeat: **no compulsory redundancies**.  
That red line stands.

And “structural adjustment” must not be used as code for shutting plants by attrition. Every German plant must remain open and have a future.

### 3) Shared-burden package
Yes, but burden-sharing must be real and balanced.

That means:
- management overhead cuts,
- executive restraint,
- owner-side restraint,
- procurement and complexity reduction,
- and binding investment commitments for German sites.

It does **not** mean workers take the hit while everyone else keeps strategic flexibility intact.

## What would move us
Only this:
- binding job security to 2030,
- no plant closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- meaningful capacity reduction through working-time, shifts, and redeployment,
- durable employee contribution,
- management and owner contribution,
- real co-determination in implementation.

That is a serious package. That is a responsible package. And that is the only one I will defend.

## What will not move us
This will not move us:
- closure threats,
- “voluntary” measures that are actually disguised layoffs,
- a review mechanism designed as a trap,
- or a one-sided adjustment that turns the workforce into the sole shock absorber.

If that is the direction, then we will resist it with everything co-determination gives us.

## My bottom line
The Works Council is **not** here to negotiate the terms of plant closures.  
We are here to prevent them.

Volkswagen can be restructured without closing German plants and without compulsory redundancies. The expert has not proven otherwise. Management and investors have not proven otherwise. They have only shown that they prefer the harsher route because it is simpler for them.

I reject that route.
