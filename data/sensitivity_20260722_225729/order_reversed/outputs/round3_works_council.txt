We have heard enough rhetoric. Now I will speak plainly for the workforce.

## To management
You keep saying the no-closure route is only acceptable if it lands at the upper end of the savings range. Fine. But you are still treating plant closures like a normal management instrument, and I reject that outright.

The Works Council’s position remains unchanged:

- **no plant closures**
- **no compulsory redundancies**
- **no dismantling of the job-security framework**
- **no sidelining of co-determination**

That is not an “emotional” position. It is the only responsible one if you want to preserve Volkswagen as an industrial employer in Germany.

You say the alternative must be quantified. Agreed. But then quantify it honestly:
- how much savings from management overhead?
- how much from procurement and complexity reduction?
- how much from working-time flexibility?
- how much from voluntary exits and retirement?
- how much from internal redeployment?
- how much from productivity improvements?

If you want to claim the no-closure route is insufficient, then show the calculation site by site, plant by plant. Until then, your closure talk is leverage, not necessity.

## To the investors
You keep repeating “credible margins” as if workers are the only variable in the business. They are not.

Your mistake is treating closures as the benchmark of seriousness. They are not. They are the benchmark of how quickly you can destroy trust.

The expert has already confirmed that a serious no-closure package can deliver **€2.5bn to €4bn annually**. That is not cosmetic. That is substantial restructuring. So stop pretending that anything short of closures is a fake solution.

If you want durability, then support a package that is:
- enforceable,
- quantified,
- and socially sustainable.

Because a restructuring that triggers prolonged strikes, loss of know-how, and permanent hostility is not “credible.” It is self-harm dressed up as discipline.

## To the controlling owners
You are right about one thing: confrontation destroys value.  
That is exactly why you should not back a strategy that starts with threats to close plants and break guarantees.

If you want the company to remain valuable, then use your governance power to force management into a genuine site-preserving solution. Not a cosmetic one. A real one.

Your role is not to push the workforce into accepting permanent insecurity. Your role is to ensure the company remains legitimate, investable, and industrially strong. That requires keeping German plants open and preserving co-determination. Anything else weakens the whole model.

## To Lower Saxony
You have spoken forcefully, and I agree with your core point: legitimacy matters. But I will be blunt — legitimacy is not preserved by polite statements. It is preserved by using your blocking position to stop closures.

The workforce needs more than sympathy. It needs you to act with us.

If there is a real no-closure solution, then support it fully and refuse any attempt to force plant rationalization through the back door. If you really believe Volkswagen should remain a regional anchor, then you must defend the sites, not just the principle.

## On the expert assessment
The expert’s numbers matter, but they do **not** move me off the red lines.

Why? Because the expert did not say closures are necessary. The expert said a no-closure package can produce meaningful savings, and may be sufficient depending on the size of the gap.

That means the burden is on management and investors to prove that the non-closure route cannot work. Not on workers to accept closures in advance.

And even if the savings gap were large, closure is still not an acceptable answer when the company has other levers:
- real management cuts,
- real overhead cuts,
- real complexity reduction,
- real productivity improvements,
- real labor contributions negotiated fairly,
- voluntary exits and redeployment.

The plant is not the first lever to pull. It is the last thing you threaten when you want to break resistance.

## What the Works Council will accept
We are prepared to support a serious restructuring package if it includes all of the following:

- **binding job security to 2030**
- **no compulsory redundancies**
- **no plant closures**
- **capacity adjustments through working-time, shifts, and redeployment**
- **management overhead restraint**
- **fair labor contribution from the company as a whole**
- **binding investment commitments for German sites**
- **real co-determination in shaping implementation**

That is a serious package. It is also the only one that protects both competitiveness and social peace.

## What we will not accept
We will not accept:
- closures disguised as “footprint adjustment”
- forced layoffs disguised as “efficiency”
- temporary deals that expire and leave the same threat hanging over workers
- a settlement that treats labor as the only adjustable cost
- a review mechanism that is really a trapdoor to shut sites later

If that is the direction, then I will say it again: we will resist it with everything co-determination gives us.

## My position on the compromise options

### 1) No-closure first, with automatic review trigger
This is the only route worth discussing.

But it must be a **true no-closure first phase**, not a staging post toward plant shutdowns.  
That means:
- clear targets,
- clear obligations on management,
- transparent measurement,
- and no pre-cooked conclusion that “if the numbers are not perfect, then closures happen.”

If management wants a review, then the review must also examine management’s delivery, owners’ restraint, and non-labor savings — not just workforce concessions.

### 2) No compulsory redundancies plus voluntary footprint adjustment
This is acceptable only if it does **not** become a closure by attrition.

I can accept:
- voluntary exits,
- early retirement,
- redeployment,
- internal transfers,
- and site-specific capacity reduction

if and only if every German plant remains open and has a viable future.  
If “voluntary footprint adjustment” means the slow disappearance of plants, then no.

### 3) Shared-burden package
Yes, everyone must contribute. But “everyone” means everyone:
- management,
- owners,
- overhead structures,
- procurement,
- and top-level cost discipline.

It does **not** mean the workforce sacrifices jobs while the rest of the system is spared.

## Final word
The Works Council is not here to negotiate the terms of plant closures. We are here to prevent them.

If Volkswagen wants a durable restructuring, it can have one — **without closures and without compulsory redundancies**. That is the real alternative on the table.

If management and investors refuse that, then they are choosing conflict, not necessity. And they should be under no illusion: the workforce will defend its jobs, its sites, and its rights.
