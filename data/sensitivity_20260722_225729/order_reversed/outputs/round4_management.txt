I will be equally direct.

We are now past abstract agreement. The only question that matters is whether Volkswagen can restore a competitive cost base fast enough to protect the business through 2030. If the answer is yes, good. If the answer is no, then we are wasting time.

## Where I agree
I agree with the expert, investors, owners, and Lower Saxony on one point: **we need a durable, enforceable restructuring, not a cosmetic truce**.

I also agree that:
- a prolonged strike would destroy value,
- the package must be quantified,
- and burden-sharing cannot be one-sided.

## Where I disagree

### To IG Metall and the Works Council
Your red lines are still too rigid.

You keep saying:
- no closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- no erosion of guarantees,
- no meaningful footprint reduction.

That is not a restructuring concept. That is a request to preserve the structure that produced the current cost problem.

The expert has been clear: a no-closure package may be viable, but only if it lands near the upper end of the savings range, is durable, and includes real capacity and labor-cost adjustment. I can accept that as a **test**. I do **not** accept it as proof that closures are unnecessary.

You are also asking us to trust that productivity gains and voluntary measures will be enough. They may help, but they are not enough unless they produce hard, permanent savings at scale. We are not restructuring on hope.

### To Lower Saxony
I understand your political role, but you are still treating site preservation as if it were an alternative to competitiveness. It is not.

If the German footprint remains structurally too expensive, then the industrial base will erode anyway — only more slowly, with less cash for investment, and under weaker market conditions. That is not protection. That is delay.

### To investors and owners
I agree with your skepticism. But I also need a real answer, not just a repeated demand for “close enough” economics.

If the no-closure route is to be taken seriously, it must be judged against a concrete savings benchmark, not sentiments.

## My position
Management will support a **serious no-closure-first settlement** only if it delivers all of the following:

- **permanent labor-cost reduction**
- **meaningful capacity reduction**
- **headcount reduction through attrition, redeployment, and voluntary exits**
- **overhead and complexity cuts**
- **a fixed review point**
- **a credible fallback if delivery falls short**

That is the minimum. Anything less leaves Volkswagen structurally uncompetitive.

## What I will not accept
I will not accept:
- a deal that merely postpones the problem,
- a package that preserves excess structure,
- or a settlement that leaves us unable to fund the technology and product investment needed to 2030.

## On the expert threshold
The expert’s savings range is useful, but it also confirms my concern: **the no-closure route only works at the upper end of the range, and only if the savings are real and durable**.

So I say this plainly to the employee side: if you want to avoid closures, then show me the numbers:
- what annual savings,
- what capacity reduction,
- what labor-cost contribution,
- what headcount reduction,
- what overhead cuts,
- and what implementation certainty.

If you cannot, then the fallback remains on the table.

## Bottom line
We are open to a negotiated no-closure-first package, but only if it is large enough to restore competitiveness. We are not open to a symbolic compromise that protects every structure and still expects the company to fund its future.

If needed, I support a **plant-by-plant savings and capacity analysis** as the next step, because we need to compare:
1. no-closure package,
2. mixed package,
3. closure-led fallback,

on actual economics, not on slogans.
