My assessment, as an industrial finance adviser, is:

**A no-closure package can be economically close enough to a footprint-reduction scenario only if it delivers a very large, durable, and enforceable savings stream—roughly in the upper band of what has been discussed, and with real capacity and headcount effects, not just wage restraint.**  
If it cannot do that, then footprint reduction becomes financially hard to avoid.

## My bottom line

For a group like Volkswagen, a no-closure settlement is **not inherently too weak**. But it becomes credible only if it matches a closure-led scenario on three dimensions:

1. **Magnitude of savings**
2. **Permanence of savings**
3. **Certainty of delivery**

If any of those is materially inferior, the no-closure route is usually just a delay mechanism.

## What “close enough” means financially

A closure-led scenario typically creates savings from several buckets at once:

- direct labor cost reduction
- fixed overhead reduction
- plant overhead and maintenance elimination
- depreciation and impairment effects over time
- logistics and complexity reduction
- utilization improvement at remaining plants

A no-closure package can substitute for part of that, but **not all of it**.  
That means the no-closure route must compensate through:

- **meaningful workforce reduction**
- **working-time and shift reductions**
- **permanent wage and allowance concessions**
- **complexity simplification**
- **headcount and overhead cuts outside the plants**
- **higher utilization at retained sites**

If it only produces temporary wage moderation, it will be well short.

## Practical threshold

I would use the following test:

### A no-closure package is probably credible if it can deliver:
- **large annualized savings**
- **over multiple years**
- **with contractual enforcement**
- **and with visible capacity reduction**

In practical terms, for Volkswagen’s scale, that means the package should look like a **multi-billion-euro annual run-rate improvement**, not a few hundred million.

## When it stops being sufficient

A no-closure package becomes economically insufficient if:

- savings are mostly **one-off** rather than structural
- workforce reductions are too small to change the cost base
- plant utilization remains low
- overhead is not materially cut
- concessions are reversible after a short period
- there is no credible fallback if targets are missed

At that point, management may still prefer the deal for industrial-relations reasons, but from a finance perspective it is not a true restructuring.

## The key analytical point

The real comparison is not:

- “closure versus no closure”

It is:

- **how much permanent fixed-cost reduction and capacity rationalization does each path produce, net of restructuring charges and execution risk?**

That is why a no-closure package can sometimes be acceptable even without plant closures: if it achieves enough **fixed-cost removal** and **durable footprint reduction in operational terms**.

But if it does not remove enough fixed cost, then the economics do not work.

## Important caveats

- **I cannot validate the exact savings range without the company’s detailed plant-level cost data.**
- The answer depends heavily on:
  - current plant utilization
  - labor productivity by site
  - mix complexity
  - overhead allocation
  - severance and restructuring charges
  - the duration of any concessions
- A headline savings number is not enough; the question is how much of it is **real, recurring, and cash-generative**.

## What evidence would be needed to prove a no-closure deal works

I would want to see:

1. **Plant-by-plant cost baseline**
2. **Annualized savings by source**
3. **Headcount reduction path**
4. **Capacity/utilization effect**
5. **Overhead reduction plan**
6. **Duration and reversibility of concessions**
7. **Cash cost of restructuring**
8. **Fallback mechanism if delivery slips**

Without that, claims that a no-closure package is “equivalent” to a footprint reduction scenario are not well supported.

## Final judgment

**My expert view is that a no-closure package is only financially acceptable if it reaches the upper end of the savings debate and produces genuine structural change.**  
If the proposed savings are materially below that threshold, or if they rely on soft commitments and temporary concessions, then a footprint-reduction scenario remains the more credible restructuring tool.
