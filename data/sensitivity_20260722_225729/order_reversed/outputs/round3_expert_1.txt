The clearest economic threshold is this:

## A no-closure package stops being sufficient when it cannot plausibly close the structural gap to competitive domestic operating cost levels **and** restore the required margin on a durable basis within a short implementation window.

In practical terms, for Volkswagen Germany that means:

### 1) Required savings benchmark
A credible no-closure package would need to deliver roughly **€2.5bn to €4bn per year** in durable run-rate savings, depending on:
- the baseline cost base used,
- how much capacity is actually removed through shifts/variants/redeployment,
- how much labor-cost relief is permanent versus temporary,
- and whether overhead simplification is real or only partial.

If the package lands:
- **below ~€2.5bn/year**: it is usually **too small** to be economically convincing.
- **€2.5bn–€3.0bn/year**: possible, but only if paired with strong execution certainty and visible capacity reduction.
- **€3.5bn–€4.0bn/year**: economically serious and could substitute for some closure-led savings, though still not fully equivalent in all scenarios.
- **above ~€4bn/year**: very strong for a no-closure settlement, but difficult to achieve without deep labor and production changes.

### 2) What must be in the package
For a no-closure solution to remain credible, it needs all of the following:
- **permanent labor-cost reduction**  
  not just one-off wage restraint;
- **meaningful capacity reduction**  
  via shifts, utilization, model/variant simplification, or line rationalization;
- **headcount reduction through attrition, retirement, and redeployment**  
  with enough volume to matter;
- **overhead and complexity cuts**  
  in engineering, admin, and support functions;
- **binding implementation discipline**  
  so the savings are not reversible.

### 3) When footprint reduction becomes unavoidable
Footprint reduction becomes the more economically rational path if one or more of these apply:
- the no-closure deal yields **less than ~60%–70% of the savings gap** management needs;
- the savings are **mostly temporary** or depend on recurring renegotiation;
- capacity remains structurally excessive despite labor concessions;
- the company still cannot achieve an acceptable margin after the package;
- execution risk is so high that expected savings are heavily discounted.

### 4) My bottom-line judgment
A no-closure package is **not automatically insufficient**.  
It becomes insufficient when it is:
- **too small**,
- **too reversible**,
- or **too weak on capacity rationalization**.

If Volkswagen can genuinely lock in savings near the **upper end of the €2.5bn–€4bn range**, with real capacity and labor-cost changes, then a no-closure outcome is economically plausible.

If it cannot, then some form of footprint reduction — including plant closure or equivalent structural consolidation — becomes the economically unavoidable fallback.
