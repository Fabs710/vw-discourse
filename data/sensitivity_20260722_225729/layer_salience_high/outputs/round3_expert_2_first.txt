## Expert assessment: does the proposed non-closure package credibly close the margin gap at each German plant?

### Bottom line
**On the record provided, no — not yet.**  
The proposed non-closure package is **economically plausible in principle**, but **not yet demonstrated as plant-by-plant sufficient**. The conversation contains the right instruments, but **no quantified site economics**, **no savings bridge**, and **no utilization/capacity data** for any German plant. Without those, nobody can credibly say the package closes the margin gap at each site.

What is missing is not rhetoric; it is the **hard arithmetic**.

---

## What can be said with confidence

### 1) A closure-free restructuring can work in automotive, but only under specific conditions
From a plant-turnaround perspective, a non-closure package can close a margin gap if it combines enough of the following:

- **capacity reduction** that actually removes fixed cost,
- **model / line consolidation** that raises utilization,
- **working-time reduction or shift changes** that cut variable labor cost or avoid under-absorption,
- **voluntary exits** that reduce headcount structurally,
- **product allocation / platform decisions** that improve mix and volume,
- **overhead and logistics simplification**.

But that only works if the plant’s remaining output can absorb fixed costs at a competitive enough level.

### 2) “No closures” is not automatically a problem; “no closures plus no hard numbers” is
A non-closure solution is credible only if it answers these questions for **each site**:

- What is the current and post-deal **capacity utilization**?
- What is the plant’s **contribution margin** before and after restructuring?
- How much **fixed cost** is removed?
- How much of the labor-cost relief is **permanent**, versus temporary?
- What is the site’s **future product allocation**?
- Does the remaining footprint produce acceptable unit cost versus internal and external benchmarks?

None of that has been provided here.

### 3) Management is economically right to demand durable savings
Management and investors are correct on one narrow point: **temporary or cosmetic concessions are not enough** if the German cost base is structurally out of line.

But from an industrial standpoint, that does **not** mean closures are proven necessary. It means the non-closure package must be measured against the same test:
- permanent cost removal,
- permanent capacity reduction,
- and a believable path to competitive unit economics.

### B) No quantified savings bridge
The discussion mentions:
- working-time changes,
- voluntary exits,
- capacity reductions,
- labor-cost contribution,
- investment commitments.

But it does **not** quantify how many euros each element contributes.

A credible turnaround package would need something like:
- €X from labor cost moderation,
- €Y from headcount reduction,
- €Z from shift/capacity reduction,
- €A from overhead simplification,
- €B from product mix / allocation,
- and the total versus the required EBIT improvement.

That’s absent.

### C) “Capacity reduction without closure” can still fail economically
This is a critical point.

Reducing shifts or lines without closing a plant can work **only if** the remaining fixed-cost burden is still absorbed efficiently. If a site falls to too low a utilization rate, then:
- unit cost rises,
- overhead per car rises,
- and the plant remains margin-negative even if it stays open.

So the question is not “closure or not.”  
The question is: **does enough capacity come out of the system to restore plant economics?**

## Site-level judgment framework

Because no actual site data were provided, I cannot certify any individual German plant as saved or unsaved. But I can say how I would classify sites.

### A plant is likely retainable if:
- it has a clear future product pipeline,
- utilization stays reasonably high after restructuring,
- it can absorb overhead over enough volume,
- logistics are not excessively complex,
- and labor-cost concessions plus productivity measures materially improve unit cost.

### A plant is likely at risk even under a non-closure package if:
- it is structurally underutilized,
- it has no secure next-generation product,
- it carries high fixed overhead,
- it needs disproportionate capex for its output,
- or it is mainly being preserved for political reasons rather than industrial ones.

### A plant is not credibly “turned around” if:
- the package relies mostly on temporary concessions,
- capacity reduction is vague,
- no permanent headcount reduction is achieved,
- and the post-deal cost base still sits above benchmark.

## The key economic test management has not yet passed
Management must show, for each site:

1. **Current unit cost**

Without that, “equivalent economics” remains an assertion, not a conclusion.
