Let me respond directly.

I hear the repeated demand for **no closures, no compulsory redundancies, and no changes to the job-security framework**. I understand why you are drawing those lines. But I have to be equally clear: **those lines cannot become an absolute veto over necessary restructuring** if the economics do not work.

### Where I agree
I do agree on several points:
- Volkswagen needs a **durable** industrial future in Germany.
- We need **investment in future products and technology**.
- Purely cosmetic measures are not acceptable.
- A destructive conflict would be harmful to everyone.

I also agree that management, owners, and the company as a whole must contribute to the solution. This is not about تحميل the entire burden onto employees alone.

### Where I disagree
What I do **not** accept is the idea that we can preserve:
- every German plant,
- the existing fixed-cost structure,
- the historical guarantees unchanged,
- and still achieve a competitive return through 2030.

That is not a serious answer to the market reality we face.

Our diagnosis is not political theater. It is operational:
- the European market is shrinking,
- competition is more intense,
- our German cost base is too high,
- and the brand must improve its return-on-sales materially.

If the company cannot remove enough **structural** cost and capacity, then the problem does not go away — it simply comes back in a more dangerous form later.

### On “no closures”
I am not saying closures are the first or preferred outcome. I am saying they remain on the table **if** no other package achieves equivalent, durable economic effect.

That is the point you keep avoiding.

You keep asking us to rule out the very instrument that may be necessary before you have shown a credible substitute. I will not do that.

If you want to avoid closures, then you need to show — with real numbers, not slogans — how the following are achieved:
- permanent capacity reduction,
- substantial fixed-cost reduction,
- meaningful labour-cost contribution,
- and a margin path that restores competitiveness.

### On compulsory redundancies
I am open to discussing socially managed solutions first — voluntary departures, redeployment, working-time adjustments, qualification, internal transfers. But I will not pre-commit to never using stronger workforce measures if the scale of the problem requires them.

Again: the burden of proof is on anyone claiming we can fix a structural overcapacity problem without hard consequences.

### On guarantees
I cannot support a blanket guarantee through 2030 that freezes the current structure regardless of economics. That would simply tie management’s hands and make adaptation impossible.

What I can support is:
- **clear investment commitments**,
- **a credible industrial plan for the German sites**,
- and a negotiated package that gives employees real security **in exchange for real cost and capacity change**.

That is different from an unconditional promise to keep every site exactly as it is.

### To the works council and IG Metall
You say the workforce must not be treated as the adjustment variable. On that point, I partly agree. But neither can management, owners, or the state be asked to absorb the cost while the operating base remains structurally uncompetitive.

If you want to protect jobs, then help create a structure that can sustain them. That means accepting that:
- some capacity will have to leave the system,
- labour costs must come down in a lasting way,
- and flexibility must improve.

A package limited to voluntary measures and temporary savings is not sufficient if it does not close the competitiveness gap.

### To Lower Saxony and the owners
I appreciate the concern about social and regional consequences. But a refusal to confront the scale of the issue would be irresponsible. Political discomfort is not a business strategy.

I am willing to work on a negotiated solution, but it must be **real**. Not a postponement, not a symbolic compromise, not a package that preserves every legacy arrangement and hopes the market becomes kinder.

### The test for any alternative
If you want to avoid closures, then present a package that can answer four questions:
1. How much cost is removed, exactly?
2. How much capacity is reduced, exactly?
3. How durable is the saving through 2030?
4. Why does this restore the brand’s competitiveness versus rivals?

If you cannot answer those questions convincingly, then “no closures” is not a solution — it is just a refusal to face the problem.

### Bottom line
Management will not sign up to a deal that leaves the company overcapacity, over-cost, and under-returned. That would be irresponsible to the business, to the workforce in the long run, and to the future investment we need to make.

I remain open to alternatives to closures — but only if they are **credible, durable, and economically equivalent**. If not, then we must continue to discuss deeper structural measures, including closures and stronger workforce reductions.

That is the reality of this restructuring.
