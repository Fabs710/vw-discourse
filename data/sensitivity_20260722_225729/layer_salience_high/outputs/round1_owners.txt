I will be direct.

I agree with one part of the discussion so far: Volkswagen needs a durable return to competitiveness, not a temporary patch. The market reality does not disappear because it is politically uncomfortable. If margins remain structurally too low, then the whole enterprise — including jobs, investment, and the family’s long-term stake — is put at risk.

But I do **not** accept the implication that the only credible path is immediate plant closures or a restructuring framed primarily as an employee bill.

### To management
You are right that the current cost and capacity base cannot simply be preserved unchanged. However, you still have to prove that the measures you are demanding are truly necessary and proportionate.

If you come to the board with closures as the first answer, you will create a conflict that is not only socially damaging but value-destructive. A prolonged confrontation, strikes, and reputational damage would weaken the company further and erode the very competitiveness you say you want to restore.

So my position is:
- **Structural improvement is necessary.**
- **A durable cost reduction is necessary.**
- **But closures are not automatically the right answer.**

Show a credible industrial case that alternatives have been fully tested: capacity reduction, shifts, product allocation, internal redeployment, working-time flexibility, and a negotiated employee contribution. If that package can deliver the required economics, it is preferable to irreversible closure decisions.

### To the works council and IG Metall
Your rejection of forced redundancies and one-sided burden shifting is understandable. But “no closures, no compulsory redundancies” cannot be a blanket veto if the economic facts do not support it.

What I will support is:
- no rushed or symbolic plant closures,
- no management-by-threat,
- and no attempt to dismantle social partnership.

What I will not support is:
- a model that keeps all structures intact while shifting the entire adjustment burden into the cost base through endless concessions,
- or a settlement that protects every legacy arrangement but leaves the company uncompetitive.

Employee contributions may be part of the answer, but they must be tied to a real industrial plan and measurable savings. If labour expects long-term security, then the package must actually restore the business.

### To Lower Saxony
I understand the political and regional concerns, and I share the view that Volkswagen cannot be treated as a purely financial exercise. But we should be careful not to convert political discomfort into an automatic blockade of necessary change.

A negotiated restructuring is preferable, yes. But it must be **credible**. If a compromise simply delays the same problem, that is not stewardship — it is postponement at the expense of future value.

### Where I stand
My threshold is clear:

- I support a restructuring that restores competitiveness and margins.
- I support a negotiated solution that preserves labour peace if it is economically real.
- I do **not** support a settlement that permanently impairs enterprise value by refusing all meaningful structural adjustment.
- I do **not** support any move that undermines governance stability or creates a destructive public conflict.

So the burden now is on management to present a serious, durable alternative to closures that truly changes the economics. And it is on labour and the state to engage with that reality instead of treating every structural measure as illegitimate.

If there is a deal to be had, it must be a deal that secures Volkswagen’s future, not one that preserves every interest in place while the core asset weakens.
