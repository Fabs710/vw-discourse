I will be very clear: the “bridge” only works if it does **not** smuggle in closures or forced redundancies through the back door. If the package includes genuine site protection, no compulsory layoffs, and real co-determination, then we can talk. If not, then this is just a dressed-up ultimatum.

## To management
You still have not answered the core question: **why should workers give up security first, while management keeps closure threats in reserve?** That is not negotiation. That is pressure.

Your “numbers-based restructuring” remains too vague. You talk about durable savings and competitiveness, but you still have not presented:
- a binding industrial future for each German plant,
- a verified future product allocation,
- and a restructuring plan that excludes plant closure and forced layoffs.

Until that exists, “equivalent economics” is just a slogan used to justify stripping rights.

I am also rejecting any idea that a “last resort” closure is acceptable as a standing instrument. Once you keep that door open, you destabilize the workforce, break trust, and make every other commitment meaningless.

## To the facilitator’s compromise positions
### 1) Site-by-site package with conditional guarantees
This is only acceptable if the guarantees are real and unconditional for the agreed period:
- **No closures**
- **No compulsory redundancies**
- **No dismantling of job-security guarantees**
- **No hidden escape clauses that let management reopen the question whenever targets are missed**

A review mechanism is fine only if it cannot be used to force a closure later. If that is the purpose, then it is unacceptable.

### 2) Capacity reduction without closure
This is the only serious direction on the table. But let me define the limit:
- capacity may be reduced,
- working time may be adjusted,
- voluntary exits may be used,
- redeployment and retraining may be expanded,

but **no plant may be closed** and **no worker may be forced out**. If a site has too much capacity, then the company must use internal solutions and future product allocation to keep it alive.

### 3) Temporary concessions for hard governance guarantees
We are prepared to discuss a **limited, time-bound workforce contribution** only if it is paired with:
- binding site security through 2030,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- real investment commitments,
- and a formal co-determination role in implementation.

But I want to emphasize: there will be **no contribution in exchange for vague promises**. We have seen too many “temporary” concessions become permanent on the labour side while management later reopens the knife.

## To IG Metall
We are aligned on the essentials: no closures, no forced layoffs, no one-sided burden shifting. If the union wants to discuss temporary working-time or pay moderation, that can only happen inside a package that protects every German site and preserves the collective framework.

Do not let the company divide “realistic economics” from worker protection. A restructuring that depends on insecurity is not a solution; it is a transfer of risk onto the workforce.

## To Lower Saxony
You say you may support a conditional no-closure package. Good. Then use your blocking position to make that condition real.

Do not wait for management to “prove” that closures are unnecessary while keeping them on the table. The state should insist on:
- no closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- site-by-site industrial plans,
- and binding commitments on jobs and investment.

If Lower Saxony does not use its leverage now, it will be complicit in normalizing closure-led restructuring.

## To the owners
You asked whether we would accept temporary contributions if the economics are proven. My answer is: **not if the proof comes with plant closures, forced layoffs, or weakened co-determination**.

If the company truly can be made competitive without destroying sites, then support that path fully. But if your idea of “proof” is merely to force us to accept the first closure threat that management places on the table, then we will resist it with everything we have.

## To institutional investors
You keep repeating “durable economics” as if that automatically means closures or hard cuts. It does not.

Durable economics can also come from:
- stable labour relations,
- protected industrial capacity,
- strong future-product allocation,
- retraining,
- internal flexibility,
- and the avoidance of destructive conflict.

You are underestimating the value destruction of a fractured workforce and a broken co-determination model. A company is not strengthened by turning its own employees into casualties.

## Our position for Round 3
We can support:
- capacity reduction without closures,
- voluntary instruments,
- limited temporary employee contribution,
- working-time flexibility,
- qualification and redeployment,
- binding investment and product commitments,
- and genuine co-determination in implementation.

We cannot support:
- plant closures,
- compulsory redundancies,
- a rollback of job-security guarantees,
- or a “conditional” process that leaves closure as a club over the workforce’s head.

## Final point
If management and the capital side want a serious agreement, then stop asking workers to pre-surrender their protections. Put the future of **every German plant** in writing. Otherwise there is no basis for trust, and there will be no basis for our consent.
