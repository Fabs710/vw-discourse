We share the core line: **no plant closures, no compulsory redundancies, and no one-sided burden shifting onto the workforce**.

Management is treating the German sites as if they were just a cost item to be cut, but this is not an abstract balance-sheet exercise. These are living industrial bases, skilled teams, and long-term commitments. If the company wants the workforce to contribute, then it must be a **shared-sacrifice package**, not an attempt to force employees to pay for strategic failures while owners and management remain protected.

Where I agree with the works council:
- **Hard guarantees matter.** Without binding job and site security, any “restructuring” is just insecurity under another name.
- **Alternative measures must be on the table.** Capacity reductions, working-time adjustments, internal redeployment, and qualification are legitimate tools.
- **Employee contributions can be discussed.** But only if they are limited, temporary, and clearly tied to investment, product commitments, and site security.

Where I draw a clear line:
- **Plant closures are unacceptable.**
- **Forced layoffs are unacceptable.**
- **Dismantling the collective wage framework is unacceptable.**
- **Any solution that makes workers absorb the full pain while management and owners do not contribute is unacceptable.**

If management wants a durable settlement, it needs to come with concrete counter-commitments:
1. Binding guarantees for German sites and jobs.
2. Credible investment in future products and technologies.
3. Management and ownership contribution, not just labour.
4. A negotiated, time-limited employee contribution only in exchange for security.

We are not here to rubber-stamp a restructuring by threat. The peace obligation has ended, and the workforce expects us to defend its interests. If the company insists on closures or compulsory redundancies, then it should expect resistance, including industrial action.
