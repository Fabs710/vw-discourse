We are at the point where sentiment must give way to a credible industrial answer.

My position remains unchanged in substance:

- Volkswagen needs a **durable competitiveness reset**.
- That reset must be **quantified, enforceable, and lasting**.
- A solution that preserves value and labour peace is preferable.
- A solution that merely postpones the problem is not acceptable.

## Where I agree
I agree with management, investors, and Lower Saxony on the diagnosis: the current German cost and capacity structure is not sustainable over time. I also agree with the workforce side that a destructive conflict would be value-destructive and should be avoided.

I further agree that alternatives to closures should be explored seriously:
- capacity reductions,
- working-time changes,
- redeployment,
- qualification,
- voluntary exits,
- and a temporary employee contribution.

That is all sensible.

## Where I disagree
I do **not** accept that “no closures, no compulsory redundancies, no change to the framework” can be treated as the starting point and still be called a serious restructuring.

That is a boundary, not a solution.

At the same time, I also do **not** accept closures as a bargaining club used before the alternatives have been properly tested. That would create avoidable instability and destroy value through conflict.

So the burden is on management to present a **site-by-site industrial case** that shows:
1. the savings required,
2. the capacity reduction required,
3. the durability through 2030,
4. the investment and product logic for each German site,
5. and the point at which deeper measures would truly become unavoidable.

Without that, talk of “equivalent economics” is not yet convincing enough.

## To management
You are right that the economics must improve materially. But you still have not made the case, in sufficiently concrete terms, that closures are necessary rather than simply available.

If a non-closure package can deliver the required savings and capacity reduction, then that is the preferable path. If it cannot, then say so plainly and stop masking the issue in generalities.

What I will not support is a process that keeps escalating conflict while failing to pin down the numbers.

## To the works council and IG Metall
I hear your red lines. They are understandable. But you are also asking for protections that may only be sustainable if you accept some real, durable structural adjustment.

A pure “no closures, no forced layoffs” stance does not answer the competitiveness gap by itself.

If you want to avoid the harder instruments, then be prepared to engage on:
- lasting capacity reduction,
- measurable labour-cost contribution,
- flexibility in working time and deployment,
- and a time-limited package that actually changes the cost base.

In return, I would support:
- avoiding closures if a credible alternative exists,
- avoiding compulsory redundancies as a first resort,
- and tying any employee contribution to investment and product commitments.

What I will **not** support is a package that leaves the company structurally weak and calls that social responsibility.

## To Lower Saxony
Your role as anchor shareholder and political actor matters. I agree that regional employment cannot be treated casually.

But the state must also recognize that preserving every current structure is not the same as preserving the industrial base. The industrial base is preserved only if it remains competitive.

So yes: support a negotiated, non-destructive solution. But do not let “no closures” become a substitute for an economically credible restructuring.

## To investors
You are right to insist on hard economics. I support that. But I also want to be explicit: a value-maximizing outcome is not necessarily the most aggressive one. A package that avoids a prolonged strike, protects labour peace, and still achieves durable savings may create more value than a theoretically stronger but politically and operationally explosive alternative.

The real test is whether the package restores margins and competitiveness without permanent instability.

## My line
I will support a solution only if it is:
- **quantified**,
- **durable**,
- **enforceable**,
- and **value-preserving**.

That means:
- real cost reduction,
- real capacity reduction,
- credible implementation,
- and no outcome that permanently impairs enterprise value or governance stability.

## Bottom line
I am open to a negotiated path away from closures, but only if it is demonstrably equivalent on economics. If not, then we must be honest that stronger structural measures remain part of the toolkit.

What I will not do is endorse either:
- a cosmetic compromise that preserves the problem, or
- a conflict-driven solution that destroys value in the process.
