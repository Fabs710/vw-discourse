I will be direct.

We agree on the diagnosis more than the rhetoric suggests: Volkswagen needs a **durable** improvement in competitiveness, not a symbolic exercise. The current cost and capacity structure in Germany cannot simply be frozen in place and expected to support long-term value. If that were enough, we would not be here.

But I also reject the idea that the only serious option is to hold plant closures over everyone as the default starting point. A prolonged confrontation, strikes, and reputational damage would be value-destructive and would weaken the company further. That would harm the very enterprise we are all supposed to steward.

## To management
You are right that the economics must improve materially. I support that objective.

Where I disagree is your tendency to treat closures and stronger workforce reductions as the implicit baseline before the alternatives have been proven. That is not a governance-quality process. If closures are truly necessary, you will need to show that the alternatives are not merely politically inconvenient, but economically insufficient.

What I want to see is not vague language about “equivalent economics,” but a **site-by-site industrial case**:
- what each German site contributes,
- what capacity reduction is required,
- what savings are durable through 2030,
- what investment and product commitment supports the future structure,
- and what the net margin effect is.

If you can demonstrate that a negotiated package achieves the needed savings without closures, then that is preferable. If you cannot, then you need to be honest about the consequences. But I will not endorse using closure threats as a bargaining tactic while avoiding the numbers.

## To the works council and IG Metall
I understand your red lines: no plant closures, no compulsory redundancies, no one-sided burden shifting. Those are legitimate concerns.

Where I disagree is the attempt to make those lines absolute before the economics are shown. A blanket “no closures, no forced redundancies” position is not, by itself, a restructuring answer. It is a boundary. Boundaries matter, but they do not solve the competitiveness gap.

If you want a negotiated solution, then it must include:
- meaningful, durable capacity reduction,
- real labor-cost contribution,
- working-time flexibility,
- redeployment and qualification,
- and a willingness to accept that some legacy arrangements cannot remain untouched.

In return, I would support:
- avoiding closures if a real alternative exists,
- avoiding compulsory redundancies as a first resort,
- and tying employee contributions to actual investment and product commitments.

What I will not support is a package that preserves every structure and guarantees every site unchanged while asking owners to absorb the consequences of an uncompetitive base. That is not stewardship; that is postponement.

## To Lower Saxony
I appreciate your concern for regional stability and the industrial base. That concern is real and legitimate.

I also agree that Volkswagen must not be treated as a purely financial object. The company’s role in Germany matters. But the state’s responsibility cuts both ways: protecting sites matters, and so does protecting the long-term viability of those sites.

So I support your rejection of a closure-led restructuring as the first move. I do **not** support turning political discomfort into an automatic blockade of necessary change. If the company needs capacity reduction and cost reduction, then the question is how to do it without permanent damage to enterprise value and governance stability.

## On the ownership line
The family holding’s position is straightforward: we support a restructuring that restores margins and competitiveness, but we will not support a process that permanently impairs the enterprise through conflict, instability, or governance erosion.

That means:
- no value-destructive public escalation,
- no management-by-threat,
- no permanent weakening of the company’s future earning power,
- and no outcome that undermines governance control or the company’s ability to act.

I am open to a negotiated package if it is real. I am not open to a compromise that merely delays the problem or one that protects every legacy arrangement while leaving the business structurally weak.

## What would move us
A credible path would need to show, concretely:
1. the exact savings required,
2. how those savings are delivered,
3. how durable they are,
4. what capacity is actually removed,
5. what investment is secured for the German industrial base,
6. and why the package is preferable to closures on a value basis.

If alternatives to closures can meet that test, I would prefer them. If they cannot, then everyone should recognize that the issue is not whether change happens, but whether it happens in an orderly and value-preserving way.

## Bottom line
I support a **serious restructuring**. I do **not** support a fake choice between “keep everything” and “blunt closures immediately.”

But I am equally clear: no party gets a veto over economic reality. A durable solution must restore competitiveness, protect long-term value, and avoid destructive instability. That is the standard we should apply here.
