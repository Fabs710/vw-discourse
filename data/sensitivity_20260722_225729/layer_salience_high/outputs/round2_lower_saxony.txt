I will respond plainly.

Lower Saxony supports a **serious restructuring**. We do **not** support a restructuring by destruction.

## Where I agree

I agree with management, the owners, and the investors on one central point: Volkswagen needs a **durable competitive base** in Germany. The cost gap is real, and cosmetic adjustments will not solve it.

I also agree with the workforce representatives that any solution must be **socially responsible**, not a blunt exercise in dumping the burden on employees. A destructive conflict, prolonged strikes, or a package that permanently damages trust would weaken Volkswagen further.

So yes: the company needs real change, and yes: that change must preserve the possibility of a long-term industrial future.

## Where I disagree

### To management
I do **not** accept your framing that plant closures are the inevitable baseline and that everyone else must first prove why they should be avoided.

That is the wrong burden of proof.

If you want political and supervisory-board support for irreversible measures, you must demonstrate that:
- alternatives have genuinely been exhausted,
- the savings from those alternatives are insufficient,
- and the industrial logic of each site has been fairly assessed.

You have not yet made that case convincingly.

You say closures remain on the table if no equivalent alternative exists. Fine. But that does not give you a free hand to keep them as a standing threat while demanding concessions from labour in advance. In a co-determined company with a blocking minority and social responsibilities, that approach is not acceptable.

### To the investors
You are right that weak competitiveness is a problem. But you are too quick to treat closure as an economic fact rather than a governance decision with huge regional consequences.

Your insistence on “results” is understandable, but results cannot be measured only in next quarter’s margins. Volkswagen is a strategic industrial anchor, not a purely transactional asset. If the restructuring destroys regional employment and social stability, the long-term value destruction will be severe.

### To the works council and IG Metall
I share your rejection of plant closures as the starting point. I also share your demand that workers must not be the sole adjustment variable.

But I will be equally clear: a blanket “no closures, no compulsory redundancies” line is only sustainable if it is matched by a credible alternative that closes the competitiveness gap. If the company’s economics genuinely require capacity reduction, then refusing to discuss how that happens is not enough.

What I **can** support is:
- no closure-led restructuring as the first resort,
- no forced redundancies as the first resort,
- negotiated and reversible instruments first,
- and binding site-protection commitments tied to investment and product allocation.

What I **cannot** support is a package that simply freezes the current structure and hopes the problem disappears.

## The position of Lower Saxony

Lower Saxony will use its rights under the VW Law to protect:
- **regional employment,**
- **the industrial base in Germany,**
- **and co-determination as a real decision-making power, not a symbolic one.**

That means:

- **No restructuring that starts from closures.**
- **No forced mass layoffs as the default answer.**
- **No sidelining of the state’s veto and board role.**

But it also means we are prepared to support a negotiated package if it is economically credible and socially balanced.

## What would be acceptable

A possible path forward must include all of the following:

1. **Binding commitments to keep German sites open**
   not just vague intentions.

2. **A clear future product and investment plan**
   for the German plants, so the workforce sees an industrial future, not only cuts.

3. **Capacity reduction through negotiated, socially managed measures**
   before any talk of closures.

4. **A fair employee contribution**
   but temporary, limited, and tied to security and investment.

5. **Visible management and owner contribution**
   not just labour sacrifices.

6. **Preservation of co-determination**
   and no attempt to weaken the governance rights that protect regional interests.

## What is unacceptable

I want to state the boundary clearly:

- plant closures in Germany as the starting point,
- compulsory redundancies as the default solution,
- one-sided labour cost dumping,
- or any restructuring that strips Lower Saxony of its effective role in safeguarding the regional industrial base.

Those outcomes would not be supported by us.

## My direct challenge to management

If you are serious about avoiding closures, then come back with a **site-by-site industrial plan**:
- what each German plant does,
- what products it will build,
- what investment it receives,
- what capacity changes are proposed,
- and how many jobs are preserved.

Do that honestly, and we can talk.

If you cannot do that, then your talk of “equivalent economics” is just a prelude to confrontation.

## My direct challenge to the workforce side

If you insist on total immobility, you will leave the company with no room to adapt. I do not want that outcome, and neither should anyone who claims to defend long-term employment.

The best protection for jobs is not pretending every structure can remain untouched. It is creating a credible industrial future that makes the jobs sustainable.

## Bottom line

Lower Saxony stands for a **negotiated restructuring that protects jobs, plants, and Volkswagen’s role as a regional economic anchor**.

We will **not** support plant-closure-led restructuring or forced mass layoffs.

But we will also not endorse a solution that refuses any meaningful structural adjustment. The task is to find a package that restores competitiveness **without** sacrificing the German industrial base.

That is the line we will hold.
