Based on the positions stated, the central economic question is:

**Can Volkswagen’s German footprint be made competitive without German plant closures and compulsory redundancies, using capacity reduction, working-time changes, voluntary exits, and labour-cost concessions instead?**

My assessment, strictly from a plant-economics and turnaround perspective, is:

## Short answer
**Yes, possibly — but only if the required savings and capacity reduction are moderate enough, and only if the company can actually enforce the restructuring through binding operational changes.**  
If the gap is large, persistent, and structural, then **a no-closure solution may be economically insufficient**, and keeping closures off the table from the start can reduce management’s ability to close the economics.

## What matters economically

### 1) Closure is not the same as restructuring
A plant closure is the most severe instrument. It is justified economically only when:
- the site has **persistent negative contribution margin** after realistic fixes,
- no credible product reallocation exists,
- the fixed-cost burden cannot be spread adequately,
- and the closure value exceeds the cost of keeping the plant in operation.

If a site still has a viable industrial role, closure is usually a **last resort**, not the first-choice economic measure.

### 2) Overcapacity can often be reduced without closure
From a manufacturing standpoint, there are several tools short of closure:
- fewer shifts,
- lower line speed,
- model consolidation,
- line reconfiguration,
- internal transfers of output,
- temporary idling of capacity,
- voluntary exits,
- and working-time adjustments.

These can remove capacity and cost while preserving the site.

But there is a limit: if a plant’s **fixed overhead, complexity, and labour structure** remain too high relative to output, then partial measures may not be enough.

### 3) The real test is contribution margin and fixed-cost absorption
The question is not whether the workforce is willing to contribute. The question is whether the combination of:
- labour-cost relief,
- capacity reduction,
- productivity improvement,
- and product allocation

is enough to restore:
- a competitive unit cost,
- acceptable return on sales,
- and a stable medium-term production plan.

If not, the company will likely still face a structural deficit even after concessions.

## My judgment on the stated positions

### Management’s position
Management is economically credible in saying that:
- the current German structure is too costly,
- savings must be durable,
- and cosmetic concessions are not enough.

That is a standard turnaround logic.

Where management becomes weaker economically is if it treats closure as an equally valid bargaining instrument before demonstrating plant-by-plant necessity. In industrial restructurings, that often creates negotiation friction without necessarily improving the economic case.

### Workforce / union / Lower Saxony position
The labour-side position is economically credible **only if** a non-closure package can truly deliver:
- enough cost reduction,
- enough capacity reduction,
- and enough long-term flexibility.

Their mistake, economically, would be assuming that “no closure” can be guaranteed regardless of the actual site economics. A plant can be politically important and still be uneconomic.

### Owners / investors
The owners and investors are right to insist on quantified economics. Their weaker point is if they treat closures as the default proof of seriousness. In many cases, the economically superior solution is a negotiated restructuring that avoids strike damage, preserves productive capability, and reduces fixed costs in a more controlled way.

## What I would require before calling a no-closure solution credible
To judge whether closure is avoidable, I would need at minimum:

1. **Site-by-site economics**
   - output volumes,
   - utilization rates,
   - contribution margins,
   - fixed cost burden,
   - capex needs,
   - and product pipeline.

2. **A quantified savings bridge**
   - how much comes from labour cost,
   - how much from capacity removal,
   - how much from overhead cuts,
   - and how much from product mix.

3. **A durability test**
   - are savings permanent or temporary?
   - do they survive to 2030?
   - do they depend on one-off voluntary exits only?

4. **A capacity plan**
   - how much output is removed from which sites,
   - whether remaining plants can absorb it efficiently,
   - and whether logistics and complexity improve.

5. **An implementation mechanism**
   - if there is no enforceable execution framework, the deal may look good but fail in practice.

## Likely outcome in practical industrial terms
For a large carmaker with high domestic costs and overcapacity, the most common economically robust solution is **not all-or-nothing**. It is usually a mix of:
- one or more capacity reductions,
- product and model consolidation,
- working-time flexibility,
- voluntary workforce reduction,
- and targeted site role changes.

A total closure-free package is feasible **if the burden is shared and the savings gap is not too large**.  
If the company needs a very deep step-change in cost base, then some combination of **site rationalization, output transfer, or closure** may eventually become economically unavoidable.

## Bottom line
My industrial judgment is:

- **A negotiated no-closure restructuring is economically possible in principle.**
- **It is not automatically sufficient.**
- **Its credibility depends entirely on the measured economics at each site.**
- If the savings gap is large, then refusing closures as a category may make the restructuring economically incomplete.
