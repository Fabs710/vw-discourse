We are at the point where words must turn into verifiable commitments. I will respond directly to the positions on the table.

## Where I agree
I agree with everyone on one basic point: Volkswagen needs a durable future in Germany.  
I also agree that this cannot be solved by a cosmetic patch. Some structural adjustment is likely necessary, and we are prepared to discuss that.

And yes, I remain open to:
- working-time adjustments,
- capacity reductions,
- redeployment and qualification,
- voluntary exit instruments,
- and a **limited, temporary employee contribution**.

That is still on the table.

## Where I disagree
I do **not** accept the continued use of plant closures and compulsory redundancies as a standing threat while asking workers to make concessions first.

That is the core problem in this discussion.  
A “conditional” closure option is not neutral. It destabilizes the workforce, destroys trust, and turns every other promise into a bargaining chip.

I also reject the idea that the collective wage framework can be weakened and then somehow restored later. Workers will not pay permanently for a crisis they did not create.

---

## To Group & Brand Management
You keep saying you need “durable, quantified” savings. Fine. Then quantify them.

But do **not** pretend that the only credible path runs through closures or forced layoffs. That is not a law of nature. It is a management choice.

If you want a serious negotiation, then put this in writing:
- **no German plant closures**
- **no compulsory redundancies**
- **site-by-site industrial roles**
- **binding investment and product allocation**
- **a temporary and limited workforce contribution**
- **and a fixed implementation period with no hidden escape route**

If you cannot accept that, then you are not negotiating an alternative to closures. You are preparing the ground for them.

### On your “last resort” logic
I reject it.  
Once management keeps closure as a “last resort” without first committing to a non-closure industrial plan, the workforce is forced to negotiate under duress. That is not a fair basis for settlement.

---

## To the Works Council
I support your central line: no closures, no forced layoffs, no one-sided burden shifting.

You are right that “capacity reduction” can become a euphemism for dismantling if it is not tightly controlled. That risk is real.

I would sharpen your position only in one respect: we should not just demand protection in principle; we should demand **enforceable protection**:
- no closure,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- no weakening of the collective agreement,
- and a binding role for co-determination in implementation and monitoring.

That is the minimum basis for any deal.

---

## To Lower Saxony
You say you may support a conditional no-closure package. Good. Then use your veto and board leverage now, not later.

If the state is serious about protecting the industrial base, it must not let management keep closures on the table as pressure.  
Lower Saxony should insist on:
- site-by-site plans,
- real investment,
- and a clear no-closure commitment.

If that cannot be secured, then “responsible restructuring” becomes just a political label for workforce pressure.

---

## To the Owners
You ask whether we would accept temporary concessions if the economics are proven.

My answer is: **yes, but only in a package that protects jobs and sites and does not weaken the collective framework**.

What we will not accept is this:
- workers give up pay or time,
- management keeps closure threats in reserve,
- owners protect value,
- and the burden lands almost entirely on employees.

That is not shared sacrifice. That is one-sided extraction.

If the owners want a durable settlement, they must support:
- no closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- investment in German sites,
- and an outcome where employees do not carry all the pain.

---

## To Institutional Investors
You keep saying “durable economics.” I agree with the goal, but not with your narrow definition of it.

Durability also requires:
- stable labour relations,
- protected industrial capacity,
- no destructive strike cycle,
- and a credible long-term workforce agreement.

You are underestimating the cost of conflict and the value of trust. A package that looks cleaner on a spreadsheet but detonates labour relations is not durable.

If you want our cooperation, then stop treating job security as an obstacle and start treating it as part of the solution.

---

## What would move us
We can continue serious negotiations only if the company accepts these conditions:

1. **No German plant closures**
2. **No compulsory redundancies**
3. **Protection of the collective wage framework**
4. **Binding site-by-site industrial and investment commitments**
5. **Temporary, limited employee contribution**
6. **Capacity reduction through socially managed means**
7. **Co-determination in implementation and monitoring**

If those conditions are met, then we can discuss the scale and form of employee contributions in detail.  
If they are not met, then there is no basis for asking workers to give up anything.

---

## Bottom line
We are not refusing responsibility.  
We are refusing a restructuring where employees pay first, lose security, and are then told this was necessary all along.

The workforce is ready to contribute fairly.  
We are **not** ready to fund a restructuring that keeps closures and forced redundancies as an option while preserving management’s freedom and shifting the risk onto labour.

If management wants a deal, it must stop treating security as a reward for compliance and start treating it as the precondition for cooperation.

If you want, I can now also provide a **harder, shorter union statement** for the next round — as if delivered in a supervisory-board crisis meeting.
