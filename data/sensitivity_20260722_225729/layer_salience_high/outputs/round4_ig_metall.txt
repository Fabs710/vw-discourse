We are prepared to negotiate seriously, but not under a false choice between “accept closures and forced redundancies” or “be accused of blocking the future.”

## To management
You keep saying the economics must be durable. Fine. Then show durable economics **without** using plant closures and compulsory redundancies as your starting instrument.

What you have not shown is:
- a plant-by-plant case proving that closures are unavoidable,
- a quantified savings bridge that depends on closing sites,
- or why internal restructuring cannot close the gap first.

So I disagree with your framing. “Closures remain on the table” is not neutral. It is leverage. It shifts the bargaining pressure onto workers before the company has even demonstrated necessity.

If you want us to discuss real restructuring, then bring:
- site-specific economics,
- a measurable capacity plan,
- and a binding future for each German plant.

Until then, your “durable competitiveness reset” is still just a management demand, not a proven requirement.

## To the works council
I agree with your core point: no one here should be smuggling closures or forced layoffs in through the back door.

You are right that trust collapses if the company keeps the threat alive while asking workers to pre-pay the adjustment. I also agree that “last resort” closure language is destabilizing.

Where I would sharpen the line is this: we should not merely reject closures in principle; we should demand a **binding no-closure package for the agreed period**, with capacity reduction done through:
- working-time changes,
- voluntary exit instruments,
- redeployment,
- retraining,
- and product allocation.

That is the only kind of restructuring that can be socially legitimate.

## To Lower Saxony

- no German plant closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- binding site and product commitments,

## To the owners

But “enforceable” cannot mean enforceable against employees alone. If management and ownership want concessions, then they must also commit to:
- no closures,
- no forced redundancies,
- investment in German sites,
- and a real industrial future for each plant.

I disagree with the idea that the company’s future depends on keeping closures in reserve as a bargaining club. That is not stewardship. It is a threat-based negotiation strategy.

If the owners believe a non-closure solution can be economically equivalent, then back it openly. If not, then you are asking the workforce to pay for uncertainty they did not create.

## To institutional investors
You are right about one thing: the package must be economically credible. We are not asking you to fund a fantasy.

But I reject your claim that “no closures” is merely a boundary and not a business answer. It is both a social red line and a strategic framework. Volkswagen’s future is not secured by extracting pain from labour alone.

If you want our cooperation, then support a solution that:
- reduces capacity,
- lowers costs durably,
- preserves the collective wage framework,
- and does not destroy trust or labour peace.

A deal that looks efficient on paper but triggers strike escalation and long-term industrial instability is not durable.
