I’ll be direct.

## To the works council and IG Metall
I understand your insistence on no forced redundancies and on preserving co-determination. I also agree that a destructive conflict would damage the company. But you are still trying to make **security the precondition** and **competitiveness the afterthought**.

That will not work.

You keep saying “no closures” as if that were an answer. It is not. It is a boundary. A boundary does not close the margin gap, does not reduce overcapacity, and does not restore the brand’s return-on-sales target. If the German footprint stays structurally too expensive, then we have not solved the problem — we have simply postponed it.

I also reject the claim that keeping closure and stronger workforce reductions available is “pressure dressed up as strategy.” It is a reality test. If the alternative package truly delivers equivalent economics, then it should withstand that test. If it cannot, then your position is asking the company to sign up to an uncompetitive cost base and call it a victory.

## To Lower Saxony
You are right about one thing: abstractions are no longer enough. But your response still leans too far toward protection without sufficient economic proof. A regional blocking position is not a license to freeze industrial reality.

If you want to protect the industrial base, then help produce a package that actually makes the German sites viable. That means accepting that some capacity must come out of the system and that labour must contribute in a **lasting**, not symbolic, way. “No closures first” may sound politically attractive, but it is not enough if the numbers still do not add up.

## To the owners and investors
I agree with your core point: this must be quantified, durable, and enforceable. And I agree that cosmetic concessions are worthless.

Where I would push back is against any suggestion that we should wait indefinitely for a perfectly polished analysis before acting. We already know the direction of travel:
- overcapacity is real,
- the cost base is too high,
- and the current structure does not support the margins we need.

The burden now is on the other side to show a **credible, durable, measured** alternative that removes enough cost and capacity. Not vague assurances. Not open-ended guarantees. Actual economics.

## What I agree to
I agree that a closure-free restructuring is worth testing **if** it can show:
- real and durable savings,
- measurable capacity reduction,
- and a path back to competitiveness through 2030.

I also agree that this must be assessed site by site.

## What I do **not** accept
I do not accept:
- blanket “no closures” commitments upfront,
- no compulsory redundancies as an absolute before the economics are known,
- or a package that leaves the cost base materially uncompetitive.

I also do not accept a process where management is asked to surrender every restructuring instrument before the workforce offers anything durable in return. That is not a negotiation; it is an attempt to lock in the status quo.

## On the expert’s intervention
The expert was correct on the key point: a closure-free solution is possible in principle, but not automatically sufficient.

That is exactly why management is insisting on site-by-site arithmetic. The expert also said the central issue is hard numbers. Good. Then let’s have them. But let’s be clear: if the numbers show that the no-closure path does not close the gap, then the answer cannot be “no closures anyway.” That would be economically irresponsible.

## My position
I remain open to a negotiated solution, but only one that delivers:
1. **durable cost reduction**
2. **real capacity reduction**
3. **a genuine labour-cost contribution**
4. **freedom to reallocate capital to future products and technology**
5. **a credible path to 2030 competitiveness**

If the alternative package can demonstrate that, then it has a chance. If it cannot, then keeping closures and stronger structural measures in play is not optional — it is necessary.

## [SUMMON: independent industrial site-economics auditor]
We now need a **site-by-site industrial economics auditor with access to Volkswagen’s German plant data** to do one thing only: test whether the proposed closure-free package actually closes the savings gap at each site, with quantified fixed-cost absorption, utilization, and margin impact.

Until that is done, no one should pretend the issue has been solved.
