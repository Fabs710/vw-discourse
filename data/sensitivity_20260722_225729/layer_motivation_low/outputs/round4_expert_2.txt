Yes. Based on German automotive plant restructuring mechanics, the minimum credible threshold for a **non-closure package through 2030** is more specific than “efficiency measures” and less absolute than “closure-equivalent in every respect.”

## My assessment

A non-closure / no-forced-redundancy settlement is only credible if it delivers:

### 1) Real plant-level capacity reduction
Not just headline productivity measures.

That means:
- removal of shifts,
- model consolidation,
- lower planned output,
- and elimination of duplicated footprint by site.

If the package leaves the same installed capacity in place, it is not a restructuring. It is a delay.

### 2) Durable recurring cost savings
The savings must be structural, not temporary.

Typical measures would include:
- wage moderation or deferred wage growth,
- lower bonus/outperformance pay,
- working-time flexibility,
- fewer premium hours,
- attrition and redeployment,
- reduced indirect labor overhead,
- procurement and overhead cuts linked to the site plan.

One-off effects do not solve a multi-year fixed-cost problem.

### 3) Headcount reduction large enough to matter
For a large overcapacity problem, voluntary-only reductions usually underdeliver unless backed by:
- hiring restraint,
- redeployment,
- internal exits,
- and a long implementation window.

If the overcapacity is material, the total employment reduction usually needs to be in the **low five figures** across the relevant German footprint over time, not a few hundred or low thousands.

### 4) Enforceability through 2030
This is often the weak point.

A credible package needs:
- site-by-site targets,
- annual verification,
- a joint monitoring structure,
- defined consequences if targets are missed,
- and no hidden promise that the issue can simply be reopened unilaterally later.

Without enforceability, markets and management will discount the package.

### 5) Investment and product allocation
A no-closure deal is only durable if it is paired with:
- future product commitments,
- capex allocation,
- and platform relevance for the sites that remain.

Otherwise the deal risks becoming a managed decline.

---

## Practical threshold
If I were setting a restructuring test, I would look for all of the following:

- **Material capacity cut at multiple plants**
- **Recurring annual savings visible in the operating margin by 2027–2030**
- **Headcount reduction meaningful relative to the identified overcapacity**
- **Clear site-specific implementation timetable**
- **Binding governance and review mechanism**
- **Parallel investment commitments**

If these are missing, a non-closure package is unlikely to be judged economically sufficient by management, owners, or investors.

---

## Key trade-off
A non-closure package can preserve:
- sites,
- industrial peace,
- co-determination stability,
- and regional legitimacy.

But it usually comes with:
- slower implementation,
- more negotiated complexity,
- and a higher risk that savings are partially offset by weaker structural removal of fixed cost.

So the central question is not whether it is socially preferable.  
It is whether it removes enough **fixed cost and capacity** to be believed as a durable reset.
