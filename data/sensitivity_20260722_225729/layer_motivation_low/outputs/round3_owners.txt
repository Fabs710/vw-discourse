I will be direct.

Volkswagen needs a restructuring that is **real, durable, and measurable**. I agree with management, the investors, and Lower Saxony on that. I also agree with the workforce side on one point that matters greatly to us as owners: **a prolonged conflict would destroy value**. Strikes, uncertainty, and reputational damage are not acceptable. We do not want a victory that leaves the company weaker.

But I do **not** accept the idea that the discussion can be closed by declaring German plant closures and compulsory redundancies off limits in all circumstances.

## Where I agree
- **Management is right** that incremental tweaks are not enough.
- **The expert is right** that non-closure alternatives can work only if they are deep, durable, and enforced.
- **Lower Saxony is right** that industrial legitimacy and social peace matter.
- **The works council and IG Metall are right** that a cosmetic compromise would simply postpone the problem.

## Where I disagree
### To the works council and IG Metall
Your position is internally consistent, but it does **not yet solve the economics**.

You say:
- no closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- binding site guarantees,
- and meaningful savings anyway.

That is only credible if you can show a package that removes enough **fixed cost, capacity, and complexity** to restore competitiveness. The expert assessments do **not** say that such a package is impossible. They say it must be **substantial** and **durable**. That is a very different claim from “closures are unnecessary in all cases.”

I also reject the implication that management is somehow acting illegitimately simply because it keeps closures on the table. In a company with this level of structural overcapacity, keeping stronger options available is not blackmail; it is responsibility.

### To management
I support the push for a real reset, but I will also say this plainly: do **not** rely on threats alone. If management wants owner backing, it must table a **quantified alternative** that proves the economics. “Closure is possible” is not a plan. I want to see:
- how much capacity comes out,
- how much labor cost comes out,
- how overhead simplifies,
- and how the result reaches margin improvement by 2030.

## What we need
The discussion has now moved far enough that we need numbers, not slogans.

[SUMMON: independent plant-level restructuring expert to produce a quantified comparison of closure, non-closure, and hybrid packages, including fixed-cost removal, capacity reduction, job impact, durability to 2030, and implementation risk under VW’s governance constraints]

## Our owner position
As controlling owners, our test is simple:
- Does the package restore competitiveness?
- Is it durable?
- Does it avoid value-destructive instability?
- Does it preserve governance control and enterprise value?

If a non-closure solution can truly achieve that, we will support it.  
If it cannot, then we will not support a settlement that leaves Volkswagen structurally weakened just to preserve the appearance of agreement.

## Bottom line
We are open on the **mechanism**. We are not open on the **economics**.

A deal must produce:
1. material and durable capacity reduction,
2. meaningful labor-cost contribution,
3. enforceable investment and product commitments,
4. a credible path back to margin recovery,
5. and industrial peace.

If the parties can bring a package that meets those conditions without closures, fine. If not, then it is not enough to say “closures are unacceptable” and hope the cost base fixes itself.
