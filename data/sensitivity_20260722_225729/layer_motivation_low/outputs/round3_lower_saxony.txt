Lower Saxony’s position remains clear: **Volkswagen needs a real restructuring, but not one that destroys the company’s industrial base in Germany or weakens co-determination.**

## To management
I agree with you on the diagnosis that the German footprint is too expensive and that incremental tinkering is not enough. I also agree that a cosmetic deal would simply postpone the crisis.

Where I disagree is your attempt to keep **closures and compulsory redundancies** on the table as if they were ordinary bargaining tools. They are not ordinary in this company, in this region, or under this governance structure. They are a last-resort threat that would damage trust, provoke conflict, and put the regional economy at risk.

If you want Lower Saxony’s support, then you need to show a **credible non-closure package** that is genuinely strong enough to restore competitiveness. So far, you have argued that closures are “possible” if the numbers do not work. That is not a solution; it is an escalation posture.

## To the works council and IG Metall
I agree with you that **German plant closures and compulsory redundancies are unacceptable**. I also agree that the workforce cannot be asked to carry the entire burden.

Where I need to push back is this: a simple declaration of “no closures, no layoffs” is not enough by itself. If the company is to remain viable, the alternative must be **quantified, durable, and enforceable**. That means real capacity reduction, productivity gains, working-time flexibility, and a meaningful employee contribution — but all within a binding framework that protects sites and jobs.

So I support your red lines, but I need you to engage seriously on the scale of adjustment required to make a no-closure path economically real.

## To the owners and investors
I understand your concern about margins and durability. That concern is legitimate. But you are underestimating the value risk created by a confrontation that tears up the company’s social settlement. Volkswagen’s long-term strength is not only financial; it is also industrial legitimacy, skilled labour, and regional embeddedness.

A settlement that protects jobs and sites can still be value-preserving if it is economically serious. What I will not support is a deal that preserves short-term margin optics by hollowing out Volkswagen’s role as an industrial anchor.

## What would move Lower Saxony
I can support a serious restructuring if it contains all of the following:

1. **No German plant closures**
2. **No compulsory redundancies**
3. **Real capacity reduction through consolidation and shift changes**
4. **Binding investment and future-model commitments**
5. **A durable employee contribution, balanced by management and owner contributions**
6. **Protection of co-determination and the state’s veto role**
7. **A clear path to competitiveness without destroying regional employment**

That is not sentimentality. It is the minimum basis for a deal that can survive politically, industrially, and economically.

## What will not move me
I will not be moved by arguments that say:
- closures are simply “inevitable,”
- layoffs are the only serious tool,
- or site protection is somehow incompatible with competitiveness.

Those are assertions, not proof.

We now need **plant-level quantified options**, not abstract ultimatums.

## Bottom line
Lower Saxony is prepared to support a hard but fair restructuring.  
We are **not** prepared to support closures or forced redundancies.  
If management cannot produce a viable alternative that protects jobs, sites, and the company’s future, then it is not being serious about social partnership.
