From the perspective of dispersed capital-market shareholders, the discussion so far has one overriding test: **does the package credibly restore Volkswagen’s margins, cost competitiveness, and medium-term return on capital?** If the answer is unclear, we should not bless it.

### Where we agree
We agree with management, the controlling owners, and even Lower Saxony on the core diagnosis: **the current German cost base is too high** and incremental tinkering will not be enough. Volkswagen cannot keep absorbing structural overcapacity, weak European demand, and higher labor cost relative to peers while expecting the market to reward the equity.

We also agree with the unions and works council on one practical point: **a prolonged destructive confrontation would destroy value**. Strikes, reputational damage, and operational disruption are all margin-negative. They would delay the turnaround and likely force a worse outcome for everyone, including shareholders.

### Where we disagree
We do **not** support a “cosmetic compromise” that preserves the current footprint while claiming victory through temporary measures.

Specifically:

- **No settlement that leaves the cost base structurally uncompetitive**
- **No deal that postpones the problem to protect existing arrangements**
- **No vague promise of future efficiency without enforceable savings**
- **No agreement where employees contribute but the company still cannot reach a competitive return-on-sales trajectory**

That means we are not interested in rhetoric about “balance” if the economics remain unchanged. A partial burden-sharing package is only acceptable if it produces **durable, measurable, and credible** improvement in margins.

### On plant closures and layoffs
We are **not attached to closures as a principle**. If management can achieve equivalent savings through another route that is real, durable, and enforceable, that is preferable. Why? Because prolonged disruption is risky.

But we will also not support a package that rules out closures and compulsory redundancies **if that makes the restructuring economically inadequate**. The key issue is not the mechanism; it is the result. If the alternatives do not deliver the needed cost reduction, then the refusal to contemplate hard measures simply shifts the burden into the future and destroys value more slowly.

### On employee guarantees
We understand the appeal of site and job guarantees, but from an investor standpoint they are only acceptable if they do not become a shield for structural underperformance.

- A blanket “no compulsory redundancies” commitment is acceptable only if the savings are still real.
- Site guarantees are acceptable only if they do not freeze an uncompetitive structure.
- Working-time changes, productivity gains, and labor-cost concessions are useful only if they are **durable and verifiable**.

If the package is just an exchange of headline concessions for weak implementation, we would regard it as insufficient.

### What would move us
We would support an outcome that includes:

1. **A clearly quantified and durable cost reduction**
2. **A credible path to restore competitive margins**
3. **Concrete capacity adjustment, even if not via closures**
4. **Enforceable labor-cost relief**
5. **A deal that reduces the probability of renewed conflict**
6. **Visible commitment to future investment and product competitiveness**

In short: we want **deal certainty plus real economics**.

### Bottom line
Our position is conditional, not ideological. We are **open to any solution** that credibly restores competitiveness. But we will not endorse an arrangement that merely preserves employment protections and postpones the restructuring pain while leaving the equity story broken.

If the parties want investor support, they need to show us:
- the numbers,
- the durability,
- and the path back to competitive margins.

Absent that, we would view the result as value-destructive, regardless of how balanced it may sound politically.
