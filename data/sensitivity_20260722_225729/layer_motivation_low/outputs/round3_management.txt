Let me be equally direct.

I hear the repeated “no closures, no compulsory redundancies” line from the works council and IG Metall. I also hear Lower Saxony’s insistence on industrial legitimacy. And I agree with all of you on one point: **a cosmetic deal would be worse than no deal**. If we leave the current cost base essentially intact, Volkswagen will not be competitive, and we will be back here in a shorter cycle with even less room to maneuver.

But I do **not** accept the conclusion you are trying to lock in: that management should rule out closures and forced reductions before we have a package that actually closes the structural gap.

### Where I agree
- **Incremental tweaks are not enough.**
- **A strike war would destroy value.**
- **Any solution must be durable through 2030.**
- **A real package must include capacity, labor, investment, and governance elements.**

On that, there is broad alignment.

### Where I disagree
#### 1) “No closures” as an absolute red line
That is not a restructuring strategy. It is a veto on one of the few tools that can actually remove structural fixed cost.

The expert assessments did **not** show that non-closure alternatives are automatically sufficient. They said the opposite: they can work **only if** they are deep, durable, and tightly enforced — and even then they usually **do not fully match** the savings from footprint rationalization.

So if you say “no closures” and stop there, you are not solving the cost problem. You are merely insisting that management carry the burden of proof while denying one of the possible remedies.

#### 2) “No compulsory redundancies” as a universal rule
I understand the political and human sensitivity. But I will not commit in advance to a blanket prohibition if the package does not deliver the necessary savings otherwise. We need flexibility to reach a durable solution. Natural attrition and redeployment may do part of the work, but I am not going to preclude other instruments if they become necessary to restore competitiveness.

#### 3) The idea that guarantees can be absolute and still preserve competitiveness
Binding guarantees have a cost. If they freeze the footprint, freeze the workforce, and remove the ability to adjust as demand and product mix change, then they become part of the problem.

A guarantee is only acceptable if it is paired with **real, measurable, durable savings**. Otherwise it is a promise to preserve today’s inefficiency through 2030.

### On the expert request
A plant-level scenario table is indeed useful, but let’s be clear about what it must answer:
- how much fixed cost is actually removed,
- how much capacity is truly taken out,
- what labor contribution is durable,
- and whether the result is sufficient to hit the brand’s return-on-sales target.

Not “what preserves the most comfort,” but **what restores competitiveness**.

### What would move management
I am open to alternatives to closures. I am **not** married to a closure as a political symbol. But if you want me to drop closure as an immediate option, then bring a package that is genuinely equivalent in economic effect.

That means, at minimum:
1. **material capacity reduction**
2. **substantial, durable labor-cost contribution**
3. **simplification of the footprint**
4. **headcount reduction through all available means, including voluntary and, where needed, more direct measures**
5. **binding investment and product commitments**
6. **implementation mechanisms that actually work**

If that package is credible, I can judge it on its merits. If it is not, then closures remain on the table because they are still one of the few instruments that can deliver structural change.

### To the works council and IG Metall
You keep saying management is hiding behind inevitability. No — management is confronting arithmetic.

If the German footprint remains too expensive, then the numbers do not improve by repetition. You can call closures unacceptable, but that does not make the cost base competitive. If you want to avoid closures, then help produce a package that removes enough cost and capacity to make them unnecessary. So far, you have rejected the tools before demonstrating that the alternative can fully work.

### To Lower Saxony
I respect the political responsibility you carry. But your support cannot be conditioned on preserving every current site and every current guarantee. Volkswagen’s legitimacy also depends on economic survival. If we avoid hard decisions now, the result will be a weaker company, not a protected one.

### To the owners and investors
I agree with you that the package must be economically real. But that also means backing management when we say the current structure is too costly. A settlement that preserves too much fixed cost is not a responsible compromise.

### Bottom line
I am not here to provoke conflict for its own sake. I am here to restore competitiveness.

So here is the clear position:
- I am willing to consider a **serious, quantified non-closure package**.
- I am **not** willing to pre-emptively rule out closures or stronger workforce reductions.
- I will not accept a deal that leaves Volkswagen’s German cost base structurally uncompetitive.

Bring the numbers. Bring the enforceable package. Bring the actual savings. Then we can talk. Otherwise, the hard options remain on the table because the crisis is real.
