We agree with the broad diagnosis: Volkswagen needs a **real restructuring**, not cosmetics. Incremental tweaks and short-lived pacts will not restore margins, and a prolonged strike war would be value-destructive.

But from the capital markets’ point of view, the central question is still the same: **does the package credibly restore competitiveness by 2030, or does it merely postpone the problem while preserving an uncompetitive cost base?**

## Where we agree
- **With management:** the current German cost base is structurally too high.
- **With the expert:** non-closure alternatives can work, but only if they are deep, durable, and enforceable.
- **With Lower Saxony and labour:** a prolonged conflict would destroy value, and a purely cosmetic settlement would be worse than nothing.

## Where we disagree

### 1) “No closures” cannot be the only test
We do not treat plant closures as desirable. We do **not** want closures for their own sake.

But we also will not back a settlement that leaves Volkswagen with:
- structurally excessive fixed costs,
- too much capacity,
- and no credible path back to target margins.

If the no-closure path cannot remove enough cost and capacity, then it is not a solution. It is a delay.

### 2) Labour’s red lines are understandable, but not sufficient
The works council and IG Metall keep saying:
- no closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- binding site protection.

We understand why. But those are **conditions**, not an answer to the economics. For shareholders, the key issue is whether the final package is **economically equivalent enough** to deliver competitive margins.

If it is not, we cannot support it, even if it is politically tidy.

### 3) Management is right on the need for hard numbers, but wrong if it treats closures as the default answer
Management is correct that a vague promise of “trust us” is not enough. We need a quantified plan.

But management should not act as if closures are the only serious instrument before the alternative has been properly tested. That raises execution risk and bargaining hostility, both of which hurt value.

## What we require
Any package we could support must show all of the following:

1. **Material capacity reduction**
   - Real footprint simplification, not just re-labelling.

2. **Durable recurring labor-cost savings**
   - Wage moderation, bonus restraint, working-time flexibility, productivity gains.

3. **Headcount reduction that is credible by 2030**
   - Preferably through attrition/redeployment first, but enough to matter.

4. **Enforceability**
   - A settlement that survives supervisory board politics and bargaining reopeners.

5. **Investment and product allocation**
   - Otherwise cost cuts just hollow out the future.

6. **A clear margin path**
   - Return-on-sales must improve in a way that is visible and believable.

## What we will not back
We will not back:
- a cosmetic compromise,
- a frozen cost structure,
- or a settlement that protects every site in form while leaving the company weak in substance.

We also will not back a brute-force closure plan if a genuinely equivalent negotiated alternative exists. We are not ideologues for closures. We are investors for value.

## What would move us
What would move us is a **plant-by-plant quantified package** that shows:
- how much capacity is removed,
- how much fixed cost is taken out,
- what recurring annual labor savings are locked in,
- what headcount reduction is achieved by when,
- and how the result improves margins through 2030.

Without that, the discussion stays rhetorical.

## Bottom line
We are open to **any mechanism** that credibly restores competitiveness.  
We are **not** open to a deal that merely preserves employment structures while impairing enterprise value.

If the non-closure package is truly deep, durable, and economically sufficient, we can support it. If not, then the company must be honest that some form of stronger footprint reduction remains necessary.
