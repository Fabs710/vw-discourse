Let me be equally clear in round 2: **the workforce will not accept German plant closures or compulsory redundancies**. That remains non-negotiable.

I hear management repeating that the cost base is too high and that incremental measures are not enough. We do not deny that Volkswagen faces a serious challenge. What we reject is the conclusion that the only “real” answer is to break the historic job-security framework and make workers pay through site closures and forced exits.

### Where I agree
I agree with Lower Saxony, the union, and even the owners on one point: **a destructive confrontation would damage the company**. Strikes and public conflict are not our goal; they are a response to management forcing this into a corner.

I also agree that any restructuring must be durable, not cosmetic. But durability does **not** mean closure. Durability means a stable industrial plan, protected sites, and a workforce that can trust what is signed.

### Where I disagree — sharply
#### 1) On plant closures
Management says closures are not ruled out. That is precisely the problem.

A closure is not “structural realism”; it is the permanent destruction of jobs, skills, supplier networks, and regional trust. Once a site is shut, the damage cannot be reversed. That is why we reject it outright. If the company cannot solve its cost problem without closing German plants, then it has not earned the right to demand sacrifice from the workforce.

#### 2) On compulsory redundancies
We will not accept forced layoffs as a restructuring tool. Not now, not as a “fallback,” not hidden behind any euphemism about flexibility. If management needs fewer people, then management must pursue alternatives that do not throw families out of work: natural attrition, internal redeployment, qualification, reduced hours, and negotiated arrangements.

#### 3) On the argument that guarantees “freeze” the company
The owners and investors keep saying binding guarantees somehow block competitiveness. That is one-sided logic. The company is asking employees to accept risk, while management and shareholders preserve their freedom of action. We are not prepared to accept that asymmetry.

A binding no-layoff and no-closure framework is not a luxury. It is the minimum condition for trust. Without it, there is no basis for burden-sharing.

### What a real alternative looks like
If management is serious about avoiding closures, then bring a quantified package that achieves the savings through other means:

- **capacity reductions without site closures**
- **working-time reductions and flexibility**
- **temporary wage and bonus moderation, but only with hard guarantees**
- **productivity improvements developed jointly**
- **redeployment and qualification instead of forced exits**
- **binding investment commitments for future models and technologies**
- **co-determination in implementation, not just consultation after the fact**

If this needs expert validation, then fine:

[SUMMON: Independent industrial restructuring expert to compare closure-based versus alternative savings scenarios, including job impact, capacity reduction, and durability to 2030.]

Because right now management is asserting that closures are unavoidable, but it has not proven that alternatives cannot deliver equivalent savings. Assertions are not enough when tens of thousands of jobs are at stake.

### Minimum conditions for any discussion
I will put this plainly:

1. **No German plant closures**
2. **No compulsory redundancies**
3. **Binding site guarantees for all German locations**
4. **A genuine co-determination role in shaping the restructuring**
5. **A negotiated industrial plan with future products and investment**
6. **Burden-sharing from management and owners as well, not only employees**

If those conditions are not accepted, then management is not negotiating — it is demanding surrender.

### To the investors and owners
If you want value protection, then stop treating workforce protections as an obstacle and start treating them as a precondition for industrial peace. A forced restructuring may look decisive on paper, but if it triggers lasting conflict, damages the brand, and undermines labour relations, it destroys value too.

### Bottom line
We are ready to discuss a serious restructuring. We are **not** ready to discuss German plant closures or compulsory redundancies. If that is still the management line, then the conflict will continue — and the responsibility for escalation will lie with those who insist on making workers pay for a crisis they did not create.
