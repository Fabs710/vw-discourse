I’ll be equally direct.

## To the works council and IG Metall

I hear your insistence on:
- no German plant closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- binding site protection,
- and protection of the collective framework.

I understand why you are drawing that line. But I do **not** accept the attempt to make those red lines the **starting assumption** for the entire restructuring. That is not a solution to Volkswagen’s cost problem; it is a demand that management solve the arithmetic without using the full set of available instruments.

You keep saying closures are a “destructive option” and therefore should not be the benchmark. I disagree. Closures are not a political preference; they are one of the few tools that actually remove structural fixed cost. If you want to argue for a no-closure path, then you need to show that it is not just socially preferable, but economically sufficient. So far, you have asserted that it can work. You have not demonstrated that it delivers enough.

## Where I agree

I agree on three points:
1. A cosmetic deal would be worthless.
2. A prolonged strike war would damage output, reputation, and value.
3. Any settlement must be durable through 2030.

So we are not arguing about whether restructuring is needed. We are arguing about whether your preferred instrument mix is sufficient.

## Where I disagree

### 1) “No closures” is not a restructuring strategy
It is a constraint. It may be your preferred constraint, but it does not solve the underlying overcapacity. If the German footprint remains too large, too fixed-cost heavy, and too expensive, then the company will keep missing its targets. That is not acceptable.

### 2) You are asking us to prove the negative
You want management to rule out closures before the package exists. I will not do that. First, bring a package that closes the gap. Then we can evaluate whether closures are still needed. Until then, keeping them on the table is responsible management, not intimidation.

### 3) You understate the cost of freezing the structure
A deal that locks in too much capacity, too much workforce rigidity, and too much cost through 2030 is not stable — it is deferred failure. It would protect the appearance of social peace while weakening the company’s competitiveness and investment capacity.

That is exactly what I cannot accept.

## To your claim that management is using threat leverage
Yes, of course closures and workforce reductions are hard leverage. That is because the situation is hard. If the structure were already competitive, we would not be here.

I am not hiding behind “inevitability.” I am stating a simple fact: if the savings are not deep enough, the company will not recover. And if the company does not recover, then all of these guarantees become temporary anyway.

## What would actually move management

I have said this repeatedly, and I’ll say it again in concrete terms:

I am open to a **serious non-closure package** if, and only if, it delivers all of the following:

- material capacity reduction by site,
- recurring labor-cost relief,
- meaningful headcount reduction,
- simplified footprint and model consolidation,
- verifiable investment commitments,
- and enforceable governance through 2030.

That package must be quantified. Not in slogans. Not in generalities. I need plant-by-plant numbers, annual savings, and implementation mechanics.

If the alternative package is truly equivalent in effect, I will judge it on that basis. But I will not accept a framework that says “no closures, no redundancies” first and then hopes the economics work out later.

## On your insistence that closures are inherently illegitimate
They are not inherently illegitimate in a restructuring context. They are difficult. They are painful. They carry political and social cost. But they are not illegitimate if the company is structurally overcapacity and cannot regain competitiveness otherwise.

That is the uncomfortable truth.

## To Lower Saxony
You are right to say industrial legitimacy matters. It does. But legitimacy also depends on whether Volkswagen survives as a strong industrial company, not just a protected symbol. A deal that avoids every hard decision today but leaves the company weaker tomorrow is not a responsible outcome.

## To the owners and investors
I agree with your core point: the outcome must be economically real. A deal that only reshuffles costs, while preserving excess structure, would be unacceptable. Value preservation requires durable savings, not ceremonial compromise.

## Bottom line
I am prepared to negotiate alternatives.
I am **not** prepared to pre-commit to a no-closure, no-forced-layoff settlement without proof that it is economically sufficient.

Volkswagen needs a restructuring that restores competitiveness, protects future investment, and materially lowers the cost base. If your preferred framework can do that, bring it. If it cannot, then we cannot pretend it is a solution.
