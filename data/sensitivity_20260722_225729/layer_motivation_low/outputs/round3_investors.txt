From the capital markets’ perspective, the discussion is useful only if it leads to a **credible, enforceable cost reset**. We are not interested in symbolic balance. We are interested in **margin recovery, durable savings, and deal certainty**.

## Where we agree

### With management
We agree that Volkswagen’s German cost base is structurally too high and that **incremental tweaks are insufficient**. If the package does not materially improve the cost base, then it is not a restructuring.

We also agree that a prolonged strike war would be value-destructive. Lost output, launch delays, and reputation damage all hit cash flow and margins.

### With the expert
We agree with the expert’s core point: **non-closure alternatives can work, but only if they are deep, durable, and tightly enforced**. That matters. It means a no-closure solution is not impossible — but it is not free and it is not cosmetic.

### With Lower Saxony and labour
We agree that industrial peace has value. A settlement that triggers prolonged conflict can destroy more value than it saves.

## Where we disagree

### With the works council and IG Metall
Your red lines — no closures, no compulsory redundancies, binding site guarantees — are understandable from a labour standpoint, but they are **not sufficient as a shareholder answer**.

If those protections are purchased at the price of leaving the cost base structurally uncompetitive, then they are not a solution; they are a delay. We will not support an outcome that preserves employment formally while impairing the company’s earnings power and competitiveness.

You keep saying alternatives can work, and that is true in principle. But the burden is now on labour to show that the alternative is **quantified, durable, and equal in economic effect**. “No closures” by itself is not economically meaningful.

### With management
We also disagree with any implication that closures are the only credible path simply because they are the hardest tool. That is too blunt. Capital markets do **not** need a closure for its own sake; we need **results**.

If a negotiated non-closure package can deliver comparable fixed-cost removal, capacity reduction, and margin improvement, then that is preferable because it reduces execution risk. But if management cannot prove that, then it should not pretend the risk has been solved.

## What we require

Any package we could support must contain all of the following:

1. **Material and durable capacity reduction**
   - Not just efficiency language.
   - Real footprint simplification.

2. **Verifiable labor-cost contribution**
   - Wage moderation, bonus restraint, or other concessions that are large enough to matter.

3. **Headcount reduction**
   - Preferably through attrition and redeployment first.
   - But if that is insufficient, the package must still produce the necessary savings.

4. **Enforceability through 2030**
   - A settlement that expires at the first convenient bargaining round is not good enough.

5. **Investment and product commitments**
   - Because cost cutting without future product allocation is only half a restructuring.

6. **A clear margin path**
   - We need confidence that the return-on-sales target becomes realistic, not aspirational.

## What we will not back

We will not back:
- a cosmetic compromise,
- a deal that leaves overcapacity intact,
- a permanent protection regime that freezes an uncompetitive structure,
- or a settlement that merely postpones the next crisis.

We also will not back a hard-landing restructuring if a genuinely equivalent alternative exists. We are not ideologues for closures. We are investors for value.

## Our position on the current impasse

Right now, both camps are partly right and partly wrong:

- **Management is right** that there must be real structural change.
- **Labour is right** that a value-destructive conflict would be costly.
- **Labour is wrong** if it assumes employment protections alone solve the economics.
- **Management is wrong** if it treats closures and layoffs as the only way to prove seriousness.

The decisive issue is whether a negotiated package can remove enough cost and capacity, with enough durability, to restore competitiveness.

## What would move us

We would support a no-closure or hybrid package if it shows, in plant-level numbers:
- how much fixed cost is actually removed,
- how much capacity comes out,
- how much labor cost is locked in over time,
- and how the resulting structure gets Volkswagen back to a competitive margin profile.

If those numbers are not credible, then the deal is not value-preserving.

We are open to any mechanism that **credibly restores competitiveness**.

If the numbers support a durable non-closure package, we will support it. If not, then the company needs to be honest that structural footprint reduction remains necessary.
