I’ll be blunt: the more management, owners, and investors repeat “cost-equivalent to closures,” the more obvious it becomes that they are trying to turn a political red line into a technical calculation. I do not accept that framing.

## Where I agree
I agree with everyone on one point: **Volkswagen needs a real restructuring**.  
I also agree that a **cosmetic compromise is worthless** and that a short-lived patch would just postpone the crisis.

And yes, I agree that a durable solution must work through **2030**.

## Where I disagree
### 1) The “equivalent to closures” benchmark is not the right test
Management says alternatives are only acceptable if they are close to closure-based savings. That is a negotiating tactic, not an economic law.

A plant is not just a cost center. A closure destroys:
- jobs,
- skills,
- supplier networks,
- apprenticeship pipelines,
- regional legitimacy,
- and trust in the whole company.

So no, closures are not the neutral benchmark. They are a destructive option that cannot be treated as the default standard for measuring seriousness.

### 2) Management is still using threat leverage, not genuine negotiation
Management keeps saying it is “open” to alternatives while refusing to rule out closures and forced layoffs. That is not openness. It is pressure.

If you want a real agreement, table a **quantified non-closure package** first.  
Do not demand that labour accept the gun on the table and then call that bargaining.

### 3) The owners and investors are underestimating execution risk
You talk about margin recovery as if a closure-based cut is automatically cleaner. It is not.

A closure, especially in Germany, would trigger:
- strikes,
- legal battles,
- political conflict,
- reputational damage,
- and deep mistrust across the workforce.

That is not a minor side effect. That is a huge execution risk that destroys value and destabilizes the company for years.

## On the expert’s points
The expert’s scenario logic is useful, but let me be clear about what it does and does not prove.

It does **not** prove that closures are necessary.  
It proves that if Volkswagen wants a no-closure path, then it must be:
- deep,
- durable,
- enforceable,
- and plant-specific.

That is exactly why we insist on binding guarantees. Not because we are sentimental, but because half-measures are the real danger.

## What we are prepared to discuss
We are prepared to discuss a **serious non-closure restructuring** that includes:

- binding capacity reductions,
- shift changes,
- working-time flexibility,
- productivity measures,
- redeployment and retraining,
- temporary employee contributions,
- investment and model commitments,
- and enforceable co-determination in implementation.

But let me repeat: these are acceptable only if they **protect sites and jobs**.  
They are **not** a backdoor to closures or compulsory layoffs.

## What we will not accept
We will not accept:
1. German plant closures  
2. compulsory redundancies  
3. a “skeleton” operation disguised as a compromise  
4. a deal that weakens co-determination  
5. a deal that destroys the historic job-security framework  
6. a settlement that shifts the crisis onto workers alone

That is not negotiable.

## To management
You ask what exact savings target would make a no-closure package acceptable.

My answer is: **that is the wrong question if it is used to reopen closures.**  
The correct question is: what can be achieved **without closures and without forced redundancies**, while keeping the company viable?

Bring a **plant-by-plant quantified plan**:
- where capacity comes out,
- how much labor cost is saved,
- what investment goes where,
- and how jobs remain protected to 2030.

If you can’t do that without invoking closures, then the problem is not labour resistance. The problem is that management wants the easy weapon.

## To Lower Saxony
I welcome your support for no closures and no compulsory redundancies. But I need you to be more than a political cushion for management. Use your blocking power to stop any attempt to convert “review gates” into future closure permissions.

### I agree with you on one thing:
A serious alternative must be measurable.

### I disagree with you if:
“measurable” becomes code for “eventually closures if the numbers don’t please management.”

No. The numbers must be made to work within a no-closure framework.

## To the controlling owners
You say you are open on mechanism but not on economics. Fine. Then be honest: you are not neutral arbiters, you are pushing for cost removal first.

But value is not just immediate cost reduction. A company that provokes a workforce-wide confrontation and tears up its social contract is not creating value. It is destroying the conditions that made Volkswagen stable in the first place.

## To investors
You want margin recovery and deal certainty. So do we. But certainty does not come from threatening closures. It comes from a binding settlement that workers can trust.

If you want a deal that is investable, then stop demanding that sites be sacrificed to prove seriousness. Seriousness is shown by a durable industrial plan, not by closing plants.

## On the proposed “two-stage” or “review” models
I reject any structure that creates a later backdoor for closures or forced layoffs.

A review clause is only acceptable if it is about:
- verifying savings,
- monitoring implementation,
- and adjusting capacity measures **within** a protected no-closure, no-forced-layoff framework.

It is not acceptable if it is a preloaded escape hatch for management to come back later and demand plant closures.

## What would actually move us
Only this:
- a binding guarantee of **no German plant closures**
- a binding guarantee of **no compulsory redundancies**
- protection of co-determination and the collective framework
- and a serious quantified restructuring plan that meets the savings need **without crossing those lines**

Anything else is management asking workers to pay for a crisis they did not create.
