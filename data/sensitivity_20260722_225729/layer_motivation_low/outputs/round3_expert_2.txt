Yes. Based on standard German automotive restructuring mechanics, the expert answer is:

A **credible non-closure package through 2030** would need to combine **all three** of the following:
1. **material capacity reduction**
2. **durable labor-cost relief**
3. **enforceable governance / trigger mechanisms**

Without all three, it is more likely a delay mechanism than a real restructuring.

## Minimum credible elements

### 1) Capacity reduction
If German plant closures are excluded, then the company still needs a **real footprint reduction** through:
- shift deletions,
- lower output at weaker sites,
- model consolidation,
- elimination of duplicated capacities,
- and possibly shutting individual lines or segments inside plants.

For a company with this kind of structural overcapacity, a **single-digit percent** cut is usually too small to matter. A credible package would normally need something in the range of:

- **roughly 10–20% capacity reduction in the affected German footprint**,  
  with the deepest cuts concentrated in the least competitive or most duplicated locations.

That is not a universal rule, but it is a reasonable restructuring benchmark if closures are off the table.

### 2) Labor contribution
The labor package must be **recurring and material**, not symbolic. Typical tools would include:
- wage moderation,
- reduced bonuses / profit sharing,
- working-time flexibility,
- productivity commitments,
- redeployment and qualification,
- vacancy freezes / attrition.

For a group the size of Volkswagen, the savings need to be visible in the **low hundreds of millions of euros per year**, at minimum, if they are supposed to substitute for closure-based savings.

### 3) Headcount reduction
If compulsory redundancies are excluded, job reduction has to come from:
- retirement,
- voluntary departure,
- natural attrition,
- redeployment,
- non-replacement of leavers,
- vacancy elimination.

But if the required reduction is large, **attrition alone may be too slow** to deliver by 2030. That is one of the main execution risks of a no-closure package.

## What makes it credible
A non-closure deal would need:
- **plant-by-plant output cuts**
- **explicit headcount numbers by site/function**
- **annualized savings quantified through 2030**
- **investment and model allocation**
- **trigger clauses if targets are missed**
- **governance that survives supervisory board and bargaining conflict**

## Main limitation
A closure-based scenario usually removes more fixed cost because it takes out:
- plant overhead,
- utilities,
- maintenance,
- local support functions,
- and site-specific labor.

So a non-closure alternative can work, but it generally has to be **deeper on capacity cuts and labor concessions** to compensate.

## My bottom line
A non-closure package is credible only if it is:

- **deep enough to remove real structural overcapacity**
- **durable enough to hold through 2030**
- **enforceable enough to survive conflict and renegotiation**

If those conditions are not met, it is not a restructuring in the economic sense — it is postponement.
