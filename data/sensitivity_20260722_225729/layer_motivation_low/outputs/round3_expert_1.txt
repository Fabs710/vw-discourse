Based on the information in the record, the **minimum credible non-closure package through 2030** would need to combine three things at once: **material capacity reduction, durable labor-cost relief, and enforceability**. Without all three, it is likely to be seen as a delay rather than a restructuring.

## My assessment

### 1) Capacity reduction: must be real, not cosmetic
If German closures are off the table, the package still has to remove meaningful fixed cost through:
- fewer shifts,
- lower output at weaker sites,
- model consolidation,
- elimination of duplicated capacities,
- and possibly full line/area shutdowns within plants.

For VW Germany, I would treat a **single-digit percent capacity cut** as too small to matter. A credible package would usually need something more like:
- **10–20% capacity reduction at the affected German footprint**, depending on how much overcapacity exists site by site,
- with the heaviest reduction concentrated in the least competitive or most duplicated locations.

If the company cannot quantify overcapacity by plant and platform, then no one can judge sufficiency.

### 2) Labor contribution: must be large enough to change the cost base
A viable non-closure deal would normally need a mix of:
- wage moderation or temporary pay restraint,
- reduced bonuses or profit-sharing,
- longer working-time flexibility,
- fewer paid hours for some groups,
- and productivity commitments.

In industrial restructuring terms, this should not be a symbolic contribution. It needs to deliver a **material recurring annual savings block**, not a one-off accounting effect.

As a rule of thumb, if the labor package only creates modest savings relative to the brand’s structural loss, it will not be enough. For a company of this scale, the annual recurring labor-cost contribution would need to be **clearly visible in the low hundreds of millions of euros**, and likely more if no closure savings are available.

### 3) Headcount: attrition alone may not be sufficient
If the aim is to avoid compulsory redundancies, then headcount reduction must come from:
- retirement,
- voluntary departure,
- redeployment,
- not replacing leavers,
- and vacancy freezes.

But whether that is enough depends on the starting overcapacity. If the required job reduction is large, then **attrition may be too slow** to meet the 2030 target. In that case, management will likely argue that stronger instruments are needed.

So the real question is:  
**How many jobs must disappear to make the footprint competitive, and can natural attrition achieve that by 2030?**

Without a plant-by-plant headcount model, that cannot be answered reliably.

## What would make a no-closure package credible

A non-closure restructuring would need all of the following:

1. **Binding plant-level output cuts**
   - Not just promises.
   - Actual volume and shift reductions by site.

2. **A quantified labor-cost package**
   - Annualized savings with clear duration to 2030.

3. **Headcount reduction plan**
   - With explicit numbers by site and function.
   - Attrition is fine if it is enough and timed correctly.

4. **Investment and model allocation**
   - Otherwise the sites can become stranded operations.

5. **A trigger mechanism**
   - If savings or volume targets are missed, escalation rules must be defined in advance.

6. **Governance durability**
   - A deal that can survive supervisory-board politics, wage round reopening, and local resistance.

## Where such a package would likely fail

It would fail if:
- overcapacity is only shifted around, not removed;
- labor concessions are temporary while fixed costs remain;
- capacity reductions are too small or too slow;
- investment is promised but not allocated;
- or the agreement expires before the cost reset has paid through.

In that case, the package is not durable and would probably be revisited before 2030.

## On the closure-based benchmark
A closure-based scenario usually delivers the strongest fixed-cost removal because it takes out:
- plant overhead,
- local support functions,
- maintenance and utilities,
- and a large slice of site-specific labor.

That is why management prefers it as a benchmark. A no-closure package can still work, but it usually has to compensate through **more aggressive capacity cuts, more labor concessions, and stronger productivity gains** than labour initially expects.

So if the question is whether a non-closure package can be “economically equivalent,” my answer is:

- **Possible in principle**
- **Hard in practice**
- **Unlikely unless it is deep, site-specific, and tightly enforced**

## What data would be needed to assess it properly
To judge credibility, I would need:
- plant-by-plant capacity utilization,
- fixed cost by plant,
- variable vs fixed labor cost breakdown,
- product allocation through 2030,
- expected BEV and ICE mix by site,
- headcount by function and age profile,
- natural attrition potential,
- and the quantified savings from each proposed labor measure.

## Bottom line
A credible no-closure settlement through 2030 would probably require:
- **material capacity reduction**, likely in the low double digits at the affected sites,
- **meaningful recurring labor-cost concessions**,
- **substantial headcount reduction through attrition/redeployment plus possibly stronger measures if attrition is insufficient**,
- and **binding enforcement mechanisms**.

If those elements are not present, the package is likely too weak to be durable.
