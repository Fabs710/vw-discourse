Lower Saxony’s position remains firm: **we support a real restructuring, but not one built on German plant closures, compulsory redundancies, or any weakening of co-determination and the state’s veto role.**

## To management
I agree with you on the diagnosis: Volkswagen’s German footprint is too costly, and incremental measures are not enough. I also agree that a cosmetic compromise would only postpone the problem.

Where I strongly disagree is your insistence that closures and stronger workforce reductions must remain on the table as ordinary instruments until someone proves otherwise. That is the wrong starting point for a company with VW’s governance, history, and regional responsibility.

You say a no-closure approach is only acceptable if it is “economically sufficient.” Fine — but then bring a **credible plant-by-plant package** that proves what can be removed without closing German sites. Do not ask the state and workforce to accept closure threats as the benchmark for seriousness. That is not negotiation; it is leverage.

## To the works council and IG Metall
I agree with you on the core red lines:
- no German plant closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- protection of sites and jobs,
- and preservation of co-determination.

Those are not sentimental positions; they are legitimate safeguards for the company’s industrial base and the regional economy.

Where I would press you is this: a simple “no” is not enough by itself. If we want to keep the plants open and still restore competitiveness, we need **real, measurable adjustments**:
- capacity reductions,
- shift changes,
- productivity gains,
- working-time flexibility,
- redeployment,
- and a meaningful employee contribution.

But all of that must happen **within a binding no-closure framework**. I will not support a “review clause” that becomes a backdoor to later closures or forced layoffs.

## To the controlling owners and investors
You are right that the package must be economically real. I do not dispute that. But you are underweighting the value destruction that a closure fight in Germany would create:
- political backlash,
- industrial conflict,
- reputational damage,
- supplier disruption,
- and long-term loss of trust.

That is not a side effect. It is part of the economics.

Volkswagen’s value is not just margin on paper. It is also its industrial legitimacy, skilled workforce, and regional embeddedness. A restructuring that destroys those assets in the name of efficiency would be self-defeating.

## On the expert’s point
I take the expert’s assessment seriously: a non-closure settlement has to be **deep, durable, and enforceable**. I agree.

What I do **not** accept is the leap from “deep adjustment is needed” to “therefore closures are still needed unless proven otherwise.” That is not established here.

The expert has made clear that non-closure alternatives can work if they are substantial enough. That is the path we should test first, not treat closures as the default truth.

## What would move Lower Saxony
We can support a hard but fair settlement if it includes:

1. **No German plant closures**
2. **No compulsory redundancies**
3. **Real capacity reduction through consolidation and shift changes**
4. **A durable employee contribution**
5. **Management and owner contributions as well**
6. **Binding investment and future-model commitments**
7. **Protection of co-determination and the state’s veto role**
8. **A credible path to competitiveness without hollowing out the regional economy**

## What will not move us
We will not be moved by claims that:
- closures are “inevitable,”
- forced layoffs are the only serious tool,
- or site protection is incompatible with competitiveness.

Those are assertions, not proof.

## My line on the current discussion
I am not prepared to let the company drift into a closure-first logic. If management wants Lower Saxony’s support, it must table a genuine alternative that preserves sites, protects jobs, and still delivers measurable savings.

So yes: bring numbers. Bring a plant-level plan. Bring an enforceable settlement.  
But do **not** come back with a package that preserves the right to close plants later while asking us to sign away our red lines now.

That would be unacceptable.

## Bottom line
Lower Saxony is prepared to back a serious restructuring. We are **not** prepared to back German plant closures or compulsory redundancies.

If Volkswagen is to remain a strong industrial anchor, then the solution must protect its German base, not sacrifice it.
