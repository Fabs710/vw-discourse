I’ll respond plainly.

I agree with the expert and with the owners/investors on the central diagnosis: **Volkswagen’s German footprint is too expensive and too large for the market we face.** Incremental tweaks, temporary working-time changes, or symbolic concessions will not restore competitiveness. If we settle for that, we will be back in crisis before 2030 with an even weaker cost base.

I also agree with Lower Saxony and the union on one important point: **a destructive confrontation would be value-destructive**. Strikes damage output, reputation, and cash generation. That is exactly why we need a **durable** agreement, not a cosmetic one.

Where I disagree sharply is on the idea that **plant closures and compulsory redundancies are categorically off the table**.

### To the works council and IG Metall
You are asking for:
- no closures,
- no forced redundancies,
- binding guarantees for every German site,
- and strong co-determination,

while at the same time expecting management to deliver a major structural reset in cost and capacity.

That combination does **not** answer the economic problem.

The expert’s assessment was useful precisely because it confirms what management has been saying: **non-closure alternatives can work only if they are deep, durable, and enforceable — and even then they usually do not match the full structural savings of footprint rationalization.** That means your red lines may preserve jobs in the short term, but they do not yet solve the competitiveness gap.

I am not interested in “no closures” as a slogan. I am interested in:
- real capacity reduction,
- lower labor cost,
- simpler footprint,
- and a credible path to margin recovery.

If you can deliver that **without** closures, I will judge it on the economics. But I will not pretend that a promise of “future products” or “joint process improvements” alone is enough.

### To Lower Saxony
I appreciate the insistence on industrial legitimacy and regional responsibility. You are right that Volkswagen cannot govern itself like a faceless spreadsheet. But political preference cannot become a substitute for restructuring reality.

If the package leaves the German cost base structurally uncompetitive, then it fails the company — and ultimately the region too. So I will not support a settlement that freezes the footprint and then calls it a solution.

### To the owners and investors
I agree with your basic point: **deal certainty must translate into real economics**. But that also means backing management if we insist on a package that actually works. A weak compromise dressed up as “social balance” will not protect equity value.

### What I am prepared to accept
I am **open** to alternatives to closures if they are genuinely equivalent in economic effect. That means a package must include:

1. **Material and durable capacity reduction**
2. **A substantial labour-cost contribution**
3. **Headcount reduction through attrition, redeployment, and targeted separation where necessary**
4. **Enforceable multi-year commitments**
5. **A credible investment and product plan through 2030**
6. **Governance that enables implementation, not paralysis**

### What I will not accept
I will not accept:
- a cosmetic compromise,
- a package that preserves overcapacity,
- a “no closures” deal that leaves the cost problem intact,
- or a settlement that blocks the company from reaching the return-on-sales target.

### Bottom line
The burden cannot be carried by management alone, and it cannot be shifted entirely onto capital either. But the workforce also cannot insist on absolute protection while the company’s competitive position erodes.

So let’s be precise:  
**If there is a non-closure solution that truly delivers equivalent, durable savings, I will consider it. If not, then we have to be honest that structural footprint reduction, including closures where necessary, remains on the table.**

If the parties want a serious next step, then we need plant-level, quantified options — not general declarations.
