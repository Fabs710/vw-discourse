Let me respond directly and without ambiguity.

## To the works council
I agree with you on the essentials:
- Volkswagen needs a real restructuring, not cosmetics.
- A durable deal through 2030 is necessary.
- A one-sided burden shift onto workers is unacceptable.

But I also need to be clear: repeated declarations of “no closures, no compulsory redundancies” are not enough on their own to win a settlement. They are our **red lines**, yes. But if we stop there, management will keep claiming there is no economically credible alternative. So we must be prepared to present a **hard, quantified labour-side contribution** that makes the no-closure path real.

That does **not** mean retreating on closures or forced layoffs. It means strengthening our case with numbers, not slogans.

## To management
I disagree with your framing in several important ways.

### 1) “Equivalent to closures” is not the only meaningful benchmark
You keep treating closure-based savings as the reference point, as if a plant closure were the neutral economic standard. It is not. It is a destructive option that wipes out:
- jobs,
- skills,
- supplier networks,
- apprenticeship structures,
- regional stability,
- and trust in the company.

So I reject the idea that labour must prove a no-closure package is “close enough” to closures before it can be considered serious. The real test is whether the package is:
- **durable**,
- **binding**,
- **measurable**,
- and sufficient to restore competitiveness **without** destroying sites.

### 2) Threat-posture is not negotiation
You say you are “open” to alternatives while keeping closures and stronger workforce reductions on the table. That is not openness; it is leverage through fear.

If you want a serious settlement, bring a **plant-by-plant quantified alternative** and show the savings. Do not ask the workforce to accept the threat of closure as the price of being allowed to negotiate.

### 3) You are understating execution risk
A closure in Germany is not a clean accounting measure. It would likely trigger:
- industrial conflict,
- legal challenge,
- political backlash,
- reputational damage,
- and long-term mistrust.

That is not efficiency. That is a high-risk path with potentially huge hidden costs.

## To the owners and investors
I agree with you on one point: Volkswagen must be economically real, not just socially comforting.

But you are wrong if you think “harder options” automatically equal better value creation. A deal that tears up labour relations and provokes prolonged strikes can destroy more value than it saves. Your focus on margin recovery is legitimate, but margin recovery that comes with structural labour conflict is not a free gain.

What you should demand is:
- a measurable cost reset,
- a binding investment plan,
- and durable savings through 2030.

What you should **not** demand is a closure-first logic as the only proof of seriousness.

## To Lower Saxony
I appreciate your support for no closures and no compulsory redundancies. That matters, and it is politically important.

But I need you to be equally firm that any “review” or “trigger” mechanism must **not** become a hidden backdoor to later closures or forced layoffs. If a review clause exists, it must be about:
- monitoring savings,
- verifying implementation,
- and correcting course **within a protected no-closure framework**.

Not about keeping the knife for later.

## On the expert’s intervention
The expert has clarified something important:
A non-closure package must be **deep, durable, and enforceable**. I agree.

What the expert has **not** shown is that closures are necessary. That distinction matters. The expert has in fact supported our position that alternatives can work if they are serious enough.

So yes, we need numbers. But those numbers must be used to design a no-closure restructuring, not to retroactively justify closures.

## What we are prepared to do
IG Metall is still prepared to negotiate a serious package with real labour-side contributions, including:

- working-time flexibility
- temporary wage moderation
- bonus restraint
- productivity and process improvements
- redeployment and retraining
- vacancy freezes / attrition
- capacity reductions through shifts and model consolidation
- investment and future-product commitments

That is a real contribution. It is not a free ride.

But we will only sign such a package if it comes with:
1. **no German plant closures**
2. **no compulsory redundancies**
3. **binding site protection**
4. **protection of the collective wage framework**
5. **management and owner contributions as well**

## What we will not accept
Let me repeat the line clearly:
- no closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- no skeleton operations,
- no weakening of co-determination,
- no one-sided burden shift onto workers.

That remains non-negotiable.

## What would move us
A **plant-by-plant quantified plan** showing:
- how much capacity is removed without closures,
- how much recurring labour-cost savings are created,
- what management and owners contribute,
- what investment and product allocation follows,
- and how the settlement is durable through 2030.

If management can produce that within the no-closure framework, we are ready to talk seriously. If management insists that only closures prove seriousness, then it is management refusing the basis for a fair deal.
