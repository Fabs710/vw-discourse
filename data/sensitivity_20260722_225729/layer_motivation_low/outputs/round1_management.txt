I’ll be equally clear from management’s side: **the status quo is not an option**.

I hear the demand for no closures and no compulsory redundancies. I understand why that is your opening position. But it does not answer the actual problem in front of us: **Volkswagen’s German cost and capacity base is structurally too high** for the market we face. European demand is shrinking, competition is intensifying, and we cannot finance our 2030 product and technology agenda on an uncompetitive domestic footprint.

So let me respond directly:

- **I agree** that we need a viable future for all German operations, not chaos.
- **I agree** that restructuring should be sustainable, not just a one-off headline.
- **I disagree** that binding guarantees on every plant and a blanket no-layoff commitment can be the starting point.
- **I strongly disagree** that we can solve this with working-time tweaks and temporary contributions alone.

Those measures may help at the margin, but they do **not** create the structural cost reduction we need. If we leave the cost base fundamentally unchanged, we will be back here in a short time — weaker, not stronger.

Management is prepared to negotiate, but the outcome must deliver **real and durable savings**:
1. **Material capacity reduction**
2. **A substantial labour-cost contribution**
3. **Structural simplification of the footprint**
4. **A credible path to restore margins and fund future investment**

That means I am not prepared to rule out **plant closures**, and I am not prepared to pretend that every site can be insulated from the adjustment. If alternatives can produce the same economic result, we can discuss them. But they must be **equivalent in scale, durability, and credibility** — not cosmetic.

On compulsory redundancies: I would prefer to avoid them, because they are disruptive and legally complex. But I will not commit to a blanket exclusion if we do not get the necessary restructuring result. What I can say is that I am open to negotiated solutions that minimize forced exits, if the savings are real and enforceable.

On co-determination: yes, the workforce has a decisive role in shaping the solution. But co-determination is not a veto on reality. A deal must reflect the economic facts, not wish them away.

So my message back is simple:

- **No cosmetic compromise**
- **No preservation of overcapacity**
- **No settlement that leaves German plants uncompetitive**
- **Yes to negotiation, but only on a package that restores competitiveness**

If the works council and IG Metall are serious about a future for Volkswagen, then they need to engage with the scale of the problem — not just the protection of the existing structure.
