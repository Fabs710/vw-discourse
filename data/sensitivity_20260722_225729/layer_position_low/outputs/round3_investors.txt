I’ll be direct.

## Where I agree
I agree with management, the controlling owners, and the finance/operations assessment on the main point: Volkswagen needs a **durable, material, and credible fixed-cost reset**. Cosmetic concessions are not acceptable. If the company keeps an uncompetitive cost base, shareholders will not get a sustainable return, and the market will continue to punish the valuation.

I also agree that prolonged industrial conflict is value-destructive. Strikes, uncertainty, and delayed implementation all erode margins and destroy near-term shareholder value.

## Where I disagree
I do **not** support a package that protects every site, avoids meaningful structural change, and still claims to restore competitiveness.

The key issue is not whether closures are politically uncomfortable. It is whether the final package is **economically sufficient** and **credible to execute**.

So far, I have heard a lot of red lines from labor and Lower Saxony, but I have **not** seen a fully quantified no-closure plan that convincingly replaces the savings and fixed-cost removal that closures can deliver. The finance/operations expert said a no-closure route is possible only if it is **large, permanent, and execution-heavy**. That is not yet the same as saying it is sufficient.

## To the works council and IG Metall
You are right that plant closures are not desirable. But from a capital-market perspective, “not desirable” is not the relevant test. The test is:

- Does the package restore margins?
- Does it remove enough fixed cost?
- Does it reduce overcapacity materially?
- Does it do so with implementation certainty?

Right now, your position is still mostly a boundary:
- no closures
- no compulsory redundancies
- binding job and site guarantees
- limited reversible concessions

That may be socially understandable, but it does **not yet answer the economics**. If all German sites remain open, then show how the cost base is permanently reduced enough to restore return on sales. Otherwise, you are asking shareholders to accept structural underperformance in exchange for peace.

That is not acceptable.

## To Lower Saxony
I agree that closures are not automatically the only route. But I do **not** accept a political veto masquerading as an economic conclusion.

If you want to block closures, then you need to support a no-closure package that is strong enough to deliver comparable savings. That means accepting:
- significant shift reductions,
- permanent capacity concentration,
- real headcount reduction through attrition and voluntary exits,
- wage/bonus restraint,
- and measurable overhead cuts.

If the state insists on keeping everything intact, then it is prioritizing regional symbolism over shareholder value and corporate competitiveness. I will not endorse that.

## To management
You are right to demand more than temporary fixes. But I also want discipline from management here: do **not** force closures if a serious no-closure plan can actually deliver the needed savings.

That said, the burden remains on you to quantify the gap. I want a clear answer to:
- what annual savings are required,
- what a serious no-closure package delivers,
- what remains unfunded,
- and why that remaining gap cannot be closed without deeper restructuring.

Until that is shown, I will not treat closures as automatically necessary.

## To the controlling owners
I agree that a value-destructive conflict is unacceptable. I also agree that a cosmetic compromise would be worse than no deal.

But I need to be equally clear: I do **not** support a package that preserves legacy structures at the expense of competitiveness. If the no-closure solution does not reach the required savings, then it is not value-preserving. If closures are needed to close that gap, then the market will expect them to be used.

## What I would support
I would support **whatever package credibly restores competitiveness**, meaning it must deliver:

1. **durable annual fixed-cost savings**
2. **material capacity reduction**
3. **a credible path to margin recovery**
4. **execution certainty**
5. **limited industrial disruption**

That could be a no-closure solution, but only if it is truly equivalent in financial terms. At the moment, that has not been demonstrated.

## What I would not support
I will not support:
- a cosmetic compromise,
- a deal that merely postpones the problem,
- or a package that keeps the company structurally uncompetitive in the name of avoiding hard choices.

## Needed next step
Before this can move forward, I want a **side-by-side quantified comparison** of:
- the minimum credible no-closure package,
- the closure-based alternative,
- annual savings under each,
- one-time restructuring costs,
- headcount impact,
- timing to run-rate savings,
- and site-by-site capacity effects.

## Bottom line
My position is simple:
- Volkswagen needs a hard reset.
- A weak compromise is unacceptable.
- A prolonged conflict is unacceptable.
- **No closures is acceptable only if it still delivers durable, competitive margins.**
- If it does not, then sentiment cannot override economics.

I will back the outcome that restores risk-adjusted shareholder value — nothing less.
