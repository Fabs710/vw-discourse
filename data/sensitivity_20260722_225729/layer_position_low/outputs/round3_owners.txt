I will be direct.

## Where I agree

I agree with nearly everyone on the **diagnosis**:

- Volkswagen needs a **durable competitiveness reset**.
- Cosmetic measures are not enough.
- A prolonged conflict would damage value, operations, and reputation.
- The company needs **real fixed-cost reduction**, not just statements.

I also agree with the expert that a **no-closure solution is possible in principle** — but only if it is **large, permanent, and execution-heavy**.

That is the key point. “Possible in principle” is not the same as “proven sufficient.”

---

## Where I disagree with labor and Lower Saxony

I do **not** accept the repeated claim that because plant closures are undesirable, they are therefore unnecessary.

That has **not** been demonstrated.

What has been shown is this:

- a no-closure package can work **if it is strong enough**
- but it must remove **real fixed cost**
- it must reduce effective footprint materially
- it must be durable, not reversible
- and it must be credible in execution

So when the works council and IG Metall say:
- no closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- binding guarantees through 2030,
- limited concessions only,

my response is: **that does not yet add up economically.**

I understand the political and social motive. But I will not support a package that protects every legacy arrangement and calls that restructuring.

That would be value-destructive delay.

---

## Where I disagree with management

I also do **not** accept management’s implicit move to closures as the default answer.

The finance/operations analysis has made clear that closures are **not automatically required**. They are one option, and a costly one. Before anyone pushes that route, management still owes us a quantified demonstration of:

1. the **remaining savings gap** after a serious no-closure package,
2. the **site-by-site capacity effects** of internal restructuring,
3. the **headcount reduction path** under voluntary/attrition-based measures,
4. the **timing to run-rate savings**,
5. and why that package is insufficient.

Until that is shown, closures are not a necessity. They are a preference.

And I do not yet see a proven case that they are the best value outcome.

---

## To the works council and IG Metall

I hear your red lines. I do not dismiss them.

But I am not prepared to endorse a package that asks capital and management to absorb all the strategic pain while the operating structure remains substantially unchanged.

If you want to avoid closures, then the alternative must be **hard**, not symbolic. That means you need to be able to accept:

- significant shift reductions
- meaningful capacity concentration inside existing plants
- voluntary exits at scale
- attrition and redeployment
- working-time flexibility
- bonus restraint / labor-cost contribution
- productivity gains with monitoring
- management overhead cuts
- investment commitments tied to performance

If your answer is only “all sites stay open and no forced layoffs,” then it is not yet a solution. It is a boundary.

And I respect boundaries, but I do not mistake them for arithmetic.

---

## To Lower Saxony

I understand your responsibility to the regional base. It is real.

But I will be equally clear: I will not support a stance that protects sites without demanding a package that actually restores competitiveness. Regional responsibility is not the same as preserving every structure in place.

You are right that closures are not the only path. The expert confirmed that.  
But you also need to accept that **no-closure** only works if it is a **serious industrial reset**, not a political shield.

If you want our support for a no-closure route, it must be backed by:
- measurable cost reduction,
- visible capacity adjustment,
- and credible execution discipline.

---

## To institutional investors

Your demand for credibility is reasonable. But do not confuse “decisive” with “closure-based.”

What you should want is:
- durable savings,
- limited disruption,
- and a path to restored margins.

If a no-closure package can genuinely deliver that, it is value-preserving. If it cannot, then we will have to revisit the options. But we are **not there yet**.

---

## My governance position

As controlling owners, our priority is not theatrical toughness. It is **long-term enterprise value**.

That means we will support a restructuring only if it satisfies three tests:

1. **it materially improves competitiveness**
2. **it is credible and durable**
3. **it avoids unnecessary destruction of value through prolonged industrial war**

On that basis:

- I am **open** to a serious no-closure package.
- I am **not** endorsing closures as the first resort.
- I am **not** supporting a cosmetic compromise.
- I am **not** prepared to back a deal that leaves Volkswagen structurally uncompetitive.

---

## What I want next

We need the numbers, not more repetition of positions.

I want a **formal side-by-side comparison** of:

- a fully developed no-closure package,
- a closure-based package,
- annual savings under each,
- one-time restructuring cost under each,
- headcount impact under each,
- timing to run-rate savings,
- and site-by-site capacity effects.

## Bottom line

My position remains:

- Volkswagen must become materially more competitive.
- A prolonged conflict is unacceptable.
- A cosmetic compromise is unacceptable.
- **Closures are not yet proven necessary.**
- But a no-closure solution must be **large, durable, and economically equivalent** to the savings closures would provide.

If that can be shown, I will support the no-closure route.

If it cannot, then the question changes. But until then, I will not accept closures as the starting assumption.
