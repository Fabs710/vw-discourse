I’ll be direct.

## Where I agree
I agree with management, the controlling owners, and the expert on the central economic point: **Volkswagen needs a durable, material cost reset**, not a symbolic compromise. From a shareholder perspective, the main risk is not conflict in the abstract; it is **continued structural underperformance**. If German operations stay uncompetitive, margins remain weak, the market discounts the equity, and capital is tied up in a business that cannot earn its cost of capital.

I also agree that:
- prolonged industrial conflict destroys value,
- temporary concessions are not enough,
- and the restructuring must be credible enough to restore margins, not just postpone the problem.

## Where I disagree
I do **not** accept the current labor/state position that all of the following can be guaranteed at once:
- no plant closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- binding job guarantees through 2030,
- binding site guarantees through 2030,
- only limited employee sacrifice,

**and still** expect the company to achieve the savings needed to restore competitiveness.

That is still a **claim**, not a demonstrated solution.

The expert has been clear: a no-closure path is possible **in principle**, but only if it is **large, permanent, and execution-heavy**. That is the key phrase. “Possible in principle” is not the same as “sufficient in practice.” Until the numbers show equivalence, I cannot support treating closures as off-limits.

## To the works council and IG Metall
I respect that you want to protect jobs and sites. But from a shareholder perspective, the red lines you are drawing still do not answer the economic question:

- How much fixed cost disappears?
- How quickly?
- With what certainty?
- And with what durability?

Right now, your position is mainly a **boundary**. That is understandable politically, but it is not enough financially.

If all German plants remain open, then I need to see:
- substantial shift reductions,
- meaningful capacity concentration,
- significant voluntary exits and attrition,
- real wage/bonus restraint,
- overhead cuts,
- and a verifiable path to run-rate savings.

If those elements do not add up to a competitive cost base, then “no closures” becomes a value-destructive constraint, not a solution.

## To Lower Saxony
I agree that closures are damaging and should not be pursued casually. But I do **not** accept a political veto being treated as an economic argument. The question is not whether closures are popular. It is whether they are avoidable **without leaving the company structurally impaired**.

If you want the no-closure route to prevail, then support a package that can be independently shown to:
- close the savings gap,
- materially reduce capacity,
- and restore margin performance.

Otherwise, you are asking shareholders to carry the downside of a protected structure with no proven upside.

## To management
I agree with you on the need for a structural reset, but I want to be equally clear: **do not use closures as a shortcut if a serious no-closure package can truly deliver the same economics**. The burden is still on management to quantify the gap properly.

What I still need to see is:
1. the minimum annual savings target,
2. the fully loaded no-closure package,
3. the residual savings gap,
4. and why that gap cannot be closed without footprint reduction.

Without that, the case for closures remains incomplete.

## To the controlling owners
I agree that a cosmetic compromise would be worse than a hard decision. I also agree that long-term enterprise value matters more than short-term political comfort.

But value preservation cuts both ways:
- a weak no-closure deal can leave the company uncompetitive,
- while a closure-based plan can trigger years of conflict and execution risk.

So I want the decision made on **net value**, not ideology.

## What I would support
I would support **whatever package credibly restores competitive margins**, provided it meets these conditions:
- durable annual fixed-cost savings,
- real capacity reduction,
- execution certainty,
- and a credible path back to return-on-sales target.

That could still be a no-closure route, but only if it is financially equivalent.

## What I would not support
I will not support:
- a cosmetic compromise,
- a settlement that merely defers the issue,
- or an arrangement that preserves every site while leaving the cost base structurally too high.
