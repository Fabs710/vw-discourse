I’ll be equally direct.

## Where I agree
I agree with everyone on one central point: Volkswagen needs a **durable, structural reset**. No one in this room should be pretending that a cosmetic package, a temporary accounting fix, or a symbolic concession will restore competitiveness.

I also agree that:
- the company has a real cost problem,
- overcapacity is part of that problem,
- the outcome must be sustainable through 2030,
- and industrial conflict would be damaging.

On that, there is no real disagreement.

## Where I disagree
What I do **not** accept is the continued insistence that we can preserve:
- all German plants,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- binding job and site guarantees through 2030,
- only limited and bounded employee contributions,
- and still produce the level of structural savings this company needs.

That is not a credible restructuring plan unless someone can prove it with hard numbers. So far, nobody has.

The works council, IG Metall, and Lower Saxony keep repeating the same red lines. I understand those red lines politically. I do **not** accept them economically as sufficient on their own.

## To the works council
You say the burden of proof is on management. Fine — I accept that we must prove the economic case. But you have still not answered the hard question:

**What exact, quantified package of permanent savings do you believe fully replaces the savings from footprint reduction?**

Not general principles. Not trust language. Not “management must do more.” I mean:
- how many jobs disappear through attrition and voluntary exits,
- how much volume is concentrated,
- how much fixed cost is removed,
- what labor cost falls permanently,
- and how you close the gap without either closures or compulsory redundancies.

You say a no-closure path is possible in principle. I am saying that is not enough. “Possible in principle” is not the same as “sufficient in practice.”

And let me be blunt: management did not create this crisis overnight. But it is now management’s responsibility to fix it. That fix cannot be reduced to preserving every historical guarantee while expecting the market to forgive us.

## To IG Metall
You say closures are the most destructive route. They are destructive, yes. But that is not the relevant test. The relevant test is whether the company can remain competitive.

If you want to keep closures off the table, then the alternative must be **hard enough to be economically equivalent**. That means:
- substantial shift reductions,
- material capacity concentration,
- meaningful voluntary exits,
- attrition at scale,
- working-time flexibility,
- wage/bonus restraint,
- productivity gains that actually stick,
- and management overhead reductions.

If those measures are only partial or reversible, then they are not equivalent. They are delay.

I am not prepared to sign up to a deal where the workforce’s contribution is framed as limited, while the company’s structural problems remain largely intact.

## To Lower Saxony
I understand your political role and your responsibility for the regional base. But your position cannot be reduced to “no closures” as a permanent veto without a credible alternative.

You say closures are not necessary. That has not been demonstrated.

The expert has made something clear: a no-closure route can work only if it is **large, permanent, and execution-heavy**. That is a very high bar. If you want to block closures, then help deliver a package that actually meets it. If not, you are protecting the status quo and calling it industrial policy.

That is not acceptable to us, and it should not be acceptable to the owners or investors either.

## To the controlling owners
I agree with you on one important point: a cosmetic compromise would be worse than a hard decision.

But I disagree if the conclusion is that closures are automatically the answer. They are not automatically the answer. They are one route — a costly one — and we still do not have a fully proven case that they are unavoidable.

At the same time, I will not pretend that a weak no-closure package is sufficient just because it is politically easier. The question is not whether we like closures. The question is whether the business needs a deeper footprint reset than labor is willing to acknowledge.

That is why I keep asking for the numbers.

## To institutional investors
You are right to demand execution certainty and durable savings. I agree with that. But you should not treat closures as a moral preference or a spreadsheet reflex.

Your real interest is value creation. That means:
- restoring margins,
- reducing fixed cost,
- removing overcapacity,
- and creating room to invest.

If a no-closure package can genuinely do that, it is acceptable. If it cannot, then we should not hide behind sentiment. But nobody has yet shown that the no-closure route reaches the same economic endpoint.

## My position remains unchanged
Let me state this as clearly as possible:

We are prepared to negotiate **means**:
- internal capacity concentration,
- shift reductions,
- voluntary exits,
- attrition,
- working-time changes,
- productivity improvements,
- limited wage and bonus restraint,
- management overhead cuts,
- and investment commitments.

We are **not** prepared to accept an outcome that leaves the company structurally uncompetitive.

That means we need:
- a durable reduction in labor costs,
- a real reduction in capacity,
- and a package that restores the brand’s return on sales.

If the no-closure route can do that, then show it.

If it cannot, then closures remain on the table. I will not rule them out just because they are unpopular. I will also not choose them blindly without proof that the alternative fails.

## What is missing
The discussion has now become repetitive because the core missing piece is still missing:

**a fully quantified, plant-level comparison of the no-closure package versus a closure-based restructuring.**

We need:
- annual savings gap,
- site-by-site capacity effects,
- headcount impact,
- one-time costs,
- and timing to run-rate savings.

## Bottom line
I am not moving off the core requirement:
- Volkswagen must become structurally cheaper,
- structurally leaner,
- and structurally more competitive.

I am open on the mechanism.

But I am **not** open to a cosmetic no-closure compromise that leaves the cost base uncompetitive.

If the employee side wants to keep closures off the table, then it must bring a package that is truly equivalent in economic effect. Until then, closures cannot be removed from consideration.
