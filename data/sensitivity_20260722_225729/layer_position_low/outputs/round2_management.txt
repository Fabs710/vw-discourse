I will respond directly, because we are still not talking about a package that matches the scale of the problem.

## Where I agree
I agree on one point with all sides: Volkswagen needs a **durable** solution, not a symbolic one. A cosmetic deal, a temporary pause, or a formula that merely postpones the same crisis to 2027 or 2028 would be irresponsible.

I also agree that a prolonged industrial conflict would damage the company. That is true for production, for reputation, and for value. Nobody benefits from months of paralysis.

## Where I disagree
I do **not** accept the premise that we can achieve the required competitiveness improvement while preserving all German sites, all guarantees, and the current cost structure.

Let me be explicit: the company is not facing a small efficiency gap. We are facing a **structural cost and capacity problem**. If we do not reduce the footprint and lower labour costs materially, then we will not restore margins, we will not fund the product cycle we need, and we will not be competitive against lower-cost rivals.

So when I hear:
- no plant closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- binding job guarantees through 2030,
- limited reversible concessions only,

I have to say: that does **not** yet add up to a viable answer.

## To the works council
You say closures are a management choice, not a law of nature. That is correct in the abstract. But the economic reality is that some facilities and structures are carrying costs that the business can no longer absorb. If you rule out the instruments that can deliver real structural change, you are not solving the problem — you are pushing it into the future.

You also ask for binding site and job guarantees through 2030 before any serious discussion. I cannot commit to preserving every current structure until 2030 if the business case does not support it. That would be a false promise, and it would weaken the company further.

What I can say is this:
- if labour can help deliver **real, durable savings**
- if capacity is reduced in a way that materially lowers fixed costs
- if labour costs come down in a meaningful and sustainable way
- if the package restores competitiveness rather than just protecting the status quo

then management is prepared to discuss how that is implemented.

But I will not sign up to a framework that locks in uncompetitive structures and calls that a solution.

## To IG Metall
I hear the language of “shared sacrifice,” but so far the proposed sacrifices from your side are too small and too reversible to solve the actual problem. Temporary bonus waivers and limited flexibility measures are not enough if the underlying cost base remains too high.

You say management and owners must also contribute. That is true, and we are. Management is putting forward a restructuring that carries significant operational and reputational risk because we believe it is necessary to secure the company’s future. Owners are being asked to support a reset because the current model is not sustainable.

But there is a difference between contribution and pretending that capital can compensate indefinitely for structural inefficiency.

I also reject the idea that asking for deep restructuring is “submission.” It is accountability. If Volkswagen is to remain a leading industrial company, then all sides must face reality.

## To Lower Saxony
I respect the political concern, but I cannot treat regional preference as overriding the company’s economics. We need a solution that keeps Volkswagen viable, not one that simply avoids difficult decisions because they are politically uncomfortable.

If the state wants to help preserve industrial strength in Lower Saxony, then the best contribution is to support a package that actually restores competitiveness. That means accepting that some capacity will need to be removed and some labour-cost contribution will need to be made.

## To the controlling owners and investors
I agree with you that a cosmetic package would destroy value. I also agree that prolonged conflict is bad for value. Where I would caution is against reading that as a reason to hesitate indefinitely. We need a decisive package, not endless ambiguity.

## The minimum I require
If we are to move forward, the package must contain all of the following:
1. **material and durable capacity reduction**
2. **a genuine labour-cost contribution**
3. **structural savings large enough to restore the brand’s return-on-sales path**
4. **a credible implementation path within the co-determination framework**
5. **investment protection for future products and technology**

What I cannot accept is a deal that preserves every site, forbids every hard instrument, and asks management to declare victory on the basis of limited concessions.

## My position on the options
- **Plant closures**: not a goal in themselves, but they remain on the table if they are necessary to achieve the required structural savings.
- **Forced redundancies**: not our preferred route, but I will not rule out all workforce reductions if no credible alternative is offered.
- **Alternative measures**: yes, if they truly produce equivalent durable savings. So far, I have not heard a package that convincingly does.

## Bottom line
Management is ready to negotiate, but not to accept a solution that leaves Volkswagen uncompetitive. If the employee side wants to avoid closures and redundancies, then it must bring a package that genuinely replaces them in economic terms — not just in language.

Until then, I will continue to insist on:
- deep structural cost reduction,
- real capacity adjustment,
- and a settlement that restores Volkswagen’s competitiveness for 2030 and beyond.
