# 1) Executive view

From an operations economics standpoint:

- A **no-closure package can work** only if it still removes a **large, permanent fixed-cost base**.
- If the savings gap is truly structural, then **capacity reduction without closures** must be substantial and must be paired with:
  - headcount reduction through attrition/voluntary exits,
  - shift and working-time changes,
  - overhead cuts,
  - and labor-cost concessions.

My assessment is:

- **Scenario A: no closures, no forced redundancies** is feasible only if it delivers roughly **€1.0–1.5 billion per year** of durable savings, depending on current gap and volume assumptions.
- **Scenario B: one or more plant closures** is more likely to achieve the same savings faster and with better fixed-cost absorption, but it comes with much higher execution complexity and one-time restructuring cash costs.

In short:  
**No closures is possible, but only with deep internal footprint reduction.**  
If the package is limited to soft measures, it will probably **not be sufficient**.

# 4) What “equivalent” no-closure savings actually require

If closures are off the table, the company must replicate the savings through other levers.

A closure saves money because it removes:
- site-fixed labor,
- maintenance,
- utilities,
- logistics complexity,
- local management layers,
- and a share of indirect overhead.

To match that without closures, the no-closure package needs all of the following:

1. **Lower direct labor cost per vehicle**
2. **Lower indirect labor**
3. **Fewer shifts / lower operating hours**
4. **Higher utilization in the remaining plants**
5. **Permanent volume concentration**
6. **Headcount removal that is not just temporary**

If those components are weak, the economics usually do not add up.

---

# 5) Practical test of sufficiency

A good decision rule is:

## A no-closure deal is probably sufficient if it achieves:
- at least **€1bn annual durable savings**, and preferably more if the structural gap is large
- visible reduction in fixed cost, not just one-off cash savings
- a credible reduction in headcount and plant complexity
- a utilization improvement at the remaining German plants

## A no-closure deal is probably insufficient if it only achieves:
- temporary bonus restraint
- short-term pay cuts
- soft flexibility promises
- hiring freezes without real exits
- vague productivity targets without enforcement

Those measures help, but they rarely solve a structural competitiveness problem on their own.

---

# 6) Cash cost versus P&L savings

This is important operationally.

A restructuring can improve annual profit while still being painful on cash in the first 1–2 years.

### No-closure package
- Lower one-time cash cost
- Slower savings ramp
- More uncertainty about whether enough jobs actually leave

### Closure-based package
- Higher one-time cash cost
- Faster and more reliable fixed-cost removal
- Better long-run unit economics if executed well

So from a finance perspective, the choice is often:

- **lower near-term pain, higher risk of under-delivery**  
  versus
- **higher near-term pain, higher probability of target attainment**

- deep capacity reduction inside existing sites,
