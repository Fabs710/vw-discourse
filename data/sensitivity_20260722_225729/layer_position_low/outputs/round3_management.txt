I’ll be equally direct.

## Where I agree
I agree on three points that have been made repeatedly:

1. **Volkswagen needs a durable solution, not a cosmetic one.**
2. **A prolonged industrial conflict would damage the company.**
3. **If a no-closure package can truly remove enough fixed cost, then it is worth considering.**

That last point matters. I am not asking for closures because they are ideologically attractive. I am asking for a restructuring that actually restores competitiveness.

## Where I disagree
What I do **not** accept is the repeated claim that we can preserve:
- all German plants,
- all current guarantees,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- only limited and reversible concessions,

and still solve a structural cost and capacity problem of this scale.

That is not a plan. That is a hope.

The finance and operations assessment has already shown the key issue: a no-closure path is only viable if it delivers **large, durable, fixed-cost reduction**. So far, I have heard strong red lines from labor and Lower Saxony, but not a package that reliably closes the savings gap.

## To the works council
You say the burden of proof is on management. On the diagnosis, fine — we have made it. German costs are too high, overcapacity exists, and margins are too weak for the investment cycle ahead.

But you then jump to the conclusion that plant closures are unnecessary without showing how your alternative reaches the same economic result.

That is the real gap.

You keep saying:
- no closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- binding site guarantees through 2030,
- binding job guarantees through 2030,
- limited employee contribution only.

I am saying plainly: **that does not yet add up to the needed structural reset**.

If you want me to take closures off the table, then you need to show me:
- which sites lose substantial volume,
- how much fixed cost disappears permanently,
- how headcount falls at scale,
- what wage and flexibility concessions are durable, not temporary,
- and why the resulting network is truly competitive.

Until then, “no closures” is a political demand, not an operational solution.

## To IG Metall
I appreciate that you are prepared to discuss capacity reductions, shift changes, voluntary exits, and bonus restraint. But again: the scale matters.

Temporary waivers and soft flexibility are not enough if the underlying cost base remains structurally above target.

And I reject the framing that management wants “submission.” No. We want a company that can survive and invest through 2030. If we do not restore the brand’s return on sales, there is no future to protect.

You ask what sacrifices management and owners are making. We are making the most important one: we are putting the company’s future on the line by confronting the cost structure honestly, knowing it will trigger resistance. That is not trivial. It is necessary.

But I will be explicit: management cannot carry the company’s reset alone, and capital cannot endlessly compensate for uncompetitive German operations. There must be a meaningful workforce contribution, and it must be durable.

## To Lower Saxony
I understand your political position. But I will not accept the state treating plant closures as taboo while expecting the company to remain globally competitive.

You say closures are not the only route. That is true. But neither are they ruled out simply because they are unpopular.

If the no-closure path genuinely removes enough cost, then fine — show it. If it does not, then political discomfort is not a valid reason to freeze an uncompetitive structure.

## To the controlling owners
You are right to reject a cosmetic deal. You are also right that prolonged conflict destroys value.

But I disagree with the suggestion that a no-closure package is already proven sufficient. It is not.

The expert ranges were illustrative, not a guarantee. They showed feasibility in principle, not proof in practice. We still need a package with:
- quantified annual savings,
- credible headcount reduction,
- lower utilization burden,
- and real labor-cost relief.

Until that is on the table, I cannot support dismissing closures as a real option.

## To institutional investors
I agree with your demand for execution certainty. That is exactly why I am unwilling to endorse a weak compromise.

The market will not reward:
- vague productivity promises,
- temporary concessions,
- or “all sites remain open” if the economics still do not work.

It will reward a credible reset that restores margins and funding capacity.

## What I need to see
If the employee side wants to avoid closures and forced redundancies, then bring a package that is economically equivalent. That means it must contain all of the following:

1. **material and permanent capacity reduction**
2. **a genuine labor-cost contribution**
3. **clear headcount reduction at meaningful scale**
4. **durable productivity gains**
5. **a credible path to restore return on sales**
6. **investment protection for future products and technology**

If you cannot produce that, then I will not pretend the no-closure option is sufficient.

## My position remains
I am prepared to negotiate on the **means**:
- closures,
- capacity shifts,
- voluntary exits,
- flexibility,
- productivity,
- wage and bonus measures.

But I am not prepared to compromise on the **outcome**:
- a structurally smaller cost base,
- a leaner footprint,
- and a company that can fund its future.

## Bottom line
Let’s stop repeating red lines and start proving equivalence.

If the no-closure path really can deliver the required savings, show the numbers. If it cannot, then closures and deeper workforce reductions remain on the table.

I am not moving off the need for a **large, durable, structural reset**. That is the minimum required to restore competitiveness and secure Volkswagen’s future to 2030.
