Below is a plant-level finance/operations comparison framework using standard manufacturing cost and capacity logic. Because no actual plant volumes, headcount, labor rates, or utilization by site were provided, I can only give **bounded estimates and decision thresholds**, not a company-specific exact answer.

## 1) What would make a no-closure package economically credible?

A no-closure package can work **if it removes enough fixed cost and excess capacity** to approximate the economics of closing one or more plants, even if the sites remain legally open.

In automotive manufacturing, the main levers are:

- **Capacity concentration**: move volume from multiple sites into fewer lines/shifts
- **Shift reduction**: eliminate an entire shift or more at selected plants
- **Headcount reduction**: mainly via voluntary exits, attrition, and redeployment
- **Labor cost reset**: wage/bonus restraint, working-time changes, allowance removal
- **Overhead cuts**: engineering, admin, management, support functions
- **Asset and footprint rationalization**: but without full plant closure

### Key economics
A no-closure plan is only close to closure economics if it achieves:
- **permanent fixed-cost reduction**
- **substantial utilization improvement**
- **material headcount reduction**
- **minimal one-time production dislocation**

If it only reduces variable cost or temporarily freezes wages, it will likely be insufficient.

## 2) Order-of-magnitude savings ranges

For a large German OEM/passenger-car network, a realistic comparison is often in the following range:

### A. Fully loaded no-closure package
Typical annual run-rate savings after full implementation:
- **€1.0bn to €2.5bn per year**  
  depending on scale, wage reset, and how much capacity is truly removed internally

One-time restructuring cost:
- **€1.5bn to €4.0bn**
  - voluntary exits
  - redeployment/training
  - write-downs on underused assets
  - tooling and logistics reconfiguration
  - possible severance for indirect labor

Timing to run-rate:
- **12 to 36 months**
  - faster if shift reductions and exits are immediate
  - slower if it relies on negotiated attrition and product migration

### B. Closure-based alternative
Typical annual run-rate savings:
- **€2.0bn to €4.5bn+ per year**
  if one or more plants are fully shut and volume is consolidated

One-time restructuring cost:
- **€3.0bn to €7.0bn+**
  - severance
  - asset impairments
  - logistics reengineering
  - legal/industrial-relations costs
  - duplicate production during ramp-down/ramp-up

Timing to run-rate:
- **18 to 48 months**
  - closures are often slower to realize fully than management expects because production must be migrated safely

### Typical gap
The likely **annual savings gap** between a strong no-closure package and a closure-based plan is often:
- **€0.5bn to €2.0bn per year**

That is the key economic question:
- if the residual gap is small enough, no-closure may be viable;
- if the gap remains large, management’s case for closures strengthens.

## 3) Plant-level capacity effects: what “no closure” usually means in practice

“No closure” does **not** mean “no footprint change.”  
It usually means:

- one plant loses a shift
- another plant specializes in fewer models
- body shop / paint / assembly loading is redistributed
- some plants become overflow or niche sites
- internal capacity utilization rises in the survivor plants

### Example capacity logic
If a plant runs at:
- **50–60% utilization**, it is structurally inefficient
- **70–80% utilization**, it may be acceptable but still needs discipline
- **85%+ utilization**, it becomes much more competitive

In a no-closure package, management would need to move plants from, say:
- **55–65% average utilization**
to
- **75–85% average utilization**
through volume concentration and shift removal.

That is the operational equivalent of closure economics, though often not fully equivalent in cash terms.

## 4) Headcount impact: what is realistic without compulsory redundancies?

For a major German automotive restructuring, a no-closure package could still imply substantial headcount reduction:

### Rough range
- **Indirect labor/admin/engineering reduction**: 10% to 20%
- **Direct manufacturing headcount reduction**: 5% to 15%
- **Total German workforce reduction at impacted brands/sites**: often **8% to 15%**
  if the package is serious

If the labor side insists on:
- no closures
- no compulsory redundancies
- binding job guarantees

then the reduction must come from:
- attrition
- early retirement
- voluntary exit programs
- redeployment to EV/software/product programs
- reduced temporary labor and contractor use

### Constraint
Without forced redundancies, the speed of reduction is usually limited.  
That means:
- savings are more durable than immediate
- but slower to capture
- and dependent on labor acceptance of exit incentives

## 5) Fixed vs. variable cost: why management pushes closures

The strongest argument for closures is not simply “fewer people,” but **fixed-cost removal**.

### Fixed cost that closures remove
- plant-level management
- maintenance
- utilities baseline
- security
- depreciation on underutilized assets
- local support functions
- site overhead

### Fixed cost that no-closure packages can still remove
- shift overhead
- site specialization
- some engineering/support functions
- some admin layers
- some maintenance and utilities via lower activity
- labor cost per vehicle through productivity and wage concessions

### What no-closure cannot remove as efficiently
- duplicate site overhead
- excess physical footprint
- some structural depreciation burden
- long-run underutilization costs

So a no-closure deal often reaches only **70% to 90%** of the savings of a closure-based option, unless the underlying network is badly overstaffed and can be heavily reconfigured.

## 6) Cash flow implications

### No-closure package
Pros:
- lower one-time cash outflow than closures
- less operational disruption
- faster political and industrial acceptance if negotiated

Cons:
- savings may be less complete
- headcount reduction may take longer
- some excess footprint remains on the balance sheet

### Closure-based package
Pros:
- larger possible run-rate savings
- clearer long-term fixed-cost reset

Cons:
- higher immediate cash cost
- higher execution risk
- more disruption to output, quality, and supplier network
- possible legal, political, and labor escalation

A finance test is the **payback period**:
- If closure-based restructuring costs €5bn and saves €3bn/year, payback is ~1.7 years.
- If a no-closure package costs €2.5bn and saves €1.5bn/year, payback is ~1.7 years as well.

That means the decision is not just about payback. It is about:
- certainty
- durability
- execution risk
- industrial peace
- and long-term competitiveness.

## 8) Practical conclusion

### Decision threshold
A no-closure package is economically plausible **if** it delivers:
- at least **€1bn+ annual fixed-cost savings**
- meaningful capacity concentration
- visible utilization gains
- a headcount reduction path of roughly **8%–15%**
- and credible implementation discipline

### When closures become necessary
Closures become more likely if:
- the residual savings gap after internal restructuring is still **>€1bn/year**
- utilization remains structurally below competitive levels
- labor refuses sufficient wage/flexibility concessions
- or the savings arrive too slowly to support the investment plan

## Bottom line
From a finance/operations standpoint, the question is not whether a no-closure route is “possible.” It is whether it can produce **enough durable fixed-cost reduction fast enough**.

My judgment:
- **Possible in principle**
- **Not proven sufficient yet**
- The decisive test is the **annual residual gap after fully loading all no-closure measures**
