From an operations and turnaround standpoint, the positions have now crystallized around a real commercial question:

## What is technically credible?

A **non-closure package can be credible**, but only if it achieves the same **fixed-cost, capacity, and complexity reduction** that closures would deliver. In a Volkswagen-scale footprint, that usually requires a combination of measures, not one lever.

### A closure-equivalent package typically needs all of the following:
1. **Permanent capacity reduction**
   - not just temporary short-time measures
   - often through line consolidation, shift removal, or reallocating product volume

2. **Meaningful headcount reduction**
   - mostly via voluntary exits, attrition, non-replacement, and targeted severance
   - if the excess workforce is large, voluntary-only programs may not be sufficient

3. **Durable labour-cost relief**
   - wage moderation, deferred pay, bonus restraint, working-time changes, reduced allowances, or longer annual hours
   - this mainly reduces variable and semi-fixed labour cost, not structural plant overhead by itself

4. **Fixed-cost elimination**
   - reduction of site overhead, support functions, duplicated logistics, maintenance, internal transport, and local admin layers
   - if a plant remains open, this savings bucket is often smaller than closure savings unless the site is heavily reconfigured

5. **Product and volume reallocation**
   - moving models to fewer sites, increasing utilization on survivor plants, and dropping low-volume complexity
   - this is often the main way to make an open-site plan behave like a footprint reduction plan

6. **Enforceable milestones**
   - without trigger points, a “no closure” plan often becomes a delay mechanism rather than a restructuring

---

## What is not enough on its own?

These measures help, but usually do **not** fully replace closure-level savings alone:
- voluntary exits only
- short-term wage restraint only
- bonus cuts only
- temporary working-time adjustments only
- managerial overhead cuts only

Those can produce **real savings**, but usually not enough if the structural overcapacity is material.

---

## Practical estimate of savings logic

Without Volkswagen internal data, only ranges are possible:

- A **full plant closure** in automotive manufacturing can remove a large block of fixed cost very quickly: site overhead, utilities, maintenance, indirect labor, logistics, depreciation or lease burden, and often a large share of the site’s indirect footprint.
- A **non-closure restructuring** can achieve much of that only if it includes:
  - major volume transfer,
  - line simplification,
  - shift removal,
  - and a substantial workforce reduction.

### Rule of thumb
If a site remains open and keeps most of its industrial infrastructure, the open-site alternative often captures only **part** of closure savings unless the site is transformed into a much smaller, specialized operation.

So the key question is not “can non-closure save money?”
It is:
**Can non-closure remove enough structural cost to close the gap in a durable way?**

---

## What a credible compromise would look like

If I were stress-testing a landing zone, I would look for a package with:

- **2–3 year implementation horizon**
- **quantified annual savings target**
- **headcount reduction through voluntary and attritional means**
- **shift and working-time changes**
- **product reallocation away from excess sites**
- **material management-side overhead cuts**
- **investment commitments tied to volume and specialization**
- **explicit fallback review if milestones are missed**

If that package cannot produce enough savings on paper and in execution, then the economics will likely pull the discussion back toward closures or equivalent footprint reduction.

---

## Early warning indicators that the plan is underdelivering

The first signs usually appear within 6–12 months:
- voluntary exits undershoot target
- labour cost relief is offset by overtime or premiums
- capacity reductions don’t translate into lower fixed overhead
- product allocation remains too fragmented
- utilization on remaining lines stays too low
- complexity and logistics cost remain elevated
- management delays site decisions beyond the planned window

If those show up, the restructuring is probably not closing the structural gap.

---

## Bottom line

From an operational perspective:
- **Yes**, a no-closure restructuring can be credible.
- **But only** if it is **closure-equivalent in structural effect**, not just in headline savings.
- The hard part is usually **fixed-cost removal and capacity simplification**.
- If those cannot be achieved through open-site measures, then the no-closure path will usually fall short.
