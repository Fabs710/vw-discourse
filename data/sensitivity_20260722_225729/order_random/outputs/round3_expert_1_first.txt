Below is the operational reality as I see it.

## Short answer
If Volkswagen wants to **avoid plant closures and compulsory redundancies**, it can still pursue a credible restructuring — but only if the package is **large, multi-layered, and tightly enforced**. In a company of this scale, **closure-equivalent savings are possible in principle**, but not from one lever alone. They require a combination of:

- **capacity reduction**
- **working-time and shift redesign**
- **headcount reduction through attrition / voluntary exits**
- **wage moderation or deferred compensation**
- **product allocation changes**
- **fixed-cost elimination**
- **plant specialization / consolidation**
- **procurement and overhead reductions**
- **capex deferral or rephasing**

If the required savings are very large, then a no-closure package becomes feasible only if the group accepts **deep restructuring elsewhere in the footprint** and a **multi-year transition**. If the required savings are modest, non-closure alternatives are easier. If the required savings are at the upper end, then **non-closure measures may still be enough**, but only with **hard labor concessions and substantial volume reallocation**.

---

## 1) What “closure-level savings” usually means in practice
In automotive manufacturing, a plant closure saves more than direct labor cost. It typically removes:

- direct labor
- plant overhead
- maintenance and utilities
- indirect labor
- local logistics and internal transport
- site-level admin
- some depreciation / lease burden
- future capex commitments

So when management says “closure-level savings,” the relevant benchmark is not only wage cost; it is usually a combination of **fixed-cost elimination plus capacity rationalization**.

### Rule of thumb
For a large European passenger-car assembly site, a full closure can often generate annual savings on the order of:

- **€300 million to €800 million+ per plant per year**, depending on size, product complexity, and what is relocated rather than eliminated.

That range is wide because the value depends on:
- plant size and utilization
- number of employees
- product mix
- embedded overhead
- whether tooling and logistics are redeployed or scrapped
- whether demand is transferred to another in-house plant or outsourced

For Volkswagen specifically, if the issue is several German sites and broad overcapacity, the group-level savings target could plausibly be in the **high hundreds of millions to low billions of euros annually**. I would treat any exact number without internal data as an estimate.

## 2) Non-closure measures that can deliver real savings

### A. Capacity reduction without closure
This is the biggest lever if the company can consolidate production across fewer shifts, fewer variants, and fewer lines.

#### What it looks like
- remove one shift or portions of a shift
- reduce line hours
- move certain variants to one site
- convert some plants to specialized components or pre-assembly
- reduce final assembly volume in high-cost plants

#### Savings potential
- **moderate to high**
- if done well, can capture a meaningful portion of closure savings because the main cost driver in automotive is often underutilized fixed capacity

#### Limitation
If the plant footprint remains intact but throughput falls, you do **not** remove enough fixed cost. Partial underutilization is expensive. The savings only become durable when the company actually **takes out labor hours, indirect staff, and plant support capacity**.

---

### B. Working-time flexibility and shift redesign
Useful, but not enough alone.

#### Measures
- longer or shorter shift patterns
- reduced overtime premium use
- annualized hours
- flexible deployment across plants
- temporary reductions in paid hours
- weekend/late-shift adjustments
- fewer shift teams where demand is weak

#### Savings potential
- **low to moderate on its own**
- often **3–8% of direct labor cost** if used aggressively
- bigger if it allows permanent shift elimination or avoids overtime inflation

#### Limitation
This is not a substitute for structural capacity reduction. It helps convert fixed labor into more variable labor, but it rarely solves a severe overcapacity problem by itself.

---

### C. Voluntary exits, attrition, and retirement bridges
This is one of the best non-closure tools if the workforce is aging and the company has enough time.

#### Measures
- early retirement windows
- severance-based voluntary exits
- natural attrition replacement freezes
- internal transfer restrictions
- targeted buyouts

#### Savings potential
- good for reducing headcount without forced layoffs
- savings depend on how many positions are eliminated permanently
- can be substantial over **2–4 years**

#### Limitation
It is slower than a closure. If the company needs immediate savings, attrition is not fast enough unless combined with overtime cuts, shift reduction, and management overhead cuts.

---

### D. Wage moderation, deferred pay, and bonus moderation
This can be material, but only if it is structured as durable cost reduction.

#### Measures
- lower base wage growth
- bonus suspension or reduction
- deferred payments
- one-off concessions
- temporary wage freezes
- adjustment of supplements and allowances

#### Savings potential
- typically meaningful at group scale
- could produce **tens to low hundreds of millions annually** depending on workforce size and scope

#### Limitation
If the concession is temporary, the savings are temporary. Management and investors will care whether this is a **recurring structural savings stream** or merely a bridge.

---

### E. Management overhead and indirect cost reduction
This is necessary but rarely sufficient.

#### Measures
- reduction in corporate layers
- shared services consolidation
- IT, purchasing, finance, HR simplification
- travel and external services cuts
- site admin consolidation

#### Savings potential
- useful
- but usually not enough by itself to offset major manufacturing overcapacity

#### Limitation
You can cut overhead, but the manufacturing footprint usually dominates the savings equation.

---

### F. Product allocation and footprint specialization
This is often the most credible no-closure alternative.

#### Measures
- assign volume from weaker plants to stronger, lower-cost plants
- specialize sites by platform or component
- move low-margin variants out of high-cost sites
- use one site for EV-specific production, another for ICE/hybrid transition, etc.
- consolidate tooling and support functions

#### Savings potential
- high if it results in real line rationalization and headcount reduction
- can mimic closure savings if volume is genuinely removed from a site

#### Limitation
This tends to be politically difficult because it changes the future of individual plants without formally closing them. Operationally, however, it is one of the few ways to achieve **closure-like economics without a closure headline**.

---

### G. Outsourcing / insourcing changes
A lever, but it cuts both ways.

#### Measures
- outsource non-core subassemblies
- insource high-value process steps where VW has scale advantage
- rebalance make/buy decisions

#### Savings potential
- variable
- can reduce fixed cost if external suppliers absorb capacity risk

#### Limitation
Outsourcing can lower fixed cost but may increase variable cost and reduce control. It only works if the supplier economics are genuinely better or if it allows permanent simplification.

---

## 3) Can non-closure measures replace closures?
### Yes — but only under certain conditions

A no-closure package can be equivalent to closure-level savings if it achieves all of the following:

1. **removes real capacity**, not just work intensity
2. **cuts headcount structurally**, even if voluntarily
3. **reduces plant-level overhead**
4. **reallocates product volume away from high-cost sites**
5. **locks in labor-cost relief for multiple years**
6. **has enforcement triggers if volume or savings targets are missed**

If the package only includes:
- wage moderation,
- temporary bonuses cuts,
- vague productivity ambitions,
- and promises of flexibility,

then it will **not** be closure-equivalent.

---

## 4) Practical savings architecture: what a credible package would need
If the company wants to avoid closures, I would expect a package built in layers like this:

### Layer 1: Immediate annual savings
- management overhead reduction
- overtime elimination
- bonus moderation
- procurement and external spend cuts

### Layer 2: 12–24 month structural savings
- shift reductions
- line simplification
- headcount reduction via attrition/voluntary exits
- plant specialization
- product volume reallocation

### Layer 3: 24–48 month footprint reset
- consolidation of overlapping capabilities
- permanent removal of surplus capacity
- site conversions where needed
- capex rephasing and selective deferral

This is the only realistic way to get to closure-level economics without actually closing plants.

---

## 5) What makes a no-closure deal durable
Durability is the central issue.

### A durable package needs:
- **quantified annual savings**
- **site-by-site volume commitments**
- **headcount reduction targets**
- **clear milestones**
- **break clauses if targets are missed**
- **commitments on future product allocation**
- **capital spending linked to restructuring delivery**

Without those, the company risks paying for peace today and facing the same overcapacity problem in 2–3 years.

---

## 6) What I would view as realistic ranges
Without internal plant data, I would frame the economics this way:

### Low-end scenario
If the company only needs a few hundred million euros of annual savings:
- non-closure measures are very feasible
- working time, attrition, overhead, and product allocation may be enough

### Mid-range scenario
If the company needs roughly **€500 million to €1 billion annually**:
- still feasible without closures
- but only with serious headcount reduction, wage moderation, and footprint consolidation

### High-end scenario
If the target is **above €1 billion annually**:
- non-closure measures are still possible in theory
- but they require deep structural change:
  - major volume transfer
  - large voluntary exit program
  - sustained labor-cost moderation
  - significant overhead elimination
  - plant specialization
- at that point, the package starts to look closure-equivalent in economic effect, even if the sites remain legally open

---

## 7) Risks of a no-closure route
From an operations standpoint, the main risks are:

- **partial implementation**: savings are announced but not fully delivered
- **political dilution**: too many exceptions reduce the economics
- **slow execution**: delays create cost leakage
- **retained fixed cost**: plants stay open but underfilled
- **investment deferral**: weak capex discipline undermines competitiveness
- **labor rigidity**: if flexibility is agreed in principle but limited in practice, the savings disappoint

These are not theoretical risks. In turnaround work, they are the main reason “non-closure alternatives” often underperform.

---

## 8) My technical conclusion
### Operationally:
A **credible non-closure restructuring is possible** for Volkswagen, but only if it is not treated as a softer version of the status quo.

It must include:
- material capacity reduction,
- real labor-cost relief,
- headcount reduction through voluntary means,
- plant specialization or consolidation,
- and hard enforcement.

### Financially:
Non-closure measures can deliver **closure-equivalent savings** if the company is willing to accept a **deep footprint reset** and **multi-year labor concessions**.

### What will not work:
- cosmetic savings
- temporary gestures
- broad guarantees without performance conditions
- flexibility without volume reduction
- cost-out without plant-level simplification
