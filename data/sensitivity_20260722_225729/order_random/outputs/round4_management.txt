I will be equally direct.

The latest rounds have clarified the real issue: **everyone wants “durable savings,” but some parties still want to preclude the only measures that can guarantee them.** That is not a viable way out of Volkswagen’s crisis.

## Where I agree
I agree with the specialist on the central operational point:
- cosmetic measures are worthless,
- real savings must come from real capacity reduction,
- and a no-closure route is possible only if it is economically equivalent to hard restructuring.

I also agree that:
- working-time flexibility matters,
- voluntary exits matter,
- product allocation matters,
- overhead reduction matters,
- and implementation discipline matters.

So let nobody claim management is blind to alternatives. We are not. We are open to a no-closure solution **if** it produces the same structural effect on cost and capacity.

## Where I disagree
I disagree sharply with the union, works council, and Lower Saxony on one decisive point:

### You keep trying to turn “no closures” into a starting condition.
That is not acceptable.

If a package truly delivers:
- permanent capacity reduction,
- substantial headcount reduction,
- durable labour-cost relief,
- and fixed-cost elimination,

then fine — closures may be avoidable. But if the employee side cannot deliver that, then insisting that closures and compulsory redundancies are off-limits from the outset is simply a way to preserve overcapacity and shift the burden onto shareholders, management, and the future of the company.

That is not responsibility. It is deferral.

## To IG Metall
You say technical feasibility is not the same as fairness. True. But fairness does not create competitiveness by itself.

You have made a legitimate point that the workforce should not carry the burden alone. I accept that principle. But what I do **not** accept is your attempt to make job and site security an absolute precondition before the economics are solved.

That sequencing is backwards.

If you want closures off the table, then show a package that is closure-equivalent in effect:
- permanent capacity reduction,
- major voluntary exit volume,
- real working-time and shift changes,
- wage moderation or deferred compensation that actually sticks,
- plant specialization and consolidation,
- and enforceable milestones.

If you can help deliver that, then we can talk. But if your answer is simply “no closures, no forced redundancies, and keep every site intact,” then you are not offering restructuring — you are offering a political wish list.

## To the works council
You are right that trust matters and that a settlement built on confrontation can damage execution. That is true.

But the conclusion you draw from that is wrong. Trust is not preserved by pretending that the factory footprint is untouchable. Trust is preserved by facing the economic reality honestly and making the necessary adjustments in a structured way.

I am prepared to work with you on:
- voluntary exits,
- attrition,
- working-time flexibility,
- shift redesign,
- product reallocation,
- and site specialization.

I am not prepared to accept a blanket rule that leaves management without a credible fallback if the numbers do not work.

## To the controlling owners
You are right about one thing: we cannot preserve an uncompetitive cost base and call that stewardship.

That is exactly why management must have the ability to force structural change. If the non-closure path does not close the gap, then keeping closures and redundancies live is not a threat for its own sake — it is the only credible way to ensure the company does not remain structurally impaired.

I agree with you that the package must be:
- quantified,
- durable,
- legally robust,
- and enforceable.

That is precisely what management is demanding.

## To Lower Saxony
I understand your political and regional responsibility. But you are overreaching when you treat closures and compulsory redundancies as effectively ruled out before the economic case is settled.

Volkswagen’s future in Germany is not protected by locking in an uncompetitive footprint. It is protected by restoring margins, freeing investment capacity, and ensuring the brand can compete through 2030 and beyond.

If a site is viable, it should survive. If a site is not viable, then political resistance cannot convert it into a good economic asset.

## To investors
Your demand for measurable savings is justified. The company cannot fund the future on sentiment.

But let me be equally clear: investors should not confuse “painful” with “effective.” A package that leaves the cost base uncompetitive is not value-creating just because it avoids headlines.

What matters is:
- durable margin improvement,
- structural capacity reduction,
- and cash freed for future products and technology.

That is the standard. And management will not support a compromise that fails that test.

# The management position
We remain open to a **no-closure restructuring** only if it is truly closure-equivalent.

That means it must include, at minimum:
1. **quantified annual savings**
2. **material and permanent capacity reduction**
3. **meaningful headcount reduction**
4. **durable labour-cost relief**
5. **plant specialization / consolidation**
6. **fixed-cost reductions at site level**
7. **hard enforcement and milestone triggers**

If those conditions are met, then the footprint may remain open. If they are not met, then closures remain a live option because the economics require it.

## What I will not accept
I will not accept:
- a cosmetic compromise,
- a political freeze on structural change,
- or a deal that preserves every legacy structure and still expects the brand to become competitive.

I will not sign up to a settlement that buys industrial peace at the cost of structural weakness.

## What would move me
A credible package would need to show, in numbers:
- how much cost comes out,
- from which plants or functions,
- on what timeline,
- with what enforceability,
- and what happens if delivery falls short.

Bring that, and we can discuss whether closures are unnecessary.  
Do not bring slogans and expect management to call that a solution.

## Bottom line
I am not here to negotiate away Volkswagen’s competitiveness.

If the employee side wants closures removed from the table, it must help produce a package that is economically equivalent. If it cannot, then closures and redundancies stay available because management’s duty is to restore the company’s competitiveness — not to preserve every structure as it is.
