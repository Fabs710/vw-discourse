I will be just as clear as the others have been.

Volkswagen does need a credible restructuring. I do not deny that the company faces serious cost pressure, overcapacity, and competitive problems. But there is a line between restructuring and destroying the industrial base. **Plant closures in Germany and compulsory redundancies are that line.** I will not accept them.

### Where I agree
I agree that:
- the company needs durable savings,
- capacity has to be adjusted,
- investment into future products and technologies is essential,
- and a purely cosmetic solution would be irresponsible.

I also agree that a one-sided burden on the company is unrealistic. We are prepared to discuss measures that contribute to competitiveness — but only within a framework that protects jobs, sites, and co-determination.

### Where I disagree
I disagree with management’s framing that closures must remain on the table as a normal option. Once you announce plant closures and job cuts as your default instrument, you break trust and force confrontation. That is exactly what has happened here.

I also disagree with the idea that the workforce should prove, in advance and on its own, that it can deliver “comparable savings” before management rules out closures. That turns negotiation into coercion. If management wants flexibility from the workforce, it must first give something concrete back: **binding site guarantees, no forced redundancies, and a genuine role for employee representatives in shaping the plan.**

To the controlling owners and capital markets:  
You are right that this must be economically credible. But credibility is not only a question of short-term margins. A settlement that triggers mass conflict, destroys trust, and tears up the social compact will also damage Volkswagen’s long-term value. You cannot sustainably run a company like this against its workforce.

To Lower Saxony:  
I welcome your clear stance against closures and forced redundancies. But that support must be backed by concrete pressure on management. Words are not enough. We need binding commitments.

### Our red lines
Let me state them plainly:
- **No plant closures in Germany**
- **No compulsory redundancies**
- **No unilateral erosion of collective bargaining standards**
- **No fait accompli presented to the works council**

These are not bargaining chips for us. They are the minimum basis for any settlement that can be taken seriously.

### What we are prepared to discuss
If management is serious about a settlement, then we can discuss:
- working-time flexibility,
- voluntary programs and natural attrition,
- capacity adjustments that do not close plants,
- productivity improvements,
- and a fair employee contribution, provided it is matched by real commitments from management and owners.

But that requires:
1. **site guarantees through 2030,**
2. **job guarantees through 2030,**
3. **a binding role for the works council and co-determination bodies in implementation,**
4. **and a clear investment plan for the German sites.**

Without those guarantees, I will not move. And if management continues to insist on closures and forced layoffs, then it should expect resistance — in the supervisory board and in the plants. We have already shown we can mobilise the workforce, and we will do so again if necessary.

So my message is simple:  
We are ready for a fair restructuring. We are not ready for sacrifice without security.
