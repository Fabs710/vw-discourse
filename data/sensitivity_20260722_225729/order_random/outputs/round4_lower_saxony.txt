I will be equally clear.

Lower Saxony supports a **real restructuring**, but not one that relies on **plant closures in Germany**, **compulsory redundancies**, or a one-sided transfer of the burden onto employees. That remains my line. It is not sentimentality; it is economic and political responsibility. Volkswagen is a regional anchor. Damaging that base would weaken the company’s long-term legitimacy and the state’s industrial ecosystem.

## Where I agree
I agree with management, owners, investors, and the specialist on one important point:  
**Volkswagen needs durable, measurable, enforceable savings.**  
Cosmetic measures are not enough. Temporary gestures are not enough. A settlement that leaves the cost base fundamentally unchanged is not enough.

I also agree that any viable package must include some combination of:
- capacity reduction,
- working-time flexibility,
- voluntary exits and attrition,
- productivity gains,
- overhead cuts,
- product and site reallocation,
- and a contribution from employees, management, and owners alike.

That is the correct frame.

## Where I disagree
Where I disagree sharply is with the repeated attempt to treat **closures and forced redundancies as the normal fallback** from the outset.

That is not a neutral “economic reality.” It is a governance choice. And it is one I will not accept unless every serious non-closure option has been genuinely exhausted and quantified.

The expert has made clear that **closure-level savings can be achieved without closures** if the package is deep enough:
- real capacity reduction,
- permanent removal of excess cost,
- substantial headcount reduction through voluntary means,
- durable labour-cost relief,
- site specialization or consolidation,
- and hard enforcement.

That means the argument “closures are inevitable” has **not** been proven. It means management must do the hard work of building a closure-equivalent alternative rather than using closure threats as a bargaining shortcut.

## To management
You say economics come first. Fine. Then show me the economics of a package that:
- keeps viable German sites open,
- avoids compulsory redundancies,
- preserves co-determination,
- and still delivers the required savings.

If you cannot do that, then your restructuring design is not yet complete. What I will not accept is a package that asks the state and the workforce to surrender security first while management keeps closures in reserve as a pressure tool.

## To the controlling owners
You are right about one thing: a deal that merely postpones the problem would destroy value. I agree.  
But you are still too quick to treat closure as an ordinary governance instrument.

If a non-closure package can truly deliver equivalent savings, then that is the route you should support. A responsible owner does not normalize industrial damage when there are serious alternatives.

I also expect owners to use their influence to force management to present a **real non-closure plan**, not just demand concessions from labour while preserving optionality on the capital side.

## To investors
You are right to ask for numbers and discipline. I do not dispute that.

But credibility does **not** mean headcount destruction by default. A credible no-closure package can be quantified through:
- shift reductions,
- annualized hours,
- deferred pay,
- temporary wage moderation,
- bonus restraint,
- voluntary exits,
- redeployment,
- product transfer,
- plant specialization,
- and fixed-cost cuts.

That is measurable. That is enforceable. That is a serious basis for a settlement.

What I will not accept is a logic that says “if the restructuring is painful, then it must include closures.” Pain is not the benchmark. Durability and regional responsibility are.

## To IG Metall and the works council
I agree with you that workers must not carry the crisis alone. I also agree that a settlement built on distrust will not endure.

But I would also caution you: insisting that every German plant remain untouched in any form is not the same as protecting jobs. Jobs are protected by a package that keeps the industrial base viable.

So yes:
- voluntary exits,
- working-time flexibility,
- temporary wage moderation,
- deferred compensation,
- bonus restraint,
- shift redesign,
- product and volume reallocation,
- and plant specialization

are all on the table.

But those measures must be tied to:
- meaningful capacity reduction,
- real savings,
- and binding commitments from management and owners.

We do not support employees being asked to concede first while management preserves closure threats as an always-available fallback.

## What would move Lower Saxony
I would support a tough deal if it includes all of the following:
1. a **quantified annual savings target**
2. a **concrete non-closure package**
3. **binding site and employment protection for viable plants**
4. **visible contributions** from management and owners
5. a **credible investment path** for future products and technologies in Germany
6. an **implementation mechanism** with milestones, monitoring, and enforceability

That is the minimum basis for support.

## My bottom line
I am prepared to back a **hard restructuring**.  
I am **not** prepared to back a restructuring that:
- guts regional employment,
- sidelines co-determination,
- or normalizes plant closures and compulsory redundancies as first-line tools.

If management, owners, and investors want Lower Saxony’s support, then bring forward a package that protects viable sites, preserves jobs, and restores competitiveness through deep but non-destructive restructuring.

If you cannot do that, then I will use the blocking position the VW Law gives us. Because preserving Volkswagen as a regional economic anchor is not a side issue — it is central to the company’s long-term stability.
