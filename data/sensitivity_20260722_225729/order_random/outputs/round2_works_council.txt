Let me be equally clear: I hear the insistence on a “durable” restructuring from management, owners, and investors. I do **not** dispute that Volkswagen needs a serious solution. What I dispute is the idea that the necessary solution must include **plant closures, compulsory redundancies, and a dismantling of the job-security framework**.

That is not a serious restructuring from our point of view. That is a breach of trust.

### Where I agree
I agree that:
- the current cost base is too high,
- capacity has to be adjusted,
- cosmetic measures are not enough,
- and Volkswagen must remain competitive into 2030 and beyond.

I also agree that a settlement must be implementable, not just announced. And I agree that endless conflict helps no one.

### Where I strongly disagree
I reject the repeated attempt to make closures and forced layoffs the benchmark for “credibility.”

#### To management
You keep saying the economics require closures unless we come up with a full equivalent alternative. But you are presenting that as if the workforce has to prove your preferred remedy wrong before you move. That is not negotiation; that is pressure.

We are prepared to discuss:
- working-time flexibility,
- voluntary exit programs,
- capacity reductions without closures,
- productivity improvements,
- targeted labour-cost contributions,
- and product and investment commitments for the German sites.

But we will **not** accept:
- closure of German plants,
- compulsory redundancies,
- unilateral wage attacks,
- or a settlement that weakens co-determination.

If you want savings, then bring forward **real non-closure measures** with real numbers and a real implementation plan. Do not use “competitiveness” as a slogan to justify breaking the social compact.

#### To the controlling owners
You say you do not want a value-destructive conflict. On that, we agree. But you are still treating the closure option too lightly.

A company like Volkswagen does not get stronger by threatening to shut down plants that are embedded in regions, skilled networks, and long-standing industrial relationships. That destroys trust, triggers resistance, and weakens the very execution capability you say you want.

You call our red lines “not final.” I call them **essential**. No plant closures and no compulsory redundancies are not bargaining ornaments. They are the minimum basis for any settlement that can preserve industrial peace and long-term value.

#### To Lower Saxony
I welcome your clear rejection of closures and forced redundancies. But I need you to be concrete. If management keeps pushing this line, your blocking position must be used to stop it. Workers cannot carry the burden while political actors express concern only in principle.

#### To the investors
You say you want measurable savings and enforceability. Fine. So do we. But you are too quick to equate “measurable” with “cuts to jobs and sites.” There are other measurable levers:
- shift systems,
- working time,
- voluntary reductions,
- qualification and redeployment,
- product allocation,
- component sourcing,
- and fixed-cost reductions outside headcount destruction.

A restructuring that survives legally, socially, and operationally is more durable than one that provokes mass resistance and long-term damage.

### Our position remains firm
We will not accept:
- **plant closures in Germany**
- **compulsory redundancies**
- **erosion of collective bargaining standards**
- **a fait accompli presented to the works council**

What we are prepared to discuss is a package that includes:
1. **binding site guarantees through 2030**
2. **binding job guarantees through 2030**
3. **a genuine role for the works council in shaping implementation**
4. **fair employee contributions only if matched by management and owner commitments**
5. **investment commitments for future products and technologies at German sites**

### On the “economics first” argument
I want to be very direct: “economics first” cannot mean “the workforce gives up its protections first.” If management claims there is no alternative to closures, then management must explain why it has not exhausted the serious alternatives. We have not accepted that burden shift.

If a package cannot be built without closures, then the package is not good enough as far as we are concerned.

### Bottom line
We are not here to protect comfort. We are here to protect jobs, sites, and the industrial future of Volkswagen in Germany.

So my answer is simple:
- **No to closures.**
- **No to forced redundancies.**
- **Yes to a negotiated, durable, socially fair restructuring.**
- **Yes to real employee contributions — but only under binding guarantees.**

If management and owners want a settlement, they need to come back with a plan that respects these red lines. If they do not, then they should expect continued resistance — in the supervisory board and in the plants.
