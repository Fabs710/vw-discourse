We share the objective of restoring Volkswagen’s competitiveness and securing its industrial future. On that point, there is no disagreement.

Where I differ is on the means and on the limits of what can be ruled out in advance.

A credible restructuring must be judged against one test: does it materially and durably improve margins, productivity, and investment capacity? If the answer is no, then it is not a solution — it is delay. The current cost base and capacity structure cannot simply be preserved by declaration.

That said, I do not support a value-destructive confrontation either. A prolonged conflict, open-ended strikes, or a plan that damages the company’s reputation and execution capability would also destroy value for all stakeholders, including the workforce and the owners. So I am not asking for theatrics; I am asking for results.

My position is therefore:

- I support structural cost improvement and capacity adjustment.
- I do not support cosmetic measures that avoid hard decisions but fail to restore competitiveness.
- I am open to alternatives to outright plant closures if, and only if, they produce savings of comparable scale, with durable enforceability.
- I am not prepared to endorse a settlement that permanently locks in an uncompetitive cost structure or weakens the company’s ability to invest.

On your points specifically:

- No compulsory redundancies: understandable as a preference, but not something I would make absolute if the economics cannot otherwise work.
- No plant closures: I would not accept that as a blanket veto. If a site is structurally uneconomic, it cannot be protected indefinitely at the expense of the whole group.
- No erosion of the collective wage framework: the framework matters, but it cannot become a shield against necessary adjustment.
- Fair burden sharing: yes, but it must be real, measurable, and tied to competitiveness outcomes — not just promises.

If the workforce and the union are serious about preserving the industrial base, then a package built around working-time flexibility, wage moderation, job reductions through attrition, site-specific productivity improvements, and targeted investment commitments may be possible. But that must be judged against hard numbers, not slogans.

So my message is simple: I am prepared to back a tough but balanced solution. I will not back a settlement that avoids pain today only to guarantee decline tomorrow.
