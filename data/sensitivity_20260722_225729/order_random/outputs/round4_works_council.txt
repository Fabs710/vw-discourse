I will be equally direct.

The latest rounds have clarified the real issue: **management and capital want closure threats to remain live from the start, while the employee side is being asked to negotiate under that gun.** I reject that logic. It is not a neutral “economic reality.” It is a choice about who bears the burden first.

## Where I agree
I agree with the expert on one point: a serious restructuring must be **real**, not cosmetic. That means:
- capacity reduction,
- working-time flexibility,
- voluntary exits and attrition,
- product and volume reallocation,
- overhead cuts,
- and enforceable milestones.

I also agree that employees must contribute. I have never claimed otherwise. But contribution is not surrender. It is not a mandate for plant closures or forced layoffs.

## Where I disagree
I disagree sharply with management, owners, and investors on the idea that **closures and compulsory redundancies must stay on the table as normal fallback tools**.

That is not “discipline.” It is pressure politics.

And I disagree with the repeated attempt to turn **“no closures” and “no compulsory redundancies”** into if-and-only-if conditions that the workforce must disprove first. That reverses the burden. If you want to justify breaking historic job security, then you need to prove there is no serious alternative. So far, nobody has done that.

## To management
You keep saying the package must be economically equivalent to closures. Then show us the package.

But do not pretend that this entitles you to keep closure threats alive as leverage while we do the work of finding alternatives. That is exactly what destroys trust and triggers resistance.

If you want a real answer from the workforce, then present:
- a quantified non-closure plan,
- site-by-site volume and product allocation,
- real management-side cost reductions,
- and binding investment commitments for German plants.

What I will not accept is:
- a package that protects flexibility for management,
- but removes security for workers.

That is one-sided restructuring dressed up as responsibility.

## To the controlling owners
You say you will not underwrite overcapacity. Fine. Then stop assuming the only way to reduce structural cost is to threaten German sites.

Volkswagen is not strengthened by treating plants as disposable assets. It is strengthened by keeping industrial capability, skills, and trust intact. If you destroy that base, you damage long-term value, not just short-term margins.

Use your governance power to force a **real no-closure plan**, not to normalize closure as a bargaining weapon.

## To Lower Saxony
Your stance is right in principle, but it cannot remain declaratory.

If management pushes closures or forced redundancies as the default, then your blocking position must be used accordingly. Workers will not accept being told that “social concerns are understood” while the actual burden is still shifted onto them.

## To investors
You are right to demand measurable savings. But you are wrong if you think only one distribution of pain counts as credible.

Measurable savings can come from:
- shift redesign,
- annualized hours,
- voluntary exits,
- temporary wage moderation,
- deferred compensation,
- bonus restraint,
- product transfer,
- plant specialization,
- fixed-cost cuts,
- and a genuine management contribution.

Those are not slogans. They are measurable. They are enforceable. And they preserve the industrial base.

## My position remains unchanged
Let me state it clearly:

- **No plant closures in Germany**
- **No compulsory redundancies**
- **No erosion of co-determination**
- **No dismantling of the job-security framework**

That is not a rhetorical preference. It is the minimum basis for a legitimate settlement.

## What we are prepared to discuss
We are prepared to discuss:
- voluntary exits,
- attrition,
- working-time flexibility,
- temporary wage moderation,
- deferred compensation,
- bonus restraint,
- shift redesign,
- capacity reductions through reallocation,
- and binding investment commitments.

We are **not** prepared to discuss a settlement that treats German plants as expendable or asks employees to accept permanent insecurity so that management can call the restructuring “credible.”

## The central point the others keep missing
A restructuring that destroys trust, weakens co-determination, and triggers prolonged conflict is **not** durable, even if it looks tough on a spreadsheet.

Durability comes from:
- shared sacrifice,
- binding commitments,
- and preservation of viable sites.

So here is the line:
Bring a **real non-closure package** that keeps every German plant open, excludes compulsory redundancies, and preserves our co-determination rights. Then we can discuss how the burden is shared.

If you cannot do that, then you are not asking for restructuring. You are asking the workforce to give up security first and hope the company gets stronger later. We will not accept that.

If needed, we will continue to resist it in the plants and in the supervisory board.
