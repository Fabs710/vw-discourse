I will be just as direct as the others.

The expert input is useful because it confirms something we have been saying all along: if you want to avoid closures, then you need **real structural measures**, not cosmetic ones. On that point, I agree.

Where I do **not** agree is the conclusion management, owners, and investors keep trying to force: that because the savings must be real, **plant closures and compulsory redundancies must stay on the table** as a normal fallback. That is not an economic law. It is a choice. And for Volkswagen in Germany, it is a choice I reject.

## What I agree with
I agree that:
- Volkswagen needs durable savings.
- Capacity has to be reduced.
- Working-time flexibility, voluntary exits, product allocation changes, and overhead cuts all belong in the package.
- A weak or purely symbolic compromise would be damaging.
- Implementation must be measurable and enforceable.

I also agree with the expert that a no-closure solution must be **deep**, not superficial.

## What I reject
I reject three things outright:

1. **Using closures as pressure from the start**  
   That is not negotiation. It is coercion.

2. **Treating compulsory redundancies as a routine fallback**  
   That destroys trust and breaks the social compact.

3. **Demanding that the workforce give up job and site security before management and owners show real reciprocity**  
   That is one-sided burden shifting.

The expert has shown that closure-equivalent savings can be achieved without closures. That matters. It means the argument “closures are inevitable” is not proven. It means management must do the hard work of designing a real alternative instead of reaching first for plant closures.

## To management
You keep saying “economics first.” Fine. Then show me the economics of a **no-closure** plan that preserves every German plant and avoids forced layoffs.

But do not confuse that with asking us to sign away our red lines in advance. We will not do that.

If you want us to examine a serious alternative, it must include:
- permanent capacity reduction without plant closure,
- product and volume reallocation,
- shift and working-time changes,
- voluntary exit and attrition programs,
- management-side and owner-side contributions,
- and binding investment commitments for German sites.

If you cannot build that package, then that is a failure of management design — not proof that closures are the only answer.

## To the controlling owners
You say you do not want value destruction. Then stop threatening the industrial base as if it were an ordinary cost line.

Volkswagen’s value is not protected by breaking the trust of the workforce and triggering mass resistance. A German plant is not just a balance-sheet item; it is skills, logistics, suppliers, regional stability, and execution capability.

If you really want long-term value, then use your governance power to force management to produce a genuine no-closure restructuring. Do not hide behind “economic reality” as a way to normalize closures.

## To Lower Saxony
Your position is the right one: no closures as the starting point, no compulsory redundancies, and no one-sided burden shifting.

But I will be blunt with you too: your support has to be more than a statement. If management keeps pushing closure logic, then your blocking position must be used to stop it. The VW Law gives you that responsibility, not just symbolic influence.

## To investors
You are right that the package must be measurable. You are wrong if you think “measurable” automatically means jobs cut and sites closed.

The expert listed real, quantifiable levers:
- shift changes,
- working-time flexibility,
- deferred pay,
- temporary wage moderation,
- voluntary exits,
- redeployment,
- product allocation changes,
- fixed-cost reductions.

Those are not vague. They can be tracked. They can be enforced. They can deliver savings.

What you are demanding is not merely measurement. You are demanding a particular distribution of pain. We will not accept that as the standard of credibility.

## Our position remains unchanged
Let me state it clearly:

- **No plant closures in Germany**
- **No compulsory redundancies**
- **No erosion of co-determination**
- **No dismantling of the job-security framework**

That is not a rhetorical posture. It is the minimum basis for industrial peace and for a durable future for Volkswagen in Germany.

## What we are prepared to discuss
We are prepared to discuss:
- voluntary exits,
- attrition,
- working-time flexibility,
- temporary wage moderation,
- deferred compensation,
- shift redesign,
- capacity reductions through reallocation,
- product specialization,
- and genuine investment commitments.

We are **not** prepared to discuss a settlement that treats German plants as expendable, or that asks employees to accept permanent insecurity so that management can call the restructuring “credible.”

## The crucial point the others keep missing
A restructuring that destroys trust, weakens co-determination, and triggers prolonged conflict is **not** durable, even if it looks tough on a spreadsheet.

Durability comes from:
- binding commitments,
- shared sacrifice,
- and preservation of the industrial base.

If management and capital want a real settlement, then come back with a package that:
1. keeps every German plant open,
2. excludes compulsory redundancies,
3. preserves co-determination and job-security arrangements,
4. and achieves savings through the non-closure measures the expert outlined.

If you cannot do that, then you are not asking for restructuring. You are asking the workforce to surrender its protections first and hope the company gets stronger later. We will not accept that.

And if necessary, we will continue to resist it in the plants and in the supervisory board.
