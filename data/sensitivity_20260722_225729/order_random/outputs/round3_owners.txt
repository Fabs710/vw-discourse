We are now at the point where positions are clear enough to judge them against economic reality.

### To IG Metall and the works council
I agree with you on one thing: a deal that simply transfers pain to employees while leaving the company unstable would be value-destructive. That would be bad governance and bad ownership.

I also agree that non-closure tools must be fully explored:
- working-time flexibility,
- voluntary exits,
- attrition,
- site reconfiguration,
- product-line shifts,
- productivity gains,
- and some employee contribution.

Where I disagree is your insistence that **“no closures” and “no compulsory redundancies” must be absolute before the economics are solved**. That is not a workable basis for restructuring. It turns a bargaining position into a veto. If the package does not deliver closure-level savings with real durability, then you are asking the owners to underwrite permanent overcapacity.

That is not stewardship. It is delay.

Your latest proposal still does not answer the central question:
**What concrete measures close the savings gap if non-closure measures fall short?**
If the answer is simply “they must not fall short,” then that is not a plan; it is a refusal to confront the numbers.

### To management
I agree with your core diagnosis: Volkswagen needs a durable restructuring, not cosmetics. But I will also say clearly that management does not get support for toughness alone. If you want governance backing, you must bring a package that is:
- quantified,
- implementable,
- legally robust,
- and credible on timing.

I am not interested in threat-based management theatre. If closures remain on the table, it must be because the economic case is real and the alternative is equally real — not because pressure is being used as a substitute for design.

### To Lower Saxony
I understand your political constraints and your concern for the regional industrial base. That concern is legitimate.

But I do not accept a blanket rule that closures or compulsory redundancies are off-limits regardless of economics. If we lock that in before the restructuring is designed, we are effectively accepting the current cost base and hoping the market improves. That would be irresponsible.

If you want to protect viable sites, then help produce a package that actually restores competitiveness. Protection without competitiveness is temporary at best.

### To the investors
You are right to demand measurable savings and a credible path back to margin. I support that. What I do not support is treating every savings mechanism as equivalent just because it is painful.

A good restructuring is not measured by how much it hurts. It is measured by whether it:
- restores competitiveness,
- protects the industrial core that is viable,
- and avoids value-destroying conflict.

### My position, plainly
As controlling owners, we will support:
- capacity reduction,
- labour-cost moderation,
- voluntary exit schemes,
- working-time flexibility,
- productivity improvements,
- product and site reallocation,
- and targeted investment commitments.

We will **not** support:
- an outcome that permanently preserves an uncompetitive footprint,
- a deal that leaves structural overcapacity untouched,
- or a settlement that sacrifices long-term value for short-term peace.

### What would move us
I am open to a non-closure package only if it is genuinely equivalent to the savings and durability that closures would deliver. That means:
1. a quantified savings target,
2. a concrete list of non-closure measures,
3. enforceable labour-cost relief,
4. measurable capacity reduction,
5. and a credible fallback if the package underdelivers.

Without that, the closure option cannot be removed from consideration.

### Bottom line
I do not seek confrontation for its own sake. But I will not pretend that a refusal to accept hard choices is the same as a solution.

If the employee side wants closures ruled out, then it must help deliver an economically equivalent package. If it cannot, then insisting on absolute red lines is not protecting Volkswagen — it is endangering it.

We remain prepared to back a tough, balanced settlement. We are not prepared to underwrite permanent structural weakness.
