We are not far apart on the diagnosis, but we are still too far apart on the remedy.

I agree with IG Metall on one important point: **a restructuring that simply shifts the burden onto employees and triggers a prolonged industrial conflict would destroy value**. That is not in anyone’s interest, least of all ours as long-term owners. I also agree that a durable settlement must be **real, measurable, and enforceable**.

Where I disagree is on the attempt to turn **“no plant closures, no compulsory redundancies” into absolute red lines before the economics are solved**. That is not a credible governance position. If a site is structurally uneconomic, if capacity is materially above market demand, and if the current cost base continues to prevent adequate returns, then the company cannot be forced to preserve every structure regardless of consequences. That would not be stewardship; it would be value erosion dressed up as social peace.

A few direct points:

### To IG Metall and the works council
- I **do not dismiss** your concern about social and industrial damage.
- I **do** reject the idea that protection of every site can be guaranteed in advance without a package that delivers comparable savings.
- I **do** support exploring non-closure options first: working-time flexibility, voluntary exits, attrition, reallocation, line consolidation, product shifts, and productivity gains.
- But if those measures do not add up to **closure-level savings with enforceable durability**, then insisting on no closures becomes a veto on competitiveness.

Your request for job and site guarantees through 2030 is understandable, but as a controlling owner I cannot support an unconditional guarantee that locks in the current footprint regardless of future economics. That would be irresponsible to the business and the owners.

### To management
I agree with the need for a **durable** solution, but I am not here to endorse management simply because it is “tough.” Toughness without implementation is not governance. If you want support, bring a package that is:
- quantified,
- legally and operationally enforceable,
- and credible on timing and savings.

If the only plan is to threaten closures and hope that pressure alone produces a deal, that is not good enough. It will likely destroy trust and weaken execution.

### To Lower Saxony
I understand the political reality and the importance of the industrial base. But a blanket refusal of closures or forced redundancies is not a sustainable answer if the economics fail. The state’s influence should be used to shape a **better** restructuring, not to block every hard decision.

### My line
I am prepared to support:
- capacity reduction,
- labour-cost moderation,
- voluntary exit schemes,
- working-time flexibility,
- targeted investment commitments,
- and a negotiated package that protects the long-term industrial base where economically justified.

I am **not** prepared to support:
- a settlement that preserves all sites and all current cost structures by default,
- a permanent lock-in of uncompetitive labour costs,
- or a compromise that buys short-term peace and guarantees long-term decline.

### What would actually move me
If the employee side can help deliver a package that achieves:
1. material capacity reduction,
2. durable labour-cost relief,
3. measurable productivity improvements,
4. and sufficient implementation certainty,

then I would support serious protections around the sites that remain viable. But the sequence matters: **economics first, guarantees second**. Not the other way around.

So I will be blunt: I do not accept the current employee-side red lines as final. They are a starting position for bargaining, not a basis for a lasting solution. If the discussion stays there, then we remain at risk of confrontation. If it moves to a real, quantified package, I will engage constructively.
