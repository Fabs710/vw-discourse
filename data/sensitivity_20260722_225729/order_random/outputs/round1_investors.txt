We are aligned on one central point: Volkswagen needs a **real** restructuring, not a cosmetic arrangement that leaves margins weak and the cost base uncompetitive.

From the capital markets’ perspective, the question is not whether adjustment is painful; it is whether the outcome is **credible, durable, and sufficient** to restore risk-adjusted value.

### Where I agree
- I agree with management and the owners that **capacity has to come down** and costs have to fall materially.
- I agree that a deal which only preserves the current structure and buys a few quarters of peace would be value-destructive.
- I agree that savings must be **durable**, not just temporary accounting improvements.
- I also agree that prolonged strikes and open-ended conflict would damage value, execution, and investor confidence.

### Where I disagree
- I do **not** support a settlement that relies on closure threats alone without a clear, implementable path to savings.
- I do **not** support a package that preserves all sites and all current cost structures while calling it a restructuring.
- I do **not** accept a “shared sacrifice” narrative if the actual burden is vague, delayed, or insufficient to restore competitive returns.

To IG Metall and the works council:  
Your demand for no compulsory redundancies and no plant closures is understandable as a negotiating position, but it is **not enough by itself**. Investors have seen too many situations where structural problems are deferred under the guise of social peace. That is not acceptable here. If the company’s return-on-sales target is to be recovered, then the package must produce **measurable, enforceable, near- to medium-term margin improvement**.

To management:  
Your case is strongest when you present **hard numbers** and a binding implementation path. If you want capital-market support, show:
- the scale of capacity reduction,
- the timing of savings,
- the cost of transition,
- the impact on margins,
- and why the deal is more credible than prolonging conflict.

A restructuring is only convincing if it improves the earnings trajectory enough to justify the disruption. If the plan is too weak to change the economics, it should not be sold as a solution.

To the controlling owners and Lower Saxony:  
I hear the concern about value destruction from confrontation, and I agree. But political protection of every site is not a substitute for competitiveness. If the result is a compromise that leaves structural overcapacity in place, the share price and long-term capital allocation will reflect that. We will not reward a settlement that protects optics while impairing returns.

### Our position
We are prepared to support a tough restructuring **if and only if** it credibly delivers:
- durable cost reductions,
- capacity rationalisation,
- a clear path back to competitive margins,
- and enough implementation certainty to make the savings real.

What would not be acceptable:
- cosmetic savings,
- endless negotiation without execution,
- or an outcome that leaves the cost base structurally impaired.

So our message is simple:  
**Do the restructuring properly, or do not pretend it has been done.** We will back decisive action that restores competitiveness. We will not back delay dressed up as compromise.
