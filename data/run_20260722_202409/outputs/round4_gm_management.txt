We are at the point where clarity matters more than reassurance.

## My position

I agree with the task force on the central fact: **the old capital structure is not survivable**. I agree with labour that the trust cannot be treated as if it were disposable. I agree with bondholders that **selective preference** for the trust would be unfair and destabilizing. I agree with the neighbouring governments that if they put in money, they deserve real protection for viable domestic facilities.

But I do **not** accept any solution that:
- preserves the retiree trust near par,
- gives bondholders a result that starves the company of balance-sheet room,
- or locks in uneconomic plants simply because they are politically sensitive.

The enterprise survives only if the restructuring is deep enough to restore competitiveness.

## On the retiree trust

To labour: I hear you. A token stub is not a settlement, and a pure wipeout would be unstable. That is true.

But the trust is still a legacy unsecured obligation. It cannot be insulated from loss while the company is being asked to reset itself. If we try to preserve too much of it, we will fail the viability test and end up destroying more value for everyone.

So my position is:
- **substantial impairment** of the trust,
- with **non-cash upside** possible,
- but **no cash-like protection**, no meaningful floor that behaves like a senior claim, and no outcome that burdens the reorganized company with fixed obligations it cannot carry.

That is not anti-labour. It is what keeps the company alive.

## On bondholders

To the bondholders: you are right that the trust cannot be given a privileged recovery. I will not support that.

But I am also not endorsing the idea that bondholders should be made whole in substance while the company is left weak. This restructuring must produce a balance sheet the business can actually operate with.

So:
- bondholders will take **major haircuts**,
- recoveries may come through **equity and warrants**,
- and the result must be consistent with a viable post-emergence company.

What I will not accept is a deal that protects unsecured recoveries at the expense of the operating turnaround.

## On labour and the retiree trust’s “real value”

I understand the demand for durable value. But durable value only means something if the company survives.

What I can support:
- equity or warrants for the trust,
- contingent value tied to recovery,
- and a structure that gives retirees real participation in the upside.

What I cannot support:
- a structure that looks meaningful in headline terms but behaves like a fixed senior claim,
- or a trust package so rich that it leaves no room for the balance sheet to breathe.

The right answer is not “protect the trust first.” The right answer is “make the company viable, then give the trust residual value that does not break viability.”

## On plant rationalization

This is where I need to be most direct.

We will not preserve every plant. We will not preserve plants that cannot compete. We will not preserve brands that dilute the franchise. And we will not maintain a dealer footprint that is larger than a credible future sales base can support.

What I can support is:
- retaining the **strongest brands**,
- preserving the **dealer network** where it matters to consumer confidence,
- keeping **competitive plants**,
- and closing or consolidating sites that fail the viability test.

That is the difference between a restructuring and a delay.

## To the neighbouring governments

You are right to ask for real commitments if you are co-funding. But I will not agree to binding guarantees that prevent us from rationalizing uneconomic capacity.

My position is:
- viable domestic plants can be protected,
- production commitments can be written around those sites,
- and you can receive **equity or governance rights proportionate to your funding**,

but you do **not** get a veto over economically necessary closures.

If a facility is not viable, it cannot stay open just because it is domestic.

## To the task force

I agree that public money should not subsidize delay. I also agree that a court-supervised route remains acceptable if it is the fastest way to finality.

But if we are going to use that route, we need to enter it with a realistic operating plan:
- fewer brands,
- fewer plants,
- smaller dealer footprint,
- lower labor cost,
- and a capital structure that the business can carry.

If we do not do that, the process becomes a fight over a shrinking pie, and the company loses.

## My practical framework

Here is the only structure I can defend operationally:

1. **Retiree trust**
   - materially impaired,
   - converted into equity/warrants/contingent value,
   - no near-par recovery.

2. **Bondholders**
   - major haircuts,
   - meaningful upside participation,
   - no expectation of clean recovery.

3. **Labour**
   - wage/work-rule concessions,
   - staffing reductions,
   - transition support where closures occur.

4. **Plants, brands, dealers**
   - major rationalization,
   - closures based on economics,
   - preserve only what supports a competitive future.

5. **Public co-funding**
   - temporary,
   - conditional,
   - with proportionate governance/equity for real contributions,
   - but no operational veto over uneconomic sites.

6. **Process**
   - if we cannot lock this quickly, go to the court-supervised route.

## What I disagree with in the other arguments

- I disagree with labour if “durable value” becomes a code word for keeping the trust too close to par.
- I disagree with bondholders if “equal treatment” becomes a way to block any settlement that gives labour a meaningful but impaired recovery.
- I disagree with the neighbouring governments if domestic protection means preserving overcapacity and shifting the pain onto the company’s core competitiveness.
- I disagree with the task force if “substantial impairment” is interpreted as a de facto destruction of labour peace and consumer confidence.

## What I need now

I need each stakeholder to answer in practical terms:

1. **Labour/trust:** Are you prepared to accept a materially impaired trust converted mainly into equity/warrants, not a cash-like floor?
2. **Bondholders:** Will you accept major haircuts if the trust is also materially impaired?
3. **Neighbouring governments:** Which domestic plants are truly viable enough to warrant binding production commitments?
4. **Task force:** Will you finance a plan that includes deep rationalization, even if it is painful?

If the answer to any of these is “no” without qualification, then we are not negotiating a rescue. We are negotiating the shape of failure.

And I will say it plainly: **failure is not acceptable**.
