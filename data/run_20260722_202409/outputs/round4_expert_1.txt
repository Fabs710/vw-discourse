From a **pension / restructuring mechanics** standpoint, the immediate issue is the **economic equivalence** of the retiree trust package versus the unsecured bondholder package.

## Core valuation point
A package for the retiree trust that includes:
- a **haircut**,
- plus **equity**,
- plus **warrants**,
- plus any **contingent value right**,
- and especially any **recovery floor**,

will usually have a **higher present value** and **lower downside risk** than a package made up only of equity/warrants.

So the valuation question is not whether the trust can receive a different instrument mix. It can. The question is whether the **present value, expected value, and risk profile** are roughly equal to what similarly situated unsecured bondholders receive.

## On the “floor” issue
A **floor** matters a lot. Even if labeled contingent, it behaves economically like **credit support** if it is:
- funded,
- near-term,
- contractually enforceable,
- or otherwise likely to pay.

That means a trust floor can create a **de facto preference** unless bondholders receive an **offsetting increase in value**.

## Practical restructuring implication
If the parties want a settlement that is more defensible on parity grounds, the cleaner structure is:

- **Trust:** material impairment, primarily equity/warrants/CVRs, no meaningful floor
- **Bondholders:** material impairment, similar upside instruments, with total PV calibrated to match unsecured-parity economics
- **No class receives a hidden seniority advantage**

If the trust does get a floor, then one of two things must be true:
1. bondholders get **more equity / more warrants / more enterprise value**, or  
2. the floor is so small and remote that its PV is **de minimis**.

Otherwise, the trust is likely receiving a **better economic recovery** than the bondholders.

## On public co-funding and governance
From a capital-structure perspective, neighboring governments can justifiably ask for:
- **equity**
- **board/governance rights**
- or another instrument with measurable economic value

in exchange for funding.

But tying public money to **binding plant commitments** can become expensive very quickly if it reduces the company’s ability to rationalize excess capacity. The key is to link commitments only to **plants that pass a viability test**; otherwise the public contribution is effectively subsidizing overcapacity.

## Bottom line
- A **trust floor** is economically meaningful and often creates a preference unless offset.
- **Equity/warrants alone** are much easier to calibrate for parity.
- Any final term sheet should be tested on **present value**, not headline haircut percentages.
- Public funding should be matched with **measurable governance value**, but not with a veto over uneconomic closures.
