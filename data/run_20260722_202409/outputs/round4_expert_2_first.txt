From a restructuring valuation perspective, the key question is whether the retiree trust’s proposed package gives it **higher present value, higher expected value, or materially lower risk** than the unsecured bondholders’ package **without an offsetting economic reason**. If it does, then it is functionally a preference, even if it is dressed up as equity, warrants, or a “floor.”

## Core valuation framework

For each unsecured class, compare:

1. **Immediate cash / funded value**
2. **Equity value at emergence**
3. **Warrant value**
4. **Contingent value rights or floors**
5. **Probability of payment**
6. **Timing / discount rate**

The right comparison is **present value**, not headline face amount.

A trust package that includes:
- a recovery floor,
- plus equity,
- plus warrants,
- plus contingent upside,

can easily exceed the present value of a bondholder package that is only equity/warrants, especially if the floor is credit-like and near-term.

## Why a “floor” matters

A true floor changes the economics sharply because it reduces downside risk. Even if the floor is contingent, if it is:
- contractually protected,
- payable relatively soon,
- or backed by funding certainty,

then it behaves more like debt than equity.

That means the trust is not just getting “some upside”; it is getting **downside protection** that other unsecureds do not have. In valuation terms, that usually increases expected recovery more than an equivalent amount of pure equity or warrants.

## Likely valuation outcome under the proposed structures

### 1) Trust with floor + equity/warrants
This is typically the **highest-valued** structure for the trust among the options discussed, because:
- the floor preserves minimum recovery,
- equity gives participation in enterprise value,
- warrants add convexity.

If bondholders do not get a comparable floor or equivalent offset, they are likely receiving **lower PV** than the trust.

### 2) Trust with only equity/warrants, no floor
This is closer to parity treatment, depending on allocation and dilution.  
Still, parity requires more than equal instrument labels; it requires roughly equal **economic value** after adjusting for:
- claim size,
- seniority assumptions,
- dilution,
- lockup restrictions,
- and expected volatility.

### 3) Bondholders with higher nominal haircut but more upside
That can be fair **if** the bondholders’ equity/warrant package has enough value to equalize PV.  
But “we took a bigger haircut” is not by itself enough to prove equal treatment. The instruments matter more than the haircut percentage.

## Does the trust floor create an impermissible preference?

In valuation terms, it **can**.

A trust floor is problematic if:
- it gives the trust a **higher expected recovery** than similarly situated unsecured bondholders,
- or a **lower-risk recovery** at similar nominal value,
- without compensation to bondholders.

The question is not whether the trust can ever receive different form of consideration. It can. The question is whether the **present value and risk-adjusted value** are roughly equivalent.

## What would need to be shown to justify the trust floor

To defend a trust floor as not preferential, you would need evidence that one or more of the following is true:

1. **Bondholders receive equivalent value elsewhere**
   - more equity,
   - more warrants,
   - a better allocation of enterprise value,
   - or some other offsetting consideration.

2. **The trust’s claim is economically different in a way that justifies a different package**
   - for example, if there were a separately enforceable funding mechanism, dedicated assets, or a demonstrably different settlement basis.  
   - Absent that, unsecured parity is the default valuation anchor.

3. **The floor is so small and so contingent that its PV is de minimis**
   - though in practice, even “small” floors can matter if they are funded and near-term.

4. **The bondholder package has other value not reflected on its face**
   - for instance, anti-dilution protection, registration rights, governance rights, or a larger share of residual equity.

From a valuation standpoint, the **most likely source of dispute** is that a trust floor would cause the trust’s recovery to exceed the bondholders’ on a PV basis, unless the bondholders are explicitly compensated with more equity or warrants.

- **If both classes get different instruments, the decisive issue is PV equivalence, not identical form.**
