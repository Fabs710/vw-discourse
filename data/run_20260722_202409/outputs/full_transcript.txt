[Government Task Force (Lead Funder) (Executive-branch task force controlling rescue financing) — Round 1]
The government task force’s position is straightforward:

We will support a restructuring only if it is deep, fast, and credible enough to produce a viable company without open-ended public subsidy.

Our priorities are:

1. **Viability first**  
   The company must emerge with a cost structure, plant footprint, and balance sheet that can survive on its own in a depressed market. We will not finance a plan that merely delays failure.

2. **Shared sacrifice**  
   Management, labour, retiree obligations, and unsecured creditors must all absorb meaningful losses. Taxpayers cannot be the only party taking the hit.

3. **Speed and finality**  
   We have a 60-day window. Delay tactics are unacceptable. If consensual agreement cannot be reached quickly, we are prepared to support an expedited court-supervised process that can impose the necessary terms.

4. **Taxpayer protection**  
   Public funds are bridge financing, not a permanent subsidy. Any continued support must be tied to a credible path to exit and repayment.

What we are **not** prepared to do:
- rescue the company as currently structured,
- fund an unreformed balance sheet indefinitely,
- allow creditors or labour to hold out for a better deal at public expense,
- accept a plan that leaves excess capacity and legacy liabilities largely intact.

What we are looking for is a package that includes:
- substantial reductions in labor cost and legacy obligations,
- a serious settlement on the retiree health claim,
- significant concessions from unsecured bondholders,
- major footprint rationalization,
- and a capital structure that gives the reorganized company a real chance of survival.

If stakeholders can produce that quickly and verifiably, we will work toward it. If not, we will move to the court-supervised route rather than keep the company alive on an unrestructured basis.

If we want a company left to save, the settlement has to be hard enough to work.

[Unsecured Bondholders' Committee (Ad hoc committee of dispersed unsecured creditors (USD 27 billion)) — Round 1]
We are not signing up to be the residual shock absorber for everyone else’s politics.

Let me be clear: the unsecured bondholders accept that this company needs a real restructuring, fast. We are not denying viability, and we are not pretending the old capital structure can survive intact. But the way the other parties are framing this is drifting toward an outcome where **dispersed unsecured creditors are wiped down to token value while the retiree trust gets preferential political treatment**. That is not an acceptable starting point.

### Where we agree
- **The company needs a deep restructuring.** A cosmetic fix is pointless.
- **Time matters.** Delay destroys value.
- **Excess capacity must go.** If plants, brands, and dealer footprint are not rationalized, there is no viable future.
- **Management and labour both need to make concessions.** We are not here to subsidize prior mistakes.

### Where we disagree
#### 1) The retiree trust cannot be treated as untouchable
I reject the idea that the retiree health obligation should be preserved close to par while unsecured bondholders are told to take the hard losses because we are “financial creditors.”

That is exactly the sort of politically driven subordination that makes this process unfair. The retiree trust may have moral force, but it is still an unsecured claim in an insolvent restructuring. If anyone is going to be asked to compromise, **it has to be on all unsecured claims proportionately**, not selectively.

If the trust is getting better treatment than the bondholders, there had better be real, measurable compensation for that disparity — not rhetoric about fairness.

#### 2) “Shared sacrifice” cannot mean asymmetric sacrifice
Management says bondholders must take substantial losses, and we accept that in principle. But that sacrifice must be tied to a restructuring that is **even-handed across unsecured creditors**. We will not accept a structure where:
- the retiree trust gets meaningful equity or value,
- workers get protection on jobs and communities,
- public financing props up the transition,
- and bondholders are left with a diluted scrap.

If the government wants creditor sacrifice, then it must be distributed fairly. We are not the class that should be singled out because we are dispersed and less politically organized.

#### 3) Court-supervised process is not a threat we will ignore
The task force is hinting that it can impose terms through an expedited court process. We understand that leverage. But that does not mean we will accept confiscatory terms voluntarily.

If the alternative is a court process, then the relevant question is not “who can be bullied into agreement fastest?” It is: **what is the likely recovery if this is imposed?** If the proposed deal falls materially below what unsecured creditors can plausibly receive in court, then we will not consent just to make the paperwork easier for everyone else.

### What we will support
We are open to a deal if it includes all of the following:
- **Pro rata treatment of unsecured claims**, including the retiree trust, unless any deviation is explicitly compensated;
- **Meaningful equity or warrants** for unsecured bondholders, not a nominal stub;
- **A recoverable value that is not a wipeout**;
- **Real cuts to excess capacity and legacy costs**, including labour costs and retiree obligations;
- **Fast execution**, provided the economics are not punitive.

### What we will not support
- A deal that gives the retiree trust materially better treatment than other unsecured creditors without equalizing value.
- A token equity issue that pretends to be consideration while transferring all upside elsewhere.
- A plan that uses public urgency to force bondholders into accepting a worse outcome than they would face in a court-supervised restructuring.
- Any attempt to frame bondholders as speculators blocking rescue when many holders are ordinary savers with legitimate claims.

### Bottom line
We are willing to take losses. We are not willing to be treated as the class that must absorb the deepest cut so that more politically sensitive stakeholders can be protected.

If the task force and management want our consent, then the deal has to be **fair across unsecured creditors, fast, and real in value**. Otherwise, we will take our chances in court rather than sign away the claim for a confiscatory exchange.

[Expert: Pension valuation and restructuring spec — Round 3]
Within my domain, the key issue is **how to convert a large retiree health obligation from a cash-like unsecured legacy claim into a durable, financeable instrument without making the capital structure unworkable**.

## Technical assessment

### 1) A “real” settlement for the retiree trust cannot be near-par
If the company is insolvent or effectively insolvent on a going-concern basis, the trust claim must be **materially impaired**. A near-par promise would likely be:
- unaffordable,
- litigable as preferential relative to other unsecured claims,
- and inconsistent with the need for post-restructuring liquidity.

So the task force is technically right that the trust cannot be insulated.

### 2) But a pure wipeout is usually unstable
If the trust is reduced to a nominal stub with no meaningful value, several problems arise:
- labor may reject the deal and force delay,
- the court process may become more contentious,
- and the company may lose the “social durability” needed for operations and supplier confidence.

From a restructuring mechanics standpoint, **some non-cash value** to the trust is often useful even if not legally required:
- long-dated contingent value rights,
- equity,
- warrants,
- or a subordinated payment stream tied to enterprise performance.

That does not mean “protecting” the trust; it means giving it enough residual value to settle.

### 3) The cleanest structure is usually a split: modest present value + contingent upside
For a retiree health trust in this situation, the most workable architecture is often:

- **Base haircut:** substantial reduction in face amount or actuarial value.
- **New instrument package:** one or more of:
  - common equity,
  - warrants,
  - contingent value rights,
  - or a long-dated payment obligation tied to free cash flow / EBITDA / post-emergence equity value.

A practical range in distressed restructurings is often:
- **low cash component**, if any,
- **material haircut**, commonly very large,
- plus **contingent upside** sized to make the settlement acceptable but not to impair viability.

### 4) Equity or warrants are usually better than a “guaranteed” funding promise
If the trust receives a contractual funding stream that is too firm, it starts behaving like senior debt and can burden the reorganized company.

By contrast:
- **equity** gives the trust exposure to recovery without fixed cash drain,
- **warrants** preserve upside without immediate dilution cost,
- **contingent value rights** can be tailored to trigger only if the company recovers.

For a distressed carmaker, this is usually preferable to a hard cash-like obligation.

### 5) The trust should not be economically senior to unsecured bondholders unless there is a clear offset
If the bondholders and trust are both broadly unsecured legacy claims, then giving the trust a materially better recovery without compensation is likely to trigger objections and reduce deal durability.

If the trust gets:
- a recovery floor,
- enhanced equity,
- or a funded stream,

then bondholders generally need either:
- comparable upside,
- or a correspondingly higher face-value haircut to preserve relative fairness.

### 6) The trust’s “minimum acceptable” value should be defined in present-value terms, not nominal terms
The right metric is not “percentage of face,” but **present value of expected recovery** under reasonable scenarios.

For example, a package may look small in headline terms but still be meaningful if it includes:
- warrants with realistic strike levels,
- equity in a re-levered enterprise,
- and a contingent payment tied to operating performance.

Conversely, a nominally larger promise may be worthless if it is too deferred, too contingent, or dependent on unrealistic profit assumptions.

## What I would consider financially durable

A credible structure would usually have these features:

- **Material impairment** of the retiree claim.
- **No cash-like seniority** that burdens the reorganized firm.
- **Residual equity or warrants** for upside participation.
- **Possibly a contingent payment right** if triggered by strong performance.
- **No guaranteed par floor** unless there is a separate funding source.
- **Equal or near-equal treatment** with other unsecured legacy claims unless a specific legal priority exists.

## Sensitivity points
The size of the trust’s recovery depends on:
- enterprise value at emergence,
- post-restructuring debt load,
- pension/other legacy obligations,
- plant rationalization savings,
- and whether public financing is truly junior and temporary.

If enterprise value is tight, then even modest fixed-value claims can become destabilizing. In that case, **equity-heavy treatment is superior to debt-like treatment**.

## Bottom line
From a pension/restructuring mechanics standpoint:

- The retiree trust **cannot be preserved near par**.
- It also **should not be reduced to a meaningless token** if the goal is a durable deal.
- The most robust design is a **large haircut plus equity/warrant/contingent value participation**, with no fixed promise that undermines solvency.
- Any “minimum recovery floor” should be **small, deeply contingent, and clearly subordinated to viability**.

A true floor changes the economics sharply because it reduces downside risk. Even if the floor is contingent, if it is:
- contractually protected,
- payable relatively soon,
- or backed by funding certainty,

then it behaves more like debt than equity.

That means the trust is not just getting “some upside”; it is getting **downside protection** that other unsecureds do not have. In valuation terms, that usually increases expected recovery more than an equivalent amount of pure equity or warrants.

- claim size,
- seniority assumptions,
- dilution,
- lockup restrictions,

In valuation terms, it **can**.

A trust floor is problematic if:
- it gives the trust a **higher expected recovery** than similarly situated unsecured bondholders,
- or a **lower-risk recovery** at similar nominal value,
- without compensation to bondholders.

The question is not whether the trust can ever receive different form of consideration. It can. The question is whether the **present value and risk-adjusted value** are roughly equivalent.

1. **Bondholders receive equivalent value elsewhere**

3. **The floor is so small and so contingent that its PV is de minimis**
   - though in practice, even “small” floors can matter if they are funded and near-term.

4. **The bondholder package has other value not reflected on its face**
   - for instance, anti-dilution protection, registration rights, governance rights, or a larger share of residual equity.
