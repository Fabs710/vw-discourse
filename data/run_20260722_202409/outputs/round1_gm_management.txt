We agree with the task force on the core point: **the company must be made viable, fast, and with real sacrifice from all sides**. We also agree with labour on one important principle: **the retiree obligation cannot simply be waved away without destroying trust and undermining a settlement**.

But I need to be direct about where we stand.

### Where we agree
- **Viability comes first.** Without a cost structure and capital structure the market can support, there is no company left to protect.
- **Shared sacrifice is unavoidable.** Management, labour, retirees, and unsecured creditors all have to give something up.
- **Speed matters.** We do not have the luxury of a drawn-out process while cash runs out.

### Where we disagree with labour
We cannot accept a structure that treats the retiree trust as effectively sacrosanct while expecting the rest of the enterprise to absorb the full burden. That is not viable.

The company’s current obligations are simply too large relative to expected earnings. If the retiree claim is preserved too close to par, the result is predictable:
- too much cash burden,
- too little room to invest in product and competitiveness,
- and ultimately a weaker company that fails later.

That is exactly what we must avoid.

What we need is not abolition of the trust, but **a major restructuring of it**:
- some combination of reduced face value,
- extended maturity / delayed access,
- and a meaningful equity component rather than just another promise on top of an already stretched balance sheet.

I am prepared to say clearly: **the trust should receive real value, but not on terms that make the reorganized company nonviable.**

### On bondholders
The unsecured bondholders must take substantial losses. We agree with the task force there. They cannot be insulated while the operating company, workers, and taxpayers bear the cost. But there is a practical limit: if bondholders are wiped out too aggressively in a way that invites endless litigation or wrecks financing credibility, that also hurts the company. So the recovery must be reduced enough to be credible, but not in a way that destabilizes implementation.

### On plants, brands, and the dealer network
We will not defend every asset. The earlier plan was not aggressive enough, and that is precisely why we are here. But cuts must be tied to a defensible operating plan:
- preserve the brands and facilities that are central to the future franchise,
- eliminate excess capacity,
- simplify the network,
- and avoid keeping marginal operations alive just because they are politically sensitive.

If we fail to make those choices now, there will be no going concern left to protect.

### On process
We are open to either consensual agreement or an expedited court-supervised process, but only if it delivers:
- financing certainty,
- fast execution,
- and protection for consumer confidence, especially warranties and dealer continuity.

What we cannot support is delay or a half-measure that burns the bridge financing and leaves the company in limbo. If a court process is the only way to force the needed balance across labour and creditors, then we will work within that route.

### Bottom line
Our position is:
- **Yes** to a deeply restructured company.
- **Yes** to material losses for bondholders.
- **Yes** to meaningful restructuring of the retiree trust.
- **Yes** to deeper cuts than the prior plan contemplated.
- **No** to a solution that preserves legacy claims so fully that the company cannot survive.

If we want a company left to save, the settlement has to be hard enough to work.
