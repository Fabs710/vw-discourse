We are prepared to engage, but let’s be clear: **we will not sign up to a structure where the retiree trust is given a politically protected recovery while unsecured bondholders are asked to absorb the deepest cut.** That is not “shared sacrifice.” That is selective subordination dressed up as rescue.

## Where we agree
- The company needs a **deep, fast, final restructuring**.
- The current capital structure is broken and cannot be rolled forward.
- Excess capacity has to come out.
- A court-supervised fallback is acceptable if it produces a credible, enforceable result quickly.
- Equity and warrants are better than false promises of cash-like repayment.

## Where we disagree

### 1) On the retiree trust
To the task force, management, and labour: we accept that the trust cannot be preserved near par. We are not asking for a clean recovery, and we are not pretending the claim is sacred.

But we **do not** accept:
- a “durable” trust package that is better than other unsecured claims,
- a recovery floor that functions as a hidden preference,
- or an arrangement where bondholders are told to accept a larger impairment so the trust can be made more politically palatable.

If the trust receives meaningful equity, warrants, or contingent value, then bondholders must receive **comparable total value**. If not, you are shifting value from one unsecured class to another under pressure.

So the issue is not whether the trust gets some value. The issue is **whether the trust gets better value than similarly situated unsecured creditors without compensation**. That is the line we will not cross.

### 2) On “equal treatment”
We agree with the bondholders’ committee on one core principle: **no material preference for the retiree trust over other unsecured claims without an offset**.

The task force keeps saying the trust is not ordinary paper because it came out of labour settlements. That may be true as a political fact, but it does not create a legal priority above unsecured bondholders. If you want to deviate from parity, then the deviation must be explicit, justified, and matched by equivalent value to bondholders.

A package that gives the trust:
- a floor,
- plus equity,
- plus contingent upside,

while bondholders get a deeper haircut and a token stub, is not a compromise. It is an engineered redistribution away from bondholders.

We will not consent to that.

### 3) On court-supervised fallback
The task force says the court route may be used to impose broad sacrifice. Fine. But that route is only acceptable to us if it does **not** worsen our position relative to a voluntary deal.

We are not going to be dragged into court on the assumption that unsecured bondholders are the easiest class to haircut. If the imposed plan gives the trust a better recovery than us, we will challenge it. If the company wants finality, it needs to respect creditor parity, not invent a political hierarchy.

### 4) On public co-funding and domestic plants
We are not taking sides in the domestic footprint dispute, except on one point: **public money should not be used to preserve uneconomic capacity and then charged against unsecured recoveries.**

If neighbouring governments want equity or governance rights for their funding, that is their business. But those rights cannot be funded by squeezing bondholders below what a fair restructuring would otherwise deliver.

And let’s be blunt: if domestic protections or regional commitments increase enterprise value, fine. If they reduce value, then unsecured creditors should not be forced to subsidize them. We are not a captive source of political industrial policy.

### 5) On management’s “viability” argument
We hear the need for viability. But viability is not a blank cheque to allocate losses however management and the task force find easiest.

The moment “viability” becomes a justification for:
- giving the trust a superior recovery,
- protecting domestic constituencies,
- and leaving bondholders with whatever is left,

the process stops being a restructuring and becomes a transfer mechanism.

That is exactly what we are resisting.

## Our position on the proposed settlement architecture
From our standpoint, the only acceptable framework is:

1. **All unsecured legacy claims are materially impaired**
   - including the retiree trust.

2. **No unsecured class gets materially better treatment without offsetting value**
   - especially not the trust.

3. **Any equity/warrant package must be calibrated by present value**
   - not by headline optics.

4. **If public funding supports the deal, it cannot come out of bondholder recoveries**
   - unless everyone is sharing proportionately.

5. **If the court route is used, it must not be a backdoor cramdown that privileges labour claims**
   - we will not accept that.

## What we need to see
If anyone wants our consent, put real numbers on the table:
- trust haircut,
- bondholder recovery,
- equity/warrant allocations,
- and how the present value is equalized across unsecureds.

Not slogans. Not “durable value.” Not “political necessity.” Numbers.

## Our bottom line
We are willing to accept:
- substantial losses,
- equity or warrants,
- and a fast court-backed solution if necessary.

We are **not** willing to accept:
- a retiree trust package that is materially better than ours,
- a disguised preference for labour over bondholders,
- or a public rescue that uses bondholder value to subsidize domestic or political objectives.

If that is the direction of travel, then there is no consensual deal from our side.
