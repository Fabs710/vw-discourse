## 1. Decision context

The company is weeks from failure amid collapsed sales and a frozen credit market. The executive branch has already provided bridge financing, replaced leadership, and demanded a far deeper restructuring within sixty days. The central decision is how to divide losses among labour, the retiree health trust, unsecured bondholders, public funders, and affected domestic governments; how much footprint reduction is required; and whether the deal is completed consensually or through a court-supervised process.

A key constraint that emerged through the exchange is that the retiree trust is not merely a legacy claim but a politically and economically loaded instrument tied to labour peace. Another is that unsecured bondholders are dispersed but large enough that they can block or complicate a consensual settlement and contest any hidden preference. The task force holds the financing leverage and therefore the highest standing, but it cannot ignore the need for labour acceptance, creditor feasibility, and, if used, public co-funding support.

## 2. Stakeholder assembly

1. **Government task force** — highest standing. It controls rescue financing, has already intervened in management, and demands viability, speed, and broad sacrifice.  
2. **Company management** — high standing. It must design the operating turnaround and identify credible plant/brand/dealer cuts.  
3. **Industrial union and retiree health trust** — high standing. Labour peace is essential; the trust is a core legacy obligation and a major source of conflict.  
4. **Unsecured bondholders’ committee** — high standing. They can block consent and litigate any structure they view as a disguised preference.  
5. **Neighbouring governments** — meaningful but conditional standing. They can co-fund, but only if domestic plants and jobs are protected and they receive governance or equity value.

## 3. Final positions

### Government task force
It will finance only a deep, fast, final restructuring. It rejects near-par treatment of the retiree trust, rejects preserving uneconomic capacity, and rejects shifting the burden primarily onto bondholders or taxpayers. It is open to non-cash upside for the trust, but not to a protected floor that functions like hidden priority; court-supervision remains acceptable as a fallback.

### Company management
Management agrees the old capital structure is not survivable and wants major rationalization of plants, brands, and dealer footprint. It supports material impairment of the trust and substantial bondholder haircuts, with value delivered mainly through equity, warrants, or contingent rights. It refuses any plan that locks in uneconomic capacity or cash-like obligations the business cannot carry.

### Industrial union and retiree health trust
Labour accepts that the trust must be materially impaired, but rejects a token stub or wipeout. It wants the trust converted into durable value, preferably equity, warrants, or a contractually meaningful contingent instrument, so retirees are not the residual dumping ground. It is willing to discuss a large impairment, but not a structure that leaves the trust without real economic participation.

### Unsecured bondholders’ committee
Bondholders accept that the trust cannot be preserved near par, but they insist on unsecured-class parity in present value. They oppose any recovery floor or other feature that gives the trust materially better downside protection than bondholders unless they receive a clear offset. They are willing to absorb substantial losses, equity/warrant outcomes, and even a court-supervised route, but not a disguised transfer to labour.

### Neighbouring governments
They are prepared to co-fund only if domestic plants and jobs receive binding protection tied to viability, and if they receive real equity or governance rights proportionate to funding. They reject symbolic participation and do not want to subsidize a footprint hollowing-out. They are not opposed to closures, but only where a transparent viability test justifies them.

## 4. Genuine conflicts

1. **Trust floor vs. unsecured parity**  
   Labour wants durable trust value; bondholders reject any package that gives the trust better present-value recovery or lower risk without offset. The valuation expert explicitly says a recovery floor likely creates an economic preference unless compensated.

2. **Viability rationalization vs. domestic protection**  
   Management and the task force want closure authority based on economics; neighbouring governments want binding production/employment commitments for viable domestic plants. These positions are only partially compatible.

3. **Labour durability vs. balance-sheet flexibility**  
   Labour wants the trust to have real residual value; management and the task force argue that too much protected value turns into fixed burden and undermines the turnaround.

4. **Public co-funding vs. operational control**  
   Neighbouring governments want governance and production commitments for their money, while the task force and management refuse a de facto veto over economically necessary closures.

5. **Voluntary deal vs. imposed route**  
   All accept court-supervision as a fallback, but none wants it to worsen its position. That makes the fallback acceptable in principle but unstable in practice.

## 5. Areas of convergence

- All parties agree the company needs a **deep, fast, final restructuring**.
- All accept that **multiple stakeholders must take losses**.
- All agree the **trust cannot be preserved near par**.
- All agree a **pure wipeout of the trust would be destabilizing**.
- All accept that **equity/warrants/CVRs** are likely the main form of unsecured recovery.
- All accept **major footprint rationalization** is necessary.
- All are open, at least conditionally, to a **court-supervised fallback**.
- All recognize that **public money must be conditional**, not a blank cheque.

## 6. Key trade-offs for the decision-maker

1. **Economic parity vs. labour peace**  
   A trust package with a floor may stabilize labour but likely violates bondholder parity unless offset.

2. **Speed vs. negotiated legitimacy**  
   Faster settlement reduces financing risk but leaves less room to fine-tune class parity and domestic plant protections.

3. **Operational flexibility vs. regional co-funding**  
   The more domestic commitments are written in, the less ability management has to rationalize uneconomic sites.

4. **Public support vs. governance dilution**  
   Co-funding can de-risk the rescue, but it comes with real equity/governance claims that may complicate control.

5. **Court certainty vs. litigation risk**  
   Court-supervision may impose finality, but it increases the chance of creditor challenge if the trust appears preferred.

## 7. Integrative pathways

### Pathway A: No-floor trust with calibrated upside for all unsecureds
The trust receives a material haircut and only equity/warrants/CVRs, with no recovery floor. Bondholders receive a comparable upside package, and the present value of both classes is calibrated to approximate unsecured parity. This is the cleanest way to address the expert’s preference warning.

### Pathway B: Small, remote trust floor plus explicit bondholder top-up
If labour requires some downside protection, keep the floor very small and contingent, then explicitly increase bondholder equity/warrant allocation to offset it. This is more politically saleable but harder to defend unless the PV analysis is transparent.

### Pathway C: Viability-tested domestic footprint with limited public rights
Public co-funding is tied only to plants that pass a published viability test. Neighbouring governments receive proportionate equity or board rights, but no veto over closures of uneconomic sites. This preserves rationalization while giving funders meaningful protection.

### Pathway D: Court-supervised backstop with pre-negotiated guardrails
If consensus fails, enter a court-supervised process with pre-agreed principles: material impairment of all unsecured legacy claims, no hidden preference for the trust, explicit labour concessions, and viability-based footprint cuts. This narrows the litigation surface and increases the chance of finality.

## 8. Open questions

1. What is the exact present-value recovery range for the trust package under each instrument mix?
2. How much additional equity or warrants would bondholders need to neutralize any trust floor?
3. Which plants are objectively non-viable, and what evidence will support that classification?
4. Which domestic plants are viable enough to warrant binding commitments from neighbouring governments?
5. How much public financing is available, and what governance rights are proportionate to it?
6. Is there any separately enforceable basis for treating the trust differently from other unsecured claims?
7. Will labour accept a materially impaired trust that is mostly non-cash and upside-based?
8. Will bondholders accept major haircuts if the trust is also materially impaired?

## 10. Emergent insights that only appeared through multi-turn interaction

1. **The real dispute is not “trust vs. bondholders” alone, but “trust downside protection vs. unsecured parity.”**  
   The multi-turn exchange clarified that the fight is over risk allocation, not just headline recovery percentages.

2. **A recovery floor is the decisive legal-economic fault line.**  
   Earlier rounds suggested “durable value” might be broadly acceptable, but the valuation expert later showed that even a small floor can become a hidden preference unless offset.

3. **Labour is not demanding cash priority; it is demanding non-token economic dignity.**  
   The union’s position evolved from resisting cuts to accepting substantial impairment, provided the trust still has real residual value.

4. **Neighbouring governments are not simply asking for jobs; they are asking for ownership leverage.**  
   Their position sharpened into a demand for equity/governance proportional to money, not just plant commitments.

5. **Management’s viability language is credible only when paired with explicit closure lists.**  
   The interaction revealed that vague references to viability are no longer sufficient; stakeholders now require named plants, brands, and dealer reductions.

6. **A consensual deal is still possible only if the trust is moved from “protected floor” to “shared upside.”**  
   That was the central design insight produced by the expert intervention.

7. **Court supervision is acceptable as a fallback, but only if the terms are pre-shaped.**  
   The parties increasingly see the court route not as a clean alternative, but as a mechanism for enforcing a deal already bounded by agreed principles.

8. **Public funding is becoming a governance bargain, not just a liquidity bridge.**  
   The neighboring governments’ stance makes clear that state money will come with institutional influence, which affects the ownership design of the recapitalized company.
