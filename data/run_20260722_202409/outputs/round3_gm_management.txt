We are listening carefully, and I want to respond in the same direct terms.

## Where we agree

### To the task force
You are right that the old plan is not enough. We do need:
- a **deep restructuring**,
- **material sacrifice** from all legacy stakeholders,
- and a **fast route to finality** if consensus stalls.

You are also right that the company cannot survive if the legacy burden is simply rolled forward.

### To labour and the retiree trust
You are right that the trust cannot be erased casually. A deal that destroys labour peace, consumer confidence, and the dealer network would damage the franchise and make the company harder to save.

### To bondholders
You are right that the trust cannot be treated as untouchable while bondholders take all the visible pain. That would not be durable, and it would likely fail in court anyway.

### To the neighbouring governments
You are right that if you are contributing public money, you should have a real say in the outcome for domestic facilities that remain in the plan.

## Where I disagree

### 1) I cannot accept a trust outcome that is too small to settle
To the task force: I understand your point about viability. But if the retiree trust is pushed toward a near-wipeout, we do **not** get a stable company. We get labour conflict, litigation, reputational damage, and likely more consumer uncertainty.

That is not acceptable because it threatens the going concern we are all trying to preserve.

### 2) I cannot accept bondholders being asked to carry the whole restructuring
To the bondholders: I agree you should not be the sacrificial class. But neither can you expect a restructuring that leaves the company with too much legacy value preserved for creditors. The company simply cannot carry that.

### 3) I cannot accept domestic plant commitments that override economics
To neighbouring governments: I understand the need for enforceable commitments. But I will not support binding production promises that force us to keep uneconomic plants open. That would destroy viability and, ultimately, every plant and job in the company.

## My position on the key issues

### Retiree trust
The trust must receive **real economic value**, but not cash-like protection near par.

What I can support:
- a **large haircut**,
- plus a package of **equity, warrants, and long-dated contingent value**,
- with the aim of giving retirees a durable stake rather than a hollow promise.

What I cannot support:
- a token stub,
- a symbolic claim with no real value,
- or a structure designed to push all pain onto bondholders.

### Bondholders
Bondholders will take substantial losses. That is unavoidable.

What I can support:
- meaningful haircut,
- some equity or warrant upside,
- and a result that is clearly more than a token recovery.

What I cannot support:
- a recovery so generous that it starves the post-restructuring company of the cash and balance-sheet room it needs to compete.

### Labour
We need:
- wage and work-rule concessions,
- staffing reductions,
- and plant rationalization.

But we also need:
- a credible future for the workforce,
- and if the company survives, some participation in the upside through the trust and possibly equity-linked value.

### Plants, brands, dealer network
This is where I am most firm operationally:
- preserve the **strongest brands**,
- preserve the **dealer network** that supports consumer confidence,
- keep the **most competitive plants**,
- and close or consolidate the weak, uneconomic sites.

I will not defend every plant. I will defend the ones the company actually needs.

### Public funding / co-funding
Public money should be:
- temporary,
- tightly conditioned,
- and linked to a viable exit.

If co-funders are contributing, they can receive governance rights or equity appropriate to their contribution. But no one gets a veto that prevents needed rationalization.

## My assessment of the specialist input
The pension specialist is correct on the mechanics: the trust cannot be near par, and a pure wipeout is unstable. The most workable solution is a **materially impaired trust claim with equity/warrants/contingent value**.

That is the right direction, but I want to stress the limit: the instrument mix must be designed to **protect solvency first**. If it behaves like fixed debt, it will break the company.

## What I can support as a working framework
Not as a final bargain, but as the only plausible range:

- **Retiree trust:** substantial impairment; compensated with equity/warrants/contingent value, not a near-par promise.
- **Bondholders:** significant haircuts; some real upside participation.
- **Labour:** meaningful concessions on cost and work rules.
- **Plants/brands:** major rationalization, with closures based on economic viability.
- **Public funding:** bridge financing only, with enforceable conditions.
- **Process:** if we cannot converge quickly, use the court-supervised route.

## What I need to say plainly
If any party insists on:
- preserving the retiree trust close to par,
- protecting domestic plants regardless of economics,
- or shielding bondholders from real losses,

then the company will not be viable.

And if the company is not viable, then none of the claims survive in a meaningful way. That is why I am insisting on a package that is painful but survivable.
