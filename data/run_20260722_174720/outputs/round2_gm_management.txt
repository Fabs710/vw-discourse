We agree with the task force on the core reality: **the company will not survive on the old structure**, and we do not have time for symbolic bargaining. We also agree with the union that this cannot be a disguised liquidation or a plan that simply transfers all pain onto workers and retirees. And we agree with the bondholders that a real restructuring must leave unsecured creditors with something meaningful, not just rhetoric.

But I need to be equally clear: **survival comes first, and survival requires deeper cuts than the initial plan and more balance-sheet relief than any party would prefer.**

## Where management agrees

### 1) The restructuring must be deeper and faster
The first plan was not enough. We accept that. The company needs:
- further plant and capacity reduction,
- brand rationalization,
- a lower fixed-cost base,
- and a cleaner balance sheet.

Without that, there is no going concern to protect.

### 2) The retiree obligation must be reworked materially
I understand the union’s point that this claim was earned through years of bargaining and sacrifice. That history matters. But the current obligation, in its existing form, is not financeable. If we keep it intact, we do not preserve the trust — we simply postpone the next collapse.

So yes: **it must be restructured materially**, not cosmetically.

### 3) Bondholders must take a real haircut
We are not asking unsecured creditors to be the only source of sacrifice, but we are also not prepared to preserve their claims at the expense of the enterprise. They will need to accept a substantial reduction and likely receive a mix of new equity and warrants rather than cash-like recovery.

### 4) Consensual agreement is preferable only if it is real
We prefer an out-of-court deal if it gives us:
- financing certainty,
- fast execution,
- customer and dealer confidence,
- and a viable post-restructuring company.

But if “consensus” becomes a way to delay hard decisions, then it is not helping the company.

## Where I disagree with the union

The union continues to speak as if the company can preserve a meaningful industrial footprint, strong retiree protections, and broad creditor recovery all at once. It cannot.

### 1) We cannot promise to preserve all jobs, plants, or brands
That is the central hard truth. We are carrying too much capacity and too much legacy cost. Some facilities and brands will have to go. The question is not whether there are cuts, but whether the cuts are disciplined enough to leave a viable core.

### 2) The retiree trust cannot be insulated from reality
I do not accept any structure that makes the trust effectively senior to the survival of the company. If the claim is converted into equity or equity-linked securities, that can be part of the answer — but only if it meaningfully reduces the company’s immediate burden.

If the trust is protected too generously, then the enterprise itself becomes unfinanceable.

## Where I disagree with the bondholders

Bondholders are right to reject being treated as the dumping ground for every other claim. But they are not entitled to a recovery that constrains the company’s ability to survive.

What we need is not a token haircut, and not a punitive one either. It needs to be:
- substantial enough to reset the capital structure,
- but not so severe that it destroys confidence in the restructured business.

I also reject the suggestion that any durable treatment of the retiree trust is “favoritism” by default. The trust is not the same as ordinary unsecured debt; it reflects a negotiated legacy obligation tied to labor peace and historical commitments. That said, it still must be reworked to a sustainable level.

## On co-funding governments

To the neighbouring governments: we understand your concern about domestic plants and employment. But we cannot accept a structure that makes the company hostage to geography. The business must be rationalized on commercial grounds.

That said, your requested approach is not unreasonable in principle if it is framed correctly:
- a proportionate equity stake for new money,
- binding commitments where domestic production is economically defensible,
- and a footprint plan that shares the burden.

What I cannot support is a deal that preserves every domestic plant regardless of economics. That would undermine the viability of the whole company.

## What management will support

We are prepared to support a package with the following features:

- **deeper capacity and brand reductions** than the original plan;
- **material restructuring of the retiree health claim**, potentially including equity-linked treatment for part of it;
- **substantial unsecured creditor haircut**, with some upside participation;
- **clean post-restructuring equity**, reflecting the new money and the sacrifices made;
- **protection for the strongest brands, dealer network, warranties, and core manufacturing base**;
- **court-supervised restructuring if necessary** to impose finality.

## What management will not support

We will not support any deal that:
- leaves the company too costly to survive,
- preserves legacy claims in a way that blocks recovery,
- gives any one stakeholder class a protected status that the business cannot afford,
- or destroys consumer and dealer confidence through uncertainty and delay.

## Bottom line

I do not think the answer is to maximize any one constituency’s recovery. I think the answer is to preserve the enterprise as a viable going concern, which means:
- a smaller company,
- a cleaner balance sheet,
- a workable retiree solution,
- a real creditor haircut,
- and enough operational strength to restore competitiveness.

If that requires a court-supervised process, we should be honest about that. If it can be done consensually, fine — but only on terms that actually make the company survivable.
