We agree on one core fact: **this company needs a real restructuring, not a staged collapse.** Delay burns value, and no one here should pretend the old balance sheet can survive intact.

But from the unsecured bondholder side, there are some red lines.

### Where we agree
- **Viability has to come first.** If the company cannot emerge as a going concern, everyone recovers less.
- **The current cost base is too high.** Plant, brand, and capacity reductions are necessary.
- **The retiree health obligation cannot remain unchanged.** It has to be reworked into something the business can actually carry.
- **Speed matters.** A drawn-out process only destroys more recovery.

### Where we disagree
1. **We will not accept being the residual dumping ground for everyone else’s losses.**  
   Bondholders are unsecured, yes. But we are still legitimate contractual creditors, not a political cushion to make labor and the public whole. If the proposal is built around a deep sacrifice from bondholders while other constituencies preserve disproportionate value, we will oppose it.

2. **“Shared sacrifice” cannot mean unequal sacrifice disguised as fairness.**  
   The retiree trust’s treatment is a major issue. If that claim receives materially better treatment than the unsecured bond class without a defensible reason, then that is not balance-sheet restructuring; it is favoritism.

3. **We do not accept a token recovery.**  
   Small equity crumbs, vague warrants, or paper in a heavily diluted post-reorg company are not enough if the bondholders are taking a major haircut. We need meaningful upside participation and real value, not a symbolic allocation.

4. **We will not be framed as the obstacle.**  
   Many of these bonds are held by ordinary savers and retirees too. The notion that bondholders are simply speculators blocking a rescue is not acceptable. If the rescue depends on politically preferred treatment of labor claims while cutting unsecured creditors to the bone, then the fairness problem is on the table.

### On the retiree health trust
We understand the need to restructure it, but we will oppose any structure where:
- the trust is preserved at a materially superior level to unsecured bonds,
- bondholders fund the gap without compensation,
- or the trust receives quasi-priority treatment while we are written down far more severely.

If the trust is converted into durable equity or another real claim, then **we expect parity in principle and a commensurate allocation of value**. No special class should be insulated at our expense.

### On the form of our recovery
We are open to:
- equity in the restructured company,
- warrants,
- or a debt-for-equity exchange,

but only if the allocation is meaningful and the dilution is not so severe that the recovery becomes illusory. We can be flexible on form, not on substance.

### On process
We prefer a consensual deal **only if** it treats unsecured creditors fairly relative to comparable claims and provides actual recovery. Otherwise, we are prepared to take our chances in the expedited court process. The threat of a court-imposed outcome cuts both ways: it also disciplines proposals that try to push too much loss onto the bondholders.

### Bottom line
If the task force wants our support, the restructuring must show:
- a deep but credible operational reset,
- a real haircut spread across stakeholders,
- no unfair preference for the retiree trust over unsecured creditors,
- and a meaningful recovery for bondholders, preferably in equity and warrants with real upside.

If the proposal instead amounts to bondholders taking the deepest cuts while other unsecured-like obligations are protected, then we will not consent to it.
