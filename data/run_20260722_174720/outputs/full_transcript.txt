We are legitimate contractual creditors. A very large share of these bonds are held by ordinary savers, not hedge funds. We will not consent to a structure that treats us as the easy source of pain while labor, the retiree trust, and public funders secure better relative treatment.

### 2) The retiree trust cannot be favored over us
This is the central issue. If the retiree health claim is converted into some durable instrument, fine — but it must be **comparable in economic reality** to what unsecured creditors receive. What we will not accept is:
- the trust receiving materially better treatment than unsecured bonds,
- the trust being given priority-like value while we are written down harder,
- or the trust being protected through our dilution without compensation.

That would be unequal treatment dressed up as labor peace, and we reject it.

### 3) “Meaningful recovery” cannot mean symbolic equity crumbs
We are open to equity, warrants, or a debt-for-equity exchange. We are **not** open to a token allocation in a heavily diluted shell company that is then described as fair because it has upside in theory.

If we are taking a major haircut, then the recovery must be real:
- a meaningful equity stake,
- warrants with genuine upside,
- and a capital structure that does not immediately relegate unsecured creditors to irrelevance.

### 4) The court-supervised route is not a threat only to us
The task force keeps implying that court is the fallback if creditors hold out. That cuts both ways. If the consensual proposal is unfair, we are prepared to test it in the expedited process rather than volunteer for a wipe-out.

We are not bluffing. We have enough claim size to matter, and we will not accept a confiscatory exchange just because the deadline is short.

## On the retiree trust and labour
We recognize the trust was negotiated in a labor context. That does **not** make it sacrosanct, and it does **not** entitle it to better economic treatment than other unsecured claims. If the trust is to receive equity-linked value or a funded instrument, then bondholders must receive **parity in principle** and a commensurate share of the restructured upside.

If the union’s position is that the trust deserves special protection while unsecured creditors bear the heavier loss, then there is no deal from our side.

## On public co-funding and domestic plants
We are not in a position to decide domestic plant policy for the neighboring governments, but we do object to a structure where co-funders demand protected local plants while unsecured creditors are asked to subsidize the broader political bargain.

If public money is coming in, it should **not** be used to subordinate legitimate creditor recoveries to geographically favored industrial policy.

## What would move us
We are not rigid on form. We can accept equity, warrants, or a mix. But to have any chance of consent, the package must include:
- a **real unsecured recovery**, not a token one;
- **no worse treatment than the retiree trust without compensation**;
- a capital structure that gives creditors genuine upside participation;
- and enough certainty that the company survives, so the recovery is not purely theoretical.

## Bottom line
We are prepared to support a deep restructuring. We are **not** prepared to endorse one in which:
- unsecured bondholders are the primary sacrificial class,
- the retiree trust is treated more favorably,
- and the resulting consideration is little more than diluted paper.

If the task force wants our support, it needs to bring forward a package that shows **credible parity, meaningful recovery, and real upside**. Otherwise, the expedited court process remains an option we are fully prepared to consider.

[Neighbouring Governments (Co-Funders) (Federal and provincial governments co-funding to protect domestic plants) — Round 2]
We agree with the task force and management on the basic diagnosis: **the company needs a deep restructuring, and the 60-day window is real**. We also agree that the old structure cannot be preserved by wishful thinking.

But I need to be equally direct: **we will not co-fund a deal that protects foreign capacity while sacrificing our domestic plants, or one that asks us to pay without a commensurate ownership position and binding footprint commitments.**

## Where we agree
- The company needs a **credible, not cosmetic, restructuring**.
- There must be **shared sacrifice** from labour, creditors, and public funds.
- A **consensual deal is preferable** if it can be done quickly and fairly.
- The retiree obligation and unsecured debt both need **material reworking**.
- A fallback to the court-supervised route may be necessary if no one is serious.

## Where we disagree

### 1) Domestic plants cannot be treated as optional
Task force, your “no veto over rationalization” framing is too broad. We are not asking for a veto over every efficiency move. We are saying something narrower and firmer:

- **No co-funding if our domestic production base is disproportionately cut**
- **No co-funding if our plants are closed while foreign plants are insulated**
- **No co-funding without binding minimum production and employment commitments**

That is not jurisdictional free-riding. That is a legitimate condition for public money from elected governments accountable to workers and regions.

### 2) Proportionate equity is not negotiable
If we are putting in real funding to protect domestic industry, then a **meaningful equity stake proportional to our contribution** is the minimum acceptable term. Not a token warrant package, not a symbolic advisory seat, but real ownership that reflects the risk and public value we are taking on.

If the post-restructuring equity is being divided among the lead funder, labour trust instruments, and creditors, then our contribution must be recognized on the same basis. We are not junior helpers.

### 3) Plant cuts must be even-handed, not politically convenient
Management, we accept that not every plant can be saved. But we will oppose any footprint plan that loads the burden onto our jurisdictions while preserving capacity elsewhere for convenience. If closures are required, then the burden must be shared according to industrial logic, not political distance from the lead funder.

If the company wants our money, it needs to show:
- which plants are staying,
- which are closing,
- why,
- and how domestic production share is being preserved over the long term.

## On the retiree trust and bondholders
We understand the need for a deeper haircut and a durable new structure. But we are not taking a side in a class war between the union and bondholders. What matters to us is that the restructuring be **viable and fair**, and that it **does not hollow out domestic production** to make another claim class or another geography comfortable.

That means:
- the retiree claim can be reworked if necessary,
- bondholders can take a haircut,
- management can accept smaller footprint and tighter terms,

but **our domestic plants cannot be the quiet sacrificial class**.

## What would move us
To support co-funding, we need a package with all of the following:
1. **Binding domestic footprint commitments** for minimum production and employment.
2. **A proportionate equity stake** for the co-funding governments.
3. **A footprint plan that shares cuts fairly** across regions and facilities.
4. **A viable, funded restructuring** that is not simply deferred pain.

If those conditions are not met, then we should be honest: **we will not support the rescue**, even if that means the court-supervised route is used without our co-funding.

## Bottom line
We are constructive, but our support is conditional and substantive:
- **Protect domestic production**
- **Protect domestic employment**
- **Give co-funders real ownership**
- **Do not ask us to subsidize plant closures in our jurisdictions**

If the deal cannot do that, then it is not a partnership.

=== SYNTHESIS ===

## 1. Decision context
A large carmaker is facing imminent failure after a recession and credit freeze destroyed sales. The executive task force has provided bridge financing and is demanding a much deeper restructuring within a 60-day window, with court-supervised restructuring available as a fallback if no consensual deal is reached. The core design problem is how to distribute sacrifice across labour, unsecured creditors, public funders, and the company itself while restoring viability.

## 2. Stakeholder assembly
- **Government task force**: controlling rescue financing and setting the restructuring frame.
- **Company management**: newly installed leadership tasked with restoring solvency and competitiveness.
- **Industrial union & retiree health trust**: representing labour and the large negotiated retiree obligation.
- **Unsecured bondholders’ committee**: dispersed creditors holding a large unsecured claim.
- **Neighbouring governments**: potential co-funders seeking to protect domestic plants and employment.

## 3. Final positions
### Government task force
It wants a deep operational reset, a material rewrite of the retiree obligation, and a substantial haircut for unsecured creditors. It will support a clean equity structure and some upside participation for all impaired classes, but it rejects any outcome that preserves the legacy cost base or requires open-ended public subsidy.

### Company management
Management accepts that the initial plan was insufficient and that plant, brand, and capacity cuts must go deeper. It supports materially restructuring the retiree claim, taking a substantial creditor haircut, and possibly using court-supervised restructuring if necessary, but it insists the surviving company must remain operationally viable and commercially rational.

### Industrial union & retiree health trust
The union accepts that some sacrifice is unavoidable, including plant and capacity cuts and some reworking of labour terms. However, it insists the retiree trust must retain durable, enforceable value and must not be reduced to a hollow claim; it also opposes a restructuring where workers and retirees absorb the main permanent losses while creditors preserve disproportionate upside.

### Unsecured bondholders’ committee
Bondholders accept a meaningful haircut and some equity/warrant conversion, but only if the recovery is real and not merely symbolic. Their central objection is parity: they will not accept worse treatment than the retiree trust without compensation, and they are prepared to test an unfair package in the expedited court process.

### Neighbouring governments
They are willing to co-fund only if domestic production and employment are protected through binding commitments and if they receive a proportionate equity stake. They reject any arrangement that uses their money while closing their domestic plants disproportionately or leaving them with only a token ownership position.

## 4. Genuine conflicts (structurally incompatible positions)
- **Retiree trust treatment vs. bondholder parity**: the union wants durable value for the trust; bondholders refuse to be subordinated to it economically.
- **Commercial rationalization vs. domestic plant protection**: management/task force want closures based on viability; neighbouring governments demand binding domestic footprint protections.
- **Depth of sacrifice vs. legitimacy of claims**: the task force wants broad impairment; labour and creditors both contest any scheme that singles them out as the primary loss absorbers.
- **Consensus vs. coercive fallback**: the task force treats court-supervised restructuring as a credible enforcement path; labour and creditors worry it will be used to impose an unfair distribution of pain.
- **Ownership allocation**: all parties accept equity will be part of the bargain, but no agreement exists on who gets how much relative to the new money, impaired claims, and public support.

## 5. Areas of convergence
- The company needs a **deep, fast restructuring**.
- The original management plan was **not sufficiently aggressive**.
- **Some plant/brand/capacity reduction** is unavoidable.
- The **retiree obligation must be reworked** rather than left intact.
- **Unsecured creditors must take a haircut** and receive some form of upside.
- **Public support cannot simply preserve the old structure**.
- A **consensual deal is preferable** if it is real, executable, and quick; otherwise the court-supervised route is acceptable as fallback.

## 6. Key trade-offs for the decision-maker
1. **Viability vs. legacy protection**: how much retiree and labour protection can be retained without making the company unfinanceable.
2. **Creditor recovery vs. political feasibility**: how large a bondholder haircut is necessary to reset the balance sheet without triggering litigation or refusal.
3. **Commercial logic vs. regional industrial policy**: whether plant closures follow business necessity or are moderated for domestic political support.
4. **Public money vs. ownership/control**: how much equity and governance should co-funders receive in exchange for rescue financing.
5. **Speed vs. fairness**: whether to finalize a negotiated package quickly or move into court to impose terms and risk broader conflict.
6. **Equity allocation vs. sacrifice legitimacy**: how to distribute post-restructuring upside so that no group appears to have been singled out unfairly.

## 7. Integrative pathways
### Pathway A: Balanced consensual recapitalization
- Deep plant/brand rationalization.
- Retiree obligation split into a funded, enforceable instrument plus an equity-linked component.
- Bondholders receive a large haircut but meaningful equity and warrants.
- Co-funding governments receive equity proportional to new money, with limited but real domestic footprint commitments.
- Management and labour both concede additional operational and compensation changes.

### Pathway B: Court-supervised restructuring with negotiated side settlements
- Use the expedited court process to force finality on the core balance-sheet reset.
- Preserve enough negotiated side agreements with labour and co-funders to avoid total collapse of support.
- This pathway maximizes speed and coercive clarity, but risks hardening opposition if parity concerns are not addressed first.

### Pathway C: Geography-linked rescue package
- Allow co-funders to take meaningful equity in exchange for binding domestic production/employment floors.
- Share footprint cuts across regions rather than concentrating them.
- This can stabilize political support, but only if it does not materially weaken the company’s commercial rationalization.

## 8. Open questions
- What exact haircut and recovery mix will unsecured bondholders accept?
- How much of the retiree obligation can be converted into equity-linked value without undermining solvency?
- Which plants, brands, and dealer footprints close, and on what commercial criteria?
- What minimum domestic production and employment commitments are commercially defensible for co-funders?
- How much equity should each impaired class and new money provider receive?
- Will the parties accept a consensual solution, or is court-supervised restructuring inevitable?

## 9. Recommended next step
Require each stakeholder to submit a **quantified term sheet** within the remaining window:
- plant/brand closures and capacity reduction targets,
- cash vs. non-cash treatment of the retiree claim,
- creditor haircut plus equity/warrant allocation,
- co-funder contribution and corresponding equity stake,
- post-restructuring ownership split,
- and the minimum acceptable terms for consent.

## 10. Emergent insights that only appeared through multi-turn interaction
- The bargaining is no longer just about “who takes a haircut”; it has become a **three-way valuation problem** linking retiree claims, unsecured creditor recovery, and new equity distribution.
- The union’s position hardened from general opposition to a more specific demand for a **durable, enforceable retiree instrument**, which narrows possible deal structures.
- Bondholders shifted from a generic objection to a sharper **parity claim**: they will resist any package in which the retiree trust is treated as economically senior.
- The neighbouring governments clarified that they are not merely seeking reassurance; they are demanding **binding domestic footprint commitments plus proportionate ownership**, making co-funding conditional rather than supportive in principle.
- The task force’s use of the court-supervised route became more credible as a **discipline mechanism**, not just a fallback, which raises pressure on all parties to quantify concessions quickly.
- Across rounds, the interaction revealed a hidden constraint: the restructuring must satisfy not only insolvency logic but also **regional political legitimacy**, especially around plant closures and public funding.
