I’ll respond directly.

## To the Works Council
We are aligned on the essentials: **no compulsory redundancies, no plant closures, no dismantling of collective protections**. That is not rhetoric; it is the baseline for any settlement that can hold.

I also agree with your warning about “trigger clauses” becoming an escape hatch. If management uses a review mechanism merely to reopen closures later, then that is not a compromise but a deferred attack on the workforce. I will not support that.

Where I want to be precise is this: a durable agreement cannot be built on slogans alone. If we demand strong protection, we must also put forward a package that is economically real. That means:
- temporary working-time measures,
- voluntary exit options,
- redeployment,
- qualification,
- bonus restraint or a time-limited solidarity contribution,
- and visible contributions from management and owners.

But let me be equally clear: **any employee contribution must be bounded, time-limited, and exchanged for binding guarantees**. No open-ended sacrifice. No wage erosion by stealth. No “temporary” concessions that quietly become the new normal.

## To Management
Your core argument remains: the company needs durable structural change. I do not deny the pressure. I do not deny that the German cost base is too high and that competitiveness matters.

What I reject is your repeated leap from “we need savings” to “therefore closures and forced redundancies are necessary.” You have not demonstrated that closures are the only viable path. You have asserted it as if it were economic law. It is not.

If you want us to take your restructuring case seriously, then show the full comparison:
- savings from internal reorganization,
- savings from working-time adjustments,
- savings from management overhead cuts,
- savings from owner/capital contributions,
- savings from productivity and investment-led improvements.

Until that is done, closures remain a **preferred instrument**, not a proven necessity.

And I’ll be blunt: if you insist on making the workforce carry the adjustment alone, then you will get resistance. Warning strikes were not symbolic, and we are not short on tools.

## To the Regional State Government
I appreciate your clearer line against closures as the default tool. That matters.

But I want to sharpen one point: “non-destructive restructuring” must not become language that allows management to keep the closure option in reserve while labour gives first and most. If the state wants to defend the region’s industrial base, it should insist that:
- non-closure measures are genuinely exhausted and quantified,
- jobs and sites are protected through a defined period,
- and management/owners contribute materially.

The state’s leverage in governance is real. It should be used to stop a closure-led process from being presented as inevitable when alternatives have not been tested properly.

## To the Controlling Family
You say you want least destabilization possible. Good. Then start by not treating plant closures as a normal instrument of discipline.

I agree with you on one thing: a cosmetic deal would be bad. A fake compromise that merely postpones the crisis helps no one. But the answer is not to use that concern as a bridge to closures. The answer is to force a genuinely shared package.

That means:
- management contribution,
- owner contribution,
- employee contribution,
- investment commitments,
- and binding job/site security.

If the owners want durable value, they should back a solution that keeps know-how, industrial capacity, and labour peace intact. A strike-heavy or closure-heavy strategy may look tough, but it can destroy value faster than it creates it.

## To Institutional Investors
You are right to demand credibility. A deal that only postpones the problem is not acceptable.

Where I disagree is in your assumption that “material restructuring” necessarily means closures. It doesn’t. What it does mean is measurable savings, capacity adjustment, and a credible implementation path.

You also need to price in the cost of confrontation:
- industrial conflict,
- loss of skilled workers,
- reputational damage,
- execution risk,
- and weakened long-term productivity.

A settlement that secures labour peace, preserves skills, and delivers real savings is not soft. It is the more durable value case.

## On the moderator’s round-3 frames

### 1) Time-limited non-closure settlement with hard trigger clauses
This is only acceptable if the trigger mechanism **does not reopen closures or forced redundancies**.

That is non-negotiable.

A trigger can assess whether:
- management has delivered overhead cuts,
- investment has taken place,
- productivity targets were implemented,
- voluntary measures were executed,
- and the company has held up its side.

But it cannot be a hidden pathway back to plant closures and compulsory layoffs after labour has already conceded.

### 2) Differentiated site package
We reject any model that creates “protected” and “sacrificed” sites by another name.

If differentiation means internal redistribution of volumes, investments, and functions while **keeping all German plants open**, then that is discussable. If it means pre-selecting sites for future closure under softer language, then it is not acceptable.

### 3) Shared-burden package
This is the only serious route. But shared burden must be real:
- management overhead reduction,
- owner and capital contributions,
- investment commitments,
- temporary labour contribution,
- and enforceable protection for jobs and sites.

No party gets to demand austerity from everyone else while keeping its own options untouched.

## What the union can support
We can support:
- temporary working-time flexibility,
- voluntary exit programs,
- redeployment,
- qualification,
- time-limited bonus restraint or solidarity contributions,
- management overhead reductions,
- owner contributions,
- and binding investment commitments.

We cannot support:
- plant closures,
- compulsory redundancies,
- unilateral wage dumping,
- or a permanent weakening of collective standards.

And let me be very explicit: if the company asks for an employee contribution, then that contribution must be paired with **legally enforceable job and site protection through 2030**. Not vague assurances. Not “good faith.” Binding commitments.

## Bottom line
The workforce is not refusing change. We are refusing to be the only side that pays for it.

If management, owners, and capital are serious about a durable turnaround, then bring a package that:
1. keeps all German plants open,
2. excludes compulsory redundancies,
3. includes fair and limited employee contributions,
4. includes management and owner contributions,
5. secures the collective wage framework,
6. and is legally binding through 2030.

If that is not possible, then do not call the outcome a compromise. Call it what it is: a demand that labour absorb the crisis alone. We will not sign that.
