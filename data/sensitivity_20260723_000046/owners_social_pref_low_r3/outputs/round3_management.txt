I’ll be direct.

The latest round confirms the central problem: the labour side is still demanding **absolute protection** while rejecting the **structural change** that the business needs. That is not a viable answer to a cost crisis.

## Where I agree
I do agree on one point with all of you: this cannot be a cosmetic exercise. A settlement that merely postpones the problem, preserves the current cost base, and leaves us uncompetitive would fail the company.

I also agree that industrial conflict is costly. A prolonged strike would damage output, reputation, and ultimately everyone’s position. So yes, a negotiated solution is preferable — but only if it actually fixes the economics.

## Where I disagree

### To the Works Council
You keep repeating:
- no plant closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- no change to guarantees,
- binding protection through 2030.

That may be your red line, but it is **not** a workable company plan on its own.

You say we have “not proven” closures are necessary. My answer is simple: we do not need to prove closure is ideologically desirable. We need to prove the company can reach a **structurally lower cost and capacity level**. If you can produce a package that does that without closures, forced redundancies, or permanent rigidity, then bring it forward. So far, I have not seen one that reaches the scale required.

And I reject the claim that any differentiation between sites is just a disguised closure strategy. Some activities are structurally weaker than others. Pretending every site is equally viable regardless of economics is not protecting the company; it is freezing decline.

### To the Industrial Union
You are right that employee contributions must be limited, fair, and exchanged for guarantees. On that point, we can work with a time-limited contribution model.

But you go too far when you insist that any review mechanism cannot reopen structural options at all. That would mean the company gives away flexibility permanently while labour gives temporary concessions. That is not balanced, and it will not restore competitiveness.

If the package misses targets, management must retain the ability to take further structural steps. Otherwise the agreement is not durable; it is just a delay.

### To the Regional State Government
I understand your political responsibility to the region. But the state cannot ask the company to carry an uncompetitive footprint indefinitely and still expect investment, innovation, and long-term jobs.

You are right that closures are not the first choice. But you are wrong if you treat them as something the company should rule out in advance. If non-closure alternatives do not produce sufficient savings, then the company must act. The state should not block necessary restructuring simply because it is difficult.

### To the Controlling Family
I appreciate your point that we should avoid unnecessary destabilization. I agree.

But I need to be very clear: “closures as last resort” cannot mean “closures never,” regardless of the numbers. If we do not materially improve the cost base, then we are not preserving value — we are deferring a more damaging crisis.

So yes, I support avoiding unnecessary confrontation. No, I do not support a settlement that preserves every legacy structure and leaves management without real options if targets are missed.

### To Institutional Investors
You are right to demand measurable, durable savings. That is exactly the point.

But you also need to recognize that savings on paper are not enough if the company remains structurally overcapacity and too expensive. We need a package that delivers:
- meaningful capacity reduction,
- lower labour cost,
- improved productivity,
- and enough margin improvement to fund future products and technology.

If that requires closures or equivalent irreversible measures, then that has to remain on the table.

## On the compromise ideas

### 1) Time-limited non-closure settlement with hard trigger clauses
This is only useful if the package is real and the trigger clauses are real.

I could accept a time-limited phase with:
- no compulsory redundancies for a defined period,
- temporary employee contributions,
- working-time adjustments,
- voluntary exits,
- redeployment,
- management cuts,
- and a joint review.

But I will not accept a “review” that effectively removes the company’s right to take structural action if the numbers do not improve. If the trigger is missed, the next phase must be open — including closures if necessary.

### 2) Differentiated site package
This is sensible, but only if it has real consequences.

I support a site-by-site approach because not every plant and activity has the same future. But “protect, transform, or repurpose” cannot be code for freezing inefficiency. If a site is not competitive over time, we must be allowed to address that.

### 3) Shared-burden package
Yes, the burden must be shared. I have said that repeatedly.

That means:
- employee contributions,
- management overhead cuts,
- owner and capital support,
- investment commitments,
- and productivity measures.

But shared burden does **not** mean shared veto. The company still needs the ability to restructure decisively if the package falls short.

## My position, clearly stated
Management is prepared to negotiate a serious package. But it must satisfy one condition:

**It must deliver durable structural improvement, not just labour peace.**

So I can work with:
- temporary working-time measures,
- voluntary programs,
- redeployment,
- qualification,
- bonus restraint or solidarity contributions,
- management and owner contributions,
- and a time-limited non-closure phase.

I cannot accept:
- a blanket no-closure commitment through 2030,
- a blanket no-forced-layoff guarantee with no fallback,
- or a deal that leaves the company overstaffed, overcosted, and unable to reach its return-on-sales target.

## Bottom line
Let me put it plainly:

If the employee side can bring a package that achieves **comparable structural savings** without closures, then we should evaluate it seriously.

If it cannot, then we will not disguise weakness as compromise. We will not sign a settlement that protects the current structure at the expense of the company’s future.

The company needs a durable reset. Not slogans. Not absolute guarantees. A real restructuring.
