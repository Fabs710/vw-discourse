We will be blunt: we are **not** interested in a deal that preserves the current cost base and calls it a turnaround. The only acceptable outcome is one that **credibly restores competitive margins, lowers structural costs, and reduces overcapacity**. Anything else will be treated by capital markets as delay, not restructuring.

## To management
We agree with you on the diagnosis: this business needs a **durable reset**. The current German cost base and capacity footprint are not aligned with market reality.

Where we disagree is on the way this is being presented. You keep saying closures remain available if needed, but you still have not shown the **quantified bridge**:
- how much savings come from internal reorganization,
- how much from working-time flexibility and voluntary exits,
- how much from management overhead cuts,
- how much from owner/capital contributions,
- and how much additional savings closures or equivalent irreversible actions would add.

Until that comparison is on the table, closures are a **threat**, not yet a proven necessity. That matters for us because we are willing to back hard restructuring, but not vague escalation.

If you want investor support, give us:
1. a clear savings target,
2. a site-by-site economic case,
3. a timeline to margin recovery,
4. and a credible execution plan with milestones.

Without that, you are asking us to underwrite uncertainty.

## To the works council and union
We understand why you want no closures and no compulsory redundancies. We also understand the desire for binding security through 2030.

But your current position is too rigid if it results in **structural overcapacity staying in place**. A package that preserves every site and every guarantee while leaving the company uncompetitive is not protective; it is value destructive. Shareholders cannot support that.

That said, we also do **not** accept management’s tendency to treat closures as automatically justified. Labor is right to demand proof that alternatives are insufficient.

So our position is:
- **yes** to bounded, time-limited employee contributions,
- **yes** to redeployment, qualification, and voluntary exits,
- **yes** to temporary flexibility,
- **no** to permanent protections that block needed restructuring,
- **no** to cosmetic “review” clauses that merely postpone the hard decisions.

If the “review” mechanism is real, it must be tied to objective financial outcomes. If targets are missed, action must follow. Otherwise the agreement is just deferral.

## To the regional state government
We agree that a cosmetic compromise would be worthless and that industrial conflict destroys value.

We also agree that the closure option should not be used casually. But the state cannot demand that the company protect every legacy footprint if doing so prevents competitiveness. The state’s role should be to ensure non-destructive alternatives are genuinely tested, not to veto hard restructuring forever.

If non-closure alternatives can deliver the required savings, we support that. If they cannot, then the state should not block the measures needed to restore the business.

## To the controlling family
We agree that a fake peace deal would be damaging and that the company needs stewardship, not a short-term spectacle.

Where we differ is on the implied comfort with keeping flexibility unresolved. Investors need to know whether the family is backing:
- a real cost reset, or
- a process that delays decisions while keeping the company expensive.

If the answer is the latter, that is not stewardship from a capital perspective.

We would support a package with:
- measurable savings,
- clear capacity reduction,
- management and owner contributions,
- and a credible return to margins.

But we will not support a “protect everything now, decide later” model if it leaves the equity story broken.

## On the compromise frames
### 1) Phased non-closure agreement
This is only acceptable if phase 1 actually produces **measurable, durable savings** and if the review has teeth.

A moratorium can work if it buys time for real change. It cannot work if it is simply a pause before the same problem returns larger.

### 2) Site-by-site transformation
This is the most credible route, but only if it is economically disciplined. Not every site or activity deserves equal treatment. Some differentiation is necessary if margins are to recover.

### 3) Shared-burden package
Yes, burden must be shared. But the burden must be **real**, not symbolic:
- employee contribution,
- management overhead cuts,
- owner support,
- investment commitments,
- and capacity rationalization.

If the burden lands mainly on labor while capital gets only optics, investors will not view the package as durable.

## Our line
We can support a negotiated settlement only if it delivers:
- a **credible path to competitive margins**,
- **durable cost reduction**,
- **meaningful capacity adjustment**,
- and **deal certainty**.

We cannot support:
- a cosmetic peace deal,
- an outcome that preserves uncompetitive structures,
- or a package that postpones the adjustment and calls that stability.

## Bottom line
Bring us a quantified plan with:
- specific savings,
- specific milestones,
- specific capacity reductions,
- and a credible fallback if targets are missed.

If that plan restores competitiveness without closures, fine — we will back it.

If it does not, then no amount of rhetoric about social peace will make the equity case work.
