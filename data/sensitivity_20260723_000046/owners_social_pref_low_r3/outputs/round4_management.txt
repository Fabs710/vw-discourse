I’ll respond directly and without euphemism.

## To the Works Council
You keep repeating the same three absolutes:
- no plant closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- binding protection through 2030.

I understand why you say that. I also understand why it is **not sufficient** as a business answer.

You say management has “not proven” closures are necessary. But the burden is not to prove closures are desirable in the abstract. The burden is to deliver a **durable reduction in cost and capacity** that puts this brand back on a competitive footing. If you can show a package that does that without closures, I will evaluate it seriously. But so far, I do **not** see a package anywhere near the scale required.

What I do see is a refusal to let the company touch any of the structural levers that actually matter:
- site consolidation,
- capacity reduction,
- labour-cost reduction,
- and flexibility to respond if targets are missed.

That is not restructuring. That is a demand to preserve the current footprint and current protections while asking the company to absorb the market shock elsewhere.

I also reject your argument that differentiation between sites is just a disguised closure plan. Some plants and activities are structurally weaker than others. Pretending all sites are equally viable is not protection; it is denial.

## To the Industrial Union
You are more realistic on process than the works council, and I acknowledge that. You accept temporary employee contributions, management cuts, owner contributions, and a bounded settlement. That is the right starting point.

But you still draw the wrong line when you say the review mechanism must not reopen structural options at all. That would mean:
- labour gives temporary concessions,
- management gives up flexibility permanently,
- and the company is left without a credible fallback if the package underdelivers.

That is not balanced. It is not durable. And it will not restore competitiveness.

A review must be real. If targets are missed, then the company must retain the ability to take further structural steps. I’m not saying that means immediate closures tomorrow. I’m saying the company cannot be locked into a cost base that remains uncompetitive.

## To the Regional State Government
I respect your role and I agree on one thing: a cosmetic compromise would be worse than none.

But I cannot accept your attempt to turn “non-destructive alternatives must be tested” into a de facto veto over hard restructuring. Testing alternatives is fine. Quantifying them is fine. But if they do not get us to the required savings and capacity alignment, then the state cannot block action simply because it is politically painful.

You are right that closures are not the first choice. You are wrong if you imply they should be off the table before the numbers are clear.

The region’s industrial base is not protected by preserving every legacy structure. It is protected by ensuring the company can invest in future products, technology, and competitiveness. Without that, the jobs disappear anyway — only later and under worse conditions.

## To the Controlling Family
You say you want stewardship and value preservation. I agree with that objective.

Where I disagree is your insistence that closures can be kept in reserve only in some abstract sense while the company is expected to fund an uncompetitive structure in the meantime. If the economics do not improve materially, then refusing all irreversible options simply defers the crisis.

We can absolutely avoid unnecessary destabilization. But “avoiding destabilization” cannot mean preserving overcapacity indefinitely. If the package does not materially lower the cost base, then the company is not being stewarded — it is being slowed down.

## To Institutional Investors
You are right to insist on measurable, durable margin restoration. That is exactly what management is trying to achieve.

But you also need to stop assuming that “no closures” is somehow the lower-risk option. If a non-closure package leaves the company structurally overcosted, then the market will punish that too. And if it triggers prolonged industrial conflict because it is too shallow, it will destroy value as well.

What we need is not a slogan about investor confidence. We need a package that changes the economics:
- lower labour costs,
- lower capacity,
- improved productivity,
- and a clearer path to positive returns.

If that requires site consolidation or other irreversible measures, then that must remain available.

## On the compromise frames

### 1) Phased non-closure agreement with conditional review
This can only work if the first phase delivers **real structural savings**, not symbolic ones.

I could support:
- a time-limited no-compulsory-redundancy phase,
- temporary employee contributions,
- working-time flexibility,
- voluntary exits,
- redeployment,
- qualification,
- management overhead cuts,
- and owner contributions.

But I will **not** accept a model where the review is neutered so that nothing meaningful can happen later. If the targets are missed, the company must have real restructuring options.

### 2) Site-by-site transformation with explicit economic criteria
This is the most sensible of the three proposals.

But let’s be honest: if a site is persistently uncompetitive, “transformation” cannot become a permanent shield against hard decisions. I am willing to discuss differentiated treatment, strategic protection for some sites, and investment-led repositioning. I am not willing to pretend that every site is equally viable forever.

### 3) Shared-burden package with enforceable symmetry
Yes — shared burden is necessary.

But shared burden means **real burden** from everyone:
- employee contribution,
- management overhead reduction,
- owner and capital contributions,
- investment commitments,
- productivity improvements,
- and capacity rationalization.

It does **not** mean labour gives, and management’s only concession is to postpone structural action until later.

## My position, stated plainly
I am prepared to negotiate a serious package. But the package must meet one standard:

**It must deliver a durable, structural cost and capacity reduction sufficient to restore competitiveness.**

That means:
- no cosmetic peace deal,
- no permanent protection of an uncompetitive structure,
- no deal that preserves all legacy capacities and calls that a turnaround.

I remain open on **how** the savings are achieved:
- closures,
- capacity reductions,
- voluntary exits,
- working-time measures,
- internal redeployment,
- site differentiation,
- or other structural instruments.

But I am not open on **whether** the savings must be real. They must.

## What I would accept
A credible agreement would need to include, at minimum:
- a genuine employee contribution that is time-limited,
- management and owner contributions,
- measurable capacity reduction,
- a lower labour-cost base,
- a credible investment plan for future products and technology to 2030,
- and a mechanism that preserves the company’s ability to act if targets are missed.

## What I will not accept
I will not accept:
- a blanket no-closure commitment through 2030,
- a blanket no-forced-layoff guarantee with no fallback,
- a deal that leaves German operations structurally uncompetitive,
- or a settlement that postpones the crisis and calls it compromise.

## Bottom line
Let me be very clear:

If the other side can bring a package that achieves the necessary structural savings without closures, I will consider it.

If it cannot, then closures or equivalent irreversible measures cannot be ruled out in advance.

The company needs a real reset, not slogans. We need to restore competitiveness now, not after another round of delay, conflict, and deterioration.
