I’ll be equally direct.

The employee side keeps repeating “no closures, no compulsory redundancies, no erosion of guarantees” as if that alone solves the crisis. It does not. It simply preserves the current cost base and overcapacity under a different label. That is not a restructuring. It is a postponement.

## Where I agree
I agree with the union, works council, and state government on one point: this company needs a credible future, not just a harsh accounting fix. We do need investment in new products, technology, and industrial capability through 2030. And yes, any durable settlement has to be negotiated and implementable.

I also agree that a cosmetic deal would be unacceptable. A paper exercise that leaves German manufacturing structurally uncompetitive would fail everyone — employees, owners, and the company.

## Where I disagree
But I do **not** accept the claim that plant closures are merely an optional ideological choice while everything else can be made to work if everyone is “fair.”

The hard fact is this: **our German cost base is materially above target and structurally too high for the shrinking European market we are facing.** If we do not reduce capacity and labour cost in a durable way, there will be no sustainable return on sales and no serious funding for future investment.

### To the Works Council
You say closures are not a bargaining chip and that you demand binding guarantees through 2030. I understand the position. I reject it.

Why? Because a blanket no-closure, no-forced-redundancy stance **before** any credible savings package exists would lock in the very overcapacity we need to remove. That is not co-determination in the service of survival. That is a veto over adaptation.

You also say management has not proven closures are necessary. I disagree. The burden is not to prove closure is the only possible instrument in the abstract. The burden is to deliver a package with **comparable structural effect**. If you can do that, then present it. So far, you have not.

### To the Union
You are right that management, owners, and capital must contribute too. I have said that already. But you then jump to the conclusion that employee contributions must be paired with blanket job and site guarantees through 2030.

That is exactly the problem. If the guarantees are absolute, then they remove the flexibility needed to make the restructuring real. I will not sign up to a deal that gives away all adaptation capacity in exchange for temporary concessions.

You say temporary working-time reductions, bonus restraint, redeployment, and qualification are acceptable. Good — but by themselves they are not enough. They do not permanently bring German factory economics into line. They can be part of a package, not the package.

### To the Regional State Government
You are right to care about the region. We do too. But the state cannot simply demand industrial preservation without accepting that some parts of the footprint may be structurally non-viable.

If the company remains uncompetitive, the region loses more over time: investment, future products, and ultimately far more jobs than any targeted restructuring would involve. Regional responsibility means supporting a viable future, not preserving every site regardless of economics.

### To the Controlling Family
You are correct that we should avoid unnecessary destabilization. I appreciate that. But “closures as last resort” cannot become “closures never, no matter the numbers.”

If the company’s economics do not improve materially, then the owners will face a worse outcome later: deeper cuts, lower value, and a weaker strategic position. I am not asking for confrontation for its own sake. I am insisting on structural correction before the problem becomes irreversible.

### To Institutional Investors
You are right on the core issue: the final package must materially fix the cost base, not merely postpone adjustment. That is exactly management’s position too.

So let me be crystal clear:
- If a proposal delivers **measurable, durable savings**, management will support it.
- If it does not, we will not pretend it is a solution.

## On the compromise ideas
The moderator’s “closure moratorium” model is only useful if it leads to real structural change, not if it simply delays the decision while costs keep bleeding.

A moratorium without a credible path to permanent cost reduction is not a compromise I can support.

Likewise, “no compulsory redundancies” can only be discussable if the package still produces the necessary headcount and cost reduction through other means at meaningful scale. If the answer is merely voluntary exits and hope, then it is insufficient.

## My position
Management is prepared to negotiate, but we will not negotiate against reality.

What we need is:
1. **Durable capacity reduction**
2. **A real labour-cost contribution**
3. **A credible, enforceable savings path**
4. **Investment capacity for future products**
5. **A settlement that restores competitiveness, not one that protects every legacy structure**

That means I remain open to:
- voluntary programs,
- redeployment,
- working-time changes,
- temporary concessions,
- productivity measures,
- and site differentiation.

But I am **not** open to:
- a blanket no-closure pledge,
- a blanket no-redundancy pledge,
- or a deal that leaves the company structurally overstaffed and overcosted.

## Bottom line
If the employee side wants a non-closure solution, then bring one that genuinely matches the required scale of cost and capacity reduction. Not slogans. Not conditions that freeze the status quo. A real alternative.

If that cannot be done, then management’s conclusion stands: **the company will have to take deeper structural steps, including closures if necessary, to secure its future.**

I’ll say it plainly: we cannot protect every site, every guarantee, and every historic cost level at the same time. Something has to give — and it cannot be the company’s competitiveness.
