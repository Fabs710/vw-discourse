I will be equally direct.

The state shareholder will **not** support a restructuring that uses “competitiveness” as a cover for **plant closures, compulsory redundancies, or weakening co-determination**. That remains our line. And yes, we have the governance leverage to defend it.

At the same time, I do **not** defend the status quo. The company needs a credible, durable improvement in productivity, capacity alignment, and cost base. If we refuse all adjustment, we risk the very jobs and value creation we are trying to protect.

## To management
I agree that the company needs a real reset, not a cosmetic deal. Where I disagree is your attempt to present closures as if they are already economically proven.

You still have **not** demonstrated that non-destructive alternatives are insufficient.

Before anyone talks about irreversible steps, I want the full quantified comparison:
- internal capacity shifts,
- working-time flexibility,
- voluntary exits,
- redeployment,
- qualification,
- management overhead cuts,
- owner and capital contributions,
- productivity gains from investment.

If that case has not been made, then closure is not a necessity. It is a choice. And I will not endorse making it the default choice.

## To institutional investors
You are right to demand credible savings and durability. But your standard cannot simply be “show us closures or show us nothing.”

You also have to price in:
- strike risk,
- execution risk,
- loss of skilled labour,
- brand damage,
- and the long-term erosion of the German industrial base.

A package that looks clean in a spreadsheet but detonates industrial peace is not prudent capital allocation.

## To the controlling family
I agree with you that a fake compromise would be damaging. But “closure as last resort” must be real, not a negotiating tactic used to extract concessions first and ask questions later.

If you want stewardship, then support a process that genuinely tests non-destructive options first and treats plant closures as a last resort in fact, not just in language.

## To the works council and union
I respect your red lines on jobs, sites, and forced redundancies. They are legitimate. But I also need to be blunt: the state cannot sign off on a framework that freezes all adjustment forever.

What I can support is:
- no plant closures as the starting point,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- a defined period of binding job and site protection,
- and restructuring built on internal transformation rather than destruction.

If the workforce is asked to contribute, then those contributions must be **time-limited, bounded, and exchanged for enforceable guarantees**. That is the only fair basis.

## On the compromise frames

### 1) Phased non-closure agreement with conditional review
This is the most serious starting point, but only if the review is **not** a hidden closure trigger.

I can support a binding phase with:
- no compulsory redundancies,
- temporary working-time flexibility,
- voluntary exit programs,
- redeployment,
- qualification,
- management savings,
- owner contributions,
- and measurable targets.

What I will **not** support is a mechanism that quietly reopens closures and forced layoffs the moment labour has already conceded. A review can assess delivery; it cannot become a trapdoor.

### 2) Site-by-site transformation with explicit economic criteria
This can work only if it means **protect, transform, or consolidate inside the existing German footprint** — not “pick weak sites later and shut them.”

I am open to differentiated treatment if it helps preserve jobs and production in Germany. I am not open to a scheme that labels sites for “repurpose” as a prelude to closure by another name.

### 3) Shared-burden package with enforceable symmetry
This is essential.

A real settlement must include contributions from:
- labour,
- management,
- owners,
- and capital.

That means management overhead reductions, owner support, investment commitments, and measurable productivity measures alongside limited employee concessions. If any side demands sacrifice from others while keeping its own options untouched, the package will not hold.

## My position, clearly
The state can support a settlement if it:
1. preserves the German industrial base,
2. excludes forced redundancies,
3. avoids plant closures as the first instrument,
4. binds site and job protection for a defined period,
5. includes fair, time-limited employee contributions,
6. includes management and owner contributions,
7. and respects co-determination and the state’s board role.

If those conditions are met, I will work constructively to stabilize it.

If the package depends on closures and mass job destruction, then I will oppose it in governance.

## Bottom line
We are prepared to support a **serious restructuring that protects jobs, sites, and co-determination while restoring competitiveness**.

We will **not** support a closure-led solution presented as inevitability before alternatives are fully tested and quantified.

So the next step is not rhetorical escalation. It is a serious, quantified comparison of alternatives. If management wants the state’s support, bring a package that keeps the German base intact, shares the burden fairly, and gives the company a future — not just a cleaner short-term spreadsheet.
