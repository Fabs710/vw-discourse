I’ll respond directly.

## To the Works Council
I share the core line you’ve set out: **no compulsory redundancies, no plant closures, and no dismantling of collective protections**. That remains the union’s position too.

I also agree that a “moratorium” only matters if it is used to build a real alternative, not to stage a delayed closure. If the idea is simply to buy time and then reopen the same knife at a later date, we will not support it.

Where I want to sharpen the position is this: a durable settlement cannot rest only on defensive slogans. If we want to stop closures and forced layoffs, then we need a package that is economically credible and explicitly shared. That means:
- capacity adjustments without plant shutdowns,
- temporary working-time measures,
- voluntary exits,
- redeployment and qualification,
- and a real contribution from management and owners.

But I am not prepared to sign employees up to open-ended sacrifice. Any employee contribution must be **time-limited, bounded, and exchanged for binding guarantees**. If that condition is not met, it is not a settlement — it is a transfer of risk onto the workforce.

## To Management
Your central argument is that the cost base must come down materially. I do not dismiss that out of hand. Yes, the company needs competitiveness. Yes, structural overcapacity has to be addressed. And yes, the future product and investment side matters.

Where I strongly disagree is your repeated move from “we need savings” to “therefore closures and forced redundancies are on the table.” That is not a conclusion; it is a strategy choice.

You have not shown that closures are the only way to achieve the savings required. You keep asserting inevitability, but the burden is on management to demonstrate that all non-destructive alternatives have been exhausted. Until then, closures remain a **preferred instrument**, not a necessity.

And I will be blunt: if you try to make the workforce carry the adjustment alone — through wage cuts, loss of guarantees, and job losses — then you will get resistance. Industrial peace has ended. Warning strikes were not symbolic, and further escalation remains available.

## To the Regional State Government
Your insistence on regional responsibility is correct. The company is not just a balance sheet; it is an industrial anchor. You are also right that the region loses if the company is weakened structurally.

But that means you cannot simply stand for “jobs in general” while allowing a restructuring that shifts the pain entirely onto labour. If the state wants a non-closure solution, it should use its influence to back:
- binding site and job guarantees,
- industrial investment,
- and a clear rejection of closures as the first instrument.

At the same time, the state should support a negotiated contribution package that protects employment while improving competitiveness. That is the balance we need.

## To the Controlling Family
You say closures should be a last resort. I agree with that principle. But “last resort” must mean a genuine last resort, not a threat used to pressure the workforce into preemptive concessions.

If ownership wants long-term value, then it should support a deal that avoids destructive plant closures and preserves the company’s industrial base. That requires real contributions from management and capital, not just demands toward labour.

I do, however, take seriously your point that a cosmetic compromise would only postpone a harsher crisis. I agree with that. A deal has to be economically real. But “economically real” does not automatically mean closures. It means verifiable savings, fair burden-sharing, and a credible future.

## To Institutional Investors
You are right on one thing: we cannot accept a settlement that merely postpones the problem. The company needs measurable improvement. But you are wrong if you treat closures and mass layoffs as the only credible way to get there.

A credible deal is one that:
- secures labour peace,
- preserves skills and know-how,
- protects the industrial core,
- and delivers durable savings.

If you force a confrontation that triggers prolonged strikes, brand damage, and the loss of skilled people, you may get short-term accounting improvement and long-term value destruction. That is not prudence; that is a very expensive form of discipline.

## The union’s position
Let me state our position clearly:

We are prepared to discuss:
- temporary working-time reductions,
- voluntary options,
- bonus restraint or a solidarity contribution,
- redeployment,
- qualification,
- and negotiated flexibility in capacity.

We are **not** prepared to accept:
- plant closures,
- compulsory redundancies,
- unilateral wage dumping,
- or a permanent erosion of collective protections.

If the company wants an employee contribution, it must be paired with:
1. **binding job security**
2. **binding site security**
3. **management and owner contributions**
4. **a credible investment commitment**
5. **a time limit and clear end date for any concessions**

## Bottom line
So my answer is straightforward:

- **Yes** to a negotiated restructuring that preserves sites and jobs.
- **Yes** to employee contributions, but only if they are limited, fair, and exchanged for guarantees.
- **No** to closures as a default strategy.
- **No** to compulsory redundancies.
- **No** to a deal where labour bears the entire burden.

If management and ownership are serious about a viable future, then bring a package that proves it can achieve structural savings **without destroying the workforce and the German industrial base**. If not, then you should expect the union to escalate its response, not soften it.
