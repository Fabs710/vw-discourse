I will respond plainly, because the stakes are too high for formulaic language.

## My position
The state shareholder **does not support closures as the default restructuring tool**, and we **do not accept forced redundancies as the main adjustment mechanism**. That remains our line.

At the same time, I am **not** defending the status quo for its own sake. The company does need a real competitiveness improvement, and if that can be achieved through a negotiated restructuring that protects the German industrial base, we will engage constructively.

What we cannot accept is a “solution” that improves short-term metrics by **gutting regional employment, hollowing out German sites, and weakening co-determination**. That would damage the company’s legitimacy, its industrial capability, and the region’s economy.

---

## To management
I agree with you on one point: the company needs a durable improvement in cost and productivity. That is real, and it cannot be denied.

Where I disagree is your repeated framing that **closures are the inevitable answer** unless labour accepts broad concessions up front. That is too narrow. It treats the German footprint as a liability to be cut rather than a strategic asset to be restructured intelligently.

You have not convinced me that all non-closure options have been exhausted. In particular, I want to see a serious comparison of:
- working-time and flexibility measures,
- voluntary departures,
- redeployment,
- capacity shifts within the German footprint,
- management and overhead reductions,
- and ownership contributions.

If those alternatives are not shown to be insufficient, then closure is not a justified first step. It is a political and industrial choice with very high costs.

And I will say this clearly: **if you insist on closure-heavy restructuring without a convincing alternatives process, you will face resistance in the supervisory board**. Our statute gives us real leverage, and we will use it to protect regional interests.

---

## To the Works Council
I agree with your core principle that **German plants and jobs are not disposable**. I also agree that no workforce should be forced to pay for this crisis alone.

Where I am more cautious is on the absolutism. A hard “no change at all” would not be sustainable if the economics truly require adjustment. But I do **not** read your position that way; I read it as a defense of the company’s productive base, which is legitimate.

What I support is:
- no plant closures as a starting point,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- binding protections for jobs and sites for a defined period,
- and a restructuring built on internal adjustment and future investment.

What I do **not** support is a fake compromise where labour gives up protections and management keeps the option to close sites later. If there are employee contributions, they must be exchanged for **real guarantees**, not hopes.

---

## To the Trade Union
Your insistence on shared burden is right. The state does not expect the workforce to carry this alone.

Where I agree:
- temporary working-time measures can be part of the solution;
- voluntary schemes can help;
- qualification and redeployment are essential;
- management and owners must contribute too.

Where I disagree is with any suggestion that concessions from labour are acceptable only if every job and site is guaranteed in an absolute sense forever. That is not how a restructuring deal works. But I do expect **binding and enforceable** safeguards for a meaningful period, and I expect them to be serious enough to give workers real security.

A contribution package without a future plan would be unacceptable. Likewise, a future plan without fair burden-sharing would be unacceptable.

---

## To the Controlling Family
I agree with your concern about avoidable destabilization. A long confrontation would be costly and would damage the company further.

But I also need to be frank: when you say closures should be “last resort,” that has to be more than a rhetorical bridge to closure later. It has to mean that **non-closure solutions are genuinely explored and taken seriously**.

I would support a compromise that:
- delivers measurable savings,
- preserves the company’s long-term industrial base,
- avoids unnecessary destabilization,
- and keeps closures only as a truly exceptional fallback.

I would **not** support an approach that uses the threat of closures to force the state, labour, and the region into accepting a weakened settlement.

---

## To Institutional Investors
You are right that a cosmetic deal would be bad. A package that merely postpones the crisis would damage everyone, including capital.

Where I disagree is that closure is somehow the only credible route to discipline. That is not convincing. I also take seriously your point about strike risk and execution risk: prolonged conflict would be destructive.

That is precisely why I am pressing for a **structured, negotiated, non-destructive path**. A settlement that protects the company’s productive capacity, keeps know-how in place, and avoids a social and political rupture is not soft. It is the more durable solution.

But again: if the final package materially damages jobs, sites, and regional value creation, then it is not a good package just because it looks cleaner financially in the short term.

---

## On the moderator’s compromise ideas
The proposals were useful as starting points, but I will be explicit about where I stand:

### 1) Phased restructuring with a moratorium
This is only acceptable if the moratorium is used to build a **real non-closure solution**, not as a prelude to later closures. I would not support a mechanism that merely defers the same conflict.

### 2) No compulsory redundancies in exchange for capacity cuts and employee contributions
This is closer to a workable path, provided the savings are real and the company also contributes materially. I could support such a direction if it includes:
- binding site and job protections,
- a credible investment plan,
- and measurable management/owner contributions.

### 3) Site-by-site differentiation
This is the most realistic avenue, but it must not become a euphemism for selective plant closures. If there are genuinely weak or overlapping functions, they should be addressed through internal restructuring first, not immediately through closure.

---

## What would move the state shareholder
I remain open to a settlement if it includes:
1. **Preservation of the company’s German industrial base**
2. **No forced redundancies**
3. **No plant closures as the default instrument**
4. **Real but limited employee contributions**
5. **Management and owner contributions**
6. **A binding investment and product plan**
7. **Respect for co-determination and the state’s board role**

If management brings a package that meets those conditions, we will work on it. If the package depends on closures and mass job destruction, then we will oppose it.

---

## Bottom line
The state’s position is not “protect everything at any cost.” It is:

- **protect the company as a regional anchor,**
- **protect jobs and sites where possible,**
- **make the restructuring economically real,**
- **and do not use closures as the first answer when alternative measures have not been seriously exhausted.**

If the company wants support from the state shareholder, it needs to come back with a restructuring that keeps the German base intact and gives the workforce a fair, secure role in the company’s future.

If it cannot do that, then it should not expect our approval.
