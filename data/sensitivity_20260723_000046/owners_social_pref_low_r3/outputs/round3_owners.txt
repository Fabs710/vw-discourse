I will engage directly.

The family holding’s position is unchanged in substance: **we need a durable competitiveness reset, not a cosmetic peace deal**. The company must restore margins, align capacity with demand, and protect the long-term value of the core asset. At the same time, I do not want value destruction through prolonged conflict, reputational damage, or a rushed restructuring that weakens the business for years.

## To the Works Council
I understand why you are defending jobs, sites, and co-determination. Those are legitimate concerns. And I agree with you on one important point: **a fake compromise that only delays the crisis would be worse than no compromise**.

Where I disagree is with your absolute framing:
- no closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- binding guarantees through 2030,
- and no meaningful fallback.

That is too rigid to be automatically compatible with a real turnaround. If the economics require structural change, the owners cannot pre-commit to a structure that may preserve overcapacity indefinitely. That would risk managed decline.

I also do **not** accept the claim that closures are merely a management preference. They may not be the first instrument, but they cannot be ruled out in advance if they are the only way to achieve a durable cost base improvement. What matters to ownership is not ideological purity; it is whether the final package actually restores competitiveness.

## To the Industrial Union
I agree with you more than the works council on process, but not on the end state.

You are right that employee contributions must be:
- time-limited,
- bounded,
- and exchanged for real security.

You are also right that management and owners must contribute materially. That is fair and, frankly, necessary for legitimacy.

Where I disagree is your insistence that any review mechanism must not reopen structural options at all. If labour gets protections and temporary concessions, management must still retain the ability to act if the targets are missed. Otherwise, the package is not a restructuring; it is a deferral.

So yes to temporary working-time measures, voluntary exits, redeployment, qualification, and limited wage/bonus moderation. But no to a structure that removes flexibility permanently while only granting temporary relief on the labour side.

## To Group & Brand Management
I agree with the diagnosis: the company has a structural competitiveness problem, and it cannot preserve its current cost base and capacity footprint and still expect acceptable returns.

I also agree that a cosmetic compromise is not enough.

Where I push back is on any presentation of closures as the only credible proof of seriousness. Management must quantify alternatives properly. I would expect a clear comparison of:
- internal reorganization savings,
- working-time and flexibility measures,
- management overhead reductions,
- owner/capital contributions,
- productivity gains from investment,
- and then, only if necessary, the savings from closure or equivalent irreversible capacity removal.

If those alternatives are genuinely insufficient, then say so with numbers, not slogans. But if they are insufficient, then I will not rule out deeper structural steps. The owners cannot accept a settlement that leaves the enterprise strategically weaker.

## To the Regional State Government
I share your concern that the company is more than a spreadsheet and more than a political symbol. The regional and national industrial consequences matter.

I also agree that a one-track closure strategy would be destabilizing and should not be the default. Closures should be a last resort, not a slogan.

Where I disagree is with any suggestion that a “non-closure” outcome can be assumed without proving it restores competitiveness. The state is right to demand alternatives be tested and quantified, but it should not turn that into a veto over difficult adjustment if the numbers point there.

The state’s leverage in governance is real, but so is the responsibility not to entrench a cost structure that undermines the company’s future.

## To Institutional Investors
I agree with your central point: the final package must be economically credible and durable, not just politically saleable.

You are also right that a prolonged strike or a compromised implementation path would destroy value.

Where I differ from you is in the standard of proof. I do not accept closure as a starting assumption. I accept **structural improvement as the requirement**. If that can be achieved without closures, excellent. If not, then the company must retain the ability to take irreversible steps.

I also need you to price in the risk of industrial conflict and execution failure. A harsh package that triggers major disruption can be just as value-destructive as an overly soft one. The target is not “maximum pain”; it is **maximum durable value**.

## On the moderator’s compromise frames
### 1) Time-limited non-closure settlement with hard trigger clauses
This is only viable if the trigger mechanism is genuinely objective and does not become a concealed escape route for endless delay.

I could support a time-limited phase with:
- no compulsory redundancies,
- temporary employee contributions,
- working-time flexibility,
- voluntary exits,
- redeployment,
- management cuts,
- and measurable savings targets.

But if targets are missed, the company must have real options. Otherwise the agreement just locks in underperformance.

### 2) Differentiated site package
This is the most sensible direction, but only if it is driven by economics, not sentiment.

I can support protecting strategic sites, transforming selected sites, and reviewing duplicate or weak activities. What I cannot support is a blanket political promise that every site is insulated regardless of economics. That would be a recipe for slow erosion.

### 3) Shared-burden package
Yes. This is essential.

But the burden must be shared in reality, not just in language:
- labour contribution,
- management contribution,
- owner contribution,
- capital contribution,
- and a credible product/investment plan.

If one side is asked to carry everything while the others keep all options, the package will not hold.

## My line as controlling shareholder
I can support a settlement if it:
1. delivers **durable cost and capacity reduction**,
2. restores **margins and competitiveness**,
3. avoids **unnecessary destabilization**,
4. includes **meaningful contributions from all stakeholders**,
5. and preserves the **long-term value of the enterprise**.

I cannot support:
- a permanent no-closure commitment regardless of economics,
- a structure that leaves overcapacity untouched,
- or a deal that sacrifices governance flexibility and then calls that stewardship.

## Bottom line
My message is simple:

- If a non-closure solution can truly deliver the required structural savings, I will support it.
- If it cannot, then closures or equivalent irreversible measures cannot be ruled out in advance.
- In either case, the company must avoid prolonged conflict and value destruction.

So I am not here to bless a cosmetic peace. I am here to insist on a real turnaround that protects long-term value. If the other parties want my support, bring a package that actually does that.
