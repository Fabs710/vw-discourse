I’ll respond directly.

## To the Works Council
We are aligned on the core red lines:
- **no compulsory redundancies**
- **no plant closures**
- **no erosion of the collective wage framework**

I also agree with your point that vague “review” language can be abused later. If management uses a review mechanism as a backdoor to reopen closures after workers have already given concessions, then that is not a compromise — it is a trap.

Where I want to be precise is this: a settlement must still be economically real. That means the workforce can contribute, but only in a **bounded, time-limited, and reciprocal** way. If we give, then management, owners, and capital must give too.

## To Management
You still have not made the case that closures are necessary.

You keep saying “structural change,” “durable savings,” and “capacity reduction,” but those are not self-justifying words. We need a quantified comparison:
- internal reorganization savings,
- working-time and flexibility measures,
- voluntary exits,
- redeployment,
- qualification,
- management overhead cuts,
- owner and capital contributions,
- and only then any claimed extra effect from closures.

Until you can show that non-destructive measures are insufficient, closures are a **preferred instrument**, not a proven necessity.

And let me be blunt: if you insist that labour must absorb the crisis while management keeps the right to close plants later, you will face resistance. Warning strikes were not symbolic, and we are prepared to escalate strategically if needed.

## To the Regional State Government
Your line is closer to ours, but I need you to be more explicit.

It is not enough to say alternatives should be “tested” or “quantified.” Management has a long record of using process to delay and then returning with the same closure agenda. The state should use its governance leverage to ensure that non-destructive alternatives are **exhausted and shown to be insufficient** before any talk of closures.

If the state really wants to defend the regional industrial base, it should not leave the closure option hanging over the workforce as a default threat.

## To the Controlling Family
If you want to avoid destabilization, then start by ruling out the most destabilizing instrument: plant closures and forced layoffs.

I agree that a fake compromise would be bad. But the answer is not to keep the closure option in reserve while asking the workforce to trust the process. The answer is to back a genuine shared-sacrifice package:
- management contribution,
- owner contribution,
- employee contribution,
- investment commitments,
- and binding job and site security.

A stewardship-minded owner should not treat closure as routine discipline.

## To Institutional Investors
You are right to ask for credibility and durability. I accept that. But credibility does not mean “prove closure first.” It means prove that the package restores competitiveness **without destroying the workforce and the industrial base**.

You also need to price in the cost of confrontation:
- strike risk,
- execution failure,
- skill loss,
- reputational damage,
- and weaker long-term productivity.

A package that secures labour peace, keeps skills intact, and still delivers measurable savings is not soft. It is the more durable value case.

## On the compromise frames

### 1) Phased non-closure agreement with conditional review
This is only acceptable if the review cannot become a hidden closure trigger.

I can support a time-limited phase with:
- no compulsory redundancies,
- working-time flexibility,
- voluntary exit programs,
- redeployment,
- qualification,
- temporary bonus restraint or solidarity contribution,
- management overhead cuts,
- owner contributions,
- and investment commitments.

But the review must assess whether the parties delivered their agreed contributions. It must **not** be a doorway back to closures and layoffs after labour has already paid.

### 2) Site-by-site transformation with explicit economic criteria
This can only work if “transformation” means **internal adjustment inside the existing German footprint**, not pre-selection of weaker plants for eventual elimination.

If it means shifting volumes, functions, and investment while keeping all German plants open, then we can discuss it. If it means repackaging closure as “repurpose,” we will reject it.

### 3) Shared-burden package with enforceable symmetry
This is the only serious route.

But the symmetry must be real:
- management cuts,
- owner contributions,
- capital support,
- investment commitments,
- and time-limited employee concessions.

Not labour first and alone.

---

## What the union can support
We can support:
- temporary working-time flexibility
- voluntary exits
- redeployment
- qualification/retraining
- temporary bonus restraint or solidarity contributions
- management overhead reductions
- owner contributions
- investment commitments
- a legally enforceable no-compulsory-redundancy agreement

We cannot support:
- plant closures
- forced redundancies
- wage dumping
- permanent weakening of the collective agreement
- or “temporary” concessions that become the new normal

And to be explicit: any employee contribution must be exchanged for **binding security**, not hopeful promises.

## Bottom line
The workforce is not refusing change. We are refusing to be the only side that pays for it.

If management, owners, and investors are serious, then bring a package that:
1. keeps German plants open,
2. excludes compulsory redundancies,
3. includes fair and limited employee contributions,
4. includes management and owner contributions,
5. protects the collective wage framework,
6. and is binding for a defined period.

If that is not possible, then don’t call it compromise. Call it what it is: a demand that workers absorb the crisis alone.

We will not sign that.
