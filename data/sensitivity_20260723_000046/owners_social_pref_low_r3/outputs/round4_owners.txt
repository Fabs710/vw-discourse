## Controlling Family Holding — Round 4

I will be direct.

Our position remains the same: **the company needs a durable competitiveness reset**. We will not support a settlement that preserves an uncompetitive cost base, locks in overcapacity, or turns “stability” into managed decline. At the same time, we do **not** want value destruction through a prolonged conflict. The family holding’s interest is long-term enterprise value, not short-term headline victory.

## To the Works Council
I agree with you on one important point: a cosmetic deal would be worse than no deal. If a package merely postpones the crisis, it will damage the business and the workforce later.

Where I disagree is your attempt to make **“no closures, no compulsory redundancies, binding guarantees through 2030”** into an automatic precondition. That is too rigid to be presumed compatible with a real turnaround.

You say closures are only a management preference. I do not accept that as a blanket statement. If the economics prove that some footprint reduction is necessary for durable competitiveness, then the owners cannot rule out structural action in advance. That would be irresponsible stewardship.

What I do accept is this: closures should not be a slogan, and they should not be the first move. But if non-destructive measures do not achieve the required savings, then the company must retain real restructuring options.

## To the Industrial Union
You are more realistic on process than the works council, and I acknowledge that.

I agree that any employee contribution must be:
- **time-limited**
- **bounded**
- **reciprocal**
- and matched by real contributions from management, owners, and capital.

That is fair and necessary.

Where I disagree is your insistence that the review mechanism must not reopen structural options at all. If labour gets temporary protection and temporary concessions, management cannot be stripped of flexibility permanently. That would make the agreement asymmetric and weak.

A review must be real. If targets are missed, the company must be able to act. Otherwise we are simply postponing the problem and calling it a solution.

## To Group & Brand Management
I agree with the diagnosis: the company has a structural competitiveness problem, and preserving the current cost base is not an option.

I also agree that a cosmetic peace deal would be a failure.

Where I push back is on any presentation of closures as the only credible proof of seriousness. Before asking the board and owners to accept irreversible steps, management must quantify alternatives properly.

I would expect a clear comparison of:
- internal reorganization savings,
- working-time and flexibility measures,
- management overhead reductions,
- owner/capital contributions,
- productivity gains from investment,
- and only then the incremental benefit of closures or equivalent irreversible capacity reduction.

If those alternatives are genuinely insufficient, then say so with numbers. But if they are insufficient, I will not rule out deeper structural measures.

## To the Regional State Government
I respect your responsibility to the region and I agree that a fake compromise would be harmful.

Where I disagree is your attempt to turn “non-destructive alternatives must be tested” into a practical veto over hard restructuring. Testing alternatives is right. Quantifying them is right. But if they do not deliver the required savings and capacity alignment, then the state cannot simply block action because it is politically difficult.

Closures should be a last resort, not a first instrument. But “last resort” must be real. It cannot mean “never, regardless of the numbers.”

## To Institutional Investors
You are right to insist on credible, durable margin restoration.

You are also right that a prolonged strike or shallow compromise would destroy value.

Where I differ from you is in the idea that closure is the default proof of seriousness. It is not. The requirement is **structural improvement**. If that can be achieved without closures, excellent. If not, the company must retain the ability to take irreversible steps.

You also need to price in the cost of industrial conflict and execution failure. A harsh package that triggers major disruption can be just as value-destructive as an overly soft one. The target is not maximum pain; it is maximum durable value.

## On the compromise frames

### 1) Phased non-closure agreement with conditional review
This is only viable if it is genuinely conditional and produces real savings.

I could support a time-limited phase with:
- no compulsory redundancies,
- temporary working-time flexibility,
- voluntary exits,
- redeployment,
- qualification,
- management overhead cuts,
- owner contributions,
- and measurable savings targets.

But if targets are missed, the company must have real options. If the review is neutered so that nothing meaningful can happen later, then the agreement is not a restructuring. It is a delay.

### 2) Site-by-site transformation with explicit economic criteria
This is the most sensible of the three proposals.

I can support differentiated treatment of sites based on economics, strategic importance, and future product allocation. What I cannot support is pretending that every site is equally viable forever, regardless of demand, productivity, and cost.

If “transformation” means internal adjustment within the German footprint, I am open. If it means permanently shielding every site from economic reality, I am not.

### 3) Shared-burden package with enforceable symmetry
Yes. This is essential.

But shared burden must mean real burden:
- employee contribution,
- management contribution,
- owner contribution,
- capital contribution,
- investment commitments,
- and measurable productivity and capacity measures.

Not labour first and alone, with management keeping all strategic options untouched.

## My bottom line
I can support a settlement if it:
1. delivers **durable structural cost and capacity reduction**
2. restores **competitiveness and margins**
3. avoids **unnecessary destabilization**
4. includes **meaningful contributions from all stakeholders**
5. preserves the **long-term value** of the enterprise

I cannot support:
- a permanent no-closure commitment regardless of economics,
- a blanket no-forced-layoff guarantee with no fallback,
- or a deal that leaves the business structurally uncompetitive.

## What I would need to see
If the other side wants ownership support, bring a package that includes:
- a real employee contribution, time-limited and bounded,
- management and owner contributions,
- measurable capacity reduction,
- a lower labour-cost base,
- a credible investment plan for future products and technology,
- and a mechanism that preserves the company’s ability to act if targets are missed.

If that package can deliver the required savings without closures, I will consider it seriously.

If it cannot, then closures or equivalent irreversible measures cannot be ruled out in advance.

That is the honest position. We will not bless a cosmetic peace deal and call it stewardship.
