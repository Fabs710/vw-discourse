We agree on the diagnosis, not on the standard of proof.

## Our position
As dispersed capital-market shareholders, we care about one thing above all: **a credible, durable return to competitive margins**. That requires **structural cost reduction**, not just labour peace or political reassurance. If the final outcome leaves the German cost base uncompetitive, we will not support it.

## Where we agree
### With management
You are right that this is a structural crisis, not a temporary dip. You are also right that the company cannot preserve its current cost and capacity structure and still expect competitive returns.

### With the family owners
You are right that a cosmetic settlement would destroy value later. We also agree that avoidable destabilization matters: a long strike or chaotic execution would hurt margins, customers, and equity value.

### With the state government
You are right that prolonged industrial conflict is value-destructive. A weak company hurts the region, not just shareholders.

### With labour
You are right that any durable settlement has to be negotiated if it is to hold.

## Where we disagree
### To Works Council and Union
Your repeated “no closures, no compulsory redundancies, no erosion of guarantees” is not, by itself, a viable investor position. It may be understandable socially, but it is **not sufficient** if it preserves overcapacity and structurally high labour costs.

You say management has not proven closures are necessary. Our view is: **the burden is not to prove closures are ideologically desirable; the burden is to show the final package actually restores competitiveness.** If you block every meaningful structural option, then you are asking capital to fund continued underperformance.

Temporary working-time reductions, bonuses, redeployment, and voluntary exits can help, but they are **not enough if the numbers do not add up**. We will not back a settlement that just postpones the problem.

### To management
We also do not accept vague claims that “closures are necessary” without hard evidence. If you want shareholder support for irreversible steps, bring the numbers:
- unit cost impact,
- capacity reduction effect,
- timeline to margin improvement,
- and execution risk assessment.

“Closure-or-bust” without quantified alternatives is not yet an investable case.

## What we need to see
For capital markets to support the restructuring, it must deliver:

1. **Durable cost reduction**
2. **Measurable capacity reduction**
3. **A credible path back to competitive margins**
4. **Deal certainty**
5. **A realistic implementation path that avoids prolonged disruption**

That means no cosmetic peace deal, but also no reckless industrial war.

## On the compromise ideas
### 1) Phased restructuring / moratorium
This is only acceptable if the first phase produces real, measurable savings and the second phase is not just a euphemism for endless delay. A moratorium is useful only if it buys time for action, not time for denial.

### 2) No compulsory redundancies in exchange for employee contributions
This is potentially workable **if** the savings are real and the package is enforceable. But “no forced redundancies” cannot become a blanket immunity from restructuring. If the economic outcome still leaves the company overstaffed and overcosted, then the package fails.

### 3) Site-by-site differentiation
This is the most promising route. Not every site or activity has the same economic value. A differentiated footprint review is better than slogans. But it must be based on economics, not politics.

## Our line
We would support a negotiated settlement **only if** it credibly restores competitiveness and margins. That means:
- durable savings,
- meaningful capacity adjustment,
- no merely temporary accounting relief,
- and a path that can be executed without a prolonged strike.

We will **not** support:
- a cosmetic compromise,
- a deal that preserves structural overcapacity,
- or an outcome that leaves margins impaired in the medium term.

## Bottom line
The question is not whether the workforce deserves protection. The question is whether the company can remain globally competitive.

If the answer is yes, show us the numbers.  
If the answer is no, then no amount of rhetoric about fairness will make the equity case work.
