I’ll speak plainly.

The latest round confirms what we already knew: management and capital are still trying to turn “restructuring” into a code word for plant closures and job destruction, while asking the workforce to trust promises that have already been broken once. That is not a basis for agreement.

## To management
You keep saying the company needs “durable structural change.” We do not dispute that the company faces pressure. What we dispute is your claim that **closures and forced redundancies are the necessary solution**.

You have not proven that. You have asserted it.

And I will repeat:  
- **No German plant closures.**  
- **No compulsory redundancies.**  
- **No unilateral dismantling of job-security protections.**

That is the minimum. Not the maximum. The minimum.

You talk about “comparable structural effect.” Fine. Then show a package that actually delivers it without closing plants and without throwing people out. Until then, closures remain a management preference, not an economic law.

## To the institutional investors
You say you need credible savings and capacity reduction. That is understandable in theory. But your position is still too shallow if it treats the workforce as the primary variable and ignores the damage from confrontation, skill loss, and the destruction of trust.

A settlement that forces strikes, breaks guarantees, and hollows out the German base is not “investable”; it is destructive. If you want durable margins, then durable labour peace and durable competence are part of the equation.

And let’s be honest: “site differentiation” often becomes a polished way of selecting weaker plants for closure. We know that game. We will not accept it.

## To the controlling family
You say you want least destabilization possible. Then start by ruling out the most destabilizing instrument: plant closures.

If the owners truly want long-term value, they should back:
- binding no-closure commitments,
- no forced layoffs,
- internal capacity adjustment,
- and real management and owner contributions.

A family that values industrial stewardship should not be pushing the company into a confrontation with its own workforce.

## To the regional state government
Your line is closer to ours, but you are still leaving too much room for “review” language that later gets used against the workforce. The state should be using its leverage to **block closure-led restructuring unless and until every non-destructive alternative has been exhausted and quantified**.

Not “considered.”  
Not “discussed.”  
Exhausted and quantified.

If the state wants to defend the regional industrial base, then it has to say clearly: closures are not the first instrument here.

## To the union
I agree with your emphasis on shared burden, but I want to sharpen the boundary:

- temporary working-time measures: discussable
- voluntary exits: discussable
- qualification and redeployment: necessary
- bonus restraint or temporary solidarity contribution: discussable
- permanent erosion of guarantees: unacceptable
- “trigger clauses” that quietly reopen closure after concessions: unacceptable

I do **not** want a deal where the workforce gives and management gets the right to come back later and cut again. That is not a settlement. That is staged surrender.

## On the moderator’s round-3 frames

### 1) Time-limited non-closure settlement with hard trigger clauses
This is only acceptable if the trigger clauses do **not** reopen plant closures and forced redundancies.

That is the red line.

A “review” can be about whether management and owners have delivered their part, whether investment has happened, whether productivity measures were implemented, whether overhead cuts were made. It cannot be a hidden escape hatch back to closures.

If the company wants time, fine. But time must be used for **non-destructive restructuring**, not for building a later closure case.

### 2) Differentiated site package
We reject any categorization that makes some plants “repurpose” candidates in practice while the language pretends nobody is closing anything.

Once a site is labelled for “repurposing” without binding protection, the outcome is usually foregone. We are not naive. Every site in Germany must remain open. Internal adjustment can happen inside that framework, but not by selecting plants to be sacrificed.

### 3) Shared-burden package
Yes, burden sharing is necessary. But it must mean real contributions from:
- management,
- owners,
- capital,
- and the company itself.

Not just labour. Not just the workforce being asked to swallow losses while others keep the strategic option to close sites later.

## What I can accept
I am willing to discuss a package with:
- temporary working-time flexibility,
- voluntary exit programs,
- internal redeployment,
- qualification,
- temporary wage/bonus moderation with a strict end date,
- management overhead reductions,
- owner contributions,
- investment commitments,
- and enforceable job security.

But only if the outcome is:
1. **all German plants stay open**
2. **no compulsory redundancies**
3. **binding protection through 2030**
4. **co-determination remains real, not symbolic**

## What I will not accept
I will not accept:
- closures now or later,
- “last resort” language that functions as a threat,
- forced redundancies,
- or a so-called compromise that asks labour to pay first and promises protection later.

That path has no credibility.

## Bottom line
The company needs restructuring. Fine.  
But the restructuring must be built on:
- internal adjustment,
- investment,
- fair burden-sharing,
- and binding security for the workforce.

If management and owners insist that the only serious answer is to shut plants and force people out, then there is no agreement from us. We will resist that in the supervisory board, in collective bargaining, and in the plants.

So I ask management one final time, directly:

**Will you commit to a non-closure, no-forced-layoff restructuring with binding guarantees through 2030 — yes or no?**

If the answer is no, then let nobody pretend there is a compromise on the table.
