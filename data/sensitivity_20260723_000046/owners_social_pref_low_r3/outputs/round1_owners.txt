I appreciate the seriousness of the positions set out, and I will be equally direct.

From the perspective of the controlling owners, the starting point is not ideological. It is this: **the company must restore competitiveness, margins, and investment capacity, or it will destroy value over time**. That would ultimately harm shareholders, employees, and the regions alike. So I agree with management on the diagnosis that structural problems exist and cannot be papered over.

At the same time, I do **not** support a restructuring strategy that creates avoidable instability, especially if it triggers a prolonged confrontation that weakens the company further. A solution that is “economically correct” on paper but politically or industrially unimplementable is not a good solution.

### Where I agree
- **The company needs structural improvement.**
- **Cost levels and capacity must be addressed.**
- **A credible future product and investment plan is essential.**
- **A negotiated settlement is preferable to open-ended conflict.**
- **Management, labour, and owners should all contribute in some form.**

### Where I disagree
I do **not** accept the idea that plant closures are the only credible route to competitiveness. They may be part of the answer in extreme cases, but they are not something I would support as the default first move if there are viable alternatives that achieve comparable savings with less long-term damage.

I also do **not** support a purely symbolic compromise that preserves all structures while leaving the cost base fundamentally untouched. That would simply defer the crisis and make a harsher outcome more likely later. The workforce should not assume that ownership will back a deal that preserves every legacy guarantee without meaningful economic improvement.

Likewise, I would be cautious about any approach that makes job guarantees so rigid that management loses the ability to adapt. The company needs flexibility. But flexibility is not the same as recklessness, and it must not become a pretext for a value-destructive confrontation.

### My position on the current lines
- **To management:** you are right to insist that the economics must work. But if you present this as closure-or-bust, you may force a confrontation that is costly, slow, and potentially avoidable. I would expect serious exploration of alternatives before irreversible steps are taken.
- **To the works council and union:** your defense of jobs, sites, and co-determination is understandable and legitimate. But “no closures, no compulsory redundancies, no change” is not a workable long-term answer if the company’s cost base remains uncompetitive. If you want a durable settlement, you need to be prepared for real structural adjustments.
- **To the state government:** your insistence on regional responsibility matters, but the company cannot be reduced to a regional employment scheme. If the business model is weakened, no amount of political protection will create sustainable value.

### What would move me
What could gain support from the controlling owners is a package that:
1. **Delivers measurable and durable cost improvement**
2. **Protects the company’s long-term industrial base**
3. **Avoids unnecessary destabilization**
4. **Includes a credible investment and product roadmap**
5. **Shares the burden fairly across management, labour, and ownership**
6. **Preserves governance control and strategic flexibility**

That means I am open to:
- negotiated capacity reductions,
- working-time adjustments,
- voluntary programs,
- redeployment and qualification,
- temporary employee contributions,
- and a clear investment commitment.

But I would **not** support:
- a settlement that leaves structural overcapacity in place,
- a deal that simply buys peace until the next crisis,
- or an outcome that materially weakens the company’s governance control or long-term strategic freedom.

### Bottom line
My view is that the parties should urgently work toward a **durable restructuring with real savings, but with closures only as a last resort and only if no credible alternative exists**. If labour presents a package that genuinely restores competitiveness, the owners are open to backing it. If not, I will not support an arrangement that sacrifices the company’s long-term value for short-term calm.

So my message is simple: **show us a serious, economically credible alternative to closure-heavy restructuring, and we will engage. If you cannot, then management’s concern about the cost base cannot be ignored.**
