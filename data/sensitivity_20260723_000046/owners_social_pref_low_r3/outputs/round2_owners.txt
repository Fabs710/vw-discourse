I will be direct.

The family holding remains committed to a **durable competitiveness reset**. That means: the German cost base must come down, capacity must be aligned with demand, and the company must regain margin headroom for investment. If we fail to do that, we do not protect the workforce or the regions — we simply postpone a harder crisis and destroy value.

At the same time, I do **not** want a value-destructive confrontation. A long strike, reputational damage, or a rushed closure package that weakens the business for years would also be unacceptable. So my support is for **real restructuring with the least destabilization possible**, not for a symbolic peace deal and not for uncontrolled escalation.

## To the Works Council
I hear your red lines, but I do not accept them as absolute starting points.

- **No plant closures**
- **No compulsory redundancies**
- **Binding guarantees through 2030**

I understand why you are stating that, but I do **not** believe the owners should pre-commit to a structure that may leave overcapacity untouched and make the company uncompetitive. A blanket no-closure stance could force the company into managed decline.

Where I agree with you:
- employee trust matters,
- skills and know-how matter,
- co-determination matters,
- and a solution must be negotiated, not simply imposed.

Where I disagree:
- a restructuring cannot be ruled out before the economics are tested,
- and guarantees cannot be so rigid that management loses all ability to adapt.

If you want ownership support for a non-closure path, it has to show **comparable structural savings**, not just “less pain.”

## To the Industrial Union
Your insistence on shared sacrifice is reasonable. I also agree that management and owners must contribute materially; that is part of stewardship, not a favor.

But I do **not** accept an employee-only contribution model that comes with absolute job and site security if that package fails to deliver real cost improvement. Temporary working-time measures, bonus restraint, and voluntary tools can help — but they are only acceptable if they are part of a package that genuinely fixes the economics.

So my position is:
- yes to negotiated employee contributions,
- yes to management and owner contributions,
- no to open-ended wage erosion,
- no to a deal that preserves every legacy protection while leaving the cost base structurally uncompetitive.

## To Group & Brand Management
I support the diagnosis that the company has a structural competitiveness problem. I also support the idea that savings must be durable, not cosmetic.

Where I need to push back is on the presentation of closures as the default proof of seriousness. Closures may be necessary in some cases, but they are not automatically the best first instrument. I would expect management to exhaust and quantify alternatives before insisting on irreversible steps.

So:
- if you can show a credible non-closure package with real savings, the owners will engage;
- if you cannot, then you should be prepared to justify why more drastic structural steps are unavoidable.

What I will not support is a package that creates prolonged industrial war and still leaves the company strategically weaker.

## To the Regional State Government
I understand your political and regional concerns, and I do not dismiss them. But I must be clear: the company cannot be treated as a protected employment scheme if the underlying business model remains weak.

I agree with you on one important point: **closures should not be the default answer**. They are a last resort, not a slogan. But “last resort” must not become “never.” If the economics do not work, then the company has to act.

So I would support:
- a serious comparison of alternatives,
- capacity reduction through non-destructive measures first,
- investment commitments,
- and a solution that preserves the long-term industrial base.

What I would not support is a settlement that protects every site in name while hollowing out the company’s competitiveness.

## To Institutional Investors
You are right about one thing: a cosmetic deal would be value-destructive. I agree. We need a package that actually improves margins and restores credibility.

But I also need to say this plainly: a strike-heavy or closure-heavy process that burns customer confidence and weakens execution can also destroy value. So the owners’ standard is not “hardest possible restructuring.” It is **best durable restructuring**.

That means:
- measurable cost improvement,
- a credible path to return on sales,
- no mere postponement,
- and no unnecessary industrial destabilization.

## On the compromise ideas
The moderator’s frameworks are useful as tools, but only if they do not become a backdoor to paralysis.

### 1) Phased restructuring with a moratorium
I could support a time-limited moratorium **only if** it is tied to hard, measurable targets and a real restructuring program. It cannot be a permanent shield against structural change. If targets are missed, the company must retain options.

### 2) No compulsory redundancies in exchange for capacity cuts and employee contributions
This is more promising than the absolute no-change positions, but only if the savings are real and substantial. Voluntary exits, redeployment, working-time flexibility, and bonus restraint can contribute. They cannot be the whole answer if overcapacity is material.

### 3) Site-by-site differentiation
This is the most sensible route. Not every site is equally strategic, and not every activity deserves the same protection. I would support a differentiated review of the footprint — but not a political fiction that every location is equally viable regardless of economics.

## My line as controlling shareholder
I can support a settlement that:
1. delivers **real and durable competitiveness gains**,
2. avoids **unnecessary instability**,
3. includes **shared contribution** from labour, management, owners, and capital,
4. and preserves the company’s **long-term industrial base**.

I cannot support:
- a deal that leaves structural overcapacity in place,
- a permanent no-closure promise regardless of economics,
- or a settlement that weakens governance control or strategic flexibility.

## Bottom line
If the employee side wants the family holding’s support for a non-closure solution, then bring one that genuinely restores margins and capacity balance. If it does not do that, I will not back a compromise that simply buys time.

But I am also saying to management: do not confuse seriousness with maximal confrontation. A rushed, destructive closure strategy could weaken the company more than it helps. Show that alternatives have been genuinely tested, quantified, and found insufficient before pushing irreversible steps.

That is the position I will defend in governance.
