I’ll be direct.

I understand your red lines, but I cannot accept a restructuring that leaves the company’s German cost base fundamentally unchanged. We are in a structural crisis, not a temporary dip. European demand is shrinking, competition is intensifying, and our factory costs are materially above target. If we do not fix that now, there will be no sustainable jobs to protect later.

So let me state management’s position clearly:

- **We need deep, durable capacity reductions.**
- **We need a real labour-cost contribution.**
- **We need a restructuring that materially improves margins and frees up investment capacity.**
- **We cannot sign up to a cosmetic package that merely postpones the problem.**

On your points:

### Where I agree
I agree that the company needs a credible industrial future. I agree that a restructuring must include investment in future products and technology. I also agree that there has to be a negotiated solution, not just imposed conflict. And yes, management, owners and capital must contribute too.

### Where I disagree
But I do **not** accept the claim that site guarantees through 2030 and no compulsory redundancies can be offered as a blanket precondition **before** we know whether the package actually fixes the cost base. That would simply freeze the current overcapacity in place.

I also do **not** accept that plant closures are off the table as a matter of principle. If certain sites are structurally uncompetitive, keeping them open for political comfort is not a strategy. It is a delay of the inevitable at the expense of the whole group.

And I need to be frank: “temporary” measures, working-time tweaks, redeployment and voluntary options may contribute, but they are **not enough** on their own if the underlying capacity and cost structure remains oversized.

### What management is prepared to discuss
We are open to a negotiated package, but it must deliver real numbers, not slogans. That means:

1. **Substantial capacity reduction**
2. **A meaningful and durable labour-cost contribution**
3. **Structural productivity improvements**
4. **A serious investment commitment for future products**
5. **A settlement that restores medium-term competitiveness, not just industrial peace**

If the employee side wants to avoid closures and forced job cuts, then it must come with a package that produces comparable structural savings. I am not asking for sacrifice for its own sake. I am saying the economics have to work.

### On industrial action
Warning strikes may be a reality, but they do not change the underlying numbers. Prolonged conflict will damage output, customers, and confidence — including investor and owner confidence. That is not in anyone’s interest. But let me be equally clear: pressure tactics will not make an uncompetitive structure acceptable to management.

### The core issue
The fundamental question is simple: **Do we want a viable company in 2030, or do we want to preserve every existing site and every existing protection until the economics force a much harsher outcome later?**

Management’s duty is to prevent the second path.

So I will put the challenge back to you:
If you reject closures and forced redundancies outright, then present a package that achieves the same structural effect on cost and capacity. If you can do that credibly, we can talk. If not, then we are not discussing a real restructuring — we are discussing managed decline.
