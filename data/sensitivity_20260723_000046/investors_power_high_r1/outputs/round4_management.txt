## To the works council
I agree with you on one point: a cosmetic compromise would be worthless. We do need hard numbers. But I strongly disagree with your attempt to turn the absence of a completed savings bridge into a veto on all structural measures.

You say closures are unproven. Fine — then let the numbers prove or disprove them. But until then, you do **not** get to declare closures illegitimate in advance. That would amount to protecting the current footprint by default, and that is exactly what cannot stand.

Your “minimum acceptable outcome” is not a restructuring solution. It is a political boundary line. I respect the co-determination framework, but I also have a fiduciary duty to restore competitiveness. If the plant network remains too large and too expensive, then all sites open is not a viable end state.

## To the union
I agree that workers should not carry the entire burden alone. That is why I have consistently said the package must include a meaningful labour-cost contribution, not just operational tweaks.

Where I disagree is your insistence that a temporary contribution plus voluntary measures is enough by default. It may be part of the answer; it is not proven to be the whole answer. Temporary concessions do not automatically eliminate permanent overcapacity. If the savings bridge is large, then we need permanent capacity reduction somewhere in the system.

You are right to insist that “temporary” must not become permanent by stealth. But I am equally right to insist that “temporary” must not become a way to avoid the structural reset the company needs.

## To the regional state government
I hear your call for transparency and your insistence that closures be a last resort. That is reasonable in principle. I do **not** accept it as a practical shield against any capacity reduction.

If the company remains uncompetitive, then preserving every site in the name of regional stability just postpones the problem and eventually weakens the region’s industrial base anyway. Stability without viability is not stability; it is managed decline.

I can support a process that starts with quantified disclosure and evaluates non-closure measures first. What I cannot support is a political commitment that effectively rules out hard decisions before the numbers are known.

## To the controlling family holding
I agree that a paper compromise would be destructive. I also agree that a durable solution matters more than short-term peace.

But a durable solution is not the same as a comfortable one. If the business case shows that capacity consolidation is needed, then the owners need to back reality, not sentiment. Protecting long-term value means accepting structural change where necessary, including plant consolidation or closure if that is what the numbers justify.

What I will not accept is pressure to preserve legacy structures at the expense of competitiveness. That is not stewardship.

## To the investors
You are correct that a cosmetic deal would destroy value. We are aligned on that.

But your support for “permanent capacity reduction” is only useful if it recognizes the governance reality: we need a deal that can actually be implemented. If the package cannot survive co-determination, it has no value. That is why I have asked for quantified verification, and that is why I support the advisor’s intervention.

Your position should not be read as permission to demand closures irrespective of feasibility. It should be read as a demand for durable savings. I support that. I do **not** support a deal that leaves the cost base structurally uncompetitive.

## On the advisor’s conclusion
The advisor has said the key point plainly: **closures are not yet financially proven as necessary** because the quantified bridge is still missing.

I accept that. But I draw a different conclusion than labour does.

The correct inference is **not**: therefore closures are off the table.
The correct inference is: therefore management must now present the quantified bridge, including plant-level economics and the residual gap after non-closure measures.

And I will say this clearly: if that bridge shows a large residual gap, then permanent capacity reduction remains on the table, and I will not pre-empt that outcome to satisfy political comfort.

## My position, again and without ambiguity

### Minimum acceptable outcome
- A **durable** reduction in cost and capacity
- A **meaningful** labour-cost contribution
- A credible path back to the brand’s return-on-sales target
- Freed funds for future products and technology to 2030
- A negotiated settlement that avoids prolonged conflict **if** it still delivers structural savings

### Red line
- Any deal that leaves the cost base fundamentally uncompetitive
- Any cosmetic compromise that delays the adjustment
- Any package that rules out permanent capacity reduction before the quantified savings gap is known
- Any outcome that prevents us from restoring competitiveness and investment capacity

### Concrete fallback proposal
I am prepared to work from a package built around:
- voluntary departures
- internal redeployment
- working-time flexibility
- productivity improvements
- a temporary employee contribution
- and investment commitments

But this package must be tested against the actual savings requirement. If it does not close the gap, then capacity consolidation, including closures, must stay available. I am not retreating from that.

## What I reject outright
I reject the idea that saying “no closures” enough times makes it a responsible strategy.

I reject the idea that temporary measures alone can be assumed to solve structural overcapacity.

I reject the idea that management should lock in an uncompetitive footprint just to avoid confrontation.

And I reject any attempt to make our need for a serious restructuring look like a moral failure.

## Final point
The burden of proof cuts both ways now.

- Labour must show that its alternative package is quantitatively sufficient.
- Management must show the exact savings bridge.
- The state, family owners, and investors should stop demanding certainty without numbers.

Once the bridge is on the table, we can judge whether non-closure measures are enough. If they are, I will take that seriously. If they are not, then structural capacity reduction is necessary — and I will not apologize for saying so.
