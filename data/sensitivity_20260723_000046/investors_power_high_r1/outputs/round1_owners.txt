I’ll be direct.

I agree with management and the capital markets on one point: **doing nothing, or doing only symbolic adjustments, is not acceptable**. The company cannot carry a structurally uncompetitive cost base and expect long-term value to hold. That is clear.

But I also agree with the labour side and the state on another point: **a destructive confrontation that ends in prolonged strikes, political paralysis, or reputational damage would also destroy value**. As controlling owners, we cannot support value erosion on either side of the equation.

So my position is this:

### What I support
- A **durable restructuring** that restores competitiveness and margins.
- **Meaningful capacity reduction** and lower operating costs.
- **A negotiated employee contribution** if it is temporary, measurable, and fair.
- **Investment in future technologies and products in Germany**, but only if it is matched by real economic improvement.
- A solution that preserves **labour peace** and avoids open-ended conflict.

### What I do not support
- **Cosmetic compromise** that leaves the core cost problem unresolved.
- A settlement that protects every structure and every legacy guarantee while postponing adjustment.
- **Prolonged industrial action** that burns value.
- Any outcome that **permanently weakens the enterprise’s competitiveness** or undermines governance stability.

### To the Works Council and union
I hear your refusal of plant closures and compulsory redundancies. I understand the political and social logic of that stance. But from an ownership perspective, I cannot endorse a line that says “no closures, no layoffs” unless you can show a **fully credible equivalent** in hard numbers.

If you want us to support avoiding closures, then you need to prove that the same structural effect comes from:
- real capacity reduction,
- sustained productivity gains,
- working-time flexibility,
- voluntary departures,
- internal redeployment,
- and a labour-cost contribution that is not merely temporary in name only.

A promise of “alternatives” is not enough. The savings must be **durable and quantified**.

### To management
I support the diagnosis, but not an approach that becomes a blunt confrontation without a realistic path to agreement. If you push a maximalist package that cannot survive co-determination, you will get deadlock, not restructuring.

If you want backing from the controlling shareholder, the plan must be:
- credible,
- enforceable,
- economically measurable,
- and likely to be implemented without a multi-year fight.

### To the state shareholder
I share the concern about regional and industrial stability. But stability cannot mean preserving an uncompetitive structure forever. The company must remain investable and profitable. The state should not help entrench a status quo that slowly destroys value.

### To capital markets
Your demand for hard numbers is correct. But you should also recognize that value is not protected by forcing the company into a conflict that undermines execution. We want savings, yes — but through a settlement that can actually hold.

### Bottom line
I am prepared to support a restructuring **if and only if** it delivers:
1. real and durable cost reduction,
2. credible capacity adjustment,
3. a fair but meaningful employee contribution,
4. protection against prolonged instability,
5. and a clear investment path for the future.

What I will **not** support is a paper compromise that preserves too much of the current structure and leaves the company weaker in three years than it is today.

If management, labour, and the state can bring forward a package that meets those conditions, we can back it through governance. If not, I will not pretend that an inadequate compromise is in the owners’ long-term interest.
