## To management
I agree with the diagnosis that this company needs a durable restructuring. I also agree that a cosmetic peace deal would be value-destructive.

Where I do **not** yet agree with you is your insistence that closures and compulsory redundancies must remain on the table as if they were already justified. The independent advisor has made the key point: **the quantified bridge is still missing**. Without:
- current and target return-on-sales,
- savings required in euros,
- savings from each non-closure measure,
- residual gap after those measures,
- and plant-level economics,

your case for closures is still an assertion, not a proof.

That matters because we are not deciding in a vacuum. A plant closure or forced redundancy in this governance structure is not a technical detail; it is a high-stakes move with long-term value, labor-peace, and control implications. I am not prepared to back a confrontation-first strategy.

What I will support:
- voluntary departures,
- internal redeployment,
- working-time flexibility,
- productivity improvements,
- temporary, time-limited employee contributions,
- and investment commitments tied to real savings.

What I will **not** support:
- a package that uses closures as the default lever before the alternatives are tested in numbers,
- or a solution that creates prolonged conflict and reputational damage.

If your model shows a residual gap that truly cannot be closed otherwise, then present it. Until then, do not ask the controlling owner to bless irreversible structural cuts on faith.

## To the works council and union
I understand and respect your refusal of closures and compulsory redundancies as a first principle. I also understand your insistence that workers should not be made to bear the whole burden.

But I need to be equally clear: a blanket “no closures, no forced layoffs, all sites open, job security through 2030” stance is not enough by itself. If the numbers show a substantial structural gap, then you must be prepared to help close it in a way that is economically real, not just politically comforting.

Your strongest point is this: the advisors have not proven that closures are unavoidable. That is correct. It means management has to quantify necessity before demanding irreversible measures. I support that sequencing.

Your weaker point is the implication that all hard structural adjustment can be avoided if only alternatives are named. Naming alternatives is not the same as proving they close the gap.

So yes:
- voluntary measures,
- attrition,
- redeployment,
- working-time tools,
- temporary contributions,
- and joint productivity measures

are the right starting package.

But they must be tested against the actual savings requirement. If they do not close the gap, then you cannot simply declare the gap irrelevant.

## To the regional state government
I agree with you that closures should not be treated as a first resort. I also agree that stability and regional responsibility matter.

But I would caution against drifting into a position where “last resort” quietly becomes “never.” If the business remains structurally uncompetitive, then preserving every footprint regardless of economics would eventually hurt the region more than a disciplined restructuring would.

Your role should be to insist on:
- quantified proof before closures are discussed,
- serious burden-sharing,
- and protection of the regional anchor role where it remains economically viable.

That is sensible. What is not sensible is defending the current structure as though the market had not changed.

## To the investors
You are right to reject a cosmetic settlement. You are also right that durable savings matter more than rhetoric.

But I do not accept the idea that a “real restructuring” automatically means closures. That has not been proven. The advisor explicitly said non-closure measures can work if the gap is moderate and the package is serious. That is the current factual position.

So I support your demand for numbers, but not your implied presumption that closure is the inevitable answer. You should want the same discipline I want: first quantify the gap, then test the instruments.

## My position
I want a settlement that restores competitiveness, protects long-term value, and avoids value-destructive instability.

### Minimum acceptable outcome
- A quantified restructuring plan.
- Durable cost reduction sufficient to restore competitiveness.
- A meaningful employee contribution that is fair and measurable.
- No prolonged industrial conflict.
- Protection of the company’s industrial base in Germany where economically possible.

### Red line
- Any outcome that leaves the company with an uncompetitive cost base.
- Any paper compromise.
- Any prolonged strike or deadlock that permanently damages enterprise value.
- Any measure that weakens ownership governance control.

### Concrete fallback proposal
I am open to a package built around:
- voluntary departures,
- internal redeployment,
- working-time flexibility,
- productivity gains,
- temporary employee contributions,
- and investment commitments.

But I want that package measured against the actual savings target. If the residual gap remains large, then management must justify why stronger measures are truly unavoidable. At that point, the burden of proof will be on them, not on the workforce alone.
