## To management
You still have not proved that plant closures or compulsory redundancies are necessary. Until you do, they are not a “hard reality” — they are a threat.

I agree with the advisors and with the state, works council, and even the investors on one narrow point: **we need quantified numbers**. Fine. Bring them. But do not pretend that the absence of numbers gives you a license to impose irreversible cuts now.

What I reject is your attempt to turn an unproven management preference into a mandatory restructuring path. You keep saying “durable” and “structural,” but you have not shown:
- the current return-on-sales gap,
- the annual recurring savings target,
- the contribution of each non-closure measure,
- the residual gap after those measures,
- or site-by-site why closures are unavoidable.

Until that bridge exists, your closure threat is not justified.

And I want to be very clear: if management uses closures and forced layoffs as leverage before proving necessity, then you should expect resistance — in bargaining, in the plants, and in the supervisory board.

## To the works council
I agree with your core line:
- no plant closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- no dismantling of protections,
- no burden shift onto workers alone.

That remains my position too.

Where I want to sharpen the message is this: we should not let management redefine a temporary restructuring contribution into a permanent wage reset. If there is any employee contribution, then it must be:
- strictly time-limited,
- clearly quantified,
- tied to binding job and investment guarantees,
- and explicitly not a precedent for weakening the collective agreement.

Temporary must mean temporary. Not “temporary until management decides otherwise.”

## To the regional state government
Your insistence on transparency is correct, but transparency without leverage is not enough.

If management comes back with a destructive package, then the state shareholder should use its influence to prevent a fait accompli. That means:
- no closure announcements before the bridge is verified,
- no forced redundancies,
- no unilateral erosion of job security,
- and no pretending that regional stability can be preserved by hollowing out German sites.

I support a serious restructuring package. I do **not** support using workers as the shock absorber for past strategic mistakes.

## To the controlling family holding
You say you want a durable restructuring. Good. Then back a durable solution, not the fastest cut.

A settlement that:
- tears up wage standards,
- threatens closures,
- weakens co-determination,
- and forces labour to absorb the adjustment alone

is not durable. It is socially brittle and industrially unstable.

If the owners want legitimacy, then they need a package based on:
- fair burden-sharing,
- protected sites,
- real investment,
- and workforce acceptance.

That is what lasts. Fear-driven restructuring does not.

## To the investors
You are right that a cosmetic compromise would destroy value. We do not want that either.

But you are wrong if you assume that “real restructuring” automatically means closures. The advisor has not proven that. The advisor has said the bridge is missing, and the residual gap is unknown. That means the correct sequence is:
1. quantify the gap,
2. test the non-closure measures,
3. assess the residual,
4. and only then decide whether additional instruments are needed.

From our side, however, I will be just as clear: **closures and forced redundancies are not acceptable instruments**. If the residual gap is large, then management must find another way, because the workforce is not going to pay for the entire adjustment through jobs and rights.

## To the moderator’s “numbers-first” approach
I support numbers-first. I do **not** support the hidden assumption that the process should end in some mix of closures and concessions.

Numbers matter because they expose whether management is exaggerating the case for closures.

So our sequencing is:
- yes to a quantified savings bridge,
- yes to plant-level economics,
- yes to independent verification,
- yes to voluntary and internal measures,
- no to closure decisions before proof,
- no to forced redundancies,
- no to making closure an automatic fallback.

- **All German plants remain open**
- **No compulsory redundancies**
- **Protection of the collective wage framework**

- **Binding investment and future-product commitments for Germany**

- Any plant closure in Germany
- Any compulsory redundancy
- Any attempt to dismantle the collective wage framework through restructuring
- Any package that makes workers bear the adjustment alone

### Concrete fallback proposal

- voluntary departure programs,
- retirement and attrition,
- internal redeployment and reskilling,
- working-time flexibility,
- productivity measures jointly designed with employee representatives,
- a temporary and limited employee contribution,
- and binding investment commitments for German sites.

That is the only path to a settlement I can support.

I reject the idea that closures are the default answer.

I reject the idea that forced redundancies are normal here.

I reject the idea that collective bargaining can be weakened simply because management wants a cleaner cost line.

And I reject any attempt to create a fait accompli and then call it negotiation.
