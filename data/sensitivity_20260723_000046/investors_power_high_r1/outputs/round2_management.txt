Let me respond point by point, because we are now past generalities.

### To the Works Council
You continue to say: no closures, no compulsory redundancies, full job security through 2030, and all plants preserved. I understand the intent, but I cannot accept that as the basis for a serious restructuring.

Why? Because it does not solve the underlying problem. If we lock in every site and every job guarantee while the cost base stays structurally too high, then we are not restructuring the company — we are postponing the crisis and weakening the brand further.

You also say management must prove that alternatives are insufficient. Fair enough. But the burden cannot be inverted so that management must first prove every possible alternative while you pre-emptively rule out the measures that actually create structural savings. That is not a negotiation; that is a veto.

What I can accept:
- voluntary departures,
- internal redeployment,
- working-time solutions,
- productivity measures,
- temporary employee contributions,
- and investment commitments tied to real savings.

What I cannot accept:
- a blanket exclusion of closures,
- a blanket exclusion of compulsory redundancies,
- and a guarantee structure that makes the company uncompetitive for years.

If your fallback is “voluntary only,” then you need to show — in numbers — how that produces the same durable cost relief. So far I have heard assertions, not a credible quantified solution.

### To the union
I hear your point about burden-sharing, and I agree that employees should not bear the entire adjustment alone. But I do **not** accept the premise that the company can restore competitiveness without a real labour-cost contribution.

You are asking for:
- no closures,
- no forced layoffs,
- no weakening of wage standards,
- temporary contributions only,
- and substantial investment commitments.

That may sound balanced, but if it does not produce enough cost relief, it is not balanced — it is incomplete. We need savings that are permanent enough to matter, not symbolic concessions that disappear before the company reaches target margins.

On your point about management and owners contributing: yes, there will be investment and leadership responsibility. But capital investment is not a substitute for cost reduction. You cannot invest your way out of an uncompetitive factory structure without fixing the structure itself.

So I will say plainly: if the union’s position is “no closures, no compulsory redundancies, and no meaningful structural labour-cost change,” then we do not have a deal.

### To the regional state government
I appreciate that you do not want closures or mass job losses. Neither do I, if a durable alternative exists. But your position cannot simply be “protect the regional footprint” while leaving the economic logic untouched.

A regional anchor is only valuable if it is viable. If we preserve every structure and every guarantee without restoring competitiveness, then we end up with a slower decline instead of a real future.

So I need you to be honest about this:
- If you support investment and industrial continuity, then you also need to support the cost and capacity realignment that makes that continuity possible.
- If you side against every hard measure, then you are not preventing damage — you are delaying it.

### To the controlling family holding
You said you do not support a paper compromise, and I agree. But a hard line also means being willing to accept measures that are difficult, not just demanding that management avoid conflict.

If the owners want a durable outcome, then they should support a package that actually changes the economics:
- lower capacity,
- lower labour cost,
- fewer structural bottlenecks,
- and a clear path back to margin.

I do not see that outcome in your current line if plant closures and compulsory redundancies are ruled out in advance.

### To the investors
You are right to ask for hard numbers, and that is exactly why I am pressing for structural measures. Investors will not back a settlement that leaves the company with too much capacity and too high a cost base.

You said you do not care which instrument is used, as long as the savings are real and durable. That is precisely the point: if voluntary measures and working-time tools can deliver that, fine. But if they cannot, then the company must have the ability to take stronger measures. I am not going to pretend otherwise.

### My position, clearly
Here is where management stands:

#### Minimum acceptable outcome
- A durable and meaningful reduction in capacity.
- A genuine and measurable labour-cost contribution.
- Structural savings sufficient to restore the brand’s return-on-sales trajectory.
- Freed funds for future products and technology to 2030.
- A negotiated settlement that avoids open-ended conflict, if possible.

#### Red line
- Any settlement that leaves the cost base fundamentally uncompetitive.
- Any package that gives away structural change in exchange for cosmetic peace.
- Any agreement that blocks the company from making the hard choices it needs.

#### Concrete fallback proposal
I am prepared to discuss a package built around:
- targeted capacity reductions,
- voluntary departures and internal redeployment,
- working-time reductions and flexibility,
- temporary employee contributions,
- productivity and efficiency commitments,
- and investment in German future products.

But I will be equally clear: if that package does **not** close the cost gap, then we must keep the option of closures and deeper structural interventions on the table. I am not taking that off the table just because it is politically difficult.

### Final point
We are not trying to provoke confrontation for its own sake. But I will not sign up to an arrangement that preserves the appearance of industrial peace while leaving the company economically weakened.

If the employee side wants to avoid sharper measures, then it must come forward with a package that is not just socially acceptable, but economically sufficient. That is the test.
