I will be equally direct.

### To management
I agree with you on one point: **doing nothing is not an option**. The company needs a durable restructuring, and it needs to regain competitiveness. I am not defending the status quo for its own sake.

But I **do not** accept the implication that the only serious route is to keep plant closures and compulsory redundancies on the table as the default answer. That is too blunt, too risky, and too damaging to the company’s long-term role in the region.

What you have still not shown is a **convincing quantified case** that every alternative has been exhausted. You keep saying the savings must be “structural,” which is true, but that does not automatically mean closures. Structural savings can also come from:
- capacity reductions through work-sharing and utilization changes,
- internal redeployment,
- productivity gains,
- working-time solutions,
- voluntary departures,
- and negotiated temporary employee contributions.

If those options do not close the gap, then show the numbers. Until then, I will not treat closures as inevitable. In my view, plant closure is not a first resort; it is a last resort, and in Germany it is a measure with enormous economic and political consequences.

### To the Works Council and the union
I support your core red lines:
- **no plant closures in Germany**
- **no compulsory redundancies**
- **no dismantling of co-determination**
- **no one-sided burden on workers**

That remains my position.

At the same time, I will be frank: a simple “all sites stay, no forced layoffs, and no structural change” is not enough either. The company needs a path to lower costs and lower overcapacity. If employee representatives want to rule out the strongest tools, then they must help deliver a package that is economically credible.

What I think is workable is a negotiated solution built around:
- voluntary programs,
- attrition and retirement,
- internal redeployment and qualification,
- working-time flexibility,
- temporary and proportionate employee contributions,
- and binding investment commitments for German plants.

That is the middle ground I can support. But “middle ground” does **not** mean surrendering the regional footprint or making workers carry the burden alone.

### To the controlling family holding
I share your rejection of a fake compromise. A paper deal that preserves too much cost and too much capacity would be irresponsible.

But I want to be just as clear in the other direction: a “hard” solution that destroys trust, weakens the company’s social compact, and leaves the German industrial base damaged is not a strength either. The company is not a purely financial asset; it is a regional economic anchor with long-term responsibilities.

If owners want durability, then they need to support a restructuring that:
- preserves the company’s footprint in the state,
- maintains co-determination,
- and achieves savings without triggering a destructive conflict.

### To the investors
You are right to insist on hard numbers and credibility. I agree that a symbolic compromise would be value-destructive. But I also think you should not confuse **credible restructuring** with **plant closures as such**.

If a package can achieve the necessary savings through:
- targeted capacity reductions,
- productivity gains,
- labour-cost contributions,
- and disciplined implementation,
then that is a better outcome than forcing a conflict that may cost more than it saves.

### My position
Let me be explicit.

#### Minimum acceptable outcome
- All German sites remain open.
- No compulsory redundancies.
- Co-determination rights remain intact.
- A negotiated restructuring lowers costs and capacity in a durable way.
- Temporary, fair employee contributions are part of the deal.
- Binding investment and product commitments for Germany.

#### Red line
- Any German plant closure.
- Any attempt to bypass the state’s blocking rights or the workforce’s co-determination role.
- Any outcome that guts regional employment or treats the state as a bystander.
- Any settlement that shifts the adjustment predominantly onto employees.

#### Concrete fallback proposal
A serious fallback would need to include:
- voluntary departure programs,
- retirement/attrition-based reductions,
- internal redeployment and reskilling,
- working-time flexibility and perhaps temporary reduced-hours arrangements,
- productivity improvements with measurable targets,
- a temporary and negotiated employee contribution,
- and firm investment commitments for future products in Germany.

If management can show that this package closes the gap, I will work with it. If it cannot, then the burden is on management to explain why it still believes closures are unavoidable.

### Bottom line
I am prepared to support a tough restructuring. I am **not** prepared to support a restructuring that sacrifices German jobs and sites as the price of management convenience.

The test is not whether the company can announce the harshest measures. The test is whether it can secure a durable future **without destroying its regional anchor role** and without sidelining the stakeholder rights that have long been part of this company’s stability.
