## Financial test for whether closures are required
The correct test is:

**Required recurring savings**
minus  
**savings from non-closure measures**
equals  
**residual gap**

If the residual gap is:
- **small to moderate**, non-closure measures may be enough
- **large**, then permanent capacity reduction becomes more likely
- **very large**, closures or equivalent permanent shutdown/consolidation become financially justified

## What a credible non-closure alternative would need to include
To be finance-credible, an alternative package would need to deliver **durable, recurring annual savings**, not just a one-time benefit. Typically this means a combination of:
- voluntary departures / attrition
- retirement incentives
- internal redeployment
- working-time reductions or flexibility
- productivity improvements
- temporary employee contribution
- procurement / overhead reductions
- possibly volume consolidation without formal closure

The key point: **temporary wage concessions alone do not close a structural overcapacity problem**. They may buy time, but they do not necessarily fix the economics through 2030.

## When is capacity reduction financially justified?
Capacity reduction is justified if:
1. a site is structurally underutilized,
2. its fixed cost burden is too high,
3. future product allocation is insufficient to restore competitive utilization,
4. logistics / complexity costs outweigh redeployment benefits,
5. non-closure measures cannot fully bridge the savings gap.

If those conditions are met, then management has a plausible case for permanent capacity removal.

## What cannot yet be concluded
On the current record, it is **not possible to say that closures are required**. The statements made by management are directionally plausible, but **not numerically proven**.

Likewise, it is **not possible to say that non-closure alternatives definitely solve the problem**. That would also require hard numbers.
