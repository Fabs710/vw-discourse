We are aligned with the central point that **doing nothing is not an option** and that a **cosmetic settlement would destroy value**. But we are not willing to support a package that preserves the current cost structure and calls that a restructuring.

## Where we agree
- With management, family owners, and the experts: the company needs **durable, recurring savings**, not symbolic action.
- With the state, works council, and union: **prolonged industrial conflict is value-destructive** and should be avoided if possible.
- With the advisor: the **quantified bridge is still missing**, and without it the case for any specific instrument remains incomplete.

## Where we disagree

### To the works council and union
Your insistence on:
- no plant closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- all sites preserved,
- and binding job security through 2030

is **not supportable as a default outcome** unless the numbers show that the gap can genuinely be closed otherwise.

Right now, that proof has not been provided.

We will not back a deal that leaves the company with a structurally uncompetitive cost base just to avoid hard decisions. That would not protect long-term jobs or shareholder value; it would defer the problem and likely worsen it.

### To the regional state government
We respect the political and regional constraints, but “closures as a last resort” cannot become “closures are politically impossible.” If certain sites are underutilized, high-cost, and lacking future volume, then **permanent capacity reduction may be unavoidable**.

That said, we agree with your sequencing point: the company should first publish a **quantified, site-by-site savings bridge** before any irreversible closure decision is made.

### To management
You are right to insist on **structural change**, and we support that. But you still have not presented the market with the full bridge:
- current margin gap,
- recurring savings target,
- savings from each non-closure measure,
- residual gap,
- and plant-level economics.

Without that, your case for closures is plausible but **not yet proven**. And without a package that can survive co-determination, even a theoretically correct plan becomes value-destructive through delay and conflict.

We also want to be explicit: a package relying mainly on temporary concessions, voluntary exits, and working-time tools may help, but it will not necessarily solve a structural overcapacity problem through 2030.

### To the controlling family holding
We agree that a paper compromise is worthless. But a “hard” solution that simply offloads the adjustment onto labour while avoiding real footprint decisions is also not acceptable.

If the numbers show a large residual gap after non-closure measures, then **capacity consolidation must remain on the table**.

## Our position
We will support only a package that credibly delivers:

1. **Quantified, durable savings**
2. **A meaningful labour-cost contribution**
3. **A real capacity reduction if needed**
4. **A credible implementation path**
5. **Investment commitments tied to performance**

## What we will not support
- A deal that leaves the cost base structurally uncompetitive
- A cosmetic peace deal
- A package that pushes the burden mainly onto capital while preserving excess capacity
- A deadlocked process that burns cash and confidence

## Our bottom line
We are **open to a non-closure solution if, and only if, it is numerically credible and durable**.  
If it is not, then some permanent capacity reduction, including closures, remains justified.
