We agree with one central point from management, and we will say it plainly: **this company does need a durable restructuring, and the status quo is not investable**.

But we also want to be equally clear about what we **do not** accept. A settlement that protects every site and every guarantee while leaving the cost base structurally uncompetitive would be value-destructive. If the outcome is a cosmetic peace that postpones the problem, capital will rightly treat it as delay, not restructuring.

### Where we agree
- **Management is right** that the cost base is too high and savings must be durable.
- **The union, works council, and state are right** that prolonged industrial conflict would destroy value quickly.
- **Everyone is right** that a settlement needs hard numbers, not slogans.

### Where we disagree
To the Works Council and union:  
Your position is understandable, but your current red lines are not enough on their own. “No closures, no compulsory redundancies, all plants preserved” is not a restructuring plan unless you can show, in quantified terms, how the same structural savings are achieved elsewhere. So far, we have not seen that.

To the state shareholder:  
We understand the political need to protect the regional footprint, but that cannot become a veto on real economic adjustment. Stability without competitiveness is just managed decline.

To management:  
We support the diagnosis, but not a maximalist posture that guarantees deadlock. If you push a package that cannot survive co-determination, you will destroy value through delay and uncertainty. Investors will not reward a restructuring that never lands.

### What would move us
We will support a settlement only if it delivers all of the following:
1. **Quantified, durable savings** sufficient to restore margins.
2. **Real capacity reduction** — whether through closures, consolidation, or another equivalent permanent removal of overcapacity.
3. **A meaningful labour contribution** that is negotiated, time-limited, and economically significant.
4. **A credible implementation path** with low risk of endless renegotiation or industrial paralysis.
5. **Investment commitments** tied to concrete performance, not used as a substitute for cost discipline.

### Our red line
We will not support any outcome that leaves the company with:
- an uncompetitive cost base,
- structurally impaired margins,
- or a “peace deal” that merely masks the problem.

### Our fallback view
If plant closures are politically impossible, then management and labour must still produce **equivalent permanent savings** through a combination of:
- capacity cuts,
- productivity improvements,
- working-time adjustments,
- voluntary departures,
- redeployment,
- and a real labour-cost reset.

But that package must be large enough to close the gap. If it does not, then it is not enough.

### Bottom line
We are not a veto for closures, and we are not a veto for social protection. We are a veto on a fake solution.

If the final package credibly restores competitiveness, we will back it. If it preserves too much excess capacity and too much legacy cost, we will oppose it and price the risk accordingly.
