Let me be very clear: we are now past the stage where management can hide behind abstractions like “structural necessity” while keeping closures and forced layoffs on the table as leverage. That approach is not acceptable.

## To management
You have still not proven that plant closures or compulsory redundancies are necessary.

The independent advisors said exactly what we have been saying:
- savings are likely required,
- but the actual gap is not yet quantified,
- and closures are **not proven** to be the only way to close it.

So I reject your attempt to treat closures as a default instrument. That is not “hard realism”; it is the easiest path for management, and the most damaging one for the workforce and the company’s social compact.

You also say our position is a veto. Yes — because co-determination exists for a reason. It is not an obstacle to be bypassed when management prefers speed. It is a legally and morally legitimate counterpower against destroying jobs and sites.

### What you still need to show
Before anyone even discusses closures, you must present:
1. the current return-on-sales gap,
2. the annual savings target in euros,
3. the savings from each alternative measure,
4. the residual gap after non-closure measures,
5. the plant-level economics that supposedly make closures unavoidable.

Until then, your “need” is unsubstantiated.

## To the union
I agree with your core line:
- no plant closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- no destruction of collective protections.

That is the correct line.

Where I want to sharpen it is this: “temporary contribution” must remain temporary, strictly limited, and tied to binding guarantees. We will not allow the crisis to be used to permanently weaken the workforce’s position or turn a temporary concession into a permanent downgrade.

## To the regional state government
Your position is right on the essentials, but it has to be backed by action, not just rhetoric.

If management comes back with the same destructive package, I expect the state shareholder to use its leverage. Regional stability cannot be defended in speeches while German plants are hollowed out in practice.

## To the controlling family holding
You say you reject a paper compromise. Good. So do we.

But there is a difference between a hard restructuring and a destructive one. A hard restructuring that protects plants, jobs, and co-determination while delivering savings is one thing. A “hard” solution that tears up guarantees, threatens closures, and pushes the full burden onto labour is something else entirely.

If owners want durability, then durability comes from:
- protected sites,
- shared burden,
- credible future investment,
- and a workforce that sees the plan as fair enough to sustain.

Not from forcing confrontation until the weakest side gives way.

## To the investors
You are right about one thing: we do not want a cosmetic settlement either.

But you are wrong if you assume that “real restructuring” necessarily means plant closures. The advisors explicitly said that non-closure measures can be viable if the required savings are moderate, if volume is consolidated, and if the company is willing to pursue a serious labor and productivity reset.

So the question is not “closures or nothing.” The question is whether management can prove that closures are necessary after exhausting:
- voluntary departures,
- attrition and retirement,
- internal redeployment,
- working-time adjustments,
- productivity improvements,
- and temporary, negotiated employee contributions.

That proof has not been made.

## My position
I will state it again, without dilution.

### Minimum acceptable outcome
- **All German plants remain open**
- **No compulsory redundancies**
- **Binding job-security framework through 2030**
- **No weakening of co-determination or existing protections**
- **Voluntary, socially managed adjustments only**
- **Binding investment and future-product commitments for Germany**

### Red line
- Any German plant closure
- Any compulsory redundancy
- Any use of restructuring to dismantle the job-security framework
- Any attempt to force workers to pay for management’s strategic failures alone

### Concrete fallback proposal
If management truly needs savings, then the only acceptable path is:
- voluntary departure programs,
- retirement and attrition,
- internal redeployment and qualification,
- working-time flexibility,
- productivity improvements designed jointly with employee representatives,
- a temporary and limited employee contribution,
- and binding investment commitments for German sites.

That is the only path I will engage with.

## What I reject outright
I reject the idea that a “durable” solution must include closures. That is management’s preference, not an economic law.

I also reject any attempt to create a fait accompli and then ask the workforce to ratify it under pressure. If that is the strategy, you will get resistance — in the plants, in the boardroom, and publicly.

## One more point
The advisors made something else clear: if the savings target is modest, non-closure measures may well be enough. That means management’s first obligation is not to threaten closures, but to **prove the numbers**.

Until that is done, we will not move off our core position.
