From a turnaround and restructuring finance perspective, the key issue is not whether some change is needed — all sides already accept that it is — but **whether the claimed savings requirement truly necessitates plant closures and compulsory redundancies, or whether a negotiated non-closure package can close the margin gap at acceptable risk**.

## Executive view

**I cannot validate the necessity of closures from the information provided alone.**  
What is missing is the actual quantified bridge from current performance to target performance:
- current return on sales,
- target return on sales,
- revenue base by brand/plant,
- fixed vs variable cost structure,
- plant-level contribution margins,
- volume assumptions,
- and the size of the labor-cost gap versus peers.

However, based on standard restructuring logic:

- If the company’s issue is mainly **high fixed cost and excess installed capacity**, then **some form of permanent capacity reduction is usually economically justified**.
- If the issue is mainly **labor cost**, then **a negotiated labor-cost reset, working-time flexibility, and voluntary attrition may close part of the gap**.
- If the issue is **both** high fixed cost **and** underutilized plants, then **non-closure alternatives are often insufficient unless the savings target is modest**.

So the answer is:

### 1) Are claimed savings “required”?
**Very likely yes, but the exact amount is unknown.**  
The management case that savings are required is financially plausible given:
- shrinking European demand,
- intensified price competition,
- EV transition investment burden,
- and a reported miss versus target return on sales.

But the **quantum** is not established in the facts provided.

### 2) Is proposed capacity reduction financially justified?
**Potentially yes, if the company has persistent overcapacity and structurally negative contribution from certain plants or shifts.**  
Capacity reduction is financially justified when one or more of the following are true:
- plant utilization is materially below efficient levels,
- the plant cannot be reloaded with profitable volume,
- logistics and labor costs are above competitive thresholds,
- the avoided fixed cost from closure exceeds the lost contribution margin,
- and the closure does not destroy strategic value disproportionate to the savings.

Without plant-level data, I cannot say which site(s), if any, meet that test.

### 3) Can non-closure alternatives credibly close the margin gap?
**Possibly, but only if the gap is moderate and if the cost base is not dominated by fixed overhead and underutilization.**  
Alternatives such as:
- voluntary departures,
- attrition,
- internal redeployment,
- reduced hours,
- temporary wage concessions,
- productivity improvements,
- and working-time flexibility

can produce meaningful savings, but they usually have limits:

- **Voluntary attrition** is slow and uncertain.
- **Temporary concessions** are not durable unless converted into permanent structural change.
- **Productivity improvements** are real but often smaller and slower than management initially claims.
- **Working-time measures** help cash flow and near-term cost, but do not always fix structural overcapacity.

In a large industrial turnaround, non-closure measures often close **part** of the gap, not necessarily the entire gap, unless the required savings are relatively modest.

## What would determine the answer in numbers

To judge whether closure is necessary, I would need a bridge like this:

1. **Current margin / target margin gap**
   - e.g. brand ROS needs to move from X% to Y%
2. **Required annual EBIT improvement**
   - expressed in euros
3. **Management’s proposed savings**
   - labor, procurement, overhead, logistics, footprint
4. **Residual gap after non-closure measures**
5. **Plant-level economics**
   - fixed cost, variable cost, utilization, and contribution margin by site

Only then can one say whether:
- non-closure measures are enough,
- partial footprint reduction is enough,
- or a plant closure is financially unavoidable.

## My assessment of the stakeholder positions

### Management
Management’s claim that structural change is needed is **credible**.  
But the claim that closures are necessary is **not proven** on the facts given.

### Works council / union / state
Their position that closures are not automatically justified is **financially defensible** if they can present a quantified alternative bridge.

### Investors
Investors are right on one point: a cosmetic deal that preserves excess cost and excess capacity is value-destructive.  
They are also right that any alternative must be **durable**, not temporary.

## Bottom line

### My neutral turnaround assessment:
- **Savings are likely required.**
- **Some capacity reduction may be financially justified.**
- **But closure is not yet shown to be the only viable route.**
- **Non-closure alternatives could be sufficient only if the needed savings are modest or if management is willing to accept a deeply negotiated, structurally durable labor and productivity reset.**
- **If the savings target is large and the overcapacity is significant, non-closure measures alone are unlikely to fully close the margin gap.**
