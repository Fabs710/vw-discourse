## To the Works Council
I agree with your core line:
- **no plant closures**
- **no compulsory redundancies**
- **no dismantling of collective protections**
- **no restructuring by shifting the entire burden onto workers**

That is still my position as well.

Where I want to sharpen the point is this: we should not let management blur the distinction between **necessary restructuring** and **maximalist restructuring**. The independent advisor has confirmed what we have been saying: savings are likely required, but **closures are not proven to be the only route**. That matters.

At the same time, I want us to stay disciplined. If we are going to hold the line, then our alternative must remain credible, quantified, and implementable. “No closures” is not enough by itself; it must be paired with a real package:
- voluntary departures,
- retirement and attrition,
- internal redeployment and qualification,
- working-time flexibility,
- productivity improvements,
- and a temporary, limited employee contribution.

I support your insistence that any contribution must be strictly time-limited and tied to hard guarantees. That is essential. Temporary must not become permanent by stealth.

## To management
You still have not shown that closures or forced layoffs are necessary.

You keep saying “structural” and “durable,” but the advisor’s intervention is clear: **the quantified bridge has not been presented**. Until we see:
1. the current return-on-sales gap,
2. the annual savings target in euros,
3. the savings from each non-closure measure,
4. the residual gap after those measures,
5. and the plant-level economics,

your claim that closures are unavoidable remains an assertion, not a fact.

And let me be clear: if management uses the threat of plant closures and compulsory redundancies as leverage without first proving necessity, then that is not responsible turnaround management. It is pressure politics.

What could move us:
- no plant closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- a meaningful but **temporary** employee contribution,
- a fair contribution from management and owners,
- binding investment for German sites,
- and a genuine commitment to future products and volume here.

What will not move us:
- a package where workers carry the pain, while management keeps closures and layoffs in reserve as a bargaining club.

If that is the approach, then you should expect resistance, including industrial action.

## To the regional state government
I support your rejection of closures as a first resort, and I agree that regional stability matters. But your support will only count if it is backed by leverage when management tries to force a one-sided package.

We need the state side to do more than say “balance.” It should insist on:
- quantified proof before closures are discussed,
- a fair burden-sharing package,
- and binding safeguards for jobs, sites, and investment.

If management comes back with the same destructive design, then the state should not simply observe the conflict from the sidelines.

## To the controlling family holding
You are right to reject a paper compromise. So do we.

But a hard line is not the same as a destructive line. A settlement that:
- tears up wage standards,
- weakens co-determination,
- threatens closures,
- and forces workers to absorb the adjustment alone

is not “hard realism.” It is social and industrial short-sightedness.

If the owners want durability, then durability must be built on:
- fair burden-sharing,
- protected sites,
- credible investment,
- and a workforce that can accept the deal as legitimate.

That is the only kind of compromise that lasts.

## To the investors
You are correct that a cosmetic deal would be value-destructive. We do **not** want that either.

But you are wrong if you assume that “real restructuring” automatically means closures. The advisor did not say that. The advisor said that non-closure measures can be viable if the gap is moderate and the package is serious. That is exactly the point: first prove the gap, then judge the instruments.

So the choice is not:
- “closures or nothing.”

The real choice is:
- **quantified, balanced restructuring** versus
- **premature, socially destructive measures** presented as inevitability.

## Our position, clearly stated

- **All German plants remain open**
- **No compulsory redundancies**
- **Protection of the collective wage framework**
- **A temporary and limited employee contribution**
- **Meaningful contribution from management and owners**
- **Binding investment and future-product commitments for Germany**
- **Capacity adjustment through voluntary and socially managed measures only**

### Red line
- Any plant closure in Germany
- Any compulsory redundancy
- Any attempt to dismantle the collective wage framework through restructuring
- Any package that makes workers bear the adjustment alone

### Concrete fallback proposal
If management truly needs savings, then we are prepared to discuss:
- voluntary departure programs,
- retirement and attrition,
- internal redeployment and reskilling,
- working-time flexibility,
- productivity measures jointly designed with employee representatives,
- a temporary and limited employee contribution,
- and binding investment commitments for German sites.

That is the only path to a settlement I can support.

I reject the idea that closures are the default answer.

I reject the idea that forced redundancies are a normal part of restructuring here.

I reject the idea that the collective wage framework can be weakened just because management wants a cleaner cost line.

If management wants labour peace, then it must bring a fair package. If not, then it should prepare for escalation, not assume consent.
