As dispersed institutional investors, we are listening for one thing above all: a **credible path back to competitive margins and durable cash generation**. We are not here to defend any particular plant map, but we are also not willing to finance delay, theatre, or a compromise that leaves the cost base structurally too high.

So let me be direct.

I agree with management on the core diagnosis: the business cannot continue with a German cost base that remains above peers while the market shrinks and competition intensifies. If the result of this process is only a cosmetic agreement that preserves too much excess capacity and too many legacy cost structures, then we would view that as value-destructive.

At the same time, I also agree with labour and the state on one important point: **a prolonged conflict is bad for value**. Strikes, uncertainty, and board paralysis destroy earnings and confidence quickly. We do not want a settlement that “wins” on paper but erodes operational performance and investor confidence for years.

Where we differ is on the threshold for acceptability.

To the Works Council and the union:  
Your rejection of closures and compulsory redundancies is understandable, but it is **not sufficient** unless you can show that the same economic effect is achieved elsewhere. A promise to “discuss alternatives” is not enough. We need to see, in hard numbers:
- how much capacity comes out,
- how much labour cost is permanently reduced,
- how productivity improves,
- and how the company returns to target margins.

If the answer is simply to protect every site and every guarantee while asking management to hope for a turnaround, then we would oppose that. That would be a structural impairment of returns, not a restructuring.

To management:  
Your case for decisive action is broadly credible, but credibility will depend on **execution detail** and **deal certainty**. Investors will not reward a maximalist demand if it produces a multi-year labour war, political obstruction, or legal deadlock. If you want support from capital markets, you need a plan that is:
- financially measurable,
- enforceable,
- and likely to complete without indefinite disruption.

So our position is conditional but clear:
- We support a restructuring that restores competitiveness through **durable, verifiable cost reduction**.
- We do **not** support a package that merely defers the problem or preserves structurally uncompetitive margins.
- We also do **not** support a reckless confrontation that destroys value through prolonged industrial action.

What would move us is a settlement with:
1. **Quantified savings** sufficient to restore margins, not aspirational language.
2. **A credible capacity reduction**, whether through closures, consolidation, or equivalent permanent volume removal.
3. **A real labour contribution**, preferably negotiated and time-limited, but meaningful enough to matter.
4. **A clear implementation timetable** and minimal scope for endless renegotiation.
5. **Investment commitments** tied to measurable performance and not used as a substitute for cost discipline.

So our message to all sides is simple:  
We are not a veto for any one camp, but we are a veto on a fake solution. If the final outcome leaves the company with an uncompetitive cost base, we will treat that as unacceptable and price it accordingly. If, however, the agreement credibly resets costs and capacity, we will support it.
