I’ll respond plainly.

## Where I agree

I agree with the expert, and I agree with all sides on one central point: **this company needs a durable, structural reset**.  
I am not asking for a cosmetic agreement, and I am not interested in a short-term patch that leaves the cost base uncompetitive.

I also agree that:
- a drawn-out confrontation destroys value,
- any settlement has to be measurable and enforceable,
- and a negotiated outcome is preferable if it actually fixes the economics.

So yes: **we need a serious package, not theatre**.

## Where I disagree — sharply

### 1) A blanket “no closures” line is not acceptable
The works council and union keep presenting no closures as if it were a legitimate economic answer in itself. It is not.

If a plant is structurally overcapacity, too expensive, and not aligned with future production needs, then refusing even to consider closure is **not** a restructuring position. It is a veto on the economics.

I am not saying closure is the first or preferred instrument. I am saying **it must remain available** if the numbers require it. Without that, there is no credible leverage to achieve a real reset.

### 2) Voluntary measures alone are very unlikely to be enough
I am open to:
- voluntary exits,
- partial retirement,
- working-time adjustments,
- productivity improvements,
- and targeted investment commitments.

But let’s stop pretending those alone will reliably close a structural cost gap at a company of this scale.

If the problem were a mild adjustment, that might be enough.  
But the problem here is a **materially above-target cost base and overcapacity in a shrinking market**. That requires recurring savings, not just softer headlines.

So if the employee side says “no closures, no compulsory redundancies, no deep framework changes,” then I do **not** see a credible path to the required savings.

### 3) An unconditional site and job guarantee through 2030 is not workable
I understand why labor wants guarantees. But an **unconditional** guarantee that removes future adjustment capacity is incompatible with responsible management.

If market conditions worsen, or if a site remains structurally uncompetitive, management must retain the ability to act. A guarantee with no review mechanism is not stability; it is paralysis.

## To the works council

You keep demanding proof that closures are necessary. Fair enough — but then accept the same standard in return:

**Show me a site-preserving package that actually delivers the same permanent savings, at the same speed, with the same durability.**

Not slogans. Not principles. Numbers.

If you want no closures, then your package must include:
- real capacity reduction,
- substantial headcount reduction,
- durable labor-cost moderation,
- enforceable productivity improvements,
- and binding investment/product commitments.

If you cannot produce that, then your “no closure” position is not an alternative solution. It is simply a refusal to confront the business reality.

## To the union

You say you are ready for shared sacrifice. Good. Then let’s define “shared.”

Shared sacrifice cannot mean:
- labor gives up wages, bonuses, flexibility, and headcount,
- while capacity stays too high,
- the cost base remains uncompetitive,
- and management is left without tools to finish the restructuring.

That is not a fair burden share, and it does not solve the problem.

If labor wants to be taken seriously, the contribution must be **material and durable**. Temporary concessions and aspirational productivity promises are not enough.

## To the state government

I respect your position against closures and compulsory redundancies as primary tools. But I have to be direct: **the state cannot protect every site and still expect the company to recover competitiveness on its own**.

If the company does not get its cost base down, the region loses anyway — just more slowly, and with less control. So I need the state to stop treating restructuring as something that can be politically wished away.

If you want to help secure a deal, then support:
- real capacity adjustment,
- enforceable labor-cost contributions,
- and a binding framework that actually changes the economics.

## To the controlling family and investors

I agree that a destructive closure battle would be bad value if it triggers prolonged conflict and blockages. But I also need to be clear: **fear of conflict cannot become an excuse for an inadequate deal**.

If the final package does not materially improve competitiveness, then it will fail your own value test later. That is exactly why management cannot settle for a soft compromise that looks cooperative but leaves the cost problem intact.

## On the expert assessment

The expert has said something very important: a site-preserving solution can be credible **only if it is hard enough**. I fully agree.

But that conclusion does **not** support the employee side’s claim that closures are never necessary. It supports my point: if labor wants to exclude closures, then it must produce a package that is economically equivalent in output, speed, and durability.

So far, I have not seen that package.

## What I am prepared to discuss

I remain open to a negotiated alternative **if** it meets the economics.

That means I can discuss:
- capacity reductions without immediate closures,
- voluntary exits and retirement pathways,
- working-time measures,
- labor-cost moderation,
- productivity measures,
- investment commitments,
- and a phased implementation.

But I will not accept:
- a cosmetic compromise,
- a package that preserves too much capacity,
- a settlement that leaves recurring costs too high,
- or an arrangement that rules out the instruments needed to finish the restructuring.

## What would move me

I said at the outset what would genuinely move management: a credible, durable package of cost and capacity savings, with a meaningful labor-cost contribution, that restores medium-term margins.

That remains my position.

If the employee side can table a **quantified** proposal that delivers:
- substantial recurring savings,
- real capacity reduction,
- substantial labor-cost contribution,
- and enforceable implementation,
then I will engage seriously.

If not, then the answer cannot be “no closures forever.” The answer is that the company must retain the right tools to restore competitiveness.

## Bottom line

I am not retreating from the need for structural change.

- **No cosmetic deal**
- **No package that leaves the cost base uncompetitive**
- **No unconditional guarantee that blocks necessary adjustment**
- **No exclusion of closures or workforce reductions as instruments if they become necessary**

I would prefer a negotiated settlement. But I will not trade away the company’s 2030 competitiveness for a politically easier story.

If the other side wants agreement, it has to meet the economics — not just set red lines and call that a solution.
