I will be very clear.

## Where I agree
I agree with everyone on one point: **this company needs a hard, durable restructuring**.  
I am not defending inefficiency, and I am not arguing for a symbolic deal that leaves the cost base untouched. The company must regain competitiveness and invest in its future.

I also agree with the expert and with management on one important analytical point: **a site-preserving solution is only credible if it is tough enough to deliver real recurring savings**. I accept that. I am not interested in a weak compromise that postpones the same crisis.

## Where I disagree
I disagree with the idea that **closures and compulsory redundancies must remain normal, routine instruments** in this process.

That is where my line is. For this state, for this company, and for the regional economy, I will not support a restructuring path that:
- guts German employment,
- uses plant closures as a bargaining club,
- or treats compulsory redundancies as an ordinary management lever.

That is not a “preference.” It is a boundary.

### To management
You keep saying the company needs all tools on the table. I understand the logic, but you have not shown that closure is economically necessary **after** transition costs, legal delay, strike risk, political resistance, and spillovers are fully counted.

If you want me to accept closures as a last resort, then you need to prove:
- the exact savings gap,
- the timeline,
- the transition costs,
- and why a hard site-preserving package cannot close the gap.

Until then, closure remains a threat, not a justified conclusion.

### To the works council and union
I support your central red lines:
- no closures as the first instrument,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- no weakening of co-determination,
- no destruction of the job-security framework.

But I also need to be honest with you: **a site-preserving package must be real, not rhetorical**. If the company needs a structural reset, then voluntary exits, working-time changes, productivity gains, capacity rationalization, and a fair labour contribution must all be part of the answer. I will support that.

What I will not support is a deal that simply says “no” while leaving the economics unresolved.

### To the controlling family and investors
I hear your concern about value destruction. It is valid. A brutal closure war could absolutely destroy value. That is exactly why I do **not** want closure to become the default reflex.

But value is also destroyed by a settlement that tears apart social partnership, triggers prolonged conflict, and damages the company’s standing in its home region. So if you want a value-preserving solution, then support a negotiated package that protects the industrial base while delivering the savings.

### To the independent expert
Your assessment supports my position more than it weakens it: **closure is not automatically superior**. The question is whether a hard site-preserving package can achieve the same durable effect without the social and political damage.

That is the test. And it means the burden is on management to prove that closures are really necessary.

## What I will support
I can support a serious restructuring package if it includes:
- real capacity reduction without closures where possible,
- substantial voluntary exits and retirement instruments,
- working-time flexibility,
- productivity improvements,
- a meaningful contribution from management and owners,
- binding investment in German sites,
- and a credible job and site protection framework.

That is a real settlement. It is not a free pass, and it is not cosmetic.

## What I will not support
I will not support:
- plant closures in Germany as a first choice,
- compulsory redundancies as a routine instrument,
- a package that weakens co-determination,
- or an “unconditional” restructuring logic that leaves the state and workforce with no meaningful say.

And I want to be explicit: the state’s co-determination and veto role is not negotiable. It is part of the governance architecture that makes this company viable as a regional anchor. Removing that would be fundamentally unacceptable.

## My position
So my position remains unchanged:

- **Yes** to restructuring.
- **Yes** to hard savings.
- **Yes** to a negotiated, site-preserving solution if it is economically credible.
- **No** to closures as the default route.
- **No** to compulsory redundancies as a primary tool.
- **No** to any outcome that strips the state of its co-determination and veto role.

If management wants a settlement, it must come back with **quantified, plant-specific evidence** and a package that protects the regional industrial base while restoring competitiveness.
