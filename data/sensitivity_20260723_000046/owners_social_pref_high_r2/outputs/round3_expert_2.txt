## Bottom line
A **hard site-preserving package can be economically equivalent to closure only if it delivers very large, recurring, and enforceable savings**. In this governance and industrial-relations setting, the threshold is not just “some savings”; it must be enough to offset the structural cost gap **after** allowing for execution risk, slower realization, and the cost of preserving excess capacity.

By contrast, a closure-based strategy only wins if the **risk-adjusted NPV** remains superior after you include:
- severance and social-plan costs,
- shutdown/decommissioning costs,
- transfer or replacement-capacity costs,
- legal and co-determination delay,
- strike risk,
- supplier disruption,
- management distraction,
- and lost output during transition.

## Practical threshold
Without the company’s confidential numbers I cannot set a precise euro figure, but as a decision rule:

A site-preserving package must usually deliver:
1. **Permanent annual cost savings**, not one-offs;
2. **Material capacity reduction**, not just workforce trimming;
3. **Savings visible within 12–24 months**, because delayed savings are heavily discounted;
4. **High execution certainty**, meaning not overly dependent on optimistic voluntary take-up;
5. **A credible product/investment commitment**, so the retained sites are not stranded.

If those conditions are not met, then it is hard to say the package is economically equivalent to closure.

## Plant-by-plant logic
For each plant, the comparison should ask:

### 1) Is the site structurally uncompetitive, or just overstaffed/underutilized?
- If the plant has a genuine location or product advantage, a hard site-preserving package may be superior.
- If the plant has persistent low utilization, poor logistics, or no future product allocation, closure becomes more plausible.

### 2) Can capacity be reduced without closing the site?
Site-preserving options work best where you can:
- cut shifts,
- consolidate models,
- reduce complexity,
- redeploy volume,
- and use voluntary exits to reset staffing.

If the plant remains physically oversized relative to demand, the fixed-cost burden may survive even after headcount reduction.

### 3) How large are the transition costs of closure?
Closure costs are often underestimated. They include:
- severance,
- social plan and retraining,
- decommissioning,
- asset write-downs,
- transfer costs,
- and temporary inefficiency elsewhere in the network.

In Germany, the industrial-relations and legal friction can be large enough to erode a paper saving.

### 4) What is the strike and delay risk?
This is critical. In a co-determined, unionized environment:
- closure threats can provoke strike action,
- legal challenges can delay implementation,
- and prolonged conflict can destroy value through lost production and supplier instability.

A closure with a 12–24 month conflict tail may be worse than a harder negotiated package with slightly lower gross savings.

## What I would say to the parties
### To management
You need to specify:
- the recurring savings gap,
- the date by which savings must be locked in,
- and what minimum package you would accept short of closure.

### To labor
A no-closure package is only credible if it includes:
- meaningful headcount reduction,
- capacity reduction,
- wage/bonus moderation,
- productivity commitments,
- and enforceable implementation.

A blanket refusal to change the cost base enough makes the no-closure line economically weak.

### To owners and investors
Do not compare a clean closure spreadsheet against a fantasy-free site-preserving option. Compare realistic alternatives with execution risk included.
