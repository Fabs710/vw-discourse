I appreciate the clarity from all sides, and I want to respond just as clearly.

We in the regional state government share one basic assessment with the workforce representatives: **this company needs a viable future in Germany**, not a short-term cost exercise that leaves the region weaker, the plants hollowed out, and the company socially and politically destabilized.

So let me be explicit on what I **agree** with:

- The company does need restructuring.
- A credible competitiveness plan must include real savings.
- Management is right that the current cost and capacity situation cannot simply be ignored.
- Any durable solution must protect the company’s investment capacity and technological future.

Where I **disagree sharply** is on the method.

### On plant closures and mass layoffs
A restructuring that relies on **plant closures in Germany as the default solution** is not acceptable to us. Nor is a path that normalizes **compulsory redundancies** as the first or primary lever. That would inflict lasting damage on the regional economy, undermine trust in the social partnership model, and create political and industrial conflict that would make recovery harder, not easier.

We are not interested in preserving inefficiency for its own sake. But we are equally not prepared to accept a “solution” that destroys regional employment and sacrifices sites that remain strategically important.

### On the role of the state
Our position is not symbolic. Under the special statute, we have a real co-determination and veto role, and we intend to use it responsibly. That means we will support a restructuring **only if** it is based on:

- **site-preserving measures**
- **socially responsible capacity reduction**
- **voluntary instruments before forced measures**
- **binding investment commitments**
- **a fair contribution from management and owners**
- **protection of the regional economic anchor function**

If the proposal comes back as “close plants first, negotiate later,” then you should assume that we will not provide the necessary support.

### To management
I understand the argument that costs must come down durably. But I do not accept the implication that there is only one route: closures and labor cuts. That is too narrow and too risky.

If management wants our support, it needs to bring forward a plan that demonstrates:
- real productivity gains,
- capacity adjustments that do not require shutting German plants,
- a serious contribution from higher levels of the company,
- and a credible industrial strategy for the affected sites.

If you want the state to stand behind difficult restructuring, then you need to show that you are not simply shifting the burden onto workers and regions.

### To the works council and union
I also want to be honest with you: I will stand with you against closures that gut the regional base. But for that position to hold in the long run, we also need a plan that is economically believable. A mere refusal of change will not be enough to protect jobs.

A durable settlement has to be more than defensive. It has to secure:
- the sites,
- the skills base,
- the investment pipeline,
- and the company’s competitiveness.

That may require difficult compromises on flexibility, working time, and temporary contributions — but **not** the surrender of core sites or the removal of the state’s legitimate role.

### Bottom line
Our red line is clear: **no restructuring by regional disinvestment**.  
Our readiness is also clear: **we can support a negotiated restructuring that preserves sites, protects a large share of jobs, and restores competitiveness through alternative measures**.

If the management side is serious, then bring a package that proves closures are not necessary. If not, then do not expect the regional state to be a passive bystander while a central industrial employer is broken apart.
