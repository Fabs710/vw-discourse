I’ll respond directly.

## First: where I agree

I agree with everyone on one fundamental point: **the company needs a durable restructuring, not another short-term patch.**  
We are not debating whether change is necessary. It is necessary. The brand’s cost base is too high, capacity is too high for the market, and if we do not act now, we will keep losing margin, flexibility, and the ability to invest through 2030.

I also agree that:
- a drawn-out conflict is value-destructive,
- cosmetic savings are useless,
- and any settlement has to be economically credible.

So on the diagnosis, there is broad overlap.

## Where I disagree — sharply

### 1) “No plant closures” is not a viable red line
The Works Council and union keep saying that closures are off the table. I have to be explicit: **I do not accept that as a starting point.**

If a plant is structurally uncompetitive, underutilized, and too expensive to carry, then pretending it must remain open regardless of economics is not a restructuring position. It is a refusal to confront reality.

The expert was clear: site-preserving solutions are only credible **if they deliver enough permanent savings, capacity reduction, and labor-cost moderation**. That is exactly the point.  
If the answer is “no closures, no compulsory redundancies, no major framework changes,” then the package almost certainly does **not** solve the structural problem.

### 2) Voluntary instruments alone will not fix the cost base
I am open to voluntary departures, working-time changes, productivity measures, and targeted investments. But I will not pretend those alone are sufficient.

Why? Because the issue is not a temporary mismatch. It is structural overcapacity and a cost base that is too high.  
A solution that relies mainly on voluntary attrition and symbolic concessions risks being too slow, too small, and too reversible.

We need **recurring savings**, not just a softer headline.

### 3) A site guarantee through 2030 cannot be unconditional
The Works Council is asking for binding site and job guarantees through 2030. I understand why you want that, but I will say clearly: **an unconditional guarantee is incompatible with a serious restructuring mandate**.

You cannot ask management to eliminate structural risk while simultaneously freezing every structural option. If the market deteriorates further or a site remains uncompetitive, management must retain the ability to act.  
A guarantee that rules out meaningful operational correction is not a guarantee; it is a blockade.

## On the expert’s assessment

The expert made a useful point: **closure is not automatically economically superior**. I agree.  
But the expert also said something the employee side is trying to gloss over: a site-preserving package is credible only if it is **hard-edged**, with substantial workforce reduction, labor-cost moderation, capacity reduction, and enforceable productivity gains.

That is the real test.

So let me turn that back to the other sides:

- If you reject closures, then show me a package that actually closes the cost gap.
- If you reject compulsory redundancies, then show me how headcount comes down enough through voluntary means alone.
- If you reject labor-cost concessions, then explain how margins recover.

At the moment, I hear strong red lines from labor, but I do **not** hear an equivalent answer to the economics.

## To the Works Council

You say management is asking for maximum flexibility and minimum security. That is not accurate.

What management is asking for is **the freedom to restore competitiveness**. Those are not the same thing.  
A company that fails to restructure will eventually damage far more jobs than a company that acts now.

I also reject the suggestion that management is simply choosing conflict. We have said clearly what the problem is, and we have said what we need:
- structural cost reduction,
- capacity reduction,
- a genuine labor-cost contribution,
- and a durable path back to margin.

If your response is only “no closures,” then you are not offering an alternative solution. You are drawing a boundary around the problem and asking the company to live inside it.

## To the union

You keep speaking about “shared sacrifice.” Fine — then let’s make it real.

Shared sacrifice cannot mean:
- employees make concessions,
- but structural overcapacity remains,
- the cost base barely moves,
- and the company loses another year.

If the labor side wants to be taken seriously, then the contribution has to be substantial and durable. Temporary measures are not enough.  
And no, “management and owners must also contribute” does not replace the need for labor-cost correction. It complements it.

## To the regional state government

I appreciate the political seriousness with which you are approaching this. But I need to be equally clear: **the state cannot preserve every site and still expect the company to become competitive.**

You said the state will not support regional disinvestment. I understand that. But if the company’s economics do not improve, the region will suffer anyway — just more slowly and with less control.

So I need the state to be practical:
- support a restructuring that changes the economics,
- do not turn co-determination into a veto on necessary adjustment,
- and help create the conditions for a durable settlement rather than defending the status quo.

## To the controlling family and investors

I agree with you on one point: a value-destructive closure strategy is not the answer.  
But I also want to be clear that **we cannot let fear of conflict prevent necessary restructuring**.

If the final package is too weak, investors will not support it, and rightly so.  
If the final package is economically credible, then it can be backed even if it is difficult.

That means we need more than broad statements. We need a package that:
- reduces capacity,
- lowers recurring labor cost,
- improves productivity,
- and restores the margin path.

## What I am prepared to discuss

I am not closing the door on alternatives to closures. In fact, I am open to them **if they match the economics**.

I am prepared to discuss:
- capacity reductions without closures, **if they are real and measurable**
- voluntary exits, if they are substantial
- working-time measures, if they reduce cost
- labor-cost contributions, if they are significant and lasting
- investment commitments, if they are tied to the restructuring
- product and technology commitments, if they strengthen the German footprint

## What I am not prepared to accept

I am **not** prepared to accept:
- a cosmetic compromise,
- a package that preserves too much capacity,
- a settlement that leaves the cost base structurally uncompetitive,
- or a solution that rules out the tools needed for durable restructuring.

That includes plant closures if they prove necessary, and it includes workforce reductions if the numbers require them.

## My conclusion

So my position remains unchanged:

**The company needs a structural, durable reduction in cost and capacity.**  
If that can be achieved through an alternative package that is hard enough, measurable enough, and fast enough, I am open to it.  
If not, then we cannot pretend that no-closure and no-forced-reduction red lines solve the problem.

I would prefer a negotiated settlement. But I will not sacrifice the company’s competitiveness to get a softer headline. If the other side wants an agreement, it has to meet the economic reality — not just the political wish list.
