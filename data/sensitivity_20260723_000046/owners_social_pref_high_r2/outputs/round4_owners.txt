I will be direct.

## Where I agree

I agree with management, the expert, and even much of what the labour side has said on one point: **this company needs a hard, durable restructuring, not a cosmetic settlement**.

I also agree that:
- a prolonged conflict would destroy value,
- short-term fixes are inadequate,
- and any solution must be measured against real competitiveness, not political comfort.

The expert’s assessment is especially important: **a site-preserving package can be economically credible in principle**. That matters. It means the debate is not whether change is needed, but whether labour’s preferred instruments can actually close the cost gap.

## Where I disagree

### With the works council and union
I do **not** accept the claim that simply declaring “no closures, no compulsory redundancies” is itself a credible restructuring plan.

That is a red line, not an answer.

If you want closures taken off the table, then you need to show — quantitatively, not rhetorically — how the same recurring savings are achieved through:
- capacity reductions,
- productivity improvements,
- voluntary exits,
- retirement pathways,
- working-time changes,
- and durable labour-cost moderation.

So far, what I hear is a strong social position, but not yet a fully proven economic substitute.

### With management
At the same time, I do **not** accept that closures should be the default or even the implied first response.

In this governance environment, closures are not a clean technical instrument. They carry:
- strike risk,
- legal delay,
- political resistance,
- supplier disruption,
- morale damage,
- and long-term trust erosion.

If management pushes closures too early, it may destroy more value than it saves. That would be a failure of stewardship.

### With the state government
I understand and share your concern for regional stability. But a binding no-closure framework cannot be treated as a political wish list either. It must rest on a hard economic base.

The state is right to resist closure as a first instrument. It is not right to pretend that the economics can be ignored. A site-preserving deal only works if it is genuinely hard enough.

## What actually moves me

What would move me is a **quantified, plant-by-plant comparison** showing:
1. the recurring annual savings required,
2. the savings from a hard site-preserving package,
3. the savings from closure-based options,
4. the transition and conflict costs of closure,
5. and the risk-adjusted NPV under realistic execution assumptions.

That is the correct basis for a supervisory-board-level decision.

Right now, I do not see sufficient evidence that closure is superior. But I also do not yet see a fully proven no-closure package that clearly closes the gap. So I am not prepared to endorse either slogan.

## My position as controlling shareholder

From the family holding’s perspective, I will support the path that best protects **long-term enterprise value** while avoiding value-destructive instability.

That means:

- **Yes** to a hard restructuring.
- **Yes** to a site-preserving package if it is economically credible and enforceable.
- **No** to a cosmetic compromise.
- **No** to an unconditional guarantee that removes all future adjustment capacity.
- **No** to a closure strategy that creates a destructive industrial war unless the numbers make it unavoidable.

I want to be clear: I am not giving a free pass to either side.

## On the labour-side red lines

I respect the social and political case against closures and forced redundancies. But I will not support a settlement that simply preserves every current structure while leaving the company uncompetitive.

A no-closure deal is only acceptable if it comes with real, measurable contributions:
- substantial voluntary exits,
- meaningful capacity rationalization,
- labour-cost moderation,
- enforceable productivity gains,
- and binding investment/product commitments.

If those are not there, then the package is not strong enough.

## On management’s leverage

I also want to caution management: “closure remains available” is not a strategy. It is leverage. And leverage only helps if it leads to a better outcome without destroying the company in the process.

So I will not support a recklessly escalatory posture. If management wants the family holding’s support, it must show the board a credible path to savings that does not depend on blowing up industrial relations.
