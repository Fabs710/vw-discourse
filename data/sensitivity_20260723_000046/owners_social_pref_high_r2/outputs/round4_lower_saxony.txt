I will speak plainly.

## Where I agree
I agree with management, the family holding, investors, and the expert on one central point: **this company needs a hard, durable restructuring**. A cosmetic compromise would be irresponsible. I also agree that the comparison must be risk-adjusted and that a long conflict would destroy value.

I also agree with the expert that, **in principle, a site-preserving package can be economically credible** if it is hard enough. That is important, because it means the debate is not “change or no change.” The real question is how to change without breaking the company’s role as a regional anchor.

## Where I disagree
### On closures
I do **not** accept management’s framing that closures must remain a normal or preferred instrument. In this company’s governance setting, closures are not a neutral technical measure. They are a major escalation with serious economic, political, and social risk.

That means:
- closures cannot be the starting point,
- they cannot be used as a bargaining club,
- and they cannot be treated as justified unless management proves plant by plant that the alternatives fail.

So I reject the idea that “closure remains available” should be the baseline. For me, the baseline is the opposite: **site preservation first, closure only if absolutely proven unavoidable**.

### On compulsory redundancies
I also reject compulsory redundancies as a primary tool. They would directly damage the regional economy, deepen the conflict, and undermine the social partnership that has long been part of this company’s strength.

### On unconditional guarantees
At the same time, I am not endorsing a blank cheque. An unconditional guarantee with no economic discipline would not be responsible either. The workforce cannot be asked to shoulder all the adjustment, but management and owners also cannot be asked to surrender the company’s ability to respond if targets are missed.

So I do not support “everything fixed forever.” I support a **binding no-closure, no-forced-layoff settlement with measurable milestones, enforceable commitments, and joint oversight**.

## What I want to see
If management wants my support for a restructuring settlement, I need a **plant-by-plant quantified comparison** of:
1. the required recurring savings,
2. the savings from a hard site-preserving package,
3. the savings from closure options,
4. the transition and conflict costs,
5. and the NPV under realistic execution assumptions.

Without that, closure is just leverage, not proof.

## What would move me
What would genuinely move me is a package that:
- preserves German sites,
- protects employment as far as possible,
- delivers real recurring savings,
- includes voluntary exits, retirement pathways, working-time solutions, and productivity measures,
- includes meaningful contributions from management and owners,
- and is backed by binding investment commitments.

That is the kind of settlement I can support. It protects the company’s future **and** its role as a regional economic anchor.

## What I will not accept
I will not accept:
- plant closures as the default answer,
- compulsory redundancies as a routine tool,
- a hollowed-out job-security framework,
- or any settlement that strips the state of its co-determination and veto role.

That is not negotiable.

## My position
So my position remains:
- **Yes** to hard restructuring.
- **Yes** to real savings and structural change.
- **Yes** to a negotiated site-preserving solution if it is economically credible.
- **No** to closures as a routine instrument.
- **No** to compulsory redundancies.
- **No** to any outcome that weakens the state’s co-determination role or guts regional employment.
