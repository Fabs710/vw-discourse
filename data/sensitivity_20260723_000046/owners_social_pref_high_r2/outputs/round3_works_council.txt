We are finally at the point where the real issue is exposed: **management wants the freedom to close plants and cut jobs, while labour is being asked to sign away the company’s social contract in exchange for “competitiveness.”** I reject that framing.

## Where I agree
I agree that the company needs a durable restructuring. I agree that cosmetic savings are worthless. I agree that the numbers matter.

But let us be honest: the numbers are being used selectively.

- Management speaks of “structural overcapacity” but has not demonstrated that closures are the only way to fix it.
- Investors demand a strong reset but ignore the execution damage of a closure battle in a co-determined company.
- The family holding wants value preserved but is prepared to threaten the workforce instead of designing a credible site-preserving solution.
- The state is right to oppose closures and forced layoffs, but it must stop speaking only in generalities.

## Where I disagree
I disagree with the idea that closures and compulsory redundancies should remain “available” as routine tools. That is not a neutral position; it is a threat.

And I disagree with the suggestion that a no-closure package is only credible if it accepts forced job cuts or a hollowing-out of job security. That crosses a red line.

### On management
You keep asking what minimum concessions labour would accept. The answer is simple:

- **No plant closures**
- **No compulsory redundancies**
- **No dismantling of co-determination**
- **No destruction of the historic job-security framework**

If your business case cannot be met without breaking those pillars, then your plan is not the only serious one — it is just the harshest one.

You have not yet proven, plant by plant, that a hard site-preserving package cannot work. Until you do, closure is not justified.

### On the expert’s argument
The expert has been helpful in one respect: they confirmed that **site-preserving restructuring can be economically credible** if it is hard enough.

That is the key point management keeps trying to bury. Credible alternatives exist. The issue is whether management is willing to take them seriously.

But I also say clearly: a “hard” site-preserving package does **not** mean accepting forced layoffs or closure blackmail. It means:
- capacity reductions through shifts, lines, and product allocation,
- voluntary exits and retirement pathways,
- working-time flexibility,
- productivity improvements,
- management and owner contributions,
- binding investment commitments.

That is the route we are willing to negotiate.

### On the union
I support the union’s position that labour can contribute, but not by sacrificing jobs and sites. A contribution is possible through:
- temporary wage moderation,
- adjustments in bonuses,
- working-time solutions,
- flexibility measures,
- and voluntary instruments.

What is not acceptable is turning “shared sacrifice” into a code word for compulsory redundancies or site closures.

### On the state government
You are right to reject closures and compulsory redundancies as primary tools. But your role cannot end with declarations.

If you really want a binding settlement, then support a package with enforceable protections:
- a **site and job guarantee through 2030**
- no closure without co-determination approval
- no forced layoffs
- binding investment and product commitments
- monitoring with joint oversight

If the state is serious, it should help lock that in politically and legally.

### On the controlling family and investors
You say you want a risk-adjusted NPV comparison. Fine. Then compare the real alternatives, not a closure fantasy against a straw man.

A proper comparison must include:
- strike risk,
- legal delay,
- loss of trust,
- supplier disruption,
- morale damage,
- and the long-term cost of destroying co-determination.

If you include those factors honestly, closures stop looking like the easy answer.

And let me be blunt: if owners and investors insist on “value protection,” then they must accept that **value is also destroyed by social conflict and broken trust**. That is not a labour slogan; it is an operating reality.

## What we are prepared to support
We are prepared to discuss a serious restructuring package that includes:
- real capacity reduction **without closures**
- voluntary departures and retirement schemes
- working-time changes
- productivity measures
- temporary labour-cost contributions
- binding investment in German plants
- joint oversight of implementation
- a site and job guarantee through 2030

That is a real package. It is not a veto on change.

## What we will not accept
We will not accept:
- closures as a bargaining tool,
- forced redundancies through the back door,
- “conditional guarantees” that are really just escape clauses,
- or a deal that shifts the adjustment burden almost entirely onto workers while preserving management’s freedom of action.

## The actual question
The moderator asks what evidence would move us. Here is the answer:

We would need a plant-by-plant business case that proves:
1. closures are necessary,
2. no combination of alternatives can deliver the savings,
3. the legal and industrial-relations risks are manageable,
4. and the social cost is justified.

That evidence has not been produced.

So until it is, our position remains unchanged: **no closures, no compulsory redundancies, no weakening of co-determination, and no surrender of job security.**

If management wants an agreement, it must come back with a package that respects those limits and still delivers real savings. If it cannot, then the conflict is of management’s making, not ours.
