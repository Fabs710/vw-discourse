## 1. Decision context

This is a co-determined restructuring decision in a large German carmaker facing structural overcapacity, weak margins, and high domestic costs. Because the supervisory board is split 50/50 and major site decisions require a two-thirds majority, the workforce side, the controlling family, and the state government each have effective blocking power. The practical choice is not simply “closure or no closure,” but whether a hard, durable cost reset can be achieved through negotiated site-preserving measures or whether closure must remain available as a fallback.

## 2. Stakeholder assembly

Weighted by standing, legitimacy, and blocking power:

## 3. Final positions

### Group & Brand Management
Management maintains that restructuring must be materially deep and that closure must remain available if negotiated measures do not close the savings gap. It is open to voluntary exits, working-time changes, productivity measures, and investment commitments, but it does not yet regard these as sufficient substitutes for structural capacity cuts.

### Works Council
The works council holds firm on no German plant closures, no compulsory redundancies, and no weakening of co-determination or historic job security. It is willing to support a hard restructuring, but only if it is site-preserving and backed by binding investment and a guarantee through 2030.

### Industrial Union
The union aligns closely with the works council on rejecting closures and forced layoffs. It is prepared to negotiate real employee concessions—wage moderation, bonus restraint, flexibility, voluntary exits, retirement pathways—so long as these remain within the red lines and are matched by management and owner contributions.

### Regional State Government
The state rejects closures and compulsory redundancies as primary tools, but also wants a credible economic reset. It supports a binding no-closure settlement with enforceable milestones, though it is not willing to endorse a purely declaratory political promise without economic substance.

### Controlling Family Holding
The family holding wants a hard, durable restructuring and is not committed to closures as the default. It will back the option that best protects long-term enterprise value, but only if a negotiated package is quantitatively credible and not merely a political compromise.

### Institutional Investors
Investors support a durable cost reset and lower fixed capacity burden, but insist on proof that any site-preserving package delivers sufficient recurring savings. They are not yet convinced that either closure or no-closure is superior, and they want a risk-adjusted comparison before backing a direction.

### Independent Expert
The expert concludes that a hard site-preserving package can be economically credible in principle, but only if it delivers durable recurring savings, real capacity reduction, and low execution risk. Closure can be stronger on paper but may lose its advantage once strike risk, legal delay, supplier disruption, morale damage, and transition costs are fully included.

## 4. Genuine conflicts

1. **Closure as a legitimate instrument**
   - Management, family holding, and investors want closure to remain available.
   - Works council, union, and state want closures off the table as a bargaining instrument.

2. **Compulsory redundancies**
   - Management side does not fully exclude them.
   - Labour and state reject them outright.

3. **Job/site guarantees vs adjustment flexibility**
   - Labour wants a binding guarantee through 2030.
   - Management and investors see unconditional guarantees as incompatible with future competitiveness.

4. **Baseline for comparison**
   - Labour compares against a no-closure package.
   - Management compares against having closure available.
   - Investors compare risk-adjusted NPV under execution uncertainty.

5. **Trust and enforceability**
   - Labour fears “conditional guarantees” as disguised closure.
   - Management fears a no-closure promise will lock in underperformance.

## 5. Areas of convergence

- All sides accept restructuring is necessary.
- All sides reject cosmetic or temporary fixes.
- All sides accept that the package must be hard, durable, and economically credible.
- All sides agree that employee contributions alone are insufficient; management/owners must also contribute.
- All sides accept that plant-by-plant evidence is needed rather than abstract claims.
- All sides recognize that industrial conflict would be costly and value-destructive.

## 6. Key trade-offs for the decision-maker

1. **Economic certainty vs industrial peace**
   - Closure may look cleaner structurally, but could trigger severe conflict and delay.
   - Site-preserving restructuring may preserve peace and know-how, but risks under-delivering on savings.

2. **Durability of savings vs social stability**
   - Hard capacity cuts and labor-cost moderation can create durable savings.
   - But excessive rigidity or closures may damage trust and future execution capacity.

3. **Flexibility vs credibility**
   - Management wants fallback options if targets are missed.
   - Labour wants no hidden escape hatches.

4. **Short-term cost reset vs long-term value**
   - A sharp shutdown may reduce cost faster.
   - A negotiated package may better preserve capability, supplier stability, and future EV execution.

5. **Governance legitimacy vs managerial freedom**
   - Co-determination and state influence are not procedural details; they are binding constraints.
   - Any viable solution must fit this governance reality.

## 7. Integrative pathways

### A. Binding site-preserving package with milestones
Implement a hard no-closure package now: voluntary exits, retirement pathways, capacity reductions, working-time changes, productivity measures, and temporary labour-cost moderation. Pair it with binding milestones, joint monitoring, and pre-defined review points tied to measurable targets.

### B. Hybrid restructuring by plant category
Classify plants as retain / reshape / review. Protect strategically important sites with investment and specialization, while allowing deeper rationalization at weaker sites through shifts, lines, and model consolidation before any closure decision.

### C. Verification-first settlement
Agree first on the savings target, data set, and risk-adjusted NPV methodology. Defer any final instrument choice until the independent expert produces a plant-by-plant comparison including transition cost, strike risk, legal delay, and supplier spillovers.

### D. Shared-sacrifice package
Create a package combining employee contributions with management and owner contributions, plus binding investment commitments and retraining/internal mobility support. This is the clearest route to making labor concessions politically and socially acceptable.

## 8. Open questions

- What exact recurring annual savings are required to restore competitiveness?
- How much of that gap can a site-preserving package close, plant by plant?
- Which sites are strategically retained, which are reshaped, and which are structurally redundant?
- What level of labor-cost moderation is acceptable and enforceable?
- What management/owner concessions are real enough to make burden-sharing credible?
- How much value do strike risk, legal delay, and supplier spillovers erase from closure economics?
- What kind of review trigger, if any, would be acceptable without becoming a disguised closure clause?
- What investment commitments are sufficiently binding to justify a site and job guarantee?

## 9. Recommended next step

Commission the independent expert to deliver a **plant-by-plant, risk-adjusted comparison table** of:
1. closure-based restructuring,
2. hard site-preserving restructuring,
3. hybrid restructuring.

The table should include recurring savings, transition costs, strike/legal risk, supplier spillovers, implementation timing, and NPV under realistic execution assumptions. This is the most defensible basis for a supervisory-board-level decision.

## 10. Emergent insights that only appeared through multi-turn interaction

1. **The real dispute narrowed over time**
   - It is no longer about whether restructuring is needed. The conflict is now about which instruments are legitimate and at what point.

2. **Closure is increasingly treated as leverage, not just a tool**
   - Labour reads closure availability as bargaining pressure; management reads no-closure as a way of locking in underperformance. This trust gap became clearer only after repeated rounds.

3. **“Hard” does not mean the same thing to all sides**
   - Labour defines hard restructuring as voluntary exits, working-time changes, and productivity gains.
   - Management defines hard restructuring as durable capacity and cost reduction, potentially including closure.
   - The expert clarified that hard must mean durable recurring savings plus real capacity rationalization.

4. **The expert made the comparison framework explicit**
   - The decisive metric is not nominal savings but risk-adjusted NPV, including conflict, delay, and spillover costs.

5. **The best compromise is likely hybrid, not binary**
   - The interaction moved from absolute closure/no-closure positions toward a more realistic hybrid or phased approach, especially plant-by-plant differentiation.

6. **Governance constraints are not secondary**
   - Co-determination, state influence, and family control are not backdrop conditions; they are decisive constraints on feasible restructuring design.

7. **A blanket 2030 guarantee is a central obstacle**
   - Labour sees it as essential security; management and investors see it as too rigid unless paired with milestones or review mechanisms.
