I will be equally direct.

## Where I agree
I agree that this cannot be a cosmetic restructuring.  
I agree that the company needs a durable cost reset.  
I agree that numbers matter, and that a package which only shifts the burden onto employees would fail.

I also agree with the expert’s basic conclusion: **a site-preserving solution can be economically credible if it is hard enough**. That is precisely why I have been willing to bargain. We are not defending the status quo.

## Where I disagree
I strongly disagree with the repeated suggestion that the only “serious” restructuring must keep **plant closures and compulsory redundancies** available as normal tools.

That is not neutral language. It is a threat. And it is a threat we reject.

I also disagree with the idea that labour is being asked only for “modest” contributions. In practice, what management is asking is:
- wage and bonus restraint,
- flexibility,
- workforce reduction,
- and open-ended insecurity,
while keeping the option to close plants if we do not surrender enough.

That is not shared sacrifice. That is burden shifting.

## To management
You keep saying the cost base is too high. Fine. Then prove, plant by plant, that the alternatives do not work.

But until you do, I reject your claim that closures are justified.  
You have not shown:
- the precise savings gap,
- the exact timeline,
- the required annual recurring savings,
- and why a hard package of capacity cuts, voluntary exits, working-time changes, productivity measures, and capital contributions cannot close it.

You keep speaking as if closure is the economically rational default. It is not. In this governance environment, with co-determination, political veto points, strike risk, and supplier spillovers, closure can be **value-destructive**, not value-creating.

## To the controlling family and investors
You say you want durability and value protection. Then listen carefully: **value is not protected by provoking an industrial war**.

A closure strategy may look clean in a spreadsheet, but if it leads to strikes, legal blockage, political resistance, morale collapse, and prolonged uncertainty, it destroys value. That is not a labour talking point; it is a business reality.

So if you want a risk-adjusted comparison, include:
- strike risk,
- legal delay,
- operational spillover,
- supplier disruption,
- and the long-term cost of breaking trust.

If you do that honestly, then “closures first” looks far less attractive.

## To the state government
Your position against closures and compulsory redundancies is correct, but it cannot remain only political rhetoric.

If the state wants a binding settlement, then it must support a framework that makes no-closure promises real:
- a site and job guarantee through 2030,
- no forced layoffs,
- no closure without co-determination approval,
- binding investment commitments,
- and joint monitoring of milestones.

If the state is serious, it should help lock this in through governance and political backing, not just statements.

## To the expert
Your assessment is useful because it confirms the key point: **site-preserving restructuring is possible, but only if it is hard-edged**.

That means:
- real capacity reduction,
- substantial voluntary departures and retirement pathways,
- working-time adjustment,
- productivity gains with enforcement,
- labor-cost moderation,
- and binding investment/product commitments.

That is the package we are prepared to negotiate.

What we are **not** prepared to accept is the conclusion that because the package must be hard, it must therefore include:
- plant closures,
- compulsory redundancies,
- or the dismantling of collective bargaining protections.

That leap is not justified.

## What the union is prepared to do
We have already shown openness to a real contribution. Let me restate the basis clearly:

We can discuss:
- temporary wage moderation,
- bonus restraint,
- working-time flexibility,
- voluntary exits,
- partial retirement,
- redeployment,
- productivity improvements,
- and capacity reduction **without closures**.

But the burden must be shared. The workforce will not pay for the entire restructuring while management and owners retain all strategic freedom.

## What we will not accept
We will not accept:
- closures as a bargaining club,
- compulsory redundancies,
- a hollowed-out job-security framework,
- or a “conditional guarantee” that is really just an escape hatch for later layoffs.

And I want to be explicit: if management insists that closures and forced layoffs remain on the table, then it is choosing confrontation, not settlement.

## The actual test
The moderator asked for a quantified answer. Here it is:

If management wants labour to concede more, then it must table a credible business case showing:
1. the annual recurring savings required,
2. the exact contribution expected from management and owners,
3. the expected workforce contribution,
4. and why those measures are insufficient without closures.

Until that is on the table, there is no basis for asking the workforce to surrender job and site security.

## Our line
So our position remains:

- **no compulsory redundancies**
- **no German plant closures**
- **no weakening of co-determination**
- **no dismantling of the collective wage framework**

But we are not walking away from restructuring.  
We are prepared to negotiate a **hard, shared-sacrifice package** that protects jobs and sites, reduces costs, and gives the company a durable future.

If management and owners want that outcome, they should stop leaning on closure threats and come back with real numbers and a fair burden-sharing plan.

If they do not, then they should not confuse our refusal to accept social dumping with a refusal to restructure.
