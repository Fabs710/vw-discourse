## First: where I agree
I agree with everyone on one fundamental point: **this company needs a durable restructuring, not a cosmetic deal**.  
I also agree that:
- long industrial conflict would destroy value,
- the comparison has to be risk-adjusted,
- and any solution must be capable of restoring margins, not just buying peace for a few quarters.

The expert is right on the key analytical point: **a site-preserving solution can be economically credible in principle**. I am not disputing that.

## Where I disagree — and strongly

### 1) “No closures” as a starting line is not a solution
The works council and union keep repeating that closures are off the table and treating that as if it were an economic answer. It is not.

If a site is structurally overcapacity, persistently too expensive, and misaligned with future production needs, then refusing to consider closure is **not** a restructuring plan. It is a veto on adjustment.

I am not saying closure is the first tool. I am saying it must remain **available** if the numbers require it. Without that, there is no credible leverage to force the scale of change this business needs.

### 2) Temporary or voluntary measures alone are not enough
I am prepared to discuss:
- voluntary exits,
- partial retirement,
- working-time changes,
- productivity measures,
- and investment commitments.

But let’s be honest: those measures alone are very unlikely to close a structural cost gap of this size in a shrinking European market.

What we need is a **permanent reset**:
- lower recurring labour cost,
- lower fixed capacity burden,
- and a materially improved return-on-sales path.

If the employee side is saying “no closures, no compulsory redundancies, no deep framework change,” then it is still not showing me where the durable savings come from.

### 3) An unconditional job and site guarantee through 2030 is not workable
I understand the desire for security. But an unconditional guarantee that removes all future adjustment capacity is incompatible with responsible management.

If market conditions deteriorate further, or if a site remains structurally uncompetitive, management must retain the ability to act. A guarantee with no review mechanism is not stability; it is paralysis.

## To the works council
You say the burden is on management to prove closure is necessary. Fair enough. But then the burden is also on you to show a **quantified alternative** that matches the economics.

So far I have heard principles, red lines, and slogans. I have **not** heard a plant-by-plant package that delivers:
- substantial recurring savings,
- real capacity reduction,
- enforceable labour-cost moderation,
- and a credible timeline.

If you want no closures, then table a package that actually closes the savings gap. Otherwise your position is not an alternative; it is a refusal to decide.

## To the union
You say you are ready for shared sacrifice. Then let’s define “shared.”

Shared sacrifice cannot mean:
- labour gives up wages, bonuses, flexibility, and headcount contribution,
- while capacity stays too high,
- the cost base remains uncompetitive,
- and management is left without the tools to finish the job.

If labour wants to be taken seriously, the contribution must be **material and durable**. Temporary moderation alone will not deliver the reset we need.

## To the state government
I respect your rejection of closures and compulsory redundancies as primary tools. But I need to be blunt: **the state cannot protect every site and still expect this company to become competitive by wishful thinking**.

If the company does not get its cost base down, the regional economy loses anyway — only later, and with less control. So if you want a real settlement, support a package that actually changes the economics:
- real capacity reduction,
- enforceable labour-cost contributions,
- and a binding framework that is consistent with competitiveness.

## To the controlling family and investors
I agree that a closure battle can destroy value if it triggers prolonged conflict. But fear of conflict cannot become an excuse for an inadequate deal.

If the final package does not materially improve competitiveness, it will fail your own value test later. That is why I will not settle for a soft compromise that looks cooperative but leaves the company structurally unfit.

## On the expert assessment
The expert confirmed exactly what I said from the beginning: **a site-preserving package is only credible if it is hard enough**.

That supports my position, not the claim that closures are irrelevant. If labour wants closures off the table, then it must produce an alternative with equivalent permanent savings, equivalent timing, and acceptable execution risk.

## What I am prepared to discuss
I remain open to a negotiated alternative **if** it meets the economics.

That means I can discuss:
- capacity reductions without immediate closures,
- voluntary exits and retirement pathways,
- working-time measures,
- labour-cost moderation,
- productivity measures,
- investment commitments,
- and phased implementation.

But I will not accept:
- a cosmetic compromise,
- a package that preserves too much capacity,
- a settlement that leaves recurring costs too high,
- or a deal that rules out the instruments needed to finish the restructuring.

## What would move me
What would move management is what I said at the start: a credible, durable package of cost and capacity savings, with a meaningful labour-cost contribution, that restores medium-term margins.

If the employee side can table a **quantified** proposal that delivers:
1. substantial recurring savings,
2. real capacity reduction,
3. meaningful labour contribution,
4. and enforceable implementation,
then I will engage seriously.

If not, then “no closures forever” is not a serious answer.

I am not retreating from the need for structural change.

- **No cosmetic deal**
- **No package that leaves the cost base uncompetitive**
- **No unconditional guarantee that blocks necessary adjustment**
- **No exclusion of closures or workforce reductions as instruments if they become necessary**

I would prefer a negotiated settlement. But I will not trade away the company’s 2030 competitiveness for a politically easier story.

If the other side wants agreement, it has to meet the economics — not just set red lines and call that a solution.
