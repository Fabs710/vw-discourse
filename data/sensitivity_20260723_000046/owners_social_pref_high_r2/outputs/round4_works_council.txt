We are now at the point where everyone must stop hiding behind abstractions.

## My position remains unchanged
- **No German plant closures**
- **No compulsory redundancies**
- **No weakening of co-determination**
- **No dismantling of the historic job-security framework**

That is not a bargaining posture. It is the minimum basis for any serious settlement.

## Where I agree
I agree with management, investors, the family holding, and the expert on one thing: this company needs a **hard, durable restructuring**.  
I also agree that cosmetic savings are useless, and that a deal which merely postpones the crisis would fail.

I further agree with the expert’s core conclusion: **a site-preserving restructuring can be economically credible if it is hard enough**. That matters, because it destroys management’s repeated claim that closures are somehow the only serious option.

## Where I disagree
### With management
You keep saying closures must remain “available.” That is not neutral. It is leverage against the workforce.

The burden is on management to prove, **plant by plant**, that:
1. a hard site-preserving package cannot deliver the required recurring savings,
2. closures are truly superior after transition, legal, industrial-relations, and execution costs,
3. and the same cannot be achieved through capacity cuts, voluntary exits, working-time changes, product allocation, and productivity measures.

You have not met that burden.

And let me be blunt: in a co-determined company, a closure battle is not a clean economic tool. It is a value-destroying conflict risk. If you push it, you may damage the company more than you repair it.

### With investors and the family holding
You speak as if a spreadsheet is enough. It is not.

Any serious comparison must include:
- strike risk,
- legal delay,
- supplier disruption,
- morale damage,
- loss of trust,
- management distraction,
- and the long-term cost of breaking the social contract.

If you include those effects honestly, closure stops looking like the “disciplined” option and starts looking like an escalation with serious downside.

### With the state government
I support your rejection of closures and forced layoffs as primary tools. But declarations are not enough.

If you really want a binding settlement, then you must be prepared to back a **legally and governance-based no-closure framework**:
- site and job guarantee through 2030,
- no forced layoffs,
- no closure without co-determination approval,
- binding investment commitments,
- and joint oversight with real enforcement.

### With the expert
The expert has been useful, because they confirmed the central point: **site-preserving restructuring is possible in principle**.

But I reject any implication that “hard” automatically means “harsher on workers.”  
Hard means:
- capacity reduction through shifts and lines,
- voluntary exits,
- retirement pathways,
- working-time flexibility,
- productivity measures,
- management and owner contributions,
- and investment commitments.

It does **not** mean closure blackmail or compulsory redundancies.

## What a real no-closure package must include
If management wants an agreement, then it must put on the table a package with all of the following:
- real capacity reduction,
- measurable recurring savings,
- voluntary departures and retirement schemes,
- working-time adjustments,
- productivity commitments,
- management and owner contributions,
- binding investment in German plants,
- and a site and job guarantee through 2030.

That is the only route I will even discuss.

## What I will not accept
I will not accept:
- closures as a bargaining club,
- compulsory redundancies through the back door,
- “conditional guarantees” with hidden escape hatches,
- or a deal that shifts the adjustment burden onto workers while preserving management’s freedom to destroy sites later.

## On the moderator’s compromise ideas
### 1) “No closure unless a jointly verified trigger is met”
No.  
That is just a delayed closure clause. We are not signing away plants now in exchange for a promise that someone else will decide later when to shut them.

### 2) “Binding no-closure package in exchange for deeper concessions”
This is the only direction that even comes close to seriousness — but only if the concessions are real and balanced.

If labour is asked to contribute, then management and owners must contribute too. No one gets a free ride. But any contribution from labour must stay within the red lines:
- no forced redundancies,
- no closures,
- no erosion of co-determination,
- no destruction of job security.

### 3) “Hybrid structure with a narrow fallback list”
Also unacceptable.  
A fallback list is just a closure list in disguise. We know how this game works.

## What I would actually support
I am prepared to support a **hard site-preserving restructuring** with:
- voluntary exits,
- partial retirement,
- working-time changes,
- temporary labour-cost moderation,
- product and investment commitments,
- joint oversight,
- and enforceable site protection through 2030.

That is a real restructuring. It is not resistance to change.

2. a plant-by-plant comparison of site-preserving alternatives,
3. the cost of closures including conflict and delay,
4. and a binding legal/governance route for no closures and no forced layoffs.
