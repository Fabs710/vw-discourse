## Independent assessment: site-preserving restructuring vs. closure-based restructuring

I can assess the economics and implementation risk of both routes. I cannot make legal or political judgments about veto rights or statutory authority beyond noting how those affect execution risk.

### Bottom line
A **hard site-preserving package can be economically credible in principle**, but only if it delivers:

- **material recurring annual savings**, not just one-off cost relief,
- **real capacity reduction**, not merely headcount trimming,
- **durable labour-cost moderation**,
- **credible voluntary-exit and redeployment volume**,
- and **binding implementation mechanisms**.

By contrast, a **closure-based route** can create larger nominal savings on paper, but in this governance setting it also tends to carry:

- high **strike/industrial-action risk**,
- **legal delay**,
- supplier and logistics disruption,
- management distraction,
- morale and retention damage,
- and often substantial **transition costs**.

So closure is not automatically superior on a risk-adjusted NPV basis. In this type of German co-determined group, it becomes superior only if the structural savings gap is large enough that it still wins **after** those costs and risks are netted out.

## How I would compare the options

### 1) Site-preserving package
A credible package would usually need several elements together:

- voluntary exits / early retirement,
- working-time changes,
- line and shift reductions,
- model consolidation,
- productivity improvements,
- temporary wage or bonus moderation,
- redeployment and training,
- investment commitments tied to capacity specialization.

#### Economic strengths
- lower immediate conflict cost than closures, if negotiated well;
- less risk of operational disruption;
- better retention of tacit knowledge;
- lower political and reputational backlash;
- better odds of preserving supplier stability and productivity.

#### Economic weaknesses
- savings may be slower to realize;
- can be diluted by exemptions and guarantees;
- may not fully remove structural overcapacity;
- if guarantees are too rigid, the company can lose future flexibility;
- savings may rely on recurring concessions that need periodic renewal.

---

### 2) Closure-based restructuring
This usually means plant shutdowns, major workforce reductions, and capacity withdrawal.

#### Economic strengths
- potentially faster and deeper permanent capacity removal;
- clearer fixed-cost reduction if execution is clean;
- can solve a structural overhang that softer measures cannot fully fix.

#### Economic weaknesses
- high transition and shutdown costs;
- likely industrial conflict in a co-determined system;
- risk of legal delay and process friction;
- loss of know-how and operational continuity;
- supplier spillover effects;
- execution risk can erode much of the nominal savings.

---

## What threshold would make a site-preserving package economically equivalent?

There is no single universal number, but in practice a site-preserving package becomes competitive with closure when it achieves most of the following:

1. **Recurring annual savings close to the structural gap**
   - not just a temporary bridge;
   - ideally savings that are locked in for several years.

2. **Material capacity removal**
   - if the plant network is overbuilt, headcount cuts alone are usually insufficient;
   - at least some permanent shift, line, or model rationalization is needed.

3. **High voluntary exit volume**
   - enough to reduce payroll materially without forced redundancies;
   - if voluntary uptake is weak, the package often under-delivers.

4. **Low escalation risk**
   - if the negotiated package avoids strikes and lengthy disputes, its NPV improves sharply;
   - if conflict risk is high, the economic advantage narrows.

5. **Limited guarantee drag**
   - a site guarantee through 2030 can be valuable for industrial peace, but if it prevents later adjustment regardless of market changes, it has a real option cost.

### Practical rule of thumb
A site-preserving package is usually only economically comparable to closure if it delivers:

- **most of the recurring savings required**,
- with **clearly lower execution risk** than closure,
- and with **capacity reduction substantial enough** that the company does not simply defer the problem.

If it saves only on labour cost while leaving excess industrial capacity intact, it is usually not equivalent.

---

## Risk-adjusted NPV logic

For investors, the comparison should be:

**Risk-adjusted NPV = operational savings − transition costs − conflict costs − delay costs − residual underperformance risk**

### Site-preserving package
Often wins if:
- labour contribution is substantial,
- implementation is fast,
- industrial peace is preserved,
- and the package materially lowers fixed cost.

### Closure-based package
Only wins if:
- the permanent savings are much larger,
- and the conflict/legal/supply-chain costs are contained.

In this specific type of governance environment, closure often looks stronger in a simplified spreadsheet and weaker once execution risk is included.

---

## Short-term cash vs. long-term value

### Short-term cash
Closure can sometimes improve near-term cash flow after the first one-off charges are absorbed, but only if:
- redundancy and shutdown costs are manageable,
- disputes do not freeze implementation,
- and supply or output losses are limited.

A negotiated site-preserving package may improve cash more gradually, but with less disruption.

### Long-term value
Long-term value depends on:
- whether the company really restores margin,
- whether it retains productivity and know-how,
- whether it protects future investment capacity,
- and whether industrial relations remain stable enough to execute the EV transition.

In many German industrial settings, a badly executed closure can destroy long-term value even if it looks cheaper in isolation.
