I will answer directly.

## To management
You are still trying to make the case that closure must remain “available” as a normal lever. I reject that framing.

What you have **not** shown is the plant-by-plant proof that a hard negotiated package cannot deliver the required savings. Until you do, closure is not a necessity — it is a threat. And in this governance setting, that threat carries real industrial-relations and execution costs that you keep treating as secondary.

I agree with you on one point: this must be a **hard** restructuring, not a cosmetic one.  
But “hard” does **not** mean plant closures and compulsory redundancies. It means:
- real capacity reduction,
- working-time flexibility,
- voluntary exits and retirement pathways,
- productivity improvements,
- temporary labour-cost moderation,
- and management/owner contributions.

If you want labour to contribute, then management and owners must also contribute in a visible, enforceable way. Otherwise this is not shared sacrifice; it is burden shifting.

## To the works council
I support your insistence that the company needs a durable, site-preserving settlement. On the essentials, we are aligned:
- no compulsory redundancies,
- no plant closures,
- no erosion of collective bargaining,
- no destruction of job security.

I also agree that a hard site-preserving package can be economically credible. That is exactly why we have been willing to bargain.

Where I would sharpen your message is this: we should not just say “no.” We should be ready to say **what we can do**:
- temporary wage moderation,
- bonus restraint,
- voluntary departures,
- partial retirement,
- working-time adjustments,
- redeployment,
- and productivity commitments.

That is the basis for a real deal. But I will not accept any “conditional guarantee” that is just a hidden closure clause.

## To the state government
Your position against closures and forced layoffs is correct. But you cannot remain at the level of political declarations.

If the state wants a binding settlement, it should support a framework with:
- a site and job guarantee through 2030,
- no forced layoffs,
- no closure without co-determination approval,
- binding investment commitments,
- and joint monitoring.

That is not a slogan; it is how you make a no-closure settlement credible.

## To the controlling family
You are right that the package must be economically credible and durable. But I do not accept the logic that this automatically keeps closures on the table.

A closure battle in this company is not a clean value-maximizing move. It risks:
- strikes,
- legal delay,
- supplier disruption,
- morale collapse,
- and long-term trust damage.

If you care about long-term value, then you should seriously test the negotiated alternative on its own merits, not use closure as the default comparison point.

## To investors
I understand your demand for recurring savings and durable margin improvement. That is legitimate.

But your comparison must include the real costs of closure in this environment:
- industrial conflict,
- legal delay,
- operational spillovers,
- and the cost of destroying trust in a co-determined system.

If you include those factors honestly, the no-closure package is not a sentimental option; it may well be the better risk-adjusted one.

## On the expert assessment
The expert has confirmed the key point: a site-preserving solution can be economically credible **if it is hard enough**. That is important, because it validates the direction we are pushing.

But I want to be equally clear: “hard enough” does **not** mean accepting closure blackmail or compulsory redundancies. It means a real package with recurring savings and enforceable commitments.

## What the union is prepared to discuss
We are prepared to negotiate a serious package including:
- temporary wage moderation,
- bonus restraint,
- working-time flexibility,
- voluntary exits,
- partial retirement,
- capacity reduction through shifts and lines,
- productivity measures,
- and binding investment/product commitments.

We are **not** prepared to negotiate:
- plant closures,
- forced layoffs,
- a hollowed-out job-security framework,
- or a deal that shifts the burden almost entirely onto workers.

## What would move us
What would move us is not more pressure. It is a concrete package showing:
1. the annual recurring savings required,
2. the exact labour contribution expected,
3. the contribution from management and owners,
4. and how the target is met **without** closures and compulsory redundancies.

Until that is on the table, the union’s position remains unchanged.

## Bottom line
- We agree restructuring is necessary.
- We agree it must be hard.
- We agree labour must contribute.
- We disagree that closures and compulsory redundancies must remain routine tools.
- We reject any settlement that makes workers absorb the pain while management preserves all strategic freedom.

If management wants agreement, it should stop talking in threats and start tabling the numbers for a serious, site-preserving settlement. If it cannot, then it should not pretend that closure is the only responsible option.
