## Independent assessment

The expert’s core conclusion is the right one: **a hard site-preserving package can be economically credible, but only if it delivers durable recurring savings plus real capacity rationalization**. In this governance setting, **closure is not automatically the higher-NPV option** once transition costs, industrial conflict, legal delay, and spillovers are fully loaded.

### My answer to the central question
A site-preserving package becomes economically equivalent to closure only when it achieves, plant by plant:

1. **Most of the required recurring annual savings**  
   not just a temporary wage concession or one-off headcount reduction.

2. **Meaningful permanent capacity reduction**  
   typically through shift cuts, line consolidation, model concentration, or footprint specialization.

3. **High voluntary exit volume**  
   enough to materially reduce payroll without forcing redundancies.

4. **Durable labour-cost moderation**  
   with a credible time horizon and enforcement mechanism.

5. **Low execution risk**  
   especially low strike risk, low legal delay, and limited supplier disruption.

If those conditions are not met, then a site-preserving deal is usually **politically attractive but economically underpowered**.

---

## Practical threshold view

I would use this rule of thumb:

- If a negotiated package can close **roughly the full structural savings gap** while preserving operational continuity, it is likely superior on a risk-adjusted basis.
- If it closes only the **first half** of the gap and leaves excess capacity in place, it is usually inferior to a deeper restructuring.
- If closure triggers a major conflict, it may erase much of the apparent savings advantage.

So the comparison is not “closure vs. no closure.” It is:

> **Which package produces the best risk-adjusted permanent cost reset after transition, conflict, and execution costs?**

---

## Plant-by-plant implications

Without site data I cannot give a definitive plant ranking, but economically the plants would fall into three broad categories:

### 1) Strategically retained plants
These are sites with:
- modern assets,
- good logistics,
- future product allocation,
- and relatively low unit cost after reconfiguration.

For these, a **site-preserving package** is often superior because closure destroys capability and creates avoidable transition risk.

### 2) Marginal or redundant plants
These are sites with:
- chronic underutilization,
- weak product outlook,
- high fixed cost,
- and limited specialization potential.

For these, **closure or major contraction** can become economically stronger if the savings gap is large enough and the conflict can be contained. But in a co-determined German setting, the execution discount is usually large.

### 3) Transitional plants
These are sites that may be expensive now but could be made viable through:
- product reassignment,
- line simplification,
- working-time changes,
- and moderate headcount reduction.

For these, a **hybrid package** is usually best: preserve the site, but rationalize capacity hard.

---

## Relative assessment of the options

### 1) Closure-based restructuring
**Best when:**
- one or more plants are structurally obsolete,
- the savings gap is very large,
- and management can absorb conflict and transition cost.

**Risk:**
- high strike exposure,
- legal delay,
- supplier and morale damage,
- and destruction of tacit knowledge.

**Conclusion:**  
Economically strong in theory, but in this environment often weakened sharply by execution risk.

---

### 2) Hard site-preserving restructuring
**Best when:**
- there is still a viable future product base,
- the group can reduce capacity without full shutdown,
- and labour is willing to take real concessions.

**Risk:**
- may under-deliver if concessions are too soft,
- may preserve too much fixed cost,
- may require periodic renegotiation.

**Conclusion:**  
Often the best risk-adjusted option if the package is genuinely hard and enforceable.

---

### 3) Hybrid restructuring
**Best when:**
- the network is mixed,
- some plants are viable, others are not,
- and management wants structural change without a generalized industrial war.

**Risk:**
- can become politically messy,
- may be seen by labour as a disguised closure path,
- can produce partial, not full, savings.

**Conclusion:**  
Usually the most realistic compromise, and often the best balance of economics and feasibility.

---

## What would make me prefer closure over site preservation?
I would only favor closure if the evidence shows all of the following:

- the site is structurally uncompetitive after reconfiguration,
- the recurring savings from closure materially exceed the hard site-preserving alternative,
- voluntary and productivity measures cannot close the gap,
- and the expected conflict/legal/supply-chain cost does not erase the savings.

Absent that proof, closure is not automatically the better financial choice.

---

## What evidence is still needed
A robust judgment requires plant-level data on:

- utilization and fixed-cost absorption,
- unit labour cost by site,
- product allocation and future model pipeline,
- severance and shutdown costs,
- redeployment capacity,
- strike probability and likely duration,
- legal timetable and co-determination constraints,
- supplier dependence,
- and the cash profile of any guarantee or moderation package.

Without that, the decision remains directionally assessable but not conclusively decidable.

---

## Bottom line
My independent view is:

- **A hard negotiated site-preserving package is economically plausible and may well be superior on a risk-adjusted basis.**
- **Closure becomes superior only if the structural overcapacity is severe enough that negotiated measures cannot close the gap, even after accounting for conflict and transition costs.**
- **The most likely economically rational outcome is a hybrid: preserve strategically important sites, rationalize weaker ones, and secure real recurring savings through a binding negotiated package.**
