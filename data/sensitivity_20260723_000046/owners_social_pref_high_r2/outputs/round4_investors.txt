We are aligned on the destination, but not yet on the confidence level that the current proposals can get the company there.

## Where we agree
We agree with management, the family holding, the state, and the expert on the key point: **this cannot be a cosmetic restructuring**. The company needs:
- a durable cost reset,
- lower fixed capacity burden,
- recurring rather than one-off savings,
- and a credible path back to competitive margins.

We also agree that:
- prolonged conflict destroys value,
- execution risk matters,
- and a site-preserving solution is only acceptable if it is hard enough to actually fix the economics.

## Where we disagree

### 1) “No closures” is not, by itself, a financial solution
To the works council and union: we understand the social red lines, but from our perspective **a blanket no-closure stance is not enough** unless you can show — in numbers — that the same recurring savings will be delivered.

At the moment, we hear:
- voluntary exits,
- retirement pathways,
- working-time measures,
- productivity commitments,
- investment promises.

That may help, but it is not yet proven that it closes the structural gap in a shrinking market. If the package preserves too much capacity or leaves the cost base too high, then it is not a compromise we can support.

### 2) Management still needs to quantify the savings gap clearly
To management: you are right that the company needs hard action. But “closures must remain available” is still a bargaining position, not a quantified plan.

What we need to see is:
- the exact recurring savings required,
- by when they must be realized,
- what portion can be achieved without closures,
- and what execution assumptions sit behind the numbers.

Without that, closure remains a threat, not a demonstrated necessity.

### 3) Unconditional guarantees are not acceptable
To the state and labour side: we understand the desire for a site and job guarantee, but **an unconditional guarantee through 2030 is too rigid** if it removes all adjustment capacity regardless of market conditions.

For capital markets, that is not prudence; it is a potential lock-in of future underperformance. If guarantees are part of the deal, they must be:
- tied to measurable milestones,
- backed by real savings,
- and reviewable if performance deteriorates.

## Where the expert has helped
The expert’s contribution is valuable because it clarifies the real issue: **a site-preserving package can be economically credible in principle, but only if it is sufficiently hard and durable**.

That is the right test.

But it also means we cannot accept a package simply because it is politically easier or socially preferable. If it does not deliver:
- material recurring savings,
- real capacity reduction,
- and a credible implementation path,
then it is not economically equivalent to closure.

## What would move us
We would support a negotiated, site-preserving solution if it demonstrably delivers:
- permanent annual savings that close the margin gap,
- real capacity reduction, not just headcount trimming,
- substantial workforce contribution through voluntary exits and flexibility,
- durable labour-cost moderation,
- enforceable productivity improvements,
- and binding investment commitments.

That is the minimum for a risk-adjusted value case.

## What we will not support
We will not support:
- a cosmetic settlement,
- a package that keeps excess capacity in place,
- a deal that leaves margins structurally impaired,
- or a prolonged industrial conflict that burns cash and confidence.

And we also will not support:
- a closure-based strategy if it is not clearly superior once transition costs, legal delay, strike risk, supplier disruption, and management distraction are included.

## Our position
So our position remains:

- **Yes** to hard restructuring.
- **Yes** to a negotiated site-preserving package if it is economically equivalent.
- **No** to vague promises.
- **No** to unconditional guarantees that block necessary adjustment.
- **No** to any outcome that leaves the cost base uncompetitive.

## What we need next
Before we move further, we need the quantified comparison the moderator has been circling around:

1) closure-based restructuring,

That is the only basis on which we will back one route over another.

If the numbers show that a hard negotiated package truly restores competitiveness, we are open to it. If not, then the company must retain stronger tools — but those tools also have to clear a value test, not just a leverage test.
