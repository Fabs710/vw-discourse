My assessment, within restructuring economics and industrial relations, is:

## Bottom line

A **site-preserving restructuring can be economically credible**, but only if it is a **hard cost-reset**, not a symbolic compromise. In a co-determined German automotive group with multiple veto points, **closures are not automatically the superior option** once transition costs, legal/political delay, and strike risk are included. However, **a no-closure solution without meaningful workforce reduction, labor-cost moderation, and capacity rationalization is usually not credible** if the company truly has structural overcapacity and a materially above-target cost base.

So the real comparison is not:
- **closure vs. no closure**, but
- **closure-based savings vs. a negotiated package that removes enough fixed cost, enough fast enough, with lower execution risk**.

## What is economically true here

### 1) Closure-based restructuring
**Pros**
- largest and most permanent fixed-cost reduction on paper
- clearer capacity correction
- simpler end-state once implemented

**Cons**
- large one-off cash cost: severance, decommissioning, retooling, remediation, write-downs
- high industrial-relations conflict risk
- litigation and delay risk in a co-determined, politically sensitive setting
- supply-chain and morale spillovers
- execution can take years, during which savings are delayed

In this governance environment, a closure decision can be **economically attractive in theory but value-destructive in practice** if it triggers prolonged blockage or widespread disruption.

### 2) Site-preserving restructuring
**Pros**
- much lower transition shock
- better chance of preserving labor peace
- less risk of legal/political stalemate
- preserves know-how, supplier stability, and optionality if the market recovers

**Cons**
- savings are often smaller, slower, and more fragile
- voluntary exits alone rarely remove enough cost
- capacity remains too high unless plants are materially reconfigured
- if poorly designed, it becomes a delay mechanism rather than a restructuring

A site-preserving package is credible only if it includes **multiple hard levers at once**.

## What a credible site-preserving package would need

To be economically comparable to closure, it would usually need most of the following:

1. **Material headcount reduction**
   - mostly through voluntary exits, retirement pathways, redeployment
   - enough to cut not only direct labor but also supervision and overhead

2. **Real capacity reduction**
   - fewer shifts, line consolidation, model/variant simplification
   - otherwise unit costs stay too high even if the site remains open

3. **Labor-cost moderation**
   - wage moderation, lower bonuses, reduced supplements, or temporary concessions
   - time-limited is acceptable only if the savings are durable enough

4. **Productivity commitments with enforcement**
   - staffing ratios, work rules, attendance flexibility, and complexity reduction
   - without this, savings leak away

5. **Binding investment and product allocation**
   - to make the compromise durable and support future volumes

6. **Milestones and consequences**
   - so the package is not merely aspirational

If these are absent, the package is unlikely to close a structural competitiveness gap.

## Short-term cash vs long-term economics

This distinction matters a lot.

- **Closure** usually worsens near-term cash because of severance, write-downs, decommissioning, and legal/industrial-action costs.
- A **negotiated package** often looks better on near-term cash because it spreads cost and avoids confrontation.

But long-term value depends on whether the final cost base is actually fixed lower enough.  
A site-preserving deal is better value **if** it genuinely eliminates enough recurring cost.  
If not, it simply postpones the problem.

## Plant-by-plant logic

I cannot responsibly rank plants without site-specific data, but the decision rule is straightforward:

A plant is a stronger closure candidate if it has:
- persistent underutilization,
- high logistics complexity,
- weak model allocation,
- high conversion cost relative to alternatives,
- and limited strategic role in future platform or EV production.

A plant is a stronger site-preservation candidate if it has:
- strategic product importance,
- high switching cost,
- strong local know-how,
- network dependence with nearby operations,
- and a plausible path to specialization and capacity reduction without shutdown.

This has to be tested plant by plant with actual numbers.

## Legal and industrial-relations risk

In this German setting, legal and governance risk is not a side issue; it is central.

- Co-determination and board-level vetoes materially increase the execution risk of closures.
- Industrial action risk rises sharply if closures or forced layoffs are pursued aggressively.
- Political resistance, especially where a state shareholder is involved, can delay or dilute implementation.

That means the **expected value** of closure is not just the accounting saving minus closure cost; it is the saving adjusted for delay probability, conflict probability, and partial-execution risk.

## Regional spillovers

Closure has substantial local multiplier effects:
- direct job losses
- supplier losses
- lower household spending
- municipal revenue pressure
- skill drain and reputational damage

A site-preserving package reduces those spillovers, which matters both politically and operationally. But this social benefit is only meaningful if the company remains viable afterward.

## My judgment on the positions in the room

- **Management** is right that the restructuring must be structural and measurable.
- **The works council and union** are right that closures are not automatically the best economic solution and that a serious alternative must be tested.
- **The state government** is right to prioritize industrial stability, but it cannot replace economics with veto politics.
- **Owners and investors** are right to demand a durable cost reset, but must recognize that an aggressively closure-led strategy may destroy value if execution is blocked.

## Final conclusion

The economically defensible position is:

> **Test a hard site-preserving package first, but only if it is capable of delivering a real permanent cost reset comparable to closure once transition risk is included.**

If the company cannot show that the negotiated package:
- materially reduces recurring fixed cost,
- reduces capacity enough,
- and does so on a credible timeline,

then closure remains the stronger restructuring option despite its social and industrial-relations costs.

## What evidence would be needed for a robust judgment

To decide properly, I would need for each plant:
- current and projected volume/utilization
- fixed and variable cost breakdown
- headcount by function and shift
- logistics and platform dependencies
- closure transition cost estimates
- legal/industrial-action risk assessment
- redeployment potential
- local supplier exposure
- forecast savings under each scenario

Without that, the right conclusion is not “closure” or “no closure,” but **the site-preserving alternative is plausible only if it is quantitatively tough enough**.
