## Where I agree

I agree with management, the state, and the expert on one core point: **this cannot be a cosmetic restructuring**.  
The company needs a durable reset in cost, capacity, and competitiveness. If we fail to do that, we will keep eroding margins and long-term enterprise value. That is unacceptable.

I also agree that:
- a prolonged conflict would destroy value,
- temporary measures alone are not enough,
- and any settlement must be economically credible and implementable under this governance structure.

So the problem is not whether restructuring is needed. It is.

## Where I disagree

### 1) A blanket “no closures, no compulsory redundancies” line is not yet a complete solution
I understand the workforce’s red lines, but I do **not** accept that simply declaring them makes the restructuring plan credible.

If the company has structural overcapacity and a materially above-target cost base, then someone has to explain how the gap is actually closed.  
So far, I have heard a strong political position, but not yet a fully quantified economic answer.

### 2) But closures are not the default answer either
I also do **not** accept management’s implied logic that closures should be the natural starting point.

In this company’s governance environment, a closure battle can easily become a value-destroying stalemate:
- strikes,
- legal delays,
- supervisory-board deadlock,
- political resistance,
- supplier disruption,
- and reputational damage.

That is not stewardship. For a controlling owner with a long horizon, a closure that destroys more value through execution than it saves in theory is the wrong outcome.

### 3) An unconditional site and job guarantee through 2030 is too rigid
I cannot support a guarantee that removes all future adjustment capacity regardless of market conditions.

A guarantee can be part of a serious settlement, but only if it is:
- tied to measurable economic milestones,
- linked to investment and product commitments,
- and accompanied by review points.

An absolute guarantee with no escape mechanism would just transfer all operational risk onto the enterprise and owners, which I will not endorse.

## What the expert assessment means to me

The expert has made the key point correctly:  
**a site-preserving solution can be credible, but only if it is hard enough to deliver a real permanent cost reset.**

That means I am not looking for slogans from either side. I am looking for numbers.

If labor says no closures, then I want to see:
- substantial voluntary exits,
- meaningful capacity reduction,
- real labor-cost moderation,
- enforceable productivity gains,
- and binding investment commitments.

If management says closures are necessary, then I want to see:
- plant-specific economics,
- transition costs,
- strike and legal risk,
- and the net present value under realistic execution assumptions.

Without that, neither side has proven its case.

- capacity reduction,
- productivity improvements,

So I support your rejection of closures as a first reflex, but not a refusal to confront structural imbalance. A site-preserving deal is acceptable only if it is strong enough to actually reset the economics.
