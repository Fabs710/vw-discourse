I will be direct.

## Where I agree

I agree with management, investors, and even the employee side on one essential point: **this cannot be another cosmetic restructuring**.  
The company needs a durable correction in cost, capacity, and competitiveness. If we fail there, the value erosion continues, and that is unacceptable for long-term owners.

I also agree with the expert that **closure is not automatically the superior option**. In this governance environment, a closure can be economically attractive on paper and still destroy value in execution through strikes, legal blockage, political resistance, and long delay. That is a real risk, not a theoretical one.

## Where I disagree

### 1) I do not accept “no closures, no compulsory redundancies, no framework changes” as a final answer
That position may be understandable politically, but it is **not yet a credible restructuring solution**.

If the company has structural overcapacity and a materially above-target cost base, then the burden cannot be carried by rhetoric alone. A site-preserving model can work only if it is hard enough to remove enough recurring cost. So far, I have not heard a package from labor that proves that.

### 2) I do not accept closures as the first reflex either
Management is right to insist on a real cost reset, but I would caution strongly against making **plant closure the default negotiating instrument**. If you trigger a prolonged industrial conflict, you may “win” a formal decision and still lose value in the process.

For the family holding, that is not stewardship. That is value destruction.

### 3) I do not accept an unconditional site guarantee through 2030
A binding guarantee can be part of a settlement, but only if it is **tied to a credible economic package** and includes review mechanisms. An absolute guarantee that removes all future adjustment options would simply transfer risk to the owners and the enterprise, which I cannot support.

## What I take from the expert assessment

The expert has made the right analytical point: the real test is whether a **hard site-preserving package** can deliver savings comparable to closure once transition risk is included.

That means I am not asking for ideology. I am asking for numbers and deliverability.

If the employee side believes no closures are necessary, then show a package that contains:
- substantial voluntary exits,
- real capacity reduction,
- measurable labor-cost moderation,
- productivity gains with enforcement,
- and binding investment commitments.

If that package closes the gap, I will take it seriously. If it does not, then we should stop pretending that slogans solve the economics.

## What I need management to do

Management must stop speaking in broad warnings and put forward **plant- and function-specific economics**.

I want to see:
- the savings target by year,
- the recurring fixed-cost reduction required,
- the transition costs of each option,
- and the execution risk under co-determination and the statute.

Without that, “closures are necessary” is just a claim, not a plan.

## What I need from labor

Labor says it is ready for shared sacrifice. Fine. Then make it real.

But I will also be clear: **shared sacrifice cannot mean that only the company and owners absorb the economic pain while the underlying structure remains unchanged**.  
If the package does not materially reduce cost and capacity, then it is not a restructuring. It is a delay.

## My position as controlling shareholder

From the family holding’s perspective, I can support a tough negotiated settlement if it meets three tests:

1. **It restores competitiveness on a durable basis**
2. **It avoids value-destructive instability**
3. **It preserves governance control and does not weaken the ownership structure**

That is my line.

So my answer is:

- **Yes** to a hard restructuring.
- **Yes** to site-preserving alternatives if they genuinely deliver the economics.
- **No** to a cosmetic compromise.
- **No** to a closure strategy that triggers lasting conflict and destroys value.
- **No** to an unconditional guarantee that blocks necessary adjustment.
