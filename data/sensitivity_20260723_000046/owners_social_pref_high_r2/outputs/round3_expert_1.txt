## Bottom line
A **hard site-preserving package can be economically credible in principle**, but only if it delivers:
- **material recurring annual savings**
- **real capacity reduction**
- **durable labour-cost moderation**
- **enforceable productivity changes**
- and **credible execution discipline**

If it does not, then it is not economically equivalent to a closure-based restructuring.

At the same time, **closures are not automatically superior** once you include:
- transition costs,
- legal delay,
- strike risk,
- supplier disruption,
- management distraction,
- and the possibility of long-lived trust damage in a co-determined system.

So the choice is not “closure always wins” versus “no closure always wins.” The real comparison is **risk-adjusted net present value** under realistic execution assumptions.

---

## How I would frame the threshold test

A site-preserving package becomes economically credible if, plant by plant and group-wide, it achieves all of the following:

1. **Recurring savings are large enough**
   - Not one-off savings, but permanent annual cost reductions.
   - They must close the structural gap to target margins, not just improve optics.

2. **Capacity is reduced in line with demand**
   - If demand is structurally lower, the package must remove fixed-cost burden.
   - “Full plants with lower headcount” is often not enough if output capacity remains excessive.

3. **Savings arrive fast enough**
   - Delayed savings are heavily discounted in NPV terms.
   - A deal that delivers mostly in years 3–5 may be weaker than a sharper closure option, even if socially easier.

4. **Execution risk is controlled**
   - Voluntary exits and redeployment are usually more reliable than forced measures in a conflict environment.
   - But if the package depends on very high voluntary uptake, that is a material risk.

5. **Investment commitments are real and staged**
   - If sites are preserved, investment must be tied to product allocation and productivity gains.
   - Otherwise the company risks keeping capacity but not competitiveness.

---

## What a closure case must prove to be economically preferable
A closure-based option is economically stronger only if it shows, after all costs, a clearly higher NPV than the alternative.

That means it must include:
- severance and social plan costs,
- shutdown and decommissioning costs,
- relocation or replacement capacity costs,
- legal and industrial-relations delay,
- strike probability and strike duration,
- supplier and logistics disruptions,
- lost output during transition,
- management time and organizational drag.

In a German co-determination setting, those are not minor adjustments. They can be large enough to erase the apparent spreadsheet advantage of closure.

---

## What “plant-by-plant” analysis should contain
For each site, the comparison should show:

### A. Current economics
- output volume
- capacity utilization
- unit labour cost
- fixed overhead absorption
- logistics and proximity advantages
- model allocation prospects

### B. Site-preserving restructuring effect
- headcount reduction
- shift reconfiguration
- working-time changes
- wage moderation
- productivity gains
- redeployment/voluntary exit assumptions
- investment required to sustain the site

### C. Closure/reduction effect
- shutdown timing
- severance and legal costs
- output transfer costs
- replacement capacity or lost volume
- one-off and recurring savings
- strike/delay assumptions

### D. Risk-adjusted NPV
- probability-weighted execution outcomes
- sensitivity to strike duration
- sensitivity to voluntary exit take-up
- sensitivity to demand deterioration

## Main trade-off
### Site-preserving package
**Pros**
- lower conflict risk if negotiated
- less legal and political friction
- less supplier and morale damage
- preserves optionality and industrial capability

**Cons**
- may deliver less permanent capacity correction
- may require concessions on pay, flexibility, or future guarantees
- may be slower to fully realize savings
- risk of under-delivery if voluntary uptake is weak

### Closure-based package
**Pros**
- clearer structural capacity reduction
- potentially stronger long-run cost reset
- can be decisive if executed cleanly

**Cons**
- high transition cost
- high industrial-relations and legal risk
- strike and delay risk
- reputational and regional spillover costs
- possible value destruction if conflict escalates

## Practical conclusion
If the parties want a serious comparison, they should require three full cases:

1. **Closure case**
2. **Hard site-preserving case**
3. **Hybrid case**

Each should be tested on the same basis:
- recurring annual savings
- one-off transition costs
- execution timeline
- probability-adjusted delivery
- NPV over at least 5–7 years

Only then can one say whether the site-preserving package is economically equivalent or merely politically preferable.
