I will respond very directly.

## Where I agree

I agree with everyone on one basic point: **this company needs a durable restructuring, not a symbolic one**.  
The cost base is too high, the market is under pressure, and the company must regain competitiveness if it is to keep investing in future products.

I also agree that:
- temporary fixes alone are not enough,
- prolonged conflict would damage the company,
- and any settlement has to be economically credible.

So I am not defending complacency or a status quo that simply postpones the problem.

## Where I disagree

### 1) I do not accept plant closures in Germany as the default answer
Management, owners, and investors keep treating closures as the natural endpoint if the numbers do not work. I reject that framing.

A closure-first logic is **too narrow, too risky, and too damaging** for a company that is a central industrial anchor in our region. It would not just affect one balance sheet line; it would hit:
- employment,
- suppliers,
- local economies,
- skills,
- trust in social partnership,
- and the company’s public legitimacy.

The expert was right about one thing: **closure is not automatically economically superior**. It may look decisive, but it also carries major transition and execution risks. In a co-determined company with a blocking shareholder structure, those risks are not theoretical — they are real.

### 2) I do not accept compulsory redundancies as a primary tool
I understand management’s desire for flexibility. But “flexibility” cannot mean pushing the adjustment risk almost entirely onto workers and regions.

If the company needs workforce reduction, then I expect first:
- voluntary departures,
- retirement pathways,
- redeployment,
- working-time solutions,
- and internal capacity adjustments.

Compulsory redundancies may exist as a last resort in some abstract restructuring theory. But for this company, in this region, under this governance structure, they would be a very serious political and industrial escalation. I will not endorse them as a routine restructuring instrument.

### 3) I do not accept any package that weakens co-determination or the state’s veto role
This is non-negotiable from our side.  
The company’s governance is not an accident; it is part of the settlement that has sustained it for decades. If restructuring becomes a way to sideline the workforce and the regional state, then it will not produce durable stability — it will produce conflict and mistrust.

## Where I differ from the workforce side

I also want to be equally clear: I do **not** simply support a refusal of change.

The Works Council and union are right to defend jobs and sites, and I strongly support that. But a position that says “no closures, no compulsory redundancies, no framework changes” only works if there is also a **credible savings package** behind it.

So I do not want slogans. I want substance:
- real capacity adjustment,
- real productivity gains,
- real labor-cost contribution,
- real investment commitments,
- and a structure that can stand up economically.

## What I can support

I am prepared to support a negotiated restructuring if it is built around these principles:

1. **No closure as the first instrument**  
   Closure must not be the starting point or the bargaining club.

2. **Site-preserving capacity reduction**  
   If capacity has to come down, do it through shifts, model allocation, specialization, and other measures that keep plants open where possible.

3. **Voluntary instruments first**  
   Redeployment, early retirement, partial retirement, and agreed exits should come before forced measures.

4. **A fair burden-sharing package**  
   The workforce cannot be the only party contributing. Management, owners, and capital must also make a meaningful contribution.

5. **Binding investment and product commitments**  
   If people are asked to accept sacrifices, the company must commit credibly to future products and industrial presence in Germany.

6. **Protection of jobs and sites as a strategic objective**  
   Not as a sentimental slogan, but as part of the company’s long-term industrial logic.

## What I need to hear from management

Management says the package must be hard-edged. Fine. But then I want concrete numbers, not generalized pressure.

I want management to table:
- the exact savings gap,
- the time horizon,
- the contribution expected from labor,
- the contribution expected from owners and management,
- and the point at which site-preserving measures are judged sufficient or insufficient.

Without that, “closures are possible” is just a negotiation threat, not a serious plan.

## What I need to hear from the union and works council

I support your defense of the German sites. But I also need you to recognize this:

If the company genuinely faces structural overcapacity, then there must be a real capacity adjustment and real cost moderation.  
If your package does not produce that, then it will not be enough to secure the company’s future, and that would also endanger jobs later.

So I am with you on:
- no plant closures as the first solution,
- no compulsory redundancies as the preferred solution,
- preserving co-determination and the job-security framework,

but I expect a package that can actually carry the economics.

## What I need to hear from owners and investors

I agree that the company needs competitiveness. But I do not accept the idea that competitiveness must be bought mainly through social destruction.

If you want durable savings, then support:
- serious investment,
- a fair contribution from capital and top management,
- and a negotiated restructuring that preserves the company’s social and regional foundations.

If the package is stable, deliverable, and economically credible, then it deserves support.  
If it depends on provoking a conflict that tears apart the company’s industrial base, then it is not a good value plan.

## My bottom line

My position is this:

- **I support restructuring.**
- **I do not support German plant closures as the default route.**
- **I do not support compulsory redundancies as the first or primary instrument.**
- **I do support a hard, negotiated, site-preserving package that delivers real savings, real capacity adjustment, and real investment commitments.**

If management can show that alternative measures genuinely close the gap, I am open.  
If not, then we are not talking about a credible restructuring package, but about shifting the burden onto the region and the workforce — and that I cannot accept.
