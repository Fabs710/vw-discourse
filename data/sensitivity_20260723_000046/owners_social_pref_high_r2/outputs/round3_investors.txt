We are prepared to be pragmatic, but our support is conditional on one thing: **a credible, durable return to competitive margins**. Anything that leaves the cost base structurally uncompetitive is not a compromise we can endorse.

## Where we agree
- **Restructuring is necessary.** The current cost base and capacity mix are not acceptable in a shrinking, highly competitive market.
- **A prolonged strike or drawn-out confrontation destroys value.** We do not want a process that burns cash, distracts management, and weakens the brand.
- **A negotiated solution is preferable if it is real.** If a site-preserving package can truly reset costs, that is fine.
- **The expert is right on one key point:** closures are not automatically value-maximizing once transition, legal, political, and industrial-relations risks are included.

## Where we disagree
### 1) A blanket “no closures” line is not enough
The works council and union keep repeating no closures, no compulsory redundancies, no framework changes. That may be politically understandable, but **it is not yet a financial solution**.

If those red lines prevent a meaningful reduction in recurring cost, then they are simply a refusal to confront the economics. We cannot support a settlement that preserves the appearance of labor peace while leaving margins structurally impaired.

### 2) Management must stop speaking in threats and start speaking in numbers
Management is right that the company needs a hard reset. But “closures are possible” is not a strategy unless you can show:
- the exact savings gap,
- the annual recurring cost reduction needed,
- the transition costs,
- and why a hard negotiated package cannot close that gap.

Without that, closure is just leverage, not proof.

### 3) An unconditional site guarantee through 2030 is too rigid
We understand why labor wants security. But from an investor perspective, an absolute guarantee that blocks future adjustment is unacceptable if market conditions worsen or a site remains uncompetitive.

A guarantee can be part of a deal, but only if it is:
- tied to measurable milestones,
- linked to real savings,
- and reviewable if targets are missed.

## What would move us
We would support a negotiated, site-preserving package if it demonstrably delivers all of the following:
- **material permanent cost reduction**
- **real capacity reduction**
- **substantial workforce reduction through voluntary means or equivalent instruments**
- **durable labor-cost moderation**
- **enforceable productivity improvements**
- **credible investment commitments**
- **clear milestones and consequences**

That is the minimum for a risk-adjusted value case.

## What we will not support
- A cosmetic settlement.
- A package that keeps too much capacity in place.
- A deal that preserves all sites but leaves margins weak.
- A prolonged confrontation that destroys cash and confidence.
- A closure strategy that looks neat on paper but fails in execution.

## On the current dispute
The real question is not “closures or no closures” in the abstract. It is:

**Can the negotiated alternative deliver the same permanent cost reset, on a similar timeline, with acceptable execution risk?**

If yes, we prefer it, because it reduces execution risk.
If no, then management is right that harder measures remain on the table.

## Our position
We are not siding with either camp’s slogans. We are siding with the option that most credibly restores competitive margins.

So our answer is simple:
- **Yes** to a hard restructuring.
- **Yes** to a negotiated site-preserving solution if it is economically equivalent.
- **No** to a cosmetic compromise.
- **No** to any settlement that leaves the cost base structurally impaired.
