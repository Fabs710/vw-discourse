We agree with management and the controlling family on the **core diagnosis**: this is a structural competitiveness problem, not a temporary dip. We also agree with the state government and employee representatives on one important point: a **cosmetic settlement** would destroy value. That would be unacceptable.

But let me be equally clear: **a no-closure stance is only acceptable if it is backed by an economically equivalent, durable package**. Right now, I do not yet see that package.

## Where we agree
- The company must restore **competitive margins**.
- The solution must be **durable**, not a pause before the next crisis.
- Prolonged conflict is **value-destructive**.
- The package must include **real burden-sharing**, not just management rhetoric.
- Investment in future products and technology is essential.

## Where we disagree
### To the Works Council and Union
We respect the red lines, but as stated they are **not sufficient** unless you can show how the economics close.

You say:
- no plant closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- site guarantees,
- preservation of the job-security framework.

That is understandable, but from an investor perspective it is only acceptable if it comes with:
- **quantified, permanent cost reductions**,
- **material capacity reduction**,
- and a credible path back to target returns.

At the moment, “working-time flexibility,” “voluntary exits,” and “temporary contributions” sound like **partial friction relief**, not a structural fix. If that is all the package delivers, it will not protect shareholder value, because it leaves the cost base too high and margins structurally impaired.

### To Management
We agree with you that closures and footprint changes cannot be ruled out in principle. However, we will not support management if “restructuring” becomes a blanket justification without a hard business case.

You still need to show:
- the size of the cost gap,
- what savings come from overhead, procurement, executive restraint, and productivity,
- what remains after labour-side measures,
- and why that residual gap truly requires closures or equivalent permanent footprint reductions.

If management cannot quantify this clearly, then closures look like a negotiating weapon rather than a necessity. That would undermine confidence.

### To the State Government
We understand the political and regional constraints, but **regional stability is not a substitute for economic viability**. If the company remains uncompetitive, the region loses more over time: investment, margin, and eventually jobs.

So we support your call for shared sacrifice and social peace, but not if it blocks real restructuring. Protection of sites is not credible if the business model still cannot earn its cost of capital.

### To the Family Holding
We agree that a shallow compromise is dangerous. But we also need discipline: “economically equivalent” cannot mean “employees must absorb enough pain to satisfy ownership preferences.” It means the final package must actually close the gap.

That requires a serious, quantified burden-sharing framework:
- management contribution,
- owner acceptance of lower near-term returns,
- employee contribution,
- and residual structural action if needed.

## Our position
Dispersed capital-market shareholders will support **whatever package credibly restores competitiveness**. We are not attached to closures as an ideology. But we are equally not prepared to underwrite a deal that:
- preserves too much capacity,
- keeps fixed costs too high,
- and leaves margins structurally impaired.

## What we need next
Before any party asks for support, we want a concrete comparison of options:
1. **No-closure package**: exact savings, exact capacity reduction, exact margin impact.
2. **Partial structural change package**: what footprint reduction is involved, what is preserved, and at what cost.
3. **Management/owner/employee burden split**: in numbers, not slogans.
4. **Timing and certainty**: how quickly the savings land, and how durable they are.

If a no-closure solution can truly deliver the economics, we will back it. If it cannot, then pretending otherwise is just value destruction with better branding.

## Bottom line
We are open-minded on the form, but not on the economics.

- **No cosmetic settlement**
- **No structurally impaired margins**
- **No deal that postpones the problem**
- **Yes to decisive, durable cost reduction**

If the employee side wants to avoid closures, the burden is on them to prove that the alternative is not merely more acceptable, but **financially equivalent**. Until then, we remain unconvinced.
