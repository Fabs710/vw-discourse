We appreciate the expert input, because it confirms the central issue from our perspective: **the package has to be judged on economics, durability, and cash impact — not on political comfort**.

## Our position remains unchanged

We will support **whatever credible package restores competitive margins and fixes the cost base**.  
We will **not** support a cosmetic settlement that leaves the company structurally impaired.

That means:

- **No deal that preserves excess cost and excess capacity**
- **No deal that merely postpones the restructuring**
- **No deal that sacrifices returns today without a durable path back to target margins**
- **No prolonged conflict that destroys value through strikes and uncertainty**

## Where we agree

### With management
We agree that:
- this is a **structural competitiveness problem**
- temporary measures alone are unlikely to be enough if the gap is large
- any settlement must be **durable**
- and the company must restore room for investment

Management is right to insist that the numbers matter.

### With the expert
The expert basically confirms our concern: a **pure no-closure package is unlikely to be economically equivalent** unless the cost gap is modest and the savings are unusually deep and permanent. That is important.

### With the family holding
We agree that a shallow compromise would be value-destructive.  
A pretend settlement that leaves the economics broken would be unacceptable.

## Where we disagree

### With the works council and union
We do **not** accept their red lines as stated.

A blanket:
- **no plant closures**
- **no compulsory redundancies**
- **site guarantees to 2030**
- **preservation of the current job-security framework**

is **not acceptable unless** it is backed by a package that really closes the cost gap.

Right now, from what has been presented, the employee side is offering mainly:
- temporary concessions,
- flexibility,
- voluntary exits,
- redeployment,
- and promises of contribution.

Those may help, but the expert just said what we have been saying: **they are probably not enough if the structural gap is large**.

So no, we will not endorse a “no closure” stance just because it is socially preferable. It has to be financially equivalent. So far, that has not been shown.

### With the state government
We respect the political constraints, but regional stability is not a substitute for economic viability.

If the company remains too expensive:
- margins stay weak,
- investment stays constrained,
- and ultimately jobs remain at risk anyway.

That is why we will not back a settlement that protects every site while leaving the business model uncompetitive.

## Our reading of the expert assessment

The expert’s conclusion is the key point:

- If the cost gap is **moderate**, a no-closure package might work.
- If the gap is **large and structural**, then no-closure is unlikely to be sufficient.
- In that case, **plant closures or equivalent footprint reduction remain the more credible route**.

That is exactly the line we need to hold.

We are **not** ideologically attached to closures.  
We are attached to **economic reality**.

If a no-closure plan truly delivers:
- permanent cost reduction,
- real capacity adjustment,
- and a credible margin recovery,
then we would support it.

But if it only softens the adjustment while keeping excess capacity in place, then it is not a solution. It is delay.

## What would move us

We would support a no-closure package only if it demonstrably includes all of the following:

1. **A quantified bridge to target margins**
2. **Permanent, not temporary, cost reductions**
3. **A material capacity reduction**
4. **A credible cash and liquidity impact**
5. **A durable burden-sharing split across management, owners, employees, and the business itself**

If those conditions are not met, then closures or equivalent footprint reduction remain on the table, and we would support management on that basis.

## Our bottom line

We are open on the instrument mix, but not on the outcome.

- **Yes** to restructuring
- **Yes** to burden-sharing
- **Yes** to a no-closure solution **if it is truly economically equivalent**
- **No** to a settlement that leaves the company structurally uncompetitive
- **No** to anything that protects jobs in the short term while eroding shareholder value through weak margins and weak cash generation

So the burden now is on the employee side to show — with numbers, not slogans — that a no-closure solution can actually match the economics.  
Until then, the case for deeper footprint change remains stronger.
