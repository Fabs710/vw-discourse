Yes—within industrial production and site-utilization terms, **it is often feasible to preserve all plants while still achieving meaningful fixed-cost reduction**, but only under specific conditions. The key issue is **whether the restructuring can reduce structural overcapacity enough to lift utilization, simplify the footprint, and remove fixed costs faster than the retained network can absorb new work**.

## Short answer
A **no-closure** package can work if:
- the company has **enough transferable volume and product flexibility**,
- plants can be **reassigned to more specialized roles**,
- output can be **consolidated onto fewer shifts / fewer lines / higher utilization**,
- and labor-related measures plus overhead cuts are **durable, not temporary**.

But a no-closure solution becomes weak if:
- the network is **too fragmented**,
- each German plant must keep a broad product mix,
- demand is **too low to load all sites efficiently**,
- or the savings rely mostly on temporary concessions rather than structural changes.

## What determines feasibility

### 1) Capacity reduction does not require closure, but it does require underutilized assets to be taken out of the cost base
You can reduce capacity without formally closing a plant by:
- removing a shift,
- mothballing a hall or line,
- consolidating subassemblies,
- narrowing a plant’s product scope,
- or transferring models to another site.

Operationally, though, if a plant keeps most of its fixed infrastructure, overhead, and maintenance burden while running at low utilization, **the cost savings will be limited**. So the question is not “closure or no closure,” but:

> Can the company reduce the number of active lines, shifts, and supporting functions enough to materially lower fixed costs while keeping the site open?

That is feasible in many cases.

### 2) Product reallocation is the main lever for preserving sites
If plants are preserved, the largest durable savings usually come from:
- assigning each site a clearer role,
- moving models to the most efficient plants,
- reducing parallel production of similar products,
- and aligning plant complexity with demand.

This can improve:
- utilization,
- labor productivity,
- logistics efficiency,
- and quality consistency.

For an automotive network, **specialization usually beats duplication**. If every plant remains a generalist, savings are much harder to achieve.

### 3) Load balancing can preserve plants, but only if there is enough volume
If demand is falling, the network can often be stabilized by:
- shifting some volumes between plants,
- smoothing production schedules,
- reducing overtime volatility,
- and improving line balancing.

But load balancing has limits:
- it cannot create demand,
- it cannot fully eliminate fixed site costs,
- and it cannot compensate for large, persistent overcapacity.

If the company is materially underloaded across several German plants, then load balancing alone will not solve the structural problem.

## Durable fixed-cost reduction: what is realistic
A durable reduction can come from several layers:

### Achievable without closures
- fewer shifts
- lower internal complexity
- reduced indirect labor / overhead
- simplified production planning
- procurement savings
- higher line utilization
- automation of selected processes
- consolidation of support functions
- product specialization by plant

### Usually not sufficient on their own
- temporary wage concessions
- short-term working-time reductions
- voluntary exits without footprint change
- one-off cost cuts that do not change the site structure

These measures help, but by themselves they often do **not** create a durable step-change in fixed cost.

## The practical test
The restructuring is credible without closures if management can show, with numbers, that after:
1. product reallocation,
2. shift reduction,
3. support-function cuts,
4. voluntary exits,
5. overhead and procurement savings,
6. and some labor-cost contribution,

the remaining network still has:
- acceptable utilization,
- lower break-even volume,
- and a cost base that can compete over the medium term.

If the remaining gap is still large, then one of two things is true:
- either additional productivity and product moves are still available,
- or footprint reduction becomes structurally hard to avoid.

## Bottom line
From a production and site-utilization perspective:

> **All German plants can sometimes be preserved, but only if the restructuring meaningfully reassigns products, reduces complexity, and takes enough capacity out of the operating system to raise utilization.**

If the plan relies mainly on temporary concessions and hopes that demand will recover, it will not deliver durable fixed-cost reduction. If it combines **capacity rationalization without formal shutdowns** with **product concentration and permanent overhead reduction**, then it can be a real restructuring.
