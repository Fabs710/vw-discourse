Let me respond directly and clearly.

### First: what I agree with
I agree with all sides on one point: this company needs a **durable, legally workable restructuring**, not a cosmetic announcement.

I also agree that:
- investment in future products and technology is essential,
- prolonged industrial conflict damages everyone,
- and any final package has to be negotiated within the governance realities of co-determination and the special statute.

So yes: we need a settlement that can actually be implemented.

### Where I fundamentally disagree
I do **not** accept the repeated claim that the answer must start with **“no plant closures, no compulsory redundancies, no structural footprint change.”**

That position may be understandable politically, but it does not solve the business problem.

We are not dealing with a temporary dip. We are dealing with:
- a shrinking European market,
- intense price pressure,
- competition from lower-cost producers,
- German factory costs far above target,
- and a brand that is missing its return-on-sales target.

If the cost base and capacity remain essentially unchanged, then the company will continue to underperform and will have less money for the next product cycle, software, battery, and platform investment. That is the real threat to jobs and sites over time.

### To the Works Council
You asked for numbers. Fair enough.

The numbers matter because slogans do not restore competitiveness. But I will be equally clear: **if the only acceptable result is “no closures and no compulsory redundancies,” then the arithmetic has to close elsewhere at scale.**

That means:
- meaningful and durable labour-cost reduction,
- real capacity reduction,
- structural efficiency gains,
- and a package that lowers fixed costs in a way that survives beyond the next bargaining round.

Working-time tweaks and voluntary exits alone are **not enough** if they only soften the blow without changing the economics. I will not pretend otherwise.

You say closures are just a pressure tactic. They are not. They are a real option because the footprint has to match demand and the cost structure has to be brought down materially. I am not using that language to provoke; I am stating the reality of the situation.

### To the Industrial Union
You asked for burden sharing. I agree with the principle. But burden sharing is only meaningful if it changes the cost base in a way that management can actually rely on.

I am prepared to discuss:
- temporary employee contributions,
- working-time flexibility,
- voluntary exit programmes,
- redeployment,
- training,
- and targeted productivity measures.

But I am **not** prepared to sign up to a package where the burden-sharing language masks a permanent preservation of excess capacity.

And I need to be blunt: if you want to rule out closures, then you must bring something that is **economically equivalent**, not merely more comfortable. That means hard, measurable savings. Not aspirations. Not trust. Not symbolic restraint.

### To the Regional State Government
I understand your political responsibility and your concern for industrial stability. But the state cannot ask management to carry the competitiveness problem alone while simultaneously ruling out the instruments needed to fix it.

A company that remains structurally too expensive will not protect jobs in the long term. It will erode investment, erode margins, and eventually erode options.

So yes, we should seek to preserve as much industrial capacity as possible. But I will not accept the state framing “site protection” as if it were automatically compatible with the current cost structure. If you want to help, then help build a package that actually reduces fixed cost permanently.

### To the Controlling Family and Investors
I agree with you on one critical point: a shallow compromise that leaves the economics broken would be a mistake.

But I also need to be clear that a credible restructuring is not the same thing as “anything goes.” We do not need symbolic shock for its own sake. We need a package that produces:
- durable cost reduction,
- sustainable capacity alignment,
- and a path back to target margins.

That is the standard. Not headline management. Not theatre.

### On what would move management
What would move me is straightforward:
1. **A quantified package of sustainable cost reduction**
2. **A real and durable reduction in capacity**
3. **A meaningful labour-cost contribution**
4. **A settlement that restores medium-term margins and frees investment capacity to 2030**

If the employee side can bring a package that closes the gap materially and durably without closures, I will examine it seriously. But that package must be judged by whether it truly fixes the economics, not by whether it preserves every existing structure unchanged.

### What I will not accept
I will not accept:
- a solution that keeps the current footprint basically intact,
- a deal that merely postpones the restructuring problem,
- or a “socially balanced” arrangement that leaves the company uncompetitive.

I also will not accept the idea that management should simply absorb the gap through restraint while the structural issues remain untouched. That is not responsible management; that is delay.

### The bottom line
So my position remains firm:

- **substantial structural change is required**
- **capacity must come down**
- **labour cost must contribute materially**
- **we are open on the instrument mix**
- **but we are not open to a cosmetic compromise**

If the other stakeholders want to avoid closures, then they need to present a package that is not just politically preferable, but economically equivalent. Until then, closures remain on the table because the business case has not been solved.

If helpful, I can now put forward the management criteria for an acceptable package in a more concrete form.
