We have now heard enough to separate **wishful no-closure thinking** from a **credible restructuring path**.

My position remains disciplined:

- I support a **durable restructuring**.
- I support **burden-sharing** across all sides.
- I support a **serious no-closure test**.
- I do **not** support a blank no-closure commitment.
- I do **not** support any solution that leaves the company structurally uncompetitive.
- I do **not** support preserving every legacy structure if the numbers do not justify it.

## To the works council
I agree with you on one important point: **management has not yet proven closures are unavoidable**.

But I disagree with your implication that the burden of proof alone settles the matter. It does not.

The expert advice was clear:
- a no-closure package can work **in principle**,
- but only if it produces **durable, quantified, operationally real savings**,
- and only if the residual gap is small enough to absorb.

That means your red lines are **not self-executing**. They still have to be reconciled with the economics.

So here is the hard truth:  
If your no-closure alternative cannot show a **permanent** reset of costs, capacity, and utilization, then it is not a solution — it is a postponement.

I agree that temporary measures are not enough on their own.  
I do **not** agree that “no closures” can be treated as a moral entitlement regardless of the financial result.

## To the union
You are more useful than the works council because you at least accept that burden-sharing must be real. That said, I still disagree with the idea that a temporary employee contribution, voluntary exits, redeployment, and working-time flexibility automatically create an equivalent restructuring.

They may contribute meaningfully. They may even be necessary. But they are **not sufficient** unless they are paired with:
- real product reallocation,
- site specialization,
- overhead reduction,
- and a sustained drop in fixed cost.

If those elements are not present, then the package does not solve the structural problem.

So I agree with you on:
- no cosmetic deal,
- no one-sided burden on workers,
- no fake temporary “contribution” that becomes permanent.

But I disagree with your insistence that closures are merely a threat tactic. They remain on the table because the underlying economics may still require a deeper reset.

## To management
Management is right that we cannot confuse political comfort with competitiveness. But management also has to be careful not to overstate what the current evidence proves.

The experts did **not** say closures are automatically required.  
They said:
- the residual gap is decisive,
- savings must be quantified,
- no-closure is possible only if the restructuring is deep enough,
- and temporary measures are not enough if the gap is large.

That is exactly the standard I expect from management: **numbers, not slogans**.

So I support management only to this extent:
- show the exact cost bridge,
- show the exact residual gap,
- show why the no-closure route fails if it does fail,
- and show what structural change is actually needed.

If management cannot do that, then it should stop using closures as a blunt negotiating instrument.  
But if it can, then I will not let governance be held hostage by a politically attractive but economically weak compromise.

## To the regional state government
I understand your role and I do not dismiss it. A one-sided closure plan would be socially damaging and would deserve resistance.

But I must also say plainly: regional stability is not the same as industrial competitiveness.

You are right to insist on a real no-closure test.  
You are not right to treat no-closure as the default outcome regardless of the economics.

The special governance structure protects legitimate interests, yes. It does **not** eliminate the need to restore margins and competitiveness. If the company cannot earn its way forward, then everyone loses — including the region.

So I agree with:
- a verified no-closure test,
- burden-sharing from ownership and management,
- serious industrial planning.

I disagree with any position that effectively pre-commits the company to preserve all sites no matter what.

## Where I stand as controlling shareholder
I want this said plainly:

I am **not** attached to plant closures as a matter of principle.  
I am attached to **value preservation over the long term**.

That means:
- if a no-closure package truly restores competitiveness, I will support it;
- if it does not, I will not pretend otherwise;
- if deeper footprint change is unavoidable, I will support it in governance terms, provided it is legally workable and economically justified.

What I will **not** support is a compromise that:
- looks socially neat,
- preserves every site in the short run,
- and leaves the enterprise too expensive to invest and compete.

That would be value destruction dressed up as consensus.

## On the expert assessments
Both experts were helpful, and both actually strengthen the need for discipline.

### Industrial finance expert
They said a no-closure package is unlikely to be economically equivalent unless the gap is moderate and the concessions are unusually deep and durable.

That means:
- no-closure is **possible**, but
- not automatically credible,
- and certainly not proven yet.

### Production and site-utilization expert
They said all plants can sometimes be preserved, but only if the network is reconfigured meaningfully:
- specialization,
- product reallocation,
- lower duplication,
- better utilization,
- and permanent structural savings.

That is not a victory for the no-closure camp.  
It is a warning that preserving all sites requires a **real industrial redesign**, not just promises.

## What would actually move me
I would be moved by a package that shows, with verification:

1. **A fully itemized gap bridge**
2. **Permanent rather than temporary savings**
3. **Real specialization and product reallocation**
4. **A material drop in fixed costs**
5. **A credible path back to target returns**
6. **Investment headroom preserved through 2030**
7. **Clear burden-sharing across management, labour, ownership, and capital**

Absent that, a no-closure position is not yet a serious business answer.

## My current bottom line
So my position is:

- **Yes** to a serious, quantified no-closure test.
- **Yes** to shared sacrifice.
- **Yes** to operational redesign.
- **No** to a blank guarantee against closures.
- **No** to keeping every legacy arrangement if the economics do not support it.
- **No** to any outcome that permanently impairs enterprise value or governance control.

If the employee side wants to avoid closures, then it must bring forward a package that is not just politically attractive but financially equivalent in substance. Until then, closures and equivalent footprint reduction remain legitimate options, because the business case has not been solved.
