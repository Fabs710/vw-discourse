We appreciate the additional expert input, and it sharpens the issue rather than softening it:

**the decision must be made on durable economics, not on social preference or rhetorical red lines.**

Our position remains unchanged:

- **We support any package that credibly restores competitive margins and fixes the cost base.**
- **We do not support a cosmetic no-closure deal that leaves structural overcapacity in place.**
- **We do not support prolonged conflict that destroys value through strikes, delays, and uncertainty.**

## Where we agree

### With management
We agree that:
- this is a **structural competitiveness problem**, not a temporary hiccup;
- temporary measures alone are unlikely to be enough if the gap is large;
- the solution must be **durable, quantified, and cash-relevant**;
- and the company needs room to invest through 2030.

Management is right that **the numbers matter**.

### With the finance expert
The finance assessment confirms what we have been saying:
- a pure no-closure package is **unlikely to be economically equivalent** unless the gap is moderate;
- temporary concessions do not substitute for structural change;
- durability is the critical question.

That is a useful reality check.

### With the production/site-utilization expert
The production assessment is also useful:
- preserving all plants is **possible in principle**;
- but only if the company accepts **deeper specialization, lower duplication, and real capacity rationalization**;
- otherwise the savings will be modest to moderate.

That is exactly why we say: **show us the actual economics**.

## Where we disagree

### With the works council and union
We do **not** accept the stance that:
- no German plant closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- binding site guarantees to 2030,
- and preservation of the current job-security framework

can be treated as the default outcome **before** the economics are proven.

The latest expert assessments do not support that level of certainty. They support a test. They do **not** support a blanket guarantee.

Your position remains acceptable only if you can show, with numbers, that a no-closure package delivers:
- a material fixed-cost reset,
- a credible reduction in capacity or equivalent footprint,
- and a durable path back to target margins.

So far, we do not see that proof.

### With the state government
We respect the political constraints, but regional stability is not a substitute for competitiveness.

If the cost base remains structurally too high:
- margins stay weak,
- capital allocation remains constrained,
- and the company becomes a weaker long-term employer anyway.

That is why we will not back a settlement that protects every site while leaving the economics broken.

### With the controlling family
We agree that a cosmetic compromise would be bad. But we do **not** accept the idea that “economic reality” has already been established in favour of closures. The experts did not say that.

What they said is:
- a no-closure solution is possible only if the restructuring is deep enough;
- the residual gap is decisive;
- and management must quantify why alternatives fail.

That means the burden is still on management and the controlling owners to prove that more structural action is truly necessary.

## Our reading of the experts

The combined expert view is the key point:

- **No-closure is not automatically impossible.**
- **No-closure is not automatically sufficient either.**
- The real test is whether the package can produce:
  - permanent cost reduction,
  - real capacity adjustment,
  - improved utilization,
  - and a durable margin recovery.

That is the standard we will apply.

## What would move us

We would support a no-closure package only if it clearly includes all of the following:

1. **A fully quantified bridge from current costs to target margins**
2. **Permanent, not temporary, savings**
3. **A material and verifiable capacity adjustment**
4. **Meaningful product reallocation / plant specialization**
5. **A credible cash and liquidity impact**
6. **Evidence that the residual gap is small enough to absorb without closures**
7. **Burden-sharing across management, employees, owners, and the business**

If those conditions are not met, then closures or equivalent footprint reduction remain on the table, and we would support management in pursuing that route.

## What we reject
We reject:
- a settlement that merely postpones the problem;
- a package that preserves excess cost and excess capacity;
- a deal that buys social peace at the price of weaker margins;
- and any outcome that treats shareholders as the only party expected to absorb value destruction.

## Bottom line

Our position is simple:

- **Yes** to restructuring.
- **Yes** to burden-sharing.
- **Yes** to a serious, independently verified no-closure test.
- **No** to a cosmetic no-closure agreement.
- **No** to any outcome that leaves the company structurally uncompetitive.
- **No** to prolonged conflict that destroys enterprise value.

If the employee side wants to rule out closures, it must demonstrate — with hard numbers and operational detail — that the no-closure package is genuinely economically equivalent. Until then, the case for deeper footprint reduction remains stronger.
