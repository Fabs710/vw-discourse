We agree on one thing: **this company needs a durable restructuring, not a cosmetic exercise**. But I need to be equally clear that durability does **not** mean accepting plant closures and mass job destruction as the default answer.

## To management
You are right that the company faces a structural cost problem. I do **not** dispute the need for real savings, better productivity, and a stronger margin path. Where I strongly disagree is your insistence that the only credible route runs through **German plant closures** and a deep break with the company’s social model.

That is not a neutral “business reality.” It is a choice about who bears the adjustment burden.

You keep saying the footprint must shrink “materially.” Fine — then show us the full arithmetic:
- management overhead reductions,
- executive restraint,
- procurement and process savings,
- productivity gains,
- working-time flexibility,
- voluntary exits,
- and owner contribution.

Only after that can you honestly claim what residual gap remains. Until then, closures are not a proven necessity; they are a preferred instrument.

## To the family holding and investors
I share your concern that a fake compromise would be dangerous. A settlement that leaves the economics broken would help no one. But I reject the logic that “economically equivalent” automatically means “the workforce must accept closures or permanent insecurity.”

If you want credibility, then demand **shared sacrifice**, not one-sided sacrifice:
- management must contribute visibly,
- owners must accept lower short-term returns,
- investors must accept that long-term industrial stability is part of value preservation,
- and the workforce must be offered security in return for any temporary contribution.

That is the only form of restructuring that is politically and industrially sustainable in this company.

## To the works council and union
I support your core red lines:
- **no German plant closures**
- **no compulsory redundancies**
- **no dismantling of co-determination**
- **no gutting of the job-security framework**

That is not stubbornness; it is a legitimate defense of the company’s industrial base and the regions that depend on it.

But I also want to be practical: if you are asking for a no-closure solution, it must come with a **quantified alternative** that genuinely reduces costs and capacity in a durable way. Working-time changes, voluntary exits, internal redeployment, and temporary employee contributions can be part of that — but they must be paired with:
- serious management restraint,
- investment commitments,
- and a binding guarantee that sites in Germany remain part of the future strategy.

## To management again
I am not prepared to give you a blank cheque to “restructure” by shrinking the company’s German presence and calling that transformation. The regional state is not here to ratify industrial retreat.

We can support:
- capacity reductions through flexible and negotiated means,
- productivity improvements,
- temporary measures,
- and a real burden-sharing package.

We cannot support:
- plant closures in Germany,
- forced layoffs,
- or a restructuring that undermines the company’s role as a regional economic anchor.

## On governance and leverage
Let me also be direct: this company’s governance structure is not a formality. The special statute, the blocking minority, and co-determination all exist for a reason. Any plan that ignores those realities is not “decisive”; it is unrealistic.

If management wants a workable settlement, it must bring forward a package that:
1. protects German sites,
2. avoids compulsory redundancies,
3. preserves co-determination and our veto role,
4. and still delivers a credible competitiveness gain.

## Bottom line
We are not blocking restructuring. We are blocking a **bad restructuring**.

A real solution must:
- secure the company’s future,
- preserve jobs and plants in Germany,
- share the burden fairly,
- and maintain the company’s role in the regional economy.

If the only proposal is closures and layoffs, then I will oppose it. If there is a serious alternative that protects sites and workers while restoring competitiveness, we are prepared to work with it. But the burden of proof is on those demanding irreversible cuts, not on those defending the industrial foundation of the company.
