From the perspective of dispersed capital-market shareholders, the key test is simple: **does the package credibly restore competitive margins, or does it merely defer the problem?** That is the standard we will use.

### Where we agree
We agree with management, and to a degree with the controlling family, that:
- the company is facing a **structural** competitiveness problem, not a short-term dip;
- the cost base in Germany is too high relative to demand and competitors;
- the turnaround must be **durable**, not symbolic;
- prolonged conflict is value-destructive and should be avoided if possible.

We also agree that investors will not support a business model that keeps excess capacity and uncompetitive labour costs in place. If margins stay structurally impaired, capital will migrate elsewhere.

### Where we disagree
We do **not** accept a cosmetic “social peace” solution that leaves the cost base basically intact. A deal that avoids closures and layoffs in name but fails to deliver meaningful, permanent cost reduction would be unacceptable to us.

That means:
- **No package that merely postpones restructuring**
- **No arrangement that relies mainly on temporary concessions**
- **No settlement that preserves the existing footprint if it remains materially uncompetitive**

In particular, I want to push back on the idea that the only acceptable alternative to closures is a broad promise to protect every site and every job. If that is the constraint, then competitiveness will continue to erode, and so will equity value.

### On the works council and union position
We respect the need for co-determination and social stability, but your current red lines are too absolute **unless** you can show a package with equal or near-equal economics.

If you want no closures and no forced redundancies, then the burden is on you to produce:
- a **quantified** and **durable** cost reduction,
- a **credible capacity reduction**,
- meaningful labour-cost relief,
- and clear evidence that the company can return to target margins.

Without that, your position amounts to asking shareholders to underwrite continued underperformance.

### On management
Management is right on the substance: structural change is required. But we will judge you on execution, not rhetoric. “We need a durable solution” is fine; what matters is whether the final package actually delivers it.

We will not support management if it ends up accepting a settlement that preserves headline peace while leaving the economics broken. If you want investor confidence, bring forward a plan with:
- hard numbers,
- a credible timeline,
- enforced cost actions,
- and measurable margin improvement.

### On the state government and family owners
We understand the political and governance constraints, but anchor shareholders should not confuse stability with value preservation. A company that fails to adapt does not protect regional jobs or family legacy in the long run.

We can support a negotiated solution if it truly addresses the structural issue. But we will not endorse an outcome where the company carries too much capacity, too much fixed cost, and too little flexibility just because that is politically easier.

### Bottom line
Our position is:

- **We want durable restructuring, not theater.**
- **We will support hard cost action if it genuinely restores competitiveness.**
- **We will not support a settlement that leaves margins structurally impaired.**
- **If closures are avoided, the alternative must be economically equivalent, not merely more palatable.**

So the question for the other stakeholders is not whether restructuring is painful. It is whether your alternative actually fixes the business. If not, then resistance to structural change is just protecting an uncompetitive status quo at the expense of shareholder value.
