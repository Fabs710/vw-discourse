I appreciate the positions laid out. Let me speak plainly from the perspective of the controlling family holding.

We share the central concern that the company needs to regain competitiveness, restore margins, and protect its long-term future. We do **not** want a prolonged conflict that erodes value, damages the brand, or weakens the industrial base of the company. On that point, I am aligned with management, and I understand the concerns of the state government and employee representatives.

But I also need to be equally clear: **preserving every site and every existing condition unchanged is not a viable strategy if the current structure is uncompetitive.** If the business cannot earn its cost of capital, then neither the owners nor the workforce are protected in the long run. That is the reality we have to face.

### Where I agree
- The restructuring must be **durable**, not symbolic.
- The company needs a path that preserves its ability to invest in future products and technology.
- A negotiated solution is preferable to a destructive open conflict.
- Social peace matters, because a company of this size cannot afford permanent instability.

### Where I disagree
I do **not** think it is credible to rule out structural measures in advance.

- A blanket **“no plant closures”** position is too rigid if it prevents a genuinely effective restructuring.
- A package built mainly on **working-time tweaks, temporary measures, and voluntary exits** may reduce friction, but it does not automatically solve the structural cost gap.
- The idea that the burden can be absorbed mostly by management restraint and owner patience, while the footprint remains unchanged, is not persuasive.

This is not about using crisis as an excuse. It is about whether the company remains competitive in five and ten years. If we avoid difficult decisions now, we risk a slower erosion that is ultimately worse for everyone — including employees and the regions.

### What would move us
The family holding is open to supporting a negotiated solution, but only if it delivers three things:

1. **A credible and measurable reduction in structural cost**
   - not just a temporary accounting improvement;
   - not just a deferred problem.

2. **A real alignment of capacity with demand**
   - whether through closures, consolidation, or other permanent footprint changes;
   - the exact form can be discussed, but the result must be material.

3. **A stable settlement with labour**
   - because a turnaround that triggers ongoing industrial conflict is value-destructive.

### On the employee-side demands
I respect the insistence on protecting jobs and co-determination. But I cannot support a position that effectively says the owners must absorb all strategic risk while the operating model remains untouched. That would be irresponsible stewardship.

If the union and works council are prepared to present an alternative that truly closes the cost gap — and not just postpones it — then I would listen. That could include:
- capacity reduction without unnecessary escalation,
- targeted site adjustments rather than broad disruption where possible,
- negotiated employee contributions,
- productivity gains,
- working-time flexibility,
- and investment commitments linked to verifiable restructuring progress.

But I will not endorse a package that locks in the current structure and calls that a solution.

### On the state government
I understand the political responsibility you carry. But a regional strategy cannot be allowed to become an obstacle to the company’s competitiveness. A company that is economically weakened cannot sustain jobs, investment, or regional value over time. So while we are open to preserving as much as possible, we cannot make regional status quo the decisive criterion.

### Bottom line
Our position is this:

- **We support a serious restructuring.**
- **We are open on the precise instruments.**
- **We are not open to a solution that avoids structural change altogether.**
- **We will not support any outcome that permanently weakens the company’s value or leaves the ownership structure unable to govern effectively.**

So I would urge the parties to come back with a package that is economically credible, not just politically comfortable. If the alternatives are either a shallow compromise or a prolonged confrontation, then we should be honest that the shallow compromise is the more dangerous option for the company’s future.
