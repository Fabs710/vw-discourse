### Expert assessment: can a no-closure package realistically close the cost gap?

**Short answer:**  
A **pure no-closure package is unlikely to fully close a large structural cost gap** at a major German OEM unless the gap is moderate, the company has substantial excess overhead, and the package includes unusually deep and durable concessions across labour, management, procurement, footprint utilization, and product allocation. In a case like the one described, a no-closure deal **may cover a meaningful share of the gap**, but **it is unlikely to be economically equivalent to plant closures/capacity exits unless the residual gap is already small**.

My judgement is therefore:

- **Possible as a partial solution**
- **Not credible as a full solution unless management’s stated gap is much smaller than implied**
- **Durability is the main issue**: temporary measures rarely substitute for permanent footprint reduction
- **The decisive question is whether the residual fixed-cost base remains too high after the no-closure measures**

---

## 1) What would need to be true for a no-closure package to work?

A no-closure package can work only if the following are all true:

1. **The cost gap is not too large in absolute terms**
   - If the gap is mainly a **few hundred million euros annually**, a negotiated package might close it.
   - If the gap is **well over €1bn annually**, no-closure becomes much harder unless there is major footprint rationalization without formal closure.

2. **There is genuine over-cost, not just overcapacity**
   - If the problem is mostly **labour cost, overhead, procurement, and management complexity**, then a negotiated solution has a better chance.
   - If the problem is **structural plant underutilization and fixed-cost absorption**, then site preservation alone usually does not solve it.

3. **Savings are permanent, not one-off**
   - Voluntary exits, temporary working-time reductions, and short-term wage concessions are helpful, but they decay over time.
   - To be equivalent to closure, the package needs **permanent changes** in headcount, hours, product allocation, and fixed overhead.

4. **The company can actually redeploy capacity**
   - “No closure” is not enough if plants stay open but underloaded.
   - You need a **credible product plan** that gives each site sufficient volume and specialization.

5. **There is disciplined verification**
   - Management must quantify the baseline, each measure’s contribution, and the residual gap.
   - Without that, “no closure” is just a slogan.

---

## 2) Where no-closure packages usually do and do not work

### Measures that can create real savings
These can be credible if implemented at scale:

- **Voluntary exits / attrition management**
- **Temporary or multi-year working-time reductions**
- **Shift pattern changes and overtime elimination**
- **Executive and management pay restraint**
- **Central overhead reduction**
- **Procurement savings**
- **Product allocation changes / internal transfer of volume**
- **Lower investment duplication across sites**
- **Process automation and productivity programs**

### Measures that are often overestimated
These are helpful but usually insufficient on their own:

- **One-off wage freezes or short wage concessions**
- **Temporary employee contributions**
- **One-time management pay cuts**
- **“Efficiency” promises without headcount reduction**
- **Redeployment without real volume reduction**
- **Training commitments not linked to workload redesign**

The reason is simple: **a structural cost problem requires structural savings**. Temporary pain can bridge a year or two, but not restore a permanently uncompetitive cost base.

## 3) The key financial test: is the residual gap small enough?

The company’s management is effectively saying:

> “Even after every reasonable non-closure measure, the remaining gap is still too large.”

The employee side is saying:

> “Management has not proven that, because it has not fully quantified the alternatives.”

My assessment is that **both can be true**:
- management may be right that a residual gap remains,
- but unless the gap is quantified in detail, closure cannot be treated as proven.

### The right test
A no-closure package is only credible if, after all measures, the remaining gap is:

- **small enough to absorb through normal business improvement**, or
- **small enough that product/mix gains and productivity can bridge it within 12–24 months**

If the residual gap still requires **material capacity removal** to restore margins, then no-closure is not economically equivalent.

A line-by-line bridge from current cost base to target:

### B. Permanent elements
At least part of the package must be permanent:
- headcount reduction that is not easily reversed
- permanent overhead reset
- product-line consolidation
- sustained hourly productivity uplift
- reduced fixed site complexity

### C. Capacity realism
Each German site must have a believable future role:
- clear product assignment
- sufficient volume loading
- realistic capital-light operating model where appropriate

If volume remains too low, the package is only delaying the issue.

### D. Liquidity and covenant discipline
If restructuring savings are delayed while costs are incurred up front:
- near-term liquidity can tighten
- free cash flow may remain negative
- covenant headroom may narrow
- the company may need additional financing support or guarantees

A no-closure deal that looks good on EBITDA but weak on cash is not durable.

The package must be executable under German co-determination and collective bargaining:
- no need for unrealistic speed

## Short answer

## What determines feasibility

### 1) Capacity reduction does not require closure, but it does require underutilized assets to be taken out of the cost base
You can reduce capacity without formally closing a plant by:
- removing a shift,
- mothballing a hall or line,
- consolidating subassemblies,
- narrowing a plant’s product scope,
- or transferring models to another site.

Operationally, though, if a plant keeps most of its fixed infrastructure, overhead, and maintenance burden while running at low utilization, **the cost savings will be limited**. So the question is not “closure or no closure,” but:

> Can the company reduce the number of active lines, shifts, and supporting functions enough to materially lower fixed costs while keeping the site open?

That is feasible in many cases.

### 2) Product reallocation is the main lever for preserving sites
If plants are preserved, the largest durable savings usually come from:
- assigning each site a clearer role,
- moving models to the most efficient plants,
- reducing parallel production of similar products,
- and aligning plant complexity with demand.

This can improve:
- utilization,
- labor productivity,
- logistics efficiency,
- and quality consistency.

For an automotive network, **specialization usually beats duplication**. If every plant remains a generalist, savings are much harder to achieve.

### 3) Load balancing can preserve plants, but only if there is enough volume
If demand is falling, the network can often be stabilized by:
- shifting some volumes between plants,
- smoothing production schedules,
- reducing overtime volatility,
- and improving line balancing.

But load balancing has limits:
- it cannot create demand,
- it cannot fully eliminate fixed site costs,
- and it cannot compensate for large, persistent overcapacity.

If the company is materially underloaded across several German plants, then load balancing alone will not solve the structural problem.

## Durable fixed-cost reduction: what is realistic
A durable reduction can come from several layers:

### Achievable without closures
- fewer shifts
- lower internal complexity
- reduced indirect labor / overhead
- simplified production planning
- procurement savings
- higher line utilization
- automation of selected processes
- consolidation of support functions
- product specialization by plant

### Usually not sufficient on their own
- temporary wage concessions
- short-term working-time reductions
- voluntary exits without footprint change
- one-off cost cuts that do not change the site structure

These measures help, but by themselves they often do **not** create a durable step-change in fixed cost.

- acceptable utilization,
- lower break-even volume,
- and a cost base that can compete over the medium term.

From an industrial finance and restructuring perspective, the current debate has moved to the right question:

## Can capacity reduction without closures work?

### Yes, if the company can do all of the following:
- reduce shifts at some plants;
- remove duplicated product lines;
- specialize plants more sharply;
- transfer volume to the better-fitting sites;
- lower indirect labor and overhead;
- take out complexity in planning, logistics, and support functions;
- and make labour contributions durable enough to matter over several years.

In automotive manufacturing, **specialization and load balancing can preserve plants** while still improving economics. That part of the expert answer is sound.

### But there are limits
A site that remains open but underloaded may still carry:
- maintenance burden,
- fixed overhead,
- depreciation-like site costs,
- logistics complexity,
- and management attention.

So if the company keeps **too many sites with too much residual complexity**, savings will often be **modest to moderate**, not transformational.

---

## My judgement on scale, speed, and durability

### 1) Scale
A no-closure package can close **part** of the gap, but not necessarily the whole gap.

The biggest durable levers are:
- product allocation,
- plant specialization,
- structural overhead reduction,
- procurement,
- and permanent workforce/cost-base change.

The weakest levers are:
- temporary wage restraint,
- short-term working-time measures,
- one-off savings,
- and voluntary exits without footprint change.

If the needed gap is very large, those weaker levers will not be enough.

### 2) Speed
No-closure packages can be faster than closures in one sense:
- they avoid the long legal, operational, and political process of shutting a plant.

But they are slower in another sense:
- deep reallocation of product, equipment, and labour can take time;
- voluntary exits and redeployment are phased;
- working-time arrangements may start quickly but do not generate immediate permanent savings.

So the speed advantage exists mainly if the package is **simple and already prepared**.  
If it depends on reengineering the network, it will still take time.

### 3) Durability
This is the key issue.

A package is durable only if savings come from:
- permanent capacity rationalization,
- permanent simplification,
- permanent overhead reduction,
- and permanent changes in product mix and plant role.

If the solution depends mainly on temporary concessions, then the cost base will **spring back**.

That is why management is right to focus on the **residual structural gap**, not just nominal concessions.

## What would make a no-closure package credible?

A credible package would need all of the following:

### A. A quantified cost bridge
You need a hard bridge from:
- current cost base,
- to target cost base,
- showing savings by lever,
- with assumptions on volume, utilization, wages, overhead, and product mix.

Without this, the dispute stays rhetorical.

### B. Permanent structural measures
Examples:
- site specialization;
- fewer parallel models;
- fewer shifts;
- reduced internal duplication;
- support-function consolidation;
- procurement resets;
- permanent management overhead cuts.

### C. Durable labour contribution
Possible forms:
- pay restraint for a defined period;
- lower bonus or variable compensation;
- working-time flexibility;
- slower step-up in wage cost;
- voluntary exits paired with redeployment;
- changes to time-accounting or shift patterns.

But these must be:
- material,
- measurable,
- and time-bounded or structurally embedded.

### D. Capacity discipline
If all German plants remain open, each site must have a **new role**.  
Otherwise the network remains too fragmented.

### E. A fallback mechanism
If the residual gap remains too large after implementation, there must be a pre-agreed escalation mechanism.  
Without that, the package risks becoming a delay tactic.

## Main risks to the no-closure approach

### 1) Residual overcapacity

### 2) Temporary concessions masquerading as restructuring
A deal can look tough but fail to reset the cost base permanently.

### 3) Complexity drag
