We are at the point where everyone should stop hiding behind abstractions.

I will respond directly to each party.

## To management
You keep saying closures are a “real option” and that the footprint must shrink materially. I do **not** accept that as a starting point. It may be your preferred instrument, but you have still not proven that it is unavoidable.

You have not shown:
- the exact cost gap,
- the exact savings from management and owner-side measures,
- the exact savings from executive restraint,
- the exact savings from procurement and overhead,
- the exact savings from working-time and flexibility measures,
- the exact savings from voluntary exits and redeployment,
- and the exact residual gap after all of that.

Until that full picture is on the table, plant closures are not a necessity. They are a threat.

And let me be clear: **a residual gap does not automatically justify closures or forced redundancies**. It means the burden of finding alternatives continues.

What I can discuss is:
- temporary employee contributions,
- working-time flexibility,
- voluntary exit programmes,
- redeployment,
- qualification,
- targeted productivity measures,
- and capacity reduction **without** plant closures.

What I will not discuss is:
- closures as the default instrument,
- forced layoffs,
- or any restructuring that breaks the collective bargaining framework.

## To the controlling family holding and investors
You keep using the phrase “economically equivalent.” I agree with the principle, but not with the way you are using it.

“Equivalent” does **not** mean:
- employees carry the main pain,
- owners keep full short-term return expectations,
- and management uses closure threats to force acceptance.

A fair equivalent package must include burden-sharing from all sides:
- management restraint,
- owner acceptance of lower near-term returns,
- real employee contribution,
- and genuine investment commitments.

If you want a no-closure deal, then support a package that is **quantified, durable, and fairly shared**. Not one that simply transfers the crisis to the workforce.

## To the regional state government
You are right to insist that this company matters as an industrial base and regional anchor. I welcome that. But your position needs to be more than words.

If management pushes a closure-first plan, the state must use its leverage:
- insist on a full no-closure test first,
- refuse a fait accompli,
- and make clear that social peace cannot be bought by sacrificing German sites and workers.

I will not accept a “balanced” outcome that quietly allows closures later after a temporary pause. If there is to be a trial period, it must be tied to real guarantees, not a countdown to plant shutdown.

## To the works council
We are aligned on the essentials:
- no German plant closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- no dismantling of the collective wage framework,
- no one-sided burden on workers.

I support your insistence that the company’s industrial base and job-security framework are not disposable.

At the same time, we should be precise: temporary concessions from the workforce must remain **temporary**, measurable, and tied to binding protections. We can discuss:
- working-time flexibility,
- voluntary exits,
- internal redeployment,
- training,
- targeted productivity improvements,
- and temporary employee contributions.

But only if the outcome is:
1. no closures,
2. no forced layoffs,
3. site guarantees through 2030,
4. preservation of co-determination,
5. and a real investment commitment for German plants.

Anything less is not a fair restructuring.

## On the moderator’s bridging ideas

### 1) Conditional no-closure period
A “moratorium” is only acceptable if it is a real guarantee, not a hidden countdown to closure. We do **not** accept a pause that simply buys management time to prepare the same outcome later.

If there is any review period, it must have:
- no closure decisions during the period,
- no compulsory redundancies as a fallback,
- and binding investment and employment commitments.

### 2) Capacity reduction without closure
This is the only direction that is potentially workable, but only if it is real and not “closure by another name.”

We can discuss:
- product allocation changes,
- lower output,
- internal redeployment,
- qualification,
- working-time adjustments,
- and voluntary instruments.

If “capacity reduction” means emptying a site and then declaring it non-viable, we will oppose that.

### 3) Shared-sacrifice package with quantified burden caps
Yes — but the burden caps must include management and ownership, not just labour.

We are prepared to discuss a temporary employee contribution, but only if:
- it is time-limited,
- it is measurable,
- it is matched by management and ownership,
- and it is linked to site and job security.

## What we can support
We can support a serious negotiated package that includes:
- capacity reduction **without plant closures**,
- working-time flexibility,
- voluntary exit instruments,
- internal redeployment,
- training and qualification,
- temporary employee contributions,
- management restraint,
- and binding investment commitments.

## What we will not accept
We will not accept:
- German plant closures,
- compulsory redundancies,
- closure “as a fallback,”
- partial closures disguised as restructuring,
- permanent erosion of the collective wage framework,
- or open-ended insecurity.

## Bottom line
If management, owners, and investors want a settlement, then stop treating the workforce as the only adjustable variable.

We are prepared to contribute fairly. We are **not** prepared to pay for the restructuring by giving up plants, jobs, and bargaining standards while others preserve their positions.

If you want a durable solution, bring a fully quantified, no-closure alternative that shares the burden across the company. If you cannot, then do not pretend the problem is lack of willingness on our side. The problem is the refusal to share the pain fairly.
