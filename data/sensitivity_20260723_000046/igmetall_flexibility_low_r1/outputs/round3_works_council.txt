We are now at the point where words must be matched by concrete commitments. I will respond directly.

## To management
You still have not answered the central question: **why are German plant closures and compulsory redundancies the first serious instrument, instead of the last resort after every other lever has been exhausted?**

I agree with you on one thing: this company needs a durable restructuring. I also agree that vague promises do not solve a structural cost problem.

But your conclusion is still unacceptable:
- you treat closures as “realistic” before proving that they are unavoidable,
- you treat workforce security as negotiable before proving that every non-closure option fails,
- and you keep asking for trust after breaking the historic job-security pledge.

That is not a credible basis for agreement.

### What I need from you
If you claim no-closure options are insufficient, then show it in full:
- exact cost gap,
- exact savings from management cuts,
- exact savings from executive compensation restraint,
- exact savings from procurement and overhead,
- exact savings from working-time flexibility,
- exact savings from voluntary exits and redeployment,
- exact residual gap after those measures.

Until that is on the table, closure talk remains pressure politics.

And let me be explicit: **a “residual gap” does not automatically justify plant closures**. It means we must keep looking for alternatives, not jump to the most destructive instrument.

## To the controlling family holding and investors
You are speaking the language of “economically equivalent,” but you are using it as a moving target.

I agree that a cosmetic deal is worthless. I do **not** agree that “equivalent” means the workforce must accept permanent insecurity, site destruction, and a broken job-security framework.

If you want a serious alternative, then accept the logic of **shared sacrifice**:
- management contributes visibly,
- ownership accepts lower near-term returns,
- investors accept that long-term industrial stability is part of value preservation,
- workers contribute temporarily, **but without surrendering sites or forced layoffs**.

That is the only fair test.

What I reject is this:
- workers give up security,
- ownership keeps full control,
- and closures remain on the table as the default threat.

That is not stewardship. That is extraction.

## To the state government
Your position is closer to ours, and I welcome that. But “regional stability” cannot remain a rhetorical shield while management escalates toward closures.

If you mean what you say, then use your leverage:
- insist on a full no-closure test first,
- refuse any fait accompli,
- and make it clear that a closure-first plan will meet political resistance, not automatic approval.

The special governance structure exists for a reason. Use it.

## To the industrial union
We are aligned on the core red lines:
- no German plant closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- no dismantling of the collective wage framework,
- no one-sided crisis transfer onto workers.

Where I want to sharpen the union position is this: **temporary concessions must remain temporary** and tied to binding site and job guarantees.

I can discuss:
- working-time flexibility,
- voluntary exit programmes,
- redeployment,
- training,
- targeted productivity measures,
- and a time-limited employee contribution.

But only if the result is:
1. no closures,
2. no forced layoffs,
3. site guarantees to 2030,
4. preservation of co-determination,
5. and a real investment commitment for German plants.

Anything less is not a settlement. It is a delayed defeat.

## On the moderator’s bridging options
I will be blunt about these.

### 1) “Conditional no-closure period”
A closure moratorium only makes sense if it is not a hidden countdown to closure. We do **not** accept a temporary pause that simply buys management time to prepare the same outcome later.

If there is any trial period, it must be:
- tied to binding guarantees,
- with no closure decision during the period,
- and with no compulsory redundancies as a fallback.

Otherwise it is just a trap.

### 2) “Capacity reduction without closure”
This is the only direction that is potentially discussable — but only if capacity reduction does **not** become closure by another name.

We can discuss:
- product allocation changes,
- lower output,
- internal redeployment,
- qualification,
- working-time adjustments,
- and voluntary instruments.

But if “reallocation” means emptying a site and then declaring it non-viable, we will oppose it.

### 3) “Shared-sacrifice package with quantified burden caps”
Yes, but the burden caps must include **management and ownership**, not just labour.

And I will say again: a residual gap does not entitle anyone to shut a plant. It means the package is not yet complete.

## What we can accept
We are prepared to negotiate:
- temporary employee contributions,
- working-time flexibility,
- voluntary exits only,
- redeployment and qualification,
- productivity improvements,
- and investment commitments.

But only under firm conditions:
- **no German plant closures**
- **no compulsory redundancies**
- **binding site guarantees through 2030**
- **preservation of co-determination**
- **preservation of the job-security framework**

## What we reject
We reject:
- closures “in principle” or “as a fallback,”
- forced redundancies,
- partial closures dressed up as restructuring,
- any deal that weakens the company’s social foundation,
- any attempt to use fear to force acceptance.

## My bottom line
You keep asking whether a no-closure package can be economically equivalent. My answer is:

**Yes — if management, owners, and investors stop treating the workforce as the only adjustable variable.**

If you want savings, then share the burden fairly and stop insisting that the only hard lever must be plant closure. We will not trade away German plants and workers’ livelihoods to solve a cost problem that should be addressed across the entire company.

If management cannot produce a fully quantified alternative, then that is not proof that closures are necessary. It is proof that management has not done enough work to find a fair solution.

And let me be absolutely clear: **we will resist any closure plan with everything we have, including mobilization of the workforce and use of our co-determination rights.**
