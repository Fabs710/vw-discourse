We are now at the point where the discussion must become operational, not rhetorical.

I will be direct.

## Where I agree

I agree with everyone on the central diagnosis:
- the company has a **structural competitiveness problem**;
- a **cosmetic deal** would destroy value;
- the solution must be **durable and legally workable**;
- prolonged conflict is **bad for everyone**;
- and any serious package must include **investment in future products and technology**.

I also agree that burden-sharing should not fall on employees alone. Management, ownership, and investors all have to contribute.

## Where I disagree

I do **not** accept the employee-side position that “no German plant closures, no compulsory redundancies, site guarantees to 2030” can simply be declared as a default and then the economics are somehow expected to resolve themselves.

That is not a solution. It is a red line.

If you want to rule out closures, then you must present a package that is **economically equivalent, durable, and quantified**. So far, I have not seen that.

### To the Works Council
You keep asking management to prove that closures are unavoidable. That is fair. But you are still not answering the harder question: **how do you close a permanent cost and capacity gap without permanent structural change?**

Working-time flexibility, voluntary exits, redeployment, and temporary concessions are useful. I support discussing them. But on their own, they do not usually deliver the scale and durability needed here.

So I reject the assumption that:
- closures are “pressure tactics” only,
- any residual gap can just be absorbed by everyone else,
- and site guarantees can be made unconditional without a credible economic counterpart.

That is not stewardship. That is postponement.

### To the Union
I appreciate that you are at least willing to discuss measurable contributions and shared sacrifice. That is constructive.

But I do not accept a package where:
- workers contribute temporarily,
- management and owners make symbolic concessions,
- and the company still carries excess capacity and structurally high fixed costs.

Temporary measures are not enough unless they close the gap in a way that survives the next downturn and the next bargaining round.

### To Management
I agree with you on the need for real change, but I also want discipline from your side.

You still need to show:
- the size of the cost gap;
- the savings from overhead, procurement, executive restraint, and productivity;
- the savings from employee-side measures;
- the residual gap after those steps;
- and why that residual gap cannot be solved without deeper footprint change.

If closures are truly necessary, prove it. If not, then do not use them as a negotiating crutch.

### To the Regional State Government
You are right to push back against a bad closure-first plan. A one-sided plant shutdown strategy would be socially and politically explosive.

But regional stability cannot become a veto against economic reality. If the cost base remains structurally too high, the company will lose margin, investment capacity, and eventually options. That would hurt the region even more over time.

So I support your call for a serious no-closure test first. I do **not** support turning that into a guarantee that nothing structural can happen.

## My position as controlling shareholder

I support a **serious restructuring**. I do **not** support preserving the current footprint by default.

What I can support:
- negotiated capacity reduction,
- working-time flexibility,
- voluntary exit programmes,
- redeployment and qualification,
- temporary employee contributions,
- management restraint,
- and binding investment commitments.

What I cannot support:
- a deal that leaves the company structurally uncompetitive;
- an arrangement that preserves every legacy structure while calling that a turnaround;
- or any outcome that permanently weakens the enterprise’s value or the owners’ governance ability.

## On the bridging options

### 1) Conditional no-closure period
I am open to a time-limited no-closure review period **only if** it is not a disguised freeze.

That means:
- immediate measurable savings,
- hard operational targets,
- and a clearly defined point at which, if the package fails, deeper structural action remains available.

I will not support a fake moratorium that simply buys time while the same problem grows.

### 2) Capacity reduction without closure
This is the most promising route, but only if it is real.

If you can reduce output, reallocate product lines, and lower fixed cost materially without shutting plants, then that should be examined seriously. But “capacity reduction” cannot be code for keeping all inefficiencies in place.

### 3) Shared-sacrifice package
Yes, but the burden must be shared in a disciplined way:
- employees contribute,
- management contributes,
- ownership accepts lower near-term returns,
- and the remaining gap is closed in a way that actually restores competitiveness.

That is the standard.

## What would move me

A credible package would need to show:
1. a **material and permanent reduction in cost**;
2. a **real reduction in capacity or equivalent footprint impact**;
3. a **durable labour-cost contribution**;
4. **management and owner participation**;
5. and a clear path back to target margins with investment headroom.

If the employee side can show that a no-closure solution truly delivers those outcomes, I will examine it seriously.

If it cannot, then it is better to be honest now: closures and deeper footprint changes remain on the table because the business case has not been solved.

So my answer is:

- **Yes** to restructuring.
- **Yes** to burden-sharing.
- **Yes** to a serious no-closure test.
- **No** to a blanket no-closure commitment without economic proof.
- **No** to any solution that preserves the current cost and capacity structure.
- **No** to any outcome that damages long-term enterprise value or undermines governance control.
