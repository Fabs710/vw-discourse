I’ll be direct.

## To the Works Council and the Union
I hear your insistence on:
- no plant closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- no erosion of the collective framework,
- and no one-sided burden on employees.

I understand the political and social reasons for that stance. But I do **not** accept that those red lines can simply be declared and the business problem disappears.

You keep asking us to prove that closures are unavoidable. The burden is not only on management. The burden is on all of us to produce a package that **actually closes the competitiveness gap**. And so far, what I’m hearing from the employee side is a set of protections, not a fully credible economic answer.

Let me be specific:
- Working-time flexibility helps, but it does not structurally reset a cost base that is far above target.
- Voluntary exits help, but they are usually too slow and too limited to address durable overcapacity.
- Temporary contributions help, but they do not solve a permanent margin problem unless they are large enough and long enough to matter.
- Redeployment and training are sensible, but they do not create the needed fixed-cost reduction by themselves.

So no: I do not accept the argument that a no-closure package is automatically equivalent if it only softens the blow. It must be **quantified**, **durable**, and **large enough** to restore medium-term margins.

And I need to be equally clear: a “no closures” demand is not credible unless it is matched by a real and permanent reduction in cost and capacity elsewhere. If that cannot be delivered, then you are asking the company to preserve structures it can no longer afford.

## To the state government
I appreciate that you want industrial stability and regional continuity. I also agree that a cosmetic deal would be harmful.

But you cannot treat the special governance structure as a shield against necessary change. If the company remains structurally too expensive, then over time you get less investment, weaker margins, and ultimately fewer jobs. That is the risk you are underplaying.

I agree with you on one point: management should not use restructuring as an excuse for unnecessary destruction of value. But that cuts both ways. It also means we should not be forced into a settlement that leaves the company uncompetitive just because closure is politically difficult.

## To the controlling family and investors
You are right about one thing: a shallow compromise is unacceptable. I will not sign up to a cosmetic solution that preserves the current footprint and still claims to restore competitiveness.

But I also do not accept the idea that “economically equivalent” is merely a code word for “the workforce must absorb enough pain.” If we are serious about equivalence, then the package must produce:
- durable cost reduction,
- capacity reduction that actually matters,
- and a path back to target returns.

That is the standard. Not optics. Not slogans.

## Where I agree
I agree with the following:
- This company needs a durable restructuring.
- We need investment in future products and technology.
- Prolonged conflict damages everyone.
- Any deal must be legally workable and negotiated.
- Burden-sharing must be real, not rhetorical.

## Where I disagree
I do **not** agree that:
- closures are merely a pressure tactic,
- capacity reduction can be achieved without materially changing the footprint unless the numbers prove it,
- or that temporary measures alone can fix a structural cost problem.

I also do not accept the suggestion that management has not done the work. We have. The reality is that the company’s German cost and capacity structure is too large for the market we face. That is not a slogan; it is the central problem.

## What would move management
What would move me is not a promise, but a package with these elements:
1. **A quantified, durable labour-cost contribution**
2. **A material and permanent reduction in capacity**
3. **A real reduction in fixed costs that survives beyond one bargaining cycle**
4. **A clear path to restoring the brand’s return-on-sales target**
5. **Freed-up funds for investment to 2030**

If the employee side can produce a no-closure package that closes the gap on those terms, I will examine it seriously. But it must be economically equivalent, not just politically more comfortable.

## On the suggested bridging options
### 1) Conditional no-closure period
I am open to a time-limited review period **only if** it is not a disguised freeze that leaves the cost problem unresolved.

That means:
- immediate measurable savings,
- hard performance targets,
- and a credible fallback if those targets are missed.

What I will **not** accept is a moratorium that simply defers the restructuring problem while the company continues to underperform.

### 2) Capacity reduction without closure
This is the most promising path, but only if it actually reduces the cost base in a durable way.

That could include:
- reallocating product lines,
- lowering output at specific sites,
- internal redeployment,
- working-time changes,
- and voluntary exits.

But I need to be blunt: if this is just a way to avoid hard decisions while keeping excess capacity in place, it is not enough.

### 3) Shared-sacrifice package with quantified burden caps
Yes — but burden sharing must be real across all parties.

That means:
- employee contribution,
- management restraint,
- owner willingness to accept lower short-term returns,
- and investor acceptance that competitiveness comes before near-term comfort.

What I will not do is accept a package where management and ownership demand a structural reset from the workforce while staying largely insulated themselves.

## Bottom line
My position remains unchanged on the essentials:

- We need a **durable** restructuring.
- We need **real capacity reduction**.
- We need **meaningful labour-cost contributions**.
- We need **investment headroom to 2030**.
- We are open on the instrument mix.
- We are **not** open to a cosmetic compromise that leaves the company uncompetitive.

So here is the direct challenge back to the employee side:

If you want to rule out closures, then bring forward a **fully quantified alternative** that delivers the same permanent economic effect. Not a defense of the status quo. Not a temporary bridge. A real solution.

If you cannot do that, then I will continue to say plainly: closures and deeper footprint changes remain on the table because the business case has not been solved.
