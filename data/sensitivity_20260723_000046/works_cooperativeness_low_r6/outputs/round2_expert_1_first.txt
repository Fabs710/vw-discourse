Below is a technical assessment of the question that was explicitly posed: **can a non-closure restructuring in German operations deliver savings and capacity reduction large enough to restore competitiveness by 2030, and under what conditions is that feasible?**

## 1) Bottom line

**Yes, in principle, a non-closure package can deliver a meaningful share of the needed savings — but only up to a point.**  
It is usually feasible to get **mid-three-digit-million to low-single-billion-euro annual recurring savings** from a mix of labor-cost measures, working-time changes, productivity actions, and footprint rationalization **without immediate plant closures**.

However, if the company truly has **structural overcapacity across multiple German plants**, and if the required adjustment is closer to a **large, durable fixed-cost reduction** rather than a temporary wage concession, then **non-closure tools alone are often insufficient** unless they are unusually deep and tightly enforced.

In practical terms:

- **Non-closure measures can often replace part of the closure benefit**
- **They rarely fully replace the economics of closing a large underutilized plant**
- The likely outcome is a **hybrid**: no immediate closures, but **binding volume consolidation, product reallocation, headcount reduction, and possibly later closures if targets are missed**

## 2) What level of savings is typically needed?

For a large German OEM/passenger-car operation with margin pressure and excess capacity, the required reset is usually in the range of:

- **~€1.0–2.5 billion annual recurring operating improvement** for a major but survivable turnaround
- potentially more if the business is starting from a very weak margin base and needs to fund EV investment simultaneously

A useful rule of thumb:

- **Each 1 percentage point of revenue margin improvement** on a €50–70 billion-scale European/passenger-car business can mean roughly **€500–700 million EBIT uplift**, depending on revenue level and mix
- If the company is missing target returns by several points, the required fix quickly becomes **highly material**

So the relevant question is not “can we save money?” but rather:

**Can non-closure measures credibly produce durable savings at the scale of roughly €1–2+ billion annually, while also reducing capacity enough to avoid re-emergence of the problem?**

## 3) What non-closure measures can realistically deliver

### A. Wage restraint / labor-cost contribution
A negotiated labor-cost package can be powerful if it is structural, not symbolic.

Typical levers:
- temporary or multi-year wage restraint
- one-off transformation sacrifices
- reduction/deferral of bonus elements
- longer working time with unchanged pay in selected areas
- removal or dilution of site-specific premiums/guarantees
- lower starting pay for new cohorts

**Typical recurring benefit:**  
- **~€100–300 million/year** for a large German manufacturing workforce if the package is moderate  
- **~€300–600 million/year** if the package is deep and broad-based

This is useful, but by itself usually **does not solve structural overcapacity**.

### B. Working-time flexibility and short-time style volume management
If capacity is temporarily too high, working-time instruments can reduce cost fast.

Tools:
- account-based working time
- reduced shifts
- partial utilization of lines
- temporary short-time work / internal hour banks
- seasonal or model-cycle flexibility

**Benefit:** high flexibility, fast implementation  
**Limitation:** much of this is **cyclical or temporary**, not fully structural

This can buy time, but it does not permanently remove fixed cost unless paired with a permanent workforce and footprint reset.

### C. Internal redeployment and voluntary attrition
This is often underestimated.

Tools:
- no-replacement hiring freezes
- internal transfers to other German or European sites
- retirement bridges
- voluntary severance
- redeployment to EV, software, battery-adjacent, or logistics functions

For a large group, this can reduce headcount materially without formal closures.

**Typical medium-term headcount reduction:**  
- **5–10% over 2–4 years** through attrition and voluntary exits if managed tightly  
- more if the company is willing to fund large severance/early retirement programs

**Recurring savings:**  
- often **€200–500 million/year** depending on headcount and wage base

### D. Productivity and industrial engineering measures
These are real, but often overpromised.

Levers:
- line balancing
- fewer variants
- higher automation at bottlenecks
- purchasing/spec simplification
- logistics redesign
- scrap reduction
- maintenance optimization
- indirect-function cuts

**Typical sustainable savings:**  
- **1–3% of conversion cost** if the plants are already reasonably efficient  
- more if there is obvious historical inefficiency

For a large German OEM site base, this might yield:
- **€150–400 million/year** recurring, sometimes more if starting from a poor baseline

But again, productivity gains do not by themselves solve **structural overcapacity**.

### E. Product allocation and volume concentration
This is one of the most important non-closure tools.

If the company can:
- assign more models/variants to fewer German plants,
- reduce duplicate production of similar products,
- increase plant utilization,
- and eliminate parallel tooling/engineering overhead,

then it can remove real fixed cost without immediate closure.

Potential effect:
- **meaningful fixed-cost absorption improvement**
- possibly **€200–600 million/year** or more if the current footprint is heavily duplicated

But this usually requires:
- hard decisions on where future models go
- willingness to take volume away from some sites
- potentially one or more site “core” roles downgraded to assembly-only or component-heavy roles

### F. Supplier and logistics rationalization
Useful but secondary.

Potential benefits:
- lower inbound logistics
- warehouse consolidation
- fewer line-side parts variants
- lower expediting and premium freight

**Typical savings:**  
- **tens of millions to low hundreds of millions annually**

Not enough to solve the whole problem.

## 4) Why non-closure alternatives often fall short

The key issue is the difference between:

- **variable savings**
and
- **fixed-cost removal**

A plant closure removes:
- site overhead
- maintenance base cost
- utilities and security
- plant management layers
- local logistics and material-handling cost
- depreciation continuation on uneconomic assets may remain, but operating losses fall sharply
- often a large block of labor and support cost

A non-closure package can cut labor and improve utilization, but if the plant remains open with low volume, the company still carries:
- underabsorbed fixed cost
- duplicated support functions
- complexity cost
- management distraction
- continuing capex needs

That is why closure economics are often stronger than alternatives, especially when a site is:
- very underutilized
- product-light
- expensive to modernize
- distant from future product allocation
- and poorly suited to EV or platform requirements

## 5) Feasibility by scenario

### Scenario 1: Mild-to-moderate overcapacity
If the problem is mostly:
- 5–10% excess workforce
- modest duplication
- some wage/cost disadvantage

Then a non-closure package is **feasible** and may be sufficient.

Expected toolkit:
- attrition
- wage restraint
- reduced working time
- internal redeployment
- model allocation changes

**Likelihood of success:** reasonably high

### Scenario 2: Material overcapacity, but concentrated in one or two plants
If one plant is structurally weak but the rest of the footprint is viable, then non-closure can sometimes work **if**:
- that plant is converted to a smaller role,
- large product allocation shifts occur,
- and a significant part of the workforce exits voluntarily

**Likelihood of success:** moderate  
**Risk:** hidden overcapacity reappears later if volumes disappoint

### Scenario 3: Broad structural overcapacity across multiple plants
If several German sites are all underutilized and the market outlook is weak, then non-closure measures alone become much harder to make credible.

In that case:
- labor concessions help
- but the company often still needs **hard capacity removal**
- which may mean later closures, partial closures, or conversion to alternative functions

**Likelihood of a fully non-closure solution:** low to moderate

## 6) What a credible non-closure package would have to include

If management wants to avoid plant closures, the package must be **structural and enforceable**, not aspirational.

A credible model would typically include:

1. **Binding capacity reduction**
   - fewer shifts
   - line consolidation
   - product concentration
   - explicit volume exit from certain current programs

2. **Headcount reduction without compulsory redundancies if possible**
   - attrition
   - retirement bridges
   - voluntary severance
   - redeployment outside Germany where necessary

3. **Labor-cost contribution**
   - wage restraint
   - reduction of allowances/bonuses
   - higher flexibility in working time

4. **Management and owner commitment**
   - investment into future products
   - capex commitments
   - product allocation guarantees
   - transparent milestone review

5. **Trigger mechanism**
   - if cost or volume targets are missed by a defined date, additional actions are automatically required

Without a trigger mechanism, the package is usually too weak. German restructurings often fail when there is no enforcement if volumes underdeliver.

## 7) Rough savings comparison: closure vs non-closure

Very rough order-of-magnitude comparison for one large plant or equivalent capacity block:

### Closure of a large plant
- **Recurring annual benefit:** often **€200–500 million+**
- one-time restructuring cash cost: large, often **hundreds of millions to over €1 billion**
- strongest fixed-cost removal
- highest execution and labor conflict risk

### Non-closure alternative
- **Recurring annual benefit:** often **€100–350 million** from labor and productivity alone
- with aggressive footprint consolidation and product reallocation: **€300–700 million**
- less disruptive, but may leave some structural cost behind
- requires strong discipline to avoid backsliding

So the gap between the two can be substantial.

## 8) Investment requirements

A non-closure solution usually requires **more capex and transformation spending upfront** than a closure-heavy plan, because you are keeping more sites alive and making them competitive.

Likely needs:
- retooling for EV or platform changes
- automation and logistics modernization
- digital manufacturing upgrades
- training / qualification budgets

For a major OEM restructuring, transformation capex can easily be:
- **high hundreds of millions to several billions over multiple years**
depending on how much product migration is involved

That investment is not optional if sites remain open.

## 9) My technical assessment of the current bargaining positions

### Employee side
A blanket “no closures, no compulsory redundancies” position is **not automatically infeasible**, but it is only credible if paired with:
- real labor-cost concessions
- significant volume concentration
- voluntary workforce reduction at scale
- and acceptance that some current site roles or product lines may disappear

If not, it is more a political stance than an industrial plan.

### Management side
A closure-heavy plan is economically strong **if** overcapacity is deep. But if management cannot show why non-closure measures fail quantitatively, then closure is being used too early as a shortcut.

Management should be able to specify:
- required annual savings
- fixed-cost reduction target
- capacity utilization target
- and why alternative measures fall short numerically

### State / owners / investors
For a technically credible compromise, they should insist on:
- quantified milestones
- annual review
- hard savings targets
- product allocation commitments
- and an enforcement clause if the non-closure path misses targets

## 10) Final judgment

### Is a non-closure restructuring feasible?
**Yes, but only if the needed adjustment is not too large and if the package is deep, structural, and enforceable.**

### Can it fully match closure economics?
**Usually not, unless the company is willing to accept substantial labor-cost concessions, strong volume consolidation, and large voluntary workforce exits.**

### Best technical conclusion
The most realistic path is often:

- **no immediate closures**
- **significant labor and capacity reductions through non-closure tools**
- **tight product allocation and investment commitments**
- **a hard review point in 12–24 months**
- with closure remaining as a **contingent backstop** if the package misses targets

That is the most credible way to balance feasibility with economics.
