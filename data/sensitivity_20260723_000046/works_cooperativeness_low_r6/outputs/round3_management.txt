I’ll respond directly.

## First: where I agree
I agree with everyone on one point: **this company needs a durable restructuring, not a cosmetic truce**.  
I also agree that:
- investment in future products and technologies is essential,
- a negotiated solution is preferable to a prolonged conflict,
- and workers should not carry the burden alone if there is a broader burden-sharing package.

So yes, we are aligned on the need for a real solution.

## Where I disagree — fundamentally
What I do **not** accept is the attempt to turn **“no plant closures”** and **“no compulsory redundancies”** into absolute starting points, while also insisting that the company somehow remove a structural cost gap, eliminate overcapacity, and restore margins by 2030.

That is not a restructuring. That is a refusal to face the scale of the problem.

### To the works council
You say plant closures are destruction of value. I say preserving structurally uncompetitive capacity is also destruction of value — just slower and less visible.

You have not answered the central question: **how do you permanently remove enough fixed cost and capacity without closures if the current footprint is too expensive and too large for the market?**

You keep listing instruments:
- shifts
- lines
- redeployment
- qualification
- flexibility
- productivity

Those are all useful. But they do **not automatically add up** to a structurally sufficient solution. If they did, we would not be in this crisis.

And let me be clear:  
**A package that keeps every site open, excludes compulsory redundancies absolutely, and preserves all guarantees unchanged is not a credible restructuring package.**

### To the union
I respect that you want a socially sustainable outcome. But the union’s position still avoids the hard arithmetic.

You say the workforce will contribute through flexibility and wage restraint. Fine — but **what is the measurable, durable economic effect?**  
If you want to avoid closures, then the contribution must be real in euros and in structural capacity reduction, not just in rhetoric.

You also reject any closure backstop. That is where I draw a hard line back at you. A backstop is not coercion; it is what makes a negotiated package credible. Without a backstop, there is no incentive for the package to deliver the required effect. It becomes an open-ended promise with no consequence if it fails.

That is not bankable. It is not governable. And it is not responsible.

### To the state government
I understand your political constraints. But “no closures as default” is not enough. If you reject closures, then you must help deliver **equivalent structural savings**. Otherwise you are just shifting the burden of an unresolved industrial problem into the future.

You say you want a non-closure path first. I am open to that — **if it is quantified, enforceable, and large enough**.  
What I will not accept is a political preference masquerading as an economic solution.

### To the controlling family and investors
You are right to reject cosmetic compromise. I agree with that.

But I want to be equally clear: **shared sacrifice has to be real on the workforce side**.  
If the package only protects sites and delays hard decisions, then it is not value preservation. It is value erosion.

I am prepared to support a non-closure path **only if** it delivers:
- materially lower fixed costs,
- materially lower capacity,
- a real labor-cost contribution,
- and a credible timeline to target margins.

If that cannot be proven, then refusing closures in principle is simply a veto on competitiveness.

## On the expert input
The expert analysis was useful because it confirmed what management has been saying all along: **non-closure measures can cover part of the gap, but not necessarily all of it**.

That is the decisive point.

The expert did **not** say:
- “no closures is always sufficient,” or
- “capacity can be reduced indefinitely without structural hard decisions.”

What the expert said is:
- non-closure tools can deliver meaningful savings,
- but usually only a hybrid structure is fully credible,
- and a backstop is often needed if targets are missed.

That is exactly why I insist on a hard framework, not a soft one.

## What I am actually asking for
I am not demanding closures for their own sake.  
I am demanding a package that can answer five questions clearly:

1. **How much fixed cost comes out?**
2. **How much capacity comes out permanently?**
3. **What is the workforce contribution in euros?**
4. **What is the timeline to return to target margins?**
5. **What happens if the package misses its targets?**

If the answer to the fifth question is “nothing,” then the package is not credible.

## My line on the “closure backstop”
Let me be explicit: the employee side keeps saying a backstop is unacceptable because it creates insecurity. I understand that concern, but it does not solve the company’s problem.

A backstop is not a threat for sport. It is the enforcement mechanism that ensures the restructuring is not watered down into irrelevance.

Without it, we risk a deal that:
- looks politically comfortable,
- keeps everyone smiling for a year,
- and then leaves the company with the same structural problem and less time.

That would be unacceptable.

## What I am prepared to accept
I remain open to a **non-closure first** package if it includes:
- binding capacity reduction,
- substantial labor-cost contributions,
- flexible deployment and working-time measures,
- voluntary exits and internal redeployment,
- product concentration and site specialization,
- real investment commitments,
- and a clear backstop if targets are missed.

That is a serious restructuring path.

## What I will not accept
I will not accept:
- a blanket no-closure rule,
- a no-redundancy doctrine with no structural adjustment,
- a package that preserves every legacy arrangement,
- or a settlement that leaves the company uncompetitive and underfunded for 2030.

That would fail our fiduciary duty.

## Bottom line
We should stop pretending the choice is between “pain” and “no pain.”  
The real choice is between:
- a **hard, enforceable restructuring** that restores competitiveness, or
- a **soft compromise** that postpones the same crisis at a higher cost.

I remain prepared to negotiate a non-closure path — but only if it is economically equivalent enough to solve the problem, and only if it contains an enforceable backstop.
