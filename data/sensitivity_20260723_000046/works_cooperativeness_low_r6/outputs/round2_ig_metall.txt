I will respond directly, because the discussion is drifting toward a dangerous formula: **“credibly durable” as code for plant closures and permanent insecurity.** That is not a solution we can accept.

## Where I agree
I agree with the following points made by others:

- The company **does** need a durable restructuring.
- A temporary patch is not enough.
- Investment in future products and technologies is essential.
- A negotiated solution is better than a prolonged confrontation.
- We need something that is **economically credible**, not just politically convenient.

On those points, we are not far apart.

## Where I disagree
### 1) “No closures can’t be a starting point”
I reject that. For the union, **no plant closures in Germany is a hard position**, not a rhetorical preference. If management wants to reduce capacity, it must do so **without shutting plants** and **without compulsory redundancies**.

If the only way to make the numbers work is to close sites, then management has not yet done the work of designing a genuinely social restructuring.

### 2) “Workers must contribute meaningfully”
Yes, but not as the sole adjustment variable.

We have already shown openness to:
- wage restraint / a negotiated labour-cost contribution,
- working-time adjustments,
- internal redeployment,
- qualification and transformation measures,
- capacity reduction through non-closure means,
- and productivity improvements.

But this can only be discussed if there is a **fair burden-sharing package**. That means management, owners, and capital must also contribute. If the workforce is asked to pay while others remain protected, then that is not negotiation — it is one-sided transfer.

### 3) “The alternative must match closure savings”
I understand the demand for numbers. But I do not accept the implied benchmark that the only “real” savings are closure-based savings. There are other ways to achieve a durable cost reset:

- reducing capacity through shifts and lines rather than site shutdowns,
- flexible working-time models,
- internal mobility across the group,
- training and redeployment,
- targeted productivity agreements,
- cost contributions from labour,
- and binding investment/product commitments from management and owners.

That is the route to a restructuring that is both credible and socially sustainable.

## What is unacceptable
Let me be crystal clear about the limits:

- **No plant closures in Germany**
- **No compulsory redundancies**
- **No dismantling of the collective bargaining framework**
- **No open-ended insecurity for the workforce**
- **No restructuring where employees carry all the pain**

If any proposal depends on those elements, we will not support it.

## To management
You ask for a concrete alternative. Here is the condition for one:

- preserve all German sites,
- exclude compulsory redundancies,
- agree a negotiated employee contribution,
- require management and owners to make binding commitments too,
- and tie the package to real investment and product allocation at the sites.

If you want labour peace, that is the basis. If you want a blank cheque for closures, you will not get it.

## To the controlling family and investors
You both talk about stewardship and value. Then prove it by accepting that long-term value depends on **stable industrial relations, skilled workers, and industrial capacity in Germany**. A deal that destroys trust may produce short-term accounting improvement but damages the company’s long-term strength.

So yes, we will talk about competitiveness.  
Yes, we will talk about cost.  
No, we will not accept closures or forced layoffs as the price.

## To the state government
Your rejection of closures is welcome, but it has to become active backing for a **non-closure restructuring model**, not just a general preference. Use your position to block any attempt to force through plant shutdowns or mass redundancies.

## Our position in one sentence
We are ready to negotiate a **fair shared-sacrifice package** that protects jobs, sites, and the collective wage framework — but **not** a package built on closures, forced layoffs, or unilateral worker sacrifice.

If management wants a deal, bring a plan that:
1. keeps German sites open,
2. excludes compulsory redundancies,
3. includes real contributions from owners and management,
4. preserves co-determination,
5. and secures investment for the future.

Anything else is not a durable settlement; it is a conflict postponed.
