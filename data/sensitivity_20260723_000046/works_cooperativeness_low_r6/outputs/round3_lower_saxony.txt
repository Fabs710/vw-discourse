I will be equally direct.

## Where I agree
I agree with management, the expert, and the capital-side stakeholders on one point: **this company needs a durable, structural restructuring**. A cosmetic deal, or a short truce that leaves the same problems in place, would be irresponsible.

I also agree that:
- investment in future products is essential,
- competitiveness must improve,
- worker contributions can be part of a fair package,
- and the package must be measurable and enforceable.

## Where I disagree
I do **not** accept the repeated attempt to make **plant closures the reference point** and then ask everyone else to prove they can match that damage.

From the state’s perspective, **closures in Germany are not the default instrument**. They are a last resort. They damage regional employment, the supplier base, skills, and the company’s role as an industrial anchor. That is not a minor side effect; it is the central political and economic consequence.

### To management
You are right that the cost gap must be closed. But you have **not yet shown that closures are unavoidable**. The expert has said non-closure measures can deliver substantial savings if they are deep, structural, and enforced. That means the burden is on management to prove, with numbers, why the package cannot work without shutting plants.

What I do **not** accept is:
- using closures as the benchmark of seriousness,
- treating the workforce as the main adjustment variable,
- or presenting “no closures” as if it were the same as refusing restructuring.

It is not. A non-closure restructuring can still be tough, disciplined, and economically meaningful.

### To the works council and union
I support your rejection of closures and compulsory redundancies. That position is legitimate, and I will not water it down.

But I also need to be honest: a viable non-closure path must be more than a political line. It needs to be operationally real. That means:
- capacity reduction through shifts, lines, and product concentration,
- working-time flexibility,
- internal redeployment,
- voluntary exits,
- qualification and transformation measures,
- and a fair labor contribution.

That is the route to protecting sites and jobs **without** handing management a blank cheque for shutdowns.

### On the “closure backstop”
Here I differ sharply from management and investors.

A **closure backstop** is not neutral. In practice it means workers make concessions now while the threat of shutdown remains in the background. That undermines trust and social partnership. It also makes it harder, not easier, to secure the stable industrial relations this company needs.

So I am clear on this:  
**I will not support a deal whose logic is “take concessions now, closures later if management decides.”**

If the package is only viable because it contains a built-in threat to the plants, then it is not a true compromise. It is delayed coercion.

## What I would support
I am prepared to support a restructuring that:
1. **preserves German sites wherever possible**
2. **avoids compulsory redundancies**
3. **uses non-closure instruments first**
4. **includes measurable labor-cost and productivity contributions**
5. **includes binding management/owner commitments on investment and product allocation**
6. **keeps co-determination intact**
7. **is reviewed against hard milestones**

That is a serious restructuring path, and it is the one I will stand behind.

## What I cannot accept
I cannot accept:
- closures as the first resort,
- a plan that guts regional employment,
- any weakening of the state’s co-determination and veto role,
- or a package that makes the workforce bear all the pain while owners and management hide behind “discipline.”

I also cannot accept the idea that protecting German industrial capacity is somehow incompatible with competitiveness. That is false. The company’s long-term strength depends on both.

## On the numbers
I support the call for quantified scenarios. But the numbers must be assessed honestly:
- savings from labor restraint,
- savings from capacity rationalization without closures,
- investment needs,
- residual risks,
- and the long-term value of keeping the industrial base intact.

I would support an independent model comparing:
- a closure-heavy path,
- a deep non-closure path,
- and a hybrid only if that hybrid does **not** normalize shutdowns as the hidden endgame.

## Bottom line
My position remains unchanged:
- **No plant closures in Germany**
- **No compulsory redundancies**
- **No weakening of co-determination or the state’s veto role**
- **Yes to a serious, quantified, enforceable non-closure restructuring**
- **Yes to fair burden-sharing from all sides**
- **Yes to investment and product commitments that secure the company’s future in the region**

If management brings a real package built around that framework, I will engage constructively.  
If it comes back to closures as the default answer, I will oppose it.
