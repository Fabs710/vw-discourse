## Where I disagree

### 1) Management is still using closures as the benchmark to discipline the workforce
That logic is unacceptable.

Management keeps saying: “If you do not match closure economics, then closures remain available.”  
That is not neutral analysis. That is a threat dressed up as governance.

A plant closure is not a clean efficiency tool. It is permanent damage:
- destroyed jobs,
- destroyed skills,
- destroyed local supplier networks,
- destroyed trust,
- and a direct attack on the industrial base of this company.

So no, we will not accept closures as the yardstick that everything else must imitate.

### 2) A closure backstop is not “just enforcement”
This is the core point where we will not move.

Management, investors, and the family claim a backstop is needed for credibility. We say it is a coercive device that poisons the entire process.

If workers sign concessions while the closure threat remains alive, then the “settlement” is just a delayed shutdown plan. It creates:
- permanent insecurity,
- lower morale,
- lower trust,
- more conflict,
- and a weaker company, not a stronger one.

If you want durable restructuring, you do **not** build it on a gun pointed at the plants.

### 3) The burden-sharing discussion is still asymmetrical
Everybody says “shared sacrifice.” In practice, the burden is still being pushed toward labor.

We are told to accept:
- wage moderation,
- flexibility,
- productivity improvements,
- voluntary exits,
- and capacity reduction.

Fine. But where are the corresponding hard commitments from management and owners?
- binding investment in German sites,
- binding product allocation,
- overhead cuts at the top,
- governance guarantees,
- and a real commitment to preserving co-determination and job security?

We do not accept a model where workers make all the risky concessions first while others keep their hardest instruments in reserve.
