## 1) Bottom line

**Yes, in principle, a non-closure package can deliver a meaningful share of the needed savings — but only up to a point.**  
It is usually feasible to get **mid-three-digit-million to low-single-billion-euro annual recurring savings** from a mix of labor-cost measures, working-time changes, productivity actions, and footprint rationalization **without immediate plant closures**.

However, if the company truly has **structural overcapacity across multiple German plants**, and if the required adjustment is closer to a **large, durable fixed-cost reduction** rather than a temporary wage concession, then **non-closure tools alone are often insufficient** unless they are unusually deep and tightly enforced.

In practical terms:

- **Non-closure measures can often replace part of the closure benefit**
- **They rarely fully replace the economics of closing a large underutilized plant**
- The likely outcome is a **hybrid**: no immediate closures, but **binding volume consolidation, product reallocation, headcount reduction, and possibly later closures if targets are missed**

## 2) What level of savings is typically needed?

For a large German OEM/passenger-car operation with margin pressure and excess capacity, the required reset is usually in the range of:

- **~€1.0–2.5 billion annual recurring operating improvement** for a major but survivable turnaround
- potentially more if the business is starting from a very weak margin base and needs to fund EV investment simultaneously

A useful rule of thumb:

- **Each 1 percentage point of revenue margin improvement** on a €50–70 billion-scale European/passenger-car business can mean roughly **€500–700 million EBIT uplift**, depending on revenue level and mix
- If the company is missing target returns by several points, the required fix quickly becomes **highly material**

So the relevant question is not “can we save money?” but rather:

**Can non-closure measures credibly produce durable savings at the scale of roughly €1–2+ billion annually, while also reducing capacity enough to avoid re-emergence of the problem?**

## 3) What non-closure measures can realistically deliver

### A. Wage restraint / labor-cost contribution
A negotiated labor-cost package can be powerful if it is structural, not symbolic.

Typical levers:
- temporary or multi-year wage restraint
- one-off transformation sacrifices
- reduction/deferral of bonus elements
- longer working time with unchanged pay in selected areas
- removal or dilution of site-specific premiums/guarantees
- lower starting pay for new cohorts

**Typical recurring benefit:**  
- **~€100–300 million/year** for a large German manufacturing workforce if the package is moderate  
- **~€300–600 million/year** if the package is deep and broad-based

This is useful, but by itself usually **does not solve structural overcapacity**.

### B. Working-time flexibility and short-time style volume management
If capacity is temporarily too high, working-time instruments can reduce cost fast.

Tools:
- account-based working time
- reduced shifts
- partial utilization of lines
- temporary short-time work / internal hour banks
- seasonal or model-cycle flexibility

**Benefit:** high flexibility, fast implementation  
**Limitation:** much of this is **cyclical or temporary**, not fully structural

This can buy time, but it does not permanently remove fixed cost unless paired with a permanent workforce and footprint reset.

### C. Internal redeployment and voluntary attrition
This is often underestimated.

Tools:
- no-replacement hiring freezes
- internal transfers to other German or European sites
- retirement bridges
- voluntary severance
- redeployment to EV, software, battery-adjacent, or logistics functions

For a large group, this can reduce headcount materially without formal closures.

**Typical medium-term headcount reduction:**  
- **5–10% over 2–4 years** through attrition and voluntary exits if managed tightly  
- more if the company is willing to fund large severance/early retirement programs

**Recurring savings:**  
- often **€200–500 million/year** depending on headcount and wage base

### D. Productivity and industrial engineering measures
These are real, but often overpromised.

Levers:
- line balancing
- fewer variants
- higher automation at bottlenecks
- purchasing/spec simplification
- logistics redesign
- scrap reduction
- maintenance optimization
- indirect-function cuts

**Typical sustainable savings:**  
- **1–3% of conversion cost** if the plants are already reasonably efficient  
- more if there is obvious historical inefficiency

For a large German OEM site base, this might yield:
- **€150–400 million/year** recurring, sometimes more if starting from a poor baseline

But again, productivity gains do not by themselves solve **structural overcapacity**.

### E. Product allocation and volume concentration
This is one of the most important non-closure tools.

If the company can:
- assign more models/variants to fewer German plants,
- reduce duplicate production of similar products,
- increase plant utilization,
- and eliminate parallel tooling/engineering overhead,

then it can remove real fixed cost without immediate closure.

Potential effect:
- **meaningful fixed-cost absorption improvement**
- possibly **€200–600 million/year** or more if the current footprint is heavily duplicated

But this usually requires:
- hard decisions on where future models go
- willingness to take volume away from some sites
- potentially one or more site “core” roles downgraded to assembly-only or component-heavy roles

### F. Supplier and logistics rationalization
Useful but secondary.

Potential benefits:
- lower inbound logistics
- warehouse consolidation
- fewer line-side parts variants
- lower expediting and premium freight

**Typical savings:**  
- **tens of millions to low hundreds of millions annually**

Not enough to solve the whole problem.

## 4) Why non-closure alternatives often fall short

The key issue is the difference between:

- **variable savings**
and
- **fixed-cost removal**

A plant closure removes:
- site overhead
- maintenance base cost
- utilities and security
- plant management layers
- local logistics and material-handling cost
- depreciation continuation on uneconomic assets may remain, but operating losses fall sharply
- often a large block of labor and support cost

A non-closure package can cut labor and improve utilization, but if the plant remains open with low volume, the company still carries:
- underabsorbed fixed cost
- duplicated support functions
- complexity cost
- management distraction
- continuing capex needs

That is why closure economics are often stronger than alternatives, especially when a site is:
- very underutilized
- product-light
- expensive to modernize
- distant from future product allocation
- and poorly suited to EV or platform requirements

- internal redeployment
- model allocation changes

**Likelihood of success:** reasonably high

**Likelihood of success:** moderate  
**Risk:** hidden overcapacity reappears later if volumes disappoint

### Scenario 3: Broad structural overcapacity across multiple plants
If several German sites are all underutilized and the market outlook is weak, then non-closure measures alone become much harder to make credible.

In that case:
- labor concessions help
- but the company often still needs **hard capacity removal**
- which may mean later closures, partial closures, or conversion to alternative functions

**Likelihood of a fully non-closure solution:** low to moderate

## 1) How I would frame the economic test

**NPV of recurring savings**

- one-time restructuring costs hit early,

## 4) NPV logic: why timing matters so much

A simple DCF framing:

\[
NPV = \sum_{t=1}^{T} \frac{Savings_t - OngoingCosts_t}{(1+r)^t} - UpfrontCosts

- \(Savings_t\) = recurring annual savings

A package that produces:
- €400m annual savings starting immediately

- €600m annual savings starting 3 years later

So in negotiations, delay is not neutral. Delay destroys NPV.

- product allocation
- financing support where relevant
- disciplined overhead reduction above plant level

### 5. Enforceable backstop
If savings fall short:
- more aggressive steps are triggered
- including closure or deeper footprint action if needed

Without a backstop, the package is often not bankable from a restructuring perspective.

---

## 8) Break-even intuition

The break-even question is:

**How much recurring annual saving is required from the non-closure package to justify forgoing closure?**

That depends on:
- closure savings forgone
- one-time costs avoided
- residual inefficiency accepted
- and the probability of future remedial action

In general:
- if non-closure can capture **70%–90% of closure economics**, it may be rational
- if it can only capture **40%–60%**, closure usually wins on pure NPV
- if it creates a “temporary peace” but no structural fix, the future risk-adjusted NPV can be poor even if near-term cash burn is lower

---

## 9) Practical scenario view

### Best case for non-closure
- overcapacity is moderate, not deep
- labor is willing to make structural concessions
- product allocation can be concentrated
- voluntary exits are substantial
- capex can modernize remaining sites efficiently

In that case, non-closure can be financially acceptable.

### Worst case for non-closure
- overcapacity is broad across multiple sites
- wage and flexibility concessions are small
- product allocation remains fragmented
- capex rises to keep all sites alive
- underutilization persists

In that case, non-closure is likely suboptimal versus closure or later closure.

### Most likely case
A non-closure package buys time and captures part of the savings, but the company still needs:
- a hard review point
- and a binding consequence if targets are missed

That is usually the financially disciplined answer.

---

## 10) My bottom-line financial judgment

### If the question is:
**Can a non-closure package be economically rational?**
- **Yes**

### If the question is:
**Can it fully replace the economics of closing a materially underutilized plant?**
- **Usually not**

### If the question is:
**What is the most financially robust compromise?**
- **A deep non-closure package with quantified savings, upfront implementation, and a credible backstop**

That structure best balances:
- near-term cash preservation
- long-run competitiveness
- and downside protection

### Independent restructuring assessment: what is technically credible

I’ll stay within the economic and operational side only.

## 1) The core technical issue
The parties are arguing about whether a **deep non-closure package** can deliver enough **durable, recurring savings** to substitute for plant closures.

From a plant-economics perspective, the answer is:

- **Yes, partly**
- **Rarely fully**
- **Only if the package is unusually deep and front-loaded**

In German automotive restructuring, closures are often valued by management because they remove:
- fixed labor and overhead,
- maintenance and utilities,
- site management,
- indirect staff,
- and future capex obligations.

Non-closure measures can reduce a large share of the labor and overhead burden, but they usually leave:
- site fixed costs,
- duplicated logistics,
- some maintenance burden,
- and more of the indirect structure intact.

So the question is not “can non-closure save money?”  
It can. The question is whether it can save **enough, fast enough, and permanently enough**.

---

## 2) Typical savings ranges by measure

For a large German OEM passenger-car plant, the rough recurring annual savings potential usually looks like this:

### A. Working-time and shift reductions
- **Savings:** meaningful, but usually limited unless volume loss is real
- **Range:** roughly **€20k–€45k per affected production employee per year** in fully loaded cost effect, but only if shifts are actually removed and staffing is cut, not just hours redistributed
- **Risk:** if demand later recovers, the savings are partially reversible

### B. Voluntary exits / natural attrition
- **Savings:** strong on paper, slower in execution
- **Range:** often **€80k–€120k per job eliminated per year** in fully loaded recurring cost, depending on mix and indirect share
- **Constraint:** only works if headcount reduction is real and sustained

### C. Internal redeployment / group absorption
- **Savings:** often less than expected
- **Reason:** you may eliminate one site’s job, but if the group absorbs people elsewhere, the net group savings are smaller
- **Best use:** avoid compulsory redundancies, not as the main source of economic saving

### D. Wage moderation / contribution freeze / bonus sacrifice
- **Savings:** immediately visible, but usually not enough alone
- **Range:** for a broad workforce, maybe **2–6% of payroll** recurring in a negotiated deal, depending on design
- **Constraint:** good for closing part of the gap, not a substitute for capacity reduction

### E. Productivity improvements / process simplification
- **Savings:** potentially large, but slow and execution-dependent
- **Range:** **3–8% productivity improvement** over a few years is realistic in a strong program; more is possible but harder
- **Constraint:** if volume continues to fall, productivity gains can be offset by lower absorption

### F. Site conversion / functional consolidation
- **Savings:** potentially substantial
- **Range:** if a site is narrowed to a specific function, recurring savings can approach closure economics for the relinquished functions, but not always for the full site
- **Constraint:** capex needed; labor politics aside, the technical bottleneck is often equipment, logistics, and product architecture

---

## 3) What closures usually deliver that non-closure measures do not
A closure can eliminate:
- direct labor,
- indirect labor,
- management layers,
- utilities,
- maintenance,
- local logistics complexity,
- and future replacement capex.

A non-closure deal typically leaves some combination of:
- partial fixed site overhead,
- duplicated support functions,
- residual facilities costs,
- and higher operating complexity.

So even a very good non-closure package often reaches only **60–85% of closure economics** on a recurring basis, unless it includes a major functional consolidation or site conversion.

---

## 4) What a credible non-closure package would need
For the employee side’s position to be economically credible, the package would need all of the following:

### Minimum technical conditions
1. **Permanent capacity reduction**
   - not just “flexibility”
   - actual removal of output capability, shifts, or lines

2. **Headcount reduction with net group savings**
   - voluntary exits only is fine if the numbers are large enough
   - if redeployment merely shifts workers elsewhere, savings evaporate

3. **Indirect cost reduction**
   - not only blue-collar labor
   - overhead, engineering support, admin, and local management must shrink too

4. **Product concentration**
   - fewer models, fewer variants, fewer changeovers
   - this is often where the real productivity gain comes from

5. **One-time restructuring investment**
   - tooling moves, IT, logistics redesign, training, and line reconfiguration
   - without this, the non-closure plan is usually too shallow

### Hard financial test
A credible package should deliver:
- **at least mid-single-digit percent reduction in group operating cost base** in the affected European operations,
- and preferably **annual recurring savings in the several hundred million euro range** for a large OEM-branded passenger-car operation.

If the required structural gap is larger than that, non-closure alone becomes unlikely.

---

## 5) Capacity reduction: what is technically realistic
If the company has structural overcapacity, the practical options are:

### Option 1: remove shifts / lower utilization
- Fastest tool
- Best for near-term savings
- But only works if demand truly stays lower

### Option 2: product consolidation across sites
- Stronger structurally
- More durable
- Requires platform and logistics alignment
- Usually the best non-closure tool if closures are politically blocked

### Option 3: convert sites to narrower roles
- e.g. component manufacture, battery-related processes, special assembly, or CKD-type operations
- Can preserve the site but not the original footprint
- Technically feasible, but often requires new capex and supplier/logistics redesign

### Option 4: keep sites open but reduce fixed labor without equivalent output loss
- This is the weakest option
- You can do it temporarily through wage measures and voluntary exits
- But if the plant remains structurally too large, the problem returns

---

## 6) My estimate of the threshold where non-closure becomes insufficient
A non-closure package becomes insufficient when one or more of these is true:

### A. The residual cost gap is still material after concessions
If after labor contribution, productivity measures, and capacity rationalization, the company is still left with a structural gap of roughly:
- **more than ~€300m–€500m per year** in a large brand operation, or
- a margin shortfall that remains clearly below target by 2030,

then a no-closure solution is probably not enough on its own.

### B. Required savings depend on reversible measures
If the savings rely heavily on:
- temporary hours cuts,
- one-off bonuses being waived,
- or a short-term hiring freeze,

then the package is not durable enough.

### C. The package does not remove real capacity
If sites remain open but are only “managed harder,” with no real line, shift, or product consolidation, then the savings are usually too soft.

### D. The implementation requires continuous conflict
If the program cannot run without repeated disputes, the economic savings are quickly eaten by friction, delay, and loss of management attention.

## 7) Does a closure backstop make technical sense?
Technically, yes — as an **enforcement mechanism**.  
Economically, it is often used because it prevents a weak compromise.

But there is a narrow technical warning:
- if the backstop is too vague, it does not credibly enforce anything;
- if it is too automatic, it can undermine implementation and destabilize operations before the review point.

So the best version is not “instant closure if targets are missed.”  
It is:
- measurable milestones,
- a pre-defined remediation phase,
- then escalation if the numbers are missed.

That is the technically cleanest form of backstop.

  - deeper non-closure cuts, or
  - a harder restructuring path including closure options

## Independent restructuring finance view

The key question is not whether non-closure measures save money; they do. The question is whether they produce **enough recurring savings, fast enough, with enough durability**, to justify avoiding closures as the company’s economic backstop.

### 1) Closure-heavy path

- Highest recurring savings
- Largest one-time restructuring cost
- Strongest permanent capacity removal
- Highest execution and labor-relations risk

**Typical economics**
- Recurring savings: highest benchmark
- One-time costs: severance, asset write-downs, decommissioning, transfer/logistics, remediation
- NPV: can be attractive if the company truly has excess capacity and if the savings are durable
- Downside: industrial conflict, implementation delay, political interference

### 2) Deep non-closure path
**Financial profile**
- Lower one-time cost than closure
- Lower recurring savings than closure
- Better labor stability if negotiated successfully
- More operational complexity

**Typical economics**
- Savings from:
  - shift reductions
  - working-time flexibility
  - voluntary exits
  - wage moderation
  - productivity gains
  - product concentration
- Weak point:
  - residual site fixed costs remain
  - indirect overhead tends to shrink less than direct labor
  - savings can be partly reversible

**Financial conclusion**
- This can be credible if the company’s required gap is moderate and if the package is deep.
- It becomes weak if management needs a large fixed-cost reset and the package does not materially reduce capacity.

### 3) Hybrid path with escalation trigger
**Financial profile**
- Best compromise between credibility and flexibility
- Allows a non-closure first phase
- Preserves an enforcement mechanism if targets are missed

**Typical economics**
- Often the most investable structure
- Lets parties capture near-term savings from non-closure measures
- Keeps a backstop for underperformance
- But it only works if the trigger is objective and pre-agreed

## The practical NPV issue

From a DCF standpoint, the relevant comparison is:

**NPV of savings from restructuring**  
minus  
**one-time restructuring costs**  
minus  
**expected downside from disruption / delay / underperformance**

A closure-based plan often wins on pure recurring savings, but loses value if:
- it takes too long to implement,
- strikes delay realization,
- political vetoes reduce feasibility,
- or asset disposal costs are higher than expected.

A non-closure plan often wins on implementation speed and lower upfront cost, but loses value if:
- savings are too soft,
- capacity remains excessive,
- or the package depends on temporary measures.

A non-closure package becomes **financially insufficient** when, after all negotiated measures, it still leaves:
- a large residual cost gap,
- materially under-target margins by 2030,
- or savings that rely heavily on reversible measures.

[Expert: Independent automotive restructuring exp — Round 4]
**Independent restructuring expert response**

The key technical question is whether a **deep non-closure package** can close the structural gap **without** a plant-closure backstop. In most large German OEM cases, the answer is:

- **Yes, partially and sometimes substantially**
- **No, not always fully**
- **And usually only if the package is exceptionally deep, front-loaded, and enforceable**

## 1) Realistic savings potential from non-closure measures

For a large German passenger-car operation with structurally high fixed costs, a credible non-closure package can typically deliver:

- **Workforce cost moderation / productivity:** ~5% to 12% of controllable labor-related cost base
- **Shift reductions / line consolidation / capacity rationalization:** material fixed-cost relief, often equivalent to **10% to 20% of plant-level conversion and support cost**
- **Indirect overhead cuts / span-of-control / administrative simplification:** often another **2% to 5% of SG&A-type cost**
- **Voluntary exits / age-based attrition / redeployment:** can remove headcount, but the cash savings depend on whether replacements are avoided and whether redeployment is real
- **Product concentration:** can improve utilization and reduce complexity, but only if volume is actually transferred, not merely promised

In aggregate, a strong no-closure package can often cover **roughly 60% to 85%** of the savings that management would otherwise seek from a closure-based restructuring.

## 2) What closure usually adds that non-closure measures struggle to match

Plant closure tends to contribute savings from:

- **full elimination of fixed site overhead**
- **full removal of maintenance, utilities, logistics, and local support**
- **removal of underutilized lines and buildings**
- **deeper permanent headcount reduction**
- **simpler long-term operating model**

That is why closures often produce a cleaner result on paper. For a German carmaker plant, a closure can generate very large recurring savings, but the realized value depends on:

- severance and transfer costs
- remediation and decommissioning costs
- rerouting/logistics costs
- loss of learning curve and disruption
- political and implementation delay
- risk of volume loss elsewhere in the network

So closures are not “free savings”; they are **high-savings, high-friction, high-disruption** measures.

## 3) When a no-closure package is technically credible

A no-closure settlement can be credible if it includes all of the following:

1. **Clear capacity reduction**
   - not just efficiency rhetoric
   - actual removal of shifts, lines, or product families
   - a measurable reduction in annual build capacity

2. **Recurring labor cost reduction**
   - wage moderation
   - longer working-time flexibility
   - lower entry wages for future cohorts if legally and collectively agreed
   - voluntary exit programs with real replacement avoidance

3. **Indirect cost reduction**
   - fewer layers
   - fewer interfaces
   - leaner support functions
   - procurement and logistics simplification

4. **Product and volume concentration**
   - enough volume allocation to make remaining sites efficient
   - otherwise the network remains structurally oversized

5. **Investment commitment**
   - tooling, platform allocation, and industrialization spending
   - without this, a no-closure deal becomes a delay mechanism

6. **Enforcement**
   - milestone-based review
   - automatic deepening of measures if targets are missed
   - not necessarily closures immediately, but some hard backstop must exist if capital is to treat the package as bankable

## 4) My technical threshold view

If the question is: **At what point is non-closure insufficient?**

A practical threshold is when a non-closure package can only cover **less than about two-thirds of the required durable savings** by the main decision horizon, or when it fails to reduce capacity enough to restore utilization to an efficient band.

As a rule of thumb:

- If non-closure measures can achieve **at least ~70% to 80% of required recurring savings**, with durable capacity cuts and real labor-cost contribution, then a no-closure path can be technically defendable.
- If the package is only **50% to 60%** of what is needed, then it is usually **not** durable enough unless there is a strong additional offset from volume growth or major product improvement.
- If the residual gap is still large after all non-closure measures, then the company will likely need either:
  - a closure backstop,
  - or a materially different product/volume plan.

## 5) Enforceability matters as much as the headline savings

A package without a credible enforcement mechanism is often not sufficient, even if the numbers look decent initially. Why?

Because in automotive restructuring, the failure mode is usually:

- savings are delayed,
- utilization stays too low,
- productivity gains are not fully captured,
- and the company reopens the same problem 18 to 36 months later.

So from a restructuring-design perspective, the most robust non-closure settlement is not “no closure ever,” but:

- **no closure in the settlement period**
- **hard milestone review**
- **pre-agreed escalation**
- **possibly staged capacity actions that are reversible at first, then deeper if missed**

## 6) Bottom line

My technical view is:

- **Non-closure measures can be enough if the package is deep, quantified, and front-loaded.**
- **They are often not enough if they are vague, delayed, or depend on optimistic volume recovery.**
- A credible no-closure solution usually needs to achieve **roughly 70%+ of the structural savings gap** and a meaningful share of the capacity reduction.
- If it cannot do that, then the package is likely **insufficient on financial grounds**, regardless of its political acceptability.

## My core view
A **deep non-closure package** can be financially credible **if** it delivers:

- enough **recurring savings**
- enough **capacity reduction**
- enough **labor-cost relief**
- and enough **implementation certainty**

In practice, the main question is not “closures or no closures” in the abstract. It is whether the non-closure package can close **most of the structural gap on a recurring basis** after implementation costs.

## Technical threshold
Based on the expert’s range, I would use the following rule of thumb:

- **Below ~60% of required recurring savings:** generally not durable enough
- **Around ~70% to 80%:** potentially credible if implementation is fast and execution risk is low
- **Above ~80%:** usually financially defensible as a stand-alone non-closure path

That is not a legal or political threshold; it is a **financial durability threshold**.

## What makes closures hard to beat financially
Closures typically produce the cleanest recurring savings because they remove:

- fixed site overhead
- underutilized capacity
- maintenance and utilities
- local support functions
- some duplicated logistics and administrative cost

But closures also bring significant offsets:

- severance and termination costs
- decommissioning and remediation
- transition and logistics disruption
- possible volume loss elsewhere in the network
- labor conflict and delay risk

So the closure case is not just “higher savings.” It is **higher gross savings with higher friction and higher upfront cost**.

## Why a non-closure package can still win in NPV terms
A non-closure package can have a better NPV if:

1. **implementation is faster**
   - savings begin earlier
   - payback starts sooner

2. **upfront cash costs are lower**
   - fewer shutdown and remediation costs

3. **execution risk is lower**
   - less litigation / industrial disruption / delay

4. **the investment plan is preserved**
   - better retention of volume, skills, and launch capability

If the no-closure package reduces cash burn earlier and avoids a long implementation lag, it can outperform a closure-heavy plan even if the gross savings are somewhat smaller.

## Main downside risk
The biggest downside risk for a non-closure package is **under-delivery on recurring savings**.

Typical failure modes:

- promised headcount reductions do not fully materialize
- productivity gains are slower than expected
- capacity reductions are too shallow
- volume remains too fragmented
- cost reductions are partly one-off rather than structural

If that happens, the company ends up with:
- some concession fatigue
- limited savings realization
- and no structural fix

That is the worst NPV outcome: **costly compromise without durable benefit**.

## Break-even logic
The no-closure package becomes financially preferable if the present value of:

- recurring labor savings
- overhead savings
- capacity rationalization benefits
- and avoided closure costs

exceeds the present value of:

- severance and transition costs
- incentive payments
- retained fixed costs from keeping sites open
- and any residual underutilization

A simplified break-even test is:

> **NPV of non-closure savings – NPV of non-closure implementation costs**
> compared with
> **NPV of closure savings – NPV of closure implementation costs**

The key sensitivities are:

- timing of savings
- magnitude of recurring savings
- discount rate
- volume recovery assumptions
- duration of labor peace
- residual capacity utilization

## Sensitivity view
The non-closure package is most sensitive to:

1. **timing**
   - if savings are delayed by 12–24 months, NPV falls sharply

2. **permanence**
   - one-off savings are much less valuable than recurring reductions

3. **utilization**
   - if remaining plants stay underfilled, fixed costs persist

4. **enforcement**
   - without hard milestones, planned savings often erode in execution

5. **volume assumptions**
   - if EV demand or European demand weakens further, the package may be insufficient

## Concrete compromise positions

### Proposal 1: “No-closure settlement period” with milestone-based deepening, not closure
A time-bound arrangement could be structured so that:

- **no plant closures in Germany during the settlement period**
- **no compulsory redundancies during that same period**
- **mandatory capacity reduction and cost removal** through shifts, lines, product concentration, redeployment, and voluntary exits
- **milestone reviews** at fixed dates
- if targets are missed, the escalation path is **additional non-closure measures first**:
  - more shift reductions
  - further line consolidation
  - additional overhead cuts
  - more management/owner contributions
  - tighter product concentration

This would give management and investors an enforcement mechanism **without a closure fallback in the first instance**.

### Proposal 2: “Binding non-closure package” with reciprocal commitments
A package could be made credible by pairing labor concessions with hard commitments from the top:

- workforce contribution:
  - flexibility
  - voluntary exits
  - productivity agreement
  - redeployment
  - qualification
- counterpart commitments:
  - binding investment in German sites
  - binding product allocation
  - management overhead cuts
  - owner-funded support measures
  - co-determination in implementation
  - site guarantees for the settlement period

This bridges the burden-sharing dispute by making labor contributions conditional on **visible, enforceable commitments from management and owners**.

### Proposal 3: “Two-step restructuring” with objective trigger, not automatic closure
A staged model could separate:

- **Phase 1:** deep non-closure restructuring with hard targets and a fixed deadline
- **Phase 2:** only if targets are missed, an **independent mediator/expert** determines whether the shortfall can still be closed through additional non-closure measures, revised product allocation, or added owner contributions

This would replace an immediate closure backstop with an **objective diagnostic trigger**, reducing the sense of coercion while preserving economic discipline.

## 4. Genuine conflicts
1. **Closure benchmark vs. no-closure principle**  
   Management, family, and investors treat closure economics as the benchmark for sufficiency; the works council, union, and state reject closures as the reference point.

3. **Absolute job/site security vs. economic flexibility**  
   Employee-side stakeholders want no plant closures and no compulsory redundancies; management and owners will not commit to absolute guarantees if savings prove insufficient.

4. **Risk allocation**  
   Labor wants concessions only if management, owners, and the state share real commitments; capital-side actors argue that workers must share more of the burden because the business case is not otherwise credible.
