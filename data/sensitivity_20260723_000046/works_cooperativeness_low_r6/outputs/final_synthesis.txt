## 1. Decision context
The company needs a durable restructuring to restore competitiveness, reduce structural overcapacity, and free up investment capacity for future products. The live dispute is not about whether change is needed, but whether it can be achieved **without German plant closures and compulsory redundancies** while still delivering enough recurring savings to satisfy management, owners, and capital markets.

Governance makes compromise unusually difficult: codetermination gives the workforce an effective veto, the family holding has controlling influence, and the state government has blocking power and strong political legitimacy. This means any durable settlement must be both economically credible and politically enforceable.

## 2. Stakeholder assembly
- **Group & Brand Management:** highest operational responsibility; strong legitimacy on business performance; limited unilateral power.
- **Controlling Family Holding:** highest governance power; high legitimacy as controlling owner.
- **Regional State Government:** high legitimacy and blocking influence; politically committed to avoiding closures.
- **Works Council:** very high internal legitimacy and formal veto power through codetermination.
- **Industrial Union:** high mobilizing power and bargaining legitimacy; can escalate through strikes.
- **Institutional Investors:** significant financial legitimacy, but weaker formal governance rights.

## 3. Final positions
### Group & Brand Management
Management insists the current German footprint is structurally too expensive and that restructuring must remove enough cost and capacity to be bankable. It is open to a deep non-closure package, but only if it includes measurable savings, capacity reduction, and a credible consequence for underperformance.

### Works Council
The works council accepts the need for durable restructuring and meaningful savings, but rejects plant closures, compulsory redundancies, and any closure backstop. It supports non-closure tools such as shift reductions, line rationalization, redeployment, and voluntary exits, but only within a framework of binding site and job protection.

### Industrial Union
The union aligns closely with the works council: it accepts the need for competitiveness and employee contribution, but refuses to accept closures as the benchmark or a shutdown threat as enforcement. It is willing to negotiate flexibility, wage moderation, and voluntary exits, provided the outcome protects sites, avoids forced layoffs, and preserves collective bargaining standards.

### Regional State Government
The state wants a non-closure restructuring and sees closures as politically and economically damaging. It supports a quantified, enforceable package, but does not want a hidden shutdown path; it emphasizes regional industrial stability, co-determination legitimacy, and investment anchoring.

### Controlling Family Holding
The family holding prioritizes competitiveness, margins, and value preservation. It is open to a non-closure solution, but only if it is deep, measurable, and backed by an escalation mechanism; it rejects an absolute no-closure rule that could lock in uncompetitive capacity.

### Institutional Investors
Investors support durable restructuring and recurring savings, but will not back a deal that leaves the cost base structurally uncompetitive. They want quantified euro savings, capacity reduction, and bankable enforcement; they are open to non-closure, but only if it is economically sufficient.

## 4. Genuine conflicts
1. **Closure benchmark vs. no-closure principle**  
   Management, family, and investors treat closure economics as the benchmark for sufficiency; the works council, union, and state reject closures as the reference point.

2. **Enforcement through fallback vs. enforcement through deeper non-closure measures**  
   Capital-side stakeholders want a credible consequence if targets are missed, while labor and the state view any closure backstop as coercive and trust-destroying.

3. **Absolute job/site security vs. economic flexibility**  
   Employee-side stakeholders want no plant closures and no compulsory redundancies; management and owners will not commit to absolute guarantees if savings prove insufficient.

4. **Risk allocation**  
   Labor wants concessions only if management, owners, and the state share real commitments; capital-side actors argue that workers must share more of the burden because the business case is not otherwise credible.

## 5. Areas of convergence
- All parties accept that the company needs **durable restructuring**, not a short-term patch.
- All parties accept that **investment in future products and technologies** is essential.
- There is broad support for **non-closure measures**: capacity reduction, working-time flexibility, redeployment, qualification, productivity gains, and voluntary exits.
- Everyone agrees the package must be **quantified and credible**, not rhetorical.
- Everyone recognizes that prolonged conflict would be value-destructive.

## 6. Key trade-offs for the decision-maker
- **Competitiveness vs. social legitimacy:** deeper structural savings improve margins but increase resistance.
- **Speed vs. consent:** faster action is easier with closures, but consent is harder to secure.
- **Bankability vs. trust:** enforcement improves credibility for capital, but closure threats destroy trust with labor and the state.
- **Short-term pain vs. long-term resilience:** a deep non-closure settlement may preserve industrial capacity but may not fully match closure economics.
- **Governance stability vs. managerial flexibility:** the company’s stakeholder structure favors negotiated solutions, not unilateral execution.

## 7. Integrative pathways
1. **Time-limited no-closure settlement period**  
   No plant closures and no compulsory redundancies for a defined period, with binding capacity and cost targets, milestone reviews, and escalation through additional non-closure measures first.

2. **Deep non-closure package with reciprocal commitments**  
   Labor contributes through flexibility, voluntary exits, and productivity gains; management and owners commit to investment, product allocation, and overhead reduction; the state anchors governance and monitoring.

3. **Site-specific differentiated restructuring**  
   Stronger guarantees for sites with future product allocation, and more intensive consolidation measures at weaker sites, without an immediate closure decision.

4. **Independent mediation with quantified benchmark**  
   Use an expert/mediator to translate the no-closure option into quantified savings and compare it against closure economics, enabling a decision based on numbers rather than positional claims.

## 8. Open questions
- What is the **minimum recurring savings** required to restore competitiveness by 2030?
- How much of that gap can non-closure measures realistically cover?
- What capacity reduction is necessary, and can it be achieved without closures?
- What specific labor contributions are acceptable in euro terms?
- What binding investment and product commitments will management and owners make?
- What enforcement mechanism is acceptable if targets are missed, if not closures?
- Which sites are strategically essential, and which can be functionally consolidated?

## 9. Recommended next step
Commission an **independent quantified scenario comparison** of:
1. closure-heavy restructuring,
2. deep non-closure restructuring,
3. a staged hybrid package.

The comparison should specify recurring savings, upfront costs, capacity reduction, implementation timing, and downside risk. This should be paired with a negotiating mandate for a **time-limited no-closure settlement period** with hard milestones and non-closure escalation measures as the first fallback.

## 10. Emergent insights that only appeared through multi-turn interaction
- The real deadlock is **not on the need for restructuring**, but on **whether credibility requires a closure backstop**.
- Labor and the state do not merely dislike closures; they see a closure fallback as **contaminating the bargaining process** and destroying trust.
- Capital-side stakeholders are not insisting on closures per se; they are insisting on **economically sufficient, enforceable savings**.
- The expert clarified a useful threshold: a non-closure package may be viable if it captures roughly **70%+ of the structural savings gap** and is front-loaded and measurable.
- The interaction revealed that “shared sacrifice” is still incomplete unless management, owners, and the state also make **visible, binding commitments**.
- A purely ideological “no closures ever” position is less stable than a **time-bound no-closure arrangement with measurable milestones**, because it preserves labor legitimacy while addressing capital’s demand for discipline.
