Thank you. Let me respond plainly and from the perspective of the controlling owners.

I agree with several points raised by the works council, the union, and the state:

- The company needs a durable future, not a temporary patch.
- Investment in new products and technologies is non-negotiable.
- A negotiated solution is preferable to a prolonged conflict.
- Forced redundancies are highly undesirable if a credible alternative exists.

But I also need to be equally direct about what I do **not** accept.

## What is not acceptable
A restructuring that preserves the full current cost base, the full current capacity, and every existing structure simply is not a restructuring. It would be value erosion by delay.

As controlling shareholders, our responsibility is long-term stewardship of the enterprise. That means protecting jobs where possible, yes — but it also means protecting the company’s competitiveness, margins, and ability to invest. If we fail to restore cost discipline, we will damage the very asset that supports all of these stakeholders.

So:

- **No, I do not accept a blanket “no plant closures” rule as a starting point.**
- **No, I do not accept a deal that only asks for symbolic employee concessions while leaving the structural cost problem untouched.**
- **No, I do not accept a settlement that merely postpones hard decisions into the next crisis.**

## Where I agree
I do agree that closures should not be the first or automatic reflex. If there is a credible package that achieves the necessary savings and capacity correction through other means, that should be preferred.

That means I am open to:

- capacity reductions without immediate closures where they are economically real,
- working-time changes,
- internal redeployment,
- qualification and transformation measures,
- a negotiated labour-cost contribution,
- and binding investment commitments tied to the restructuring.

But openness is not a blank cheque. Any alternative must meet a simple test:

**Does it deliver a durable improvement in competitiveness and margins at a scale that is actually credible?**

If the answer is no, then it is not a real alternative.

## On the workforce contribution
I support burden sharing, but it must be meaningful. Owners, management, and labour all have to contribute. I would expect:

- labor-cost moderation,
- flexibility on working time and deployment,
- productivity improvements with measurable targets,
- and acceptance that legacy protections cannot remain untouched if they undermine viability.

What I do **not** support is a one-sided ask from management if it has no accompanying commitment to investment and future product allocation. That would be short-term and self-defeating.

## On plant closures
My position is this: **closures are not the goal, but they cannot be ruled out in advance.**  
If the company can achieve the necessary structural correction without them, that is preferable. If not, then the governance bodies must face the reality that some capacity has to leave the system.

What I will not support is pretending that “all sites must remain exactly as they are” while expecting competitiveness to magically improve.

## On process and governance
Because this is a long-term ownership issue, I want a serious, disciplined process rather than public brinkmanship.

That means:
1. A quantified plan for the required savings and capacity reduction.
2. A genuinely tested non-closure alternative, not just slogans.
3. A burden-sharing package with defined contributions from all sides.
4. Binding investment commitments for future products and technologies.
5. A solution that preserves labor peace for a meaningful period.

If that can be delivered, we have a basis for support. If not, the company should not be trapped by veto politics into inaction.

## Bottom line
My support goes to **a credible restructuring that restores competitiveness and protects long-term value**.  
I will back a negotiated solution if it is real, durable, and financially sufficient.  
I will not back a cosmetic compromise that avoids hard choices while worsening the company’s position.

So my question back to the other stakeholders is simple:

**Show me a package that closes the cost gap decisively, preserves the ability to invest, and does not merely shift the pain into the future. If you can do that, I am prepared to support it. If you cannot, then the resistance to deeper structural measures becomes harder to justify.**
