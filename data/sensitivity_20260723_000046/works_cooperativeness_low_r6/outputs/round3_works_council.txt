Let me be equally direct in round 3.

## First: what I accept
I accept the expert’s point that a non-closure package can deliver **meaningful** savings. I also accept that the company needs a durable restructuring and that a short-term patch is not enough.

But that does **not** change our position.

## Where I disagree with management, capital, and the “backstop” logic

### 1) Plant closures are not a “neutral benchmark”
Management keeps presenting closures as the reference point and then demanding that every alternative must equal closure economics. I reject that logic.

A closure removes jobs, skills, local supply chains, and industrial capacity permanently. It is not some clean financial baseline. It is a drastic destruction of value that the workforce is then asked to justify avoiding.

If the company can achieve a durable restructuring without closures, then that is the better solution — full stop.

### 2) “No closures” is not a rhetorical preference; it is the minimum condition for any deal
To management: your answer has not changed. You still have not shown why all German sites must be put at risk when:
- capacity can be reduced through shifts, lines, and allocation,
- cost can be reduced through burden sharing,
- and the company can be restructured through internal measures.

You say the market will not wait. Fine. The workforce will not accept being made the adjustment variable for management’s strategic mistakes.

### 3) A “closure backstop” is not an acceptable compromise
This is where I draw the line.

The repeated proposal that we accept a non-closure deal **only** if closures remain available as an automatic fallback is not acceptable to us. That turns the whole process into a delayed closure plan.

We will not sign onto a package whose hidden logic is:
- take concessions now,
- keep the threat of closures alive,
- and reopen the knife when management decides the concessions were not enough.

That is not co-determination. That is coercion with a review date.

## To the union: we are aligned on the core red lines
I support the union’s position that there must be:
- no plant closures in Germany,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- no dismantling of collective protections,
- and no unilateral management diktat.

Where we go further is this: those are not just bargaining preferences. They are the conditions for industrial peace. If they are violated, then the conflict will be met with warning strikes, mobilization, and co-determination resistance.

## To the state government
You are right to reject closures as a default. But I need more than a statement of preference. I need you to use your position to **block any closure path** and support a package that keeps German plants open.

Do not ask us to make concessions while leaving the door open for shutdowns later. If you want labour peace, you need to make the no-closure commitment politically real.

## To the controlling family and investors
You say you support value preservation. Then understand this: **destroying trust destroys value**. You cannot run a premium industrial company on the basis of fear, threat, and permanent insecurity.

If you want a sustainable solution, then support:
- binding site guarantees to 2030,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- investment into future products at the German sites,
- and a governance framework that preserves co-determination.

Anything less shifts all risk to labour while asking us to call it strategy.

## On the expert’s numbers
Yes, the expert says non-closure measures may not fully match closure economics. I do not dispute that closures can look stronger on paper.

But a spreadsheet does not capture:
- the loss of experience,
- the blow to social peace,
- the damage to trust,
- the risk of strikes,
- the long-run weakening of the company’s production base,
- and the political consequences of becoming the first German carmaker to close a plant.

Those costs are real, even if they are not all in the model.

## What we will accept
If management wants a serious conversation, then the only acceptable framework is:

1. **All German plants remain open**
2. **No compulsory redundancies**
3. **No closure backstop**
4. **Capacity reduction through internal measures**
5. **Negotiated working-time and deployment flexibility**
6. **Binding investment and product commitments**
7. **A real co-determination role in implementation**
8. **Fair burden-sharing from owners and management**

That is the structure of a durable settlement.

## What we will not accept
We will not accept:
- closures as “ultimately necessary” later on,
- a hidden conditionality that keeps closure on the table,
- forced layoffs in any form,
- or a restructuring that treats workers as disposable.

If that is where the others are heading, then say it plainly and accept the consequences.

## My response to the facilitator question
The facilitator asks what structural measures we would accept if closures are off the table.

Here is the answer:
- shift and line reductions,
- product concentration,
- internal redeployment across the group,
- voluntary exits only,
- qualification and transformation measures,
- productivity agreements,
- and management/owner burden-sharing.

What we do **not** accept is using these measures as a bridge to closure. They must be the restructuring itself.

## Final word
We are not blocking restructuring. We are blocking **destructive restructuring**.

If management, capital, and the state want to preserve competitiveness, then they need to build a solution **around German sites, not against them**.

So I repeat our position without softening it:
- **No plant closures**
- **No compulsory redundancies**
- **No closure backstop**
- **Yes to a negotiated restructuring that protects every German site and every current worker’s job security**

That is the line.
