**Independent restructuring expert response**

The key technical question is whether a **deep non-closure package** can close the structural gap **without** a plant-closure backstop. In most large German OEM cases, the answer is:

- **Yes, partially and sometimes substantially**
- **No, not always fully**
- **And usually only if the package is exceptionally deep, front-loaded, and enforceable**

## 1) Realistic savings potential from non-closure measures

For a large German passenger-car operation with structurally high fixed costs, a credible non-closure package can typically deliver:

- **Workforce cost moderation / productivity:** ~5% to 12% of controllable labor-related cost base
- **Shift reductions / line consolidation / capacity rationalization:** material fixed-cost relief, often equivalent to **10% to 20% of plant-level conversion and support cost**
- **Indirect overhead cuts / span-of-control / administrative simplification:** often another **2% to 5% of SG&A-type cost**
- **Voluntary exits / age-based attrition / redeployment:** can remove headcount, but the cash savings depend on whether replacements are avoided and whether redeployment is real
- **Product concentration:** can improve utilization and reduce complexity, but only if volume is actually transferred, not merely promised

In aggregate, a strong no-closure package can often cover **roughly 60% to 85%** of the savings that management would otherwise seek from a closure-based restructuring.

## 2) What closure usually adds that non-closure measures struggle to match

Plant closure tends to contribute savings from:

- **full elimination of fixed site overhead**
- **full removal of maintenance, utilities, logistics, and local support**
- **removal of underutilized lines and buildings**
- **deeper permanent headcount reduction**
- **simpler long-term operating model**

That is why closures often produce a cleaner result on paper. For a German carmaker plant, a closure can generate very large recurring savings, but the realized value depends on:

- severance and transfer costs
- remediation and decommissioning costs
- rerouting/logistics costs
- loss of learning curve and disruption
- political and implementation delay
- risk of volume loss elsewhere in the network

So closures are not “free savings”; they are **high-savings, high-friction, high-disruption** measures.

## 3) When a no-closure package is technically credible

A no-closure settlement can be credible if it includes all of the following:

1. **Clear capacity reduction**
   - not just efficiency rhetoric
   - actual removal of shifts, lines, or product families
   - a measurable reduction in annual build capacity

2. **Recurring labor cost reduction**
   - wage moderation
   - longer working-time flexibility
   - lower entry wages for future cohorts if legally and collectively agreed
   - voluntary exit programs with real replacement avoidance

3. **Indirect cost reduction**
   - fewer layers
   - fewer interfaces
   - leaner support functions
   - procurement and logistics simplification

4. **Product and volume concentration**
   - enough volume allocation to make remaining sites efficient
   - otherwise the network remains structurally oversized

5. **Investment commitment**
   - tooling, platform allocation, and industrialization spending
   - without this, a no-closure deal becomes a delay mechanism

6. **Enforcement**
   - milestone-based review
   - automatic deepening of measures if targets are missed
   - not necessarily closures immediately, but some hard backstop must exist if capital is to treat the package as bankable

## 4) My technical threshold view

If the question is: **At what point is non-closure insufficient?**

A practical threshold is when a non-closure package can only cover **less than about two-thirds of the required durable savings** by the main decision horizon, or when it fails to reduce capacity enough to restore utilization to an efficient band.

As a rule of thumb:

- If non-closure measures can achieve **at least ~70% to 80% of required recurring savings**, with durable capacity cuts and real labor-cost contribution, then a no-closure path can be technically defendable.
- If the package is only **50% to 60%** of what is needed, then it is usually **not** durable enough unless there is a strong additional offset from volume growth or major product improvement.
- If the residual gap is still large after all non-closure measures, then the company will likely need either:
  - a closure backstop,
  - or a materially different product/volume plan.

## 5) Enforceability matters as much as the headline savings

A package without a credible enforcement mechanism is often not sufficient, even if the numbers look decent initially. Why?

Because in automotive restructuring, the failure mode is usually:

- savings are delayed,
- utilization stays too low,
- productivity gains are not fully captured,
- and the company reopens the same problem 18 to 36 months later.

So from a restructuring-design perspective, the most robust non-closure settlement is not “no closure ever,” but:

- **no closure in the settlement period**
- **hard milestone review**
- **pre-agreed escalation**
- **possibly staged capacity actions that are reversible at first, then deeper if missed**

## 6) Bottom line

My technical view is:

- **Non-closure measures can be enough if the package is deep, quantified, and front-loaded.**
- **They are often not enough if they are vague, delayed, or depend on optimistic volume recovery.**
- A credible no-closure solution usually needs to achieve **roughly 70%+ of the structural savings gap** and a meaningful share of the capacity reduction.
- If it cannot do that, then the package is likely **insufficient on financial grounds**, regardless of its political acceptability.
