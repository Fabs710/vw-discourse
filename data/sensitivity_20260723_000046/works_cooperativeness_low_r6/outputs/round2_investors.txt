I will be direct.

## Where I agree
I agree with management, the controlling family, and the expert on one core point: **this company needs a durable, structural improvement in competitiveness and margins**. A cosmetic truce, vague promises, or a deal that simply defers the pain would destroy value. That is not acceptable for shareholders.

I also agree that:
- investment in future products matters,
- a negotiated solution is preferable to prolonged industrial conflict,
- forced redundancies are not ideal if a credible alternative achieves the same economics,
- and any package must be measurable, enforceable, and tied to milestones.

## Where I disagree
### 1) A blanket “no plant closures” line is not acceptable as a starting point
I understand the political and social logic, but from a capital-market perspective it is not enough. If closures are ruled out in advance **without** a package that clearly replaces the lost savings, then the company is being asked to carry structurally excessive costs indefinitely.

That would be value destructive. We would not support it.

### 2) “Protect every site” is not a financial strategy
The workforce’s position is understandable, but “all sites stay open, no compulsory redundancies” only works if the alternative delivers the same economic correction. Otherwise it amounts to preserving the status quo and hoping margins recover on their own. They will not.

### 3) The burden-sharing discussion is still too vague
Everyone says “shared sacrifice,” but the critical issue is **how much, from whom, and with what enforceability**. Shareholders do not support open-ended concessions from capital if labor and management commitments are not real and structural.

## What I take from the expert
The expert assessment is useful because it confirms something important:

- **Non-closure measures can deliver meaningful savings**
- but **they may not fully replace closure economics**
- so the only credible non-closure path is a **deep, enforceable package with a backstop**

That is the right framework.

But I want to be clear: a backstop only matters if it is real. A “review later” mechanism with no consequence is not enough. If targets are missed, the company must have the ability to take additional action, including closures if necessary. Otherwise the package is just a delay tactic.

## What a supportable deal would need
For us to back a restructuring, it would need all of the following:

1. **Quantified annual savings**
   - not broad language, but hard numbers

2. **Durable capacity reduction**
   - whether through plant closures or equivalent structural footprint reduction

3. **Meaningful workforce contribution**
   - labor-cost moderation, flexibility, and productivity gains with measurable effect

4. **Binding owner/management commitments**
   - investment, product allocation, and capital discipline

5. **Milestones and enforcement**
   - if the package misses targets, additional measures must automatically follow

6. **A credible timeline to competitive margins by 2030**
   - not a promise of “transformation” in the abstract

## On plant closures
I am not saying closures must happen immediately. I am saying they must **remain on the table** unless the alternative demonstrably produces equivalent economics.

That is the key point.

If management and labor can present a package that:
- lowers fixed costs materially,
- reduces capacity permanently,
- and restores margins credibly,

then we can support avoiding closures.

If they cannot, then refusing closures in principle becomes a veto on financial reality, and we would not support that outcome.

## On the workforce position
We are not asking employees to bear all the pain. But we are also not prepared to underwrite a settlement where employees contribute little, the company keeps too much capacity, and shareholders absorb the margin erosion.

That is not a fair or investable outcome.

## On management
Management must stop framing this as only a choice between closure and inaction. The real question is whether it can present a **quantified, enforceable alternative** that restores returns on capital. If yes, we are open. If no, then the restructuring is not yet credible.

## Bottom line
Our support goes to the option that:
- restores competitiveness,
- delivers durable margin improvement,
- reduces capacity and fixed cost enough to matter,
- and is enforceable if it underperforms.

We will **not** support a settlement that is merely socially comfortable while leaving the company structurally uncompetitive.
