## Executive assessment

The key question is whether a **non-closure restructuring package** can generate enough **durable, discounted economic value** to match or approximate the economics of **plant closure / deeper footprint reduction**, while also managing one-time execution costs and downside risk.

### Short answer
- **Non-closure packages can often deliver substantial savings**
- **They usually do not fully replicate closure economics unless they are very deep**
- The most realistic financially credible structure is often a **hybrid**:
  - strong non-closure measures now,
  - explicit capacity reduction and workforce exit mechanisms,
  - milestone-based enforcement,
  - and a closure backstop if targets are missed

If decision-makers insist on **no closures and no compulsory redundancies**, the package can still be viable **only if** the savings are large enough, persistent, and not offset by hidden complexity costs or future underutilization.

---

## 1) How I would frame the economic test

For each option, the relevant comparison is:

**NPV of recurring savings**
minus
**NPV of implementation / restructuring costs**
minus
**NPV of residual inefficiency / downside risk**

The question is not whether one option is socially preferable. It is whether the **present value of future cash-flow improvement** is sufficient.

The relevant time horizon is usually **5–10 years**, with a strong focus on the period through **2030**, because:
- fixed-cost savings accumulate over time,
- one-time restructuring costs hit early,
- and delayed action materially reduces NPV

A euro of savings in 2026 is far more valuable than the same euro in 2030.

---

## 2) Closure vs. non-closure: the basic economic difference

### Closure package
A plant closure or equivalent footprint elimination typically provides:
- the largest **fixed-cost removal**
- the clearest reduction in structural overcapacity
- the strongest long-run EBIT improvement

But it also brings:
- high one-time cash costs
- labor disruption
- political and execution risk
- possible supply-chain transition costs
- potential stranded asset and remediation costs

### Non-closure package
A non-closure deal usually relies on:
- wage restraint / labor-cost contribution
- working-time flexibility
- attrition and voluntary exits
- internal redeployment
- productivity gains
- product concentration and capacity rationalization without shutdowns

This can deliver meaningful savings, but in most cases:
- some fixed overhead remains
- underutilization may persist
- complexity costs can stay elevated
- savings may be more contingent on sustained discipline

So the NPV comparison often comes down to:

- **closure = larger annual savings, larger upfront cost**
- **non-closure = smaller annual savings, smaller upfront cost**

## 3) Order-of-magnitude economics

Because I do not have company-confidential numbers, I’ll use ranges consistent with a large German OEM.

### A. Closure economics
For a large plant or equivalent capacity block, you often see:

- **annual recurring savings:** roughly **€200m–€500m+**
- **one-time restructuring costs:** often **€300m–€1bn+**
  - severance
  - transfer / shutdown costs
  - asset write-downs
  - tooling relocation
  - decommissioning / remediation
  - supply-chain transition

### B. Non-closure economics
A serious non-closure package may deliver:

- **labor-cost contribution:** ~€100m–€600m/year
- **productivity / indirect cost reduction:** ~€150m–€400m/year
- **capacity concentration / footprint optimization:** ~€200m–€600m/year if very well executed

But many non-closure packages also carry:
- higher ongoing complexity
- residual underabsorption of fixed cost
- ongoing capex to keep multiple sites competitive
- a risk that savings erode after the agreement period

So a deep non-closure solution might get to:
- **~€300m–€1.2bn annual recurring improvement** in a strong case

That can be meaningful — but it only matches closure economics if the problem is not too large and the agreement is truly durable.

## 4) NPV logic: why timing matters so much

A simple DCF framing:

\[
NPV = \sum_{t=1}^{T} \frac{Savings_t - OngoingCosts_t}{(1+r)^t} - UpfrontCosts
\]

Where:
- \(r\) = discount rate
- \(Savings_t\) = recurring annual savings
- \(OngoingCosts_t\) = remaining inefficiency, capex, or support costs
- \(UpfrontCosts\) = severance, shutdown, retooling, transition costs

### Implication
A package that produces:
- €400m annual savings starting immediately
can easily outperform
- €600m annual savings starting 3 years later

So in negotiations, delay is not neutral. Delay destroys NPV.

This often gives the best risk-adjusted outcome because it:
- captures value early,
- reduces conflict risk,

## 6) The biggest financial risks in a non-closure solution

### A. Residual overcapacity
If plants remain open at low utilization, the company keeps paying:
- overhead
- maintenance
- management layers
- logistics complexity
- future capex to stay competitive

That can materially erode the headline savings.

### B. Temporary savings that revert
Some labor measures are only durable for the term of the agreement. If:
- wage restraint expires,
- flexibility is rolled back,
- or redeployment assumptions fail,

then savings may not be permanent.

### C. Underfunded transformation capex
If you keep more sites open, you often need more investment to modernize them. That can reduce near-term NPV.

### D. Execution risk
The more complex the package, the more likely:
- savings slip
- product allocations change
- headcount reductions come in below plan
- or governance disputes block implementation

### E. Downside if volume underperforms
If market volumes weaken further, a non-closure package can become too light, and the company may have to revisit the same issue later — at a higher cost and lower NPV.

---

## 7) What makes a non-closure package financially credible

A non-closure alternative is credible only if it has these features:

### 1. Hard annual savings target
Not vague intentions. A numeric target.

### 2. Permanent capacity reduction
This is crucial. If no site closes, there still must be:
- line elimination
- shift removal
- product consolidation
- reduced fixed cost base

### 3. Real workforce contribution
A mix of:
- wage moderation
- allowance reductions
- working-time changes
- attrition
- voluntary exits

### 4. Owner / management contribution
This matters for cash planning:
- capex
- product allocation
- financing support where relevant
- disciplined overhead reduction above plant level

### 5. Enforceable backstop
If savings fall short:
- more aggressive steps are triggered
- including closure or deeper footprint action if needed

Without a backstop, the package is often not bankable from a restructuring perspective.

---

## 8) Break-even intuition

The break-even question is:

**How much recurring annual saving is required from the non-closure package to justify forgoing closure?**

That depends on:
- closure savings forgone
- one-time costs avoided
- residual inefficiency accepted
- and the probability of future remedial action

In general:
- if non-closure can capture **70%–90% of closure economics**, it may be rational
- if it can only capture **40%–60%**, closure usually wins on pure NPV
- if it creates a “temporary peace” but no structural fix, the future risk-adjusted NPV can be poor even if near-term cash burn is lower

---

## 9) Practical scenario view

### Best case for non-closure
- overcapacity is moderate, not deep
- labor is willing to make structural concessions
- product allocation can be concentrated
- voluntary exits are substantial
- capex can modernize remaining sites efficiently

In that case, non-closure can be financially acceptable.

### Worst case for non-closure
- overcapacity is broad across multiple sites
- wage and flexibility concessions are small
- product allocation remains fragmented
- capex rises to keep all sites alive
- underutilization persists

In that case, non-closure is likely suboptimal versus closure or later closure.

### Most likely case
A non-closure package buys time and captures part of the savings, but the company still needs:
- a hard review point
- and a binding consequence if targets are missed

That is usually the financially disciplined answer.

---

## 10) My bottom-line financial judgment

### If the question is:
**Can a non-closure package be economically rational?**
- **Yes**

### If the question is:
**Can it fully replace the economics of closing a materially underutilized plant?**
- **Usually not**

### If the question is:
**What is the most financially robust compromise?**
- **A deep non-closure package with quantified savings, upfront implementation, and a credible backstop**

That structure best balances:
- near-term cash preservation
- long-run competitiveness
- and downside protection

---

## 11) What I would recommend as the decision standard

Before accepting either package, management should require a model showing:

1. **annual recurring savings by measure**
2. **one-time implementation costs**
3. **timing of savings**
4. **residual fixed-cost burden**
5. **probability-weighted downside case**
6. **NPV under at least three scenarios**
   - base
   - downside
   - severe downside
7. **break-even year**
8. **sensitivity to volume, wage, and execution assumptions**

That is the appropriate restructuring-finance test.
