Let me respond directly, because we are now circling around the central issue rather than confronting it.

## First: what I agree with
I agree with everyone on one point: **this company needs a durable restructuring, not a temporary patch**.  
I also agree that:
- investment in future products and technology is essential,
- a negotiated outcome is preferable to open-ended conflict,
- and forced redundancies are not the first-choice instrument if a credible alternative exists.

So far, so good.

## Where I disagree — fundamentally
I do **not** accept the repeated attempt to make **“no plant closures”** a non-negotiable starting point.  
I also do **not** accept a package that preserves the current cost base, the current capacity, and the current level of site duplication while expecting management to somehow restore competitiveness by willpower.

That is not restructuring. That is delay.

You keep saying the workforce should not pay for management’s strategic failures. I am not debating the history here. I am dealing with the fact that **German factory costs are too high, capacity is too large for the market, and the brand is missing its return-on-sales target now**. The market will not wait for an internal moral verdict.

## To the works council and union
You both say you are open to alternatives. I will judge that by whether those alternatives actually produce **durable, structural savings**.

What I do not accept is an alternative that:
- keeps every site open,
- excludes compulsory redundancies absolutely,
- preserves all legacy guarantees unchanged,
- and then asks management to pretend the cost gap has been closed.

That is not an alternative. That is a refusal to face reality.

If you want to avoid closures, then show me how you will **permanently remove capacity and cost from the system** through other measures at the same scale. Not symbolically. Not temporarily. Permanently.

That means any serious proposal must address:
- site utilization,
- fixed-cost reduction,
- labor-cost contribution,
- deployment flexibility,
- and the elimination of structural overcapacity.

If the answer is just “more dialogue, more qualification, more flexibility” while the cost base stays essentially intact, then we have not solved anything.

## To the state government
I understand your political concerns and your role as anchor shareholder. But “no closures” cannot become a political veto on economic reality.

I am not asking for closures as an end in themselves. I am saying they cannot be ruled out if they are needed to create a viable structure. If you want to reject closures, then help deliver a package that achieves the same savings and capacity correction through other binding measures. If not, you are not solving the problem — you are postponing it.

## To the controlling family and investors
You are right to insist that this cannot be a cosmetic compromise. I agree. We cannot accept a deal that protects appearances while leaving the company structurally weak.

But I also want to be clear: **“shared sacrifice” must be real on the workforce side, not just a rhetorical add-on.**  
A credible package must include a meaningful labour-cost contribution. Not a token gesture. Not a one-off. A structural contribution that helps restore margin discipline.

At the same time, I expect commitment from owners and management as well:
- investment into future products,
- product allocation for the sites that remain,
- and disciplined capital deployment.

But let me be equally plain: investment commitments do not substitute for cost correction. We need both.

## What I am actually asking for
The question is no longer abstract. It is this:

**Can we produce a structurally sufficient package that lowers the cost base and reduces capacity enough to restore competitiveness by 2030?**

If yes, then I am open on the instrument mix.

If no, then the resistance to closures is not a solution. It is a blockage.

So I need to hear concrete answers, not declarations:
1. **How much fixed cost comes out?**
2. **How much capacity comes out?**
3. **What is the workforce contribution in euros and in structural effect?**
4. **What binding flexibility is introduced?**
5. **What is the timeline to return to target margins?**

Without that, we are not discussing strategy. We are discussing who gets to postpone decisions.

## Bottom line
I remain prepared to negotiate a non-disruptive restructuring **if it is economically equivalent** to the savings we need.  
But I will not accept:
- a blanket no-closure rule,
- a no-redundancy doctrine with no structural adjustment,
- or a package that protects every historical arrangement and leaves the company uncompetitive.

That would fail the fiduciary test, and it would fail the company.

If you want to avoid closures, bring me a package that actually removes the overcapacity and cost burden. If you cannot, then we should stop pretending the outcome can be painless.
