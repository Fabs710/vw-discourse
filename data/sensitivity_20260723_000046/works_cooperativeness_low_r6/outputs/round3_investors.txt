I’ll be direct.

## Where I agree
I agree with management, the family holding, the state, and the expert on one central point: **the current cost base and capacity situation are not sustainable**. We need a durable restructuring that actually restores margins, not a cosmetic truce.

I also agree that:
- investment in future products is necessary,
- a negotiated solution is preferable to prolonged industrial conflict,
- workforce concessions can be part of the answer,
- and any package must be **quantified, enforceable, and durable**.

Those are the right standards for capital.

## Where I disagree
### 1) “No closures” cannot be an absolute red line unless the economics truly work
I understand why labor and the state are resisting closures. But from a shareholder perspective, **we cannot support an arrangement that simply preserves all current sites and all current guarantees if the result is structurally impaired margins**.

If a non-closure path genuinely removes enough fixed cost and capacity, fine.  
If it does not, then “no closures” becomes a value-destructive veto, not a restructuring position.

### 2) A “closure backstop” is not ideal, but a package with no enforcement is worse
I do **not** want a settlement that is just a delayed closure plan. That would create uncertainty, drag on morale, and keep the issue alive.

But I also cannot support a deal that removes the closure option entirely **without** a hard enforcement mechanism. The capital markets need a credible path to competitiveness, not a promise that everyone will behave later.

So I disagree with the employee side when it says any backstop is coercion.  
A backstop is not a threat for its own sake; it is what makes the economics believable.

### 3) “Shared sacrifice” must be real in euros, not just rhetoric
The employee side has been strong on red lines, but weaker on quantification.

We need to know:
- how much labor cost comes out,
- how much capacity comes out,
- how much flexibility increases,
- and what the recurring annual savings are.

Without that, “fair burden-sharing” is just language. Shareholders do not invest in language.

## What the expert clarified
The expert’s assessment is useful because it confirmed two things:
1. **A non-closure package can produce meaningful savings**
2. **It usually does not fully replicate closure economics unless it is very deep and tightly enforced**

That means the real decision is not closure vs. no closure in the abstract. It is whether the non-closure package can credibly reach a level of savings and capacity reduction that restores returns on capital by 2030.

If yes, we can support it.  
If no, then refusing closures in principle is not acceptable.

## What would make a non-closure deal investable
For us to back a settlement, it would need all of the following:

1. **Hard annual savings**
   - not broad commitments, but numbers

2. **Permanent capacity reduction**
   - not just temporary flexibility

3. **Meaningful labor contribution**
   - wage moderation, working-time changes, productivity gains, and voluntary exits with real effect

4. **Management and owner commitments**
   - product allocation, investment, and disciplined capital deployment

5. **Milestones and consequences**
   - if targets are missed, the company must have a real next step

6. **A credible timeline to return to competitive margins by 2030**
   - not an open-ended transformation story

## Where I stand on closures
I am **not** saying closures should be the first resort.  
I am saying they must **remain available** if the non-closure package is too weak or fails to deliver.

That is the key distinction.

A credible compromise is not “closures now” and it is not “closures never.”  
It is “non-closure first, but only if the economics are strong enough to stand on their own.”

## Where I stand on the employee position
I respect the desire to protect jobs and sites. But shareholders cannot underwrite a structure where:
- every site is protected,
- labor concessions are partial,
- and margins remain structurally below target.

That would just socialize the pain into future earnings and valuations.

If the employee side wants to avoid closures, it needs to engage with the hard part:
- real capacity reduction,
- real productivity improvement,
- real labor cost contribution,
- and acceptance that some historical arrangements may not survive.

## Where I stand on management
Management is right to insist on structural savings. But it also needs to be precise.

I want management to quantify:
- the minimum savings required,
- how much can be delivered without closures,
- what the residual gap is,
- and when the company would need escalation.

If management cannot produce that, then the strategy is not yet decision-ready.

## Where I stand on the state and family
The state is right to reject closures as a default. The family is right to reject cosmetic compromise. Both positions are reasonable.

But both also need to recognize that **capital will not support a deal that leaves the cost base uncompetitive**. Political comfort is not a substitute for earnings power.

## My position in one sentence
We are prepared to support a **deep, enforceable non-closure restructuring** if it delivers durable cost reduction, real capacity rationalization, and a credible return to competitive margins.  
If it does not, then excluding closures in advance is not acceptable.
