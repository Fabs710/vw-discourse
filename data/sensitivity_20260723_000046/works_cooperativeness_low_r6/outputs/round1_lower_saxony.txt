We are willing to engage seriously with the management’s competitiveness concerns, but I need to be equally clear: **a restructuring that relies on plant closures in Germany is not acceptable to the state as shareholder and political anchor.** Nor is a path that shifts the burden primarily onto workers while risking lasting damage to regional employment and the company’s role as an industrial anchor.

I agree with management on two points:
- the company needs a durable solution, not a temporary patch;
- investment in future products and technologies is essential.

Where I disagree is on the implied conclusion that **closures are the necessary starting point**. That is not yet proven. Before anyone talks about shutting plants, the burden is on management to show that all other avenues have genuinely been exhausted.

From our perspective, the right sequence is different:

1. **Protect sites and employment first.**
2. **Pursue capacity reduction through non-closure measures wherever possible.**
3. **Negotiate a fair contribution from the workforce, but not one that destroys trust or the social partnership model.**
4. **Require owners and management to contribute as well, including on capital allocation and future investment commitments.**

A few concrete points:

- **No closure decision can be taken lightly or unilaterally.** Under the governance structure here, that would face serious resistance.
- **No mass layoffs or forced redundancies.** That would be economically and politically destabilizing.
- **Yes to a negotiated restructuring package** if it preserves industrial presence, keeps jobs in the region, and strengthens competitiveness over time.

To management’s challenge: the alternative does not have to mean “no change.” It can mean:
- targeted capacity reductions,
- working-time flexibility,
- internal redeployment across sites,
- qualification and transformation programs,
- temporary or phased cost contributions,
- and binding future investment commitments tied to site retention.

That is a serious package. It may be harder to design than a closure plan, but it is also more sustainable and less destructive. If management wants the state to support a major restructuring, then it must present something that is **economically credible and socially acceptable**.

So my position is:
- **I support restructuring.**
- **I do not support closures as the default instrument.**
- **I will back a negotiated solution that preserves jobs, sites, and the company’s long-term role in the region.**

If management comes forward with a concrete alternative that achieves real savings without gutting regional employment, we are prepared to engage constructively. But if the plan remains “close plants and cut jobs first, ask questions later,” then I cannot support it.
