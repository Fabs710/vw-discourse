From the perspective of dispersed capital-market shareholders, the key issue is not ideology, regional politics, or industrial tradition. It is whether the company can restore **credible, durable competitiveness** and protect the **risk-adjusted value** of the equity.

So my position is straightforward:

## Where I agree
I agree with management and the controlling family on one central point: **the current cost base is not sustainable**. If margins remain structurally impaired, the equity story deteriorates further, and capital will continue to re-rate this company downward. We will not support a solution that simply preserves today’s overcapacity and delays the problem.

I also agree that:
- **investment in future products and technology is essential**,
- **a negotiated solution is preferable to prolonged confrontation**,
- and **forced redundancies are not the best outcome if a credible alternative can achieve the same savings**.

## Where I disagree
I do **not** support a cosmetic compromise that leaves the company with:
- an uncompetitive labor-cost base,
- unchanged overcapacity,
- or a restructuring that is “socially acceptable” but financially insufficient.

That would be value destructive. And to be blunt: if the end result is just a temporary truce with a weak margin profile, the market will punish the stock and future financing flexibility will worsen.

I also do not accept the idea that “no closures” can be an absolute starting point if no credible alternative delivers equivalent savings. A hard red line is only credible if the alternative is equally hard-nosed. Otherwise it becomes a veto on economic reality.

## What we need to see
To support any deal, we need a **quantified, enforceable path back to competitive margins**. That means:

1. **A durable reduction in capacity and fixed costs**
2. **Meaningful labor-cost contribution**
3. **Clear timelines and measurable milestones**
4. **Binding product and investment commitments**
5. **Proof that the savings are not temporary or cosmetic**

If plant closures are avoided, fine — but only if the alternative achieves the same economic effect. Capacity reductions, working-time changes, redeployment, and wage moderation may be acceptable **if and only if** they produce a real and durable reset of the cost base.

## On the workforce’s position
I understand the resistance to closures and forced layoffs. But shareholders cannot underwrite a settlement that leaves the company structurally weaker. If the workforce insists on total insulation from adjustment, then the burden shifts to shareholders through lower returns and greater downside risk. That is not acceptable.

## On management
Management has our support **only if** it pushes for a credible restructuring rather than a political compromise dressed up as strategy. We are not interested in rhetoric about transformation unless the numbers actually improve.

## Bottom line
We are prepared to back a negotiated solution, including one that avoids closures, **but only if it delivers a decisive and durable improvement in competitiveness and margins**.

If the final package is:
- too small,
- too vague,
- too slow,
- or too dependent on goodwill rather than enforceable commitments,

then we will not view it as a success. We will view it as postponing the erosion of value.

So my question is simple: **What exact package gets the company back to competitive margins by 2030, with measurable cost reduction and credible enforcement?** Without that, we cannot support an outcome that merely preserves consensus.
