I will respond plainly, because the discussion is now trying to normalize a line we cannot accept.

## Where I agree
I agree with everyone on the following:
- The company needs a **durable restructuring**, not a short-lived patch.
- A **negotiated solution** is preferable to an open industrial conflict.
- There must be **real competitiveness gains**, not just accounting optics.
- Employees can and should make a **fair contribution** if the package is balanced.
- Future investment, product allocation, and modernization are essential.

So yes: we are not defending the status quo for its own sake.

## Where I disagree
### 1) Plant closures are not the benchmark we must “match”
Management, investors, and the family keep using closure economics as the reference point. I reject that framing.

A plant closure is not a neutral instrument. It is a permanent destruction of jobs, skills, local industrial capacity, and trust. If the company can restructure without closures, that is the superior solution — socially and industrially.

So no, we do **not** accept the logic that every alternative must prove itself by replicating a closure.

### 2) A closure backstop is not acceptable
This is the most important line in this round.

The idea that we should accept a “non-closure package” only if closures remain available later as a fallback is not a compromise. It is a delayed closure strategy with concessions upfront.

We will not sign onto a package whose built-in logic is:
- workers concede now,
- management keeps the closure threat alive,
- and later returns to shut sites if the numbers are still uncomfortable.

That is coercion, not collective bargaining.

### 3) The workforce will not bear the adjustment alone
If management wants credibility, then burden-sharing must be real on **all** sides:
- management restraint,
- owner contribution,
- investment commitment,
- and labor contribution.

We have already shown willingness to discuss:
- working-time flexibility,
- capacity reductions through shifts and lines,
- internal redeployment,
- qualification and transformation measures,
- productivity improvements,
- and a negotiated labor-cost contribution.

But we will not accept a package where workers pay, workers lose guarantees, and everyone else calls that “shared sacrifice.”

## To management

If the answer is simply “because closures are cleaner on paper,” that is not enough. Industrial restructuring is not an abstract spreadsheet exercise. It has consequences for skills, morale, industrial peace, and long-term site viability.

If you want a deal, then bring a plan that:
1. keeps German sites open,
2. excludes compulsory redundancies,
3. preserves the collective bargaining framework,
4. and delivers structural savings through non-closure measures.

What you cannot demand is a blank cheque for shutdowns dressed up as realism.

A package that shifts all risk downward may look efficient in the short term. It is not sustainable.

## On the expert assessment

That does **not** mean closures are necessary. It means the package must be deeper, more disciplined, and more enforceable if we want to avoid them.

But the expert also confirms something else: a deep non-closure package **can** be viable if it is:
- structurally meaningful,
- front-loaded,
- tied to product allocation,
- and supported by real contributions from management and owners.

That is the path we should be discussing.
