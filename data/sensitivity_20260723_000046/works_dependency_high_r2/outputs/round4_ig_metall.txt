We appreciate the legal clarification, because it matters: **yes, binding no-compulsory-redundancy and site-protection commitments through 2030 are possible in principle** if they are properly structured. That is exactly the kind of answer we needed. It confirms that management has no excuse to talk as if closures and forced layoffs were the only “serious” tools.

## To management
I agree with one part of your position: this company needs real restructuring, not cosmetic action.

I **do not** agree with your conclusion that closures and forced redundancies must remain a normal fallback just because they are convenient. The expert has now shown that there are legally and operationally credible ways to bind the company to:
- no compulsory redundancies,
- no German plant closures,
- voluntary adjustment tools only,
- and a negotiated framework through 2030.

So the burden is now squarely on you: **prove that you need more than that.**  
Do not hide behind “flexibility” as a euphemism for forcing workers to carry the whole burden.

You say you need structural cost relief. Fine. Then present the quantified gap and show, line by line, what you need from:
- working time,
- allowances,
- productivity,
- product allocation,
- redeployment,
- and voluntary exits.

If, after that, there is still a residual gap, then we can discuss additional employee contributions. But **not** forced layoffs, and **not** plant closures as the starting point.

## To the works council
We are aligned on the core red lines:
- no plant closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- no dismantling of co-determination,
- protection of the collective framework.

The legal assessment strengthens our hand. It shows that these are not abstract wishes; they can be turned into binding commitments if the governance package is done properly.

I also agree with your insistence that the workforce must not be the only side making sacrifices. That remains non-negotiable for me.

Where I would sharpen our joint position is this: we should not stop at “no closures/no layoffs.” We should define the **trade** we are willing to make:
- temporary wage moderation or deferred increases,
- flexibility on working time,
- shift redesign,
- internal redeployment,
- qualification and reskilling,
- voluntary departure tools,
- and limited productivity commitments,

**in exchange for**
- binding site security,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- preservation of the collective wage framework,
- and binding investment and product commitments.

That is a fair exchange. It is not surrender.

## To the regional state government
You said you cannot support a blind defense of every structure. I understand that. But now we have a legal route to protect the industrial base **without** handing management a blank cheque.

So I am asking you to do more than “encourage” the parties:
- use your supervisory-board influence,
- insist on a binding site and employment package,
- support the no-closure / no-forced-redundancy architecture,
- and tie any state-backed support or political backing to investment, training, and future product allocation.

This is the moment for the state to stop hedging.

If the state truly wants to be an anchor shareholder, then anchor the settlement around:
- German site security,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- and a durable industrial plan.

## To the controlling family
You talk about stewardship. Then act like stewards.

The legal expert has confirmed that the company can make binding commitments through 2030 without locking itself into an impossible straightjacket. That means your claim that closures must remain in the background as a default bargaining weapon is weaker now.

If you want trust, then stop demanding that the workforce accept the most irreversible pain first. Shared sacrifice means:
- management contributes,
- owners contribute,
- and employees contribute,
but not that employees bear the job-loss risk while capital keeps all strategic options open.

I am prepared to discuss serious employee contributions, but only if you accept the architecture that protects jobs and sites. Without that, “stewardship” sounds like a slogan for transferring risk downward.

## To investors
You are right to ask for credibility, enforceability, and durable savings.

But the legal assessment also shows something important for you: a protected, negotiated package is not a fantasy. It can be contractually real. That means you should evaluate it on the basis of:
- annual run-rate savings,
- one-time transition costs,
- execution risk,
- and industrial peace.

What you should **not** do is assume that closures are automatically superior because they are harsher. Harsh is not the same as value-creating.

If a no-closure, no-forced-redundancy package can close the gap with lower strike risk and higher execution certainty, that may actually be the better risk-adjusted outcome. You should be open to that.

## What the expert report changes for us
It confirms three things:

1. **Our red lines are legally and practically defensible.**  
2. **Management can be bound to them through 2030.**  
3. **The real negotiation is about the size and form of employee contributions, not whether workers should be threatened with closure.**

That is the correct frame.

## Our position, clearly stated
We are prepared to discuss a fair and serious contribution from the workforce, including:
- temporary wage moderation,
- working-time flexibility,
- shift redesign,
- voluntary exits,
- internal redeployment,
- qualification and reskilling,
- and productivity improvements.

But we will only do that inside a binding package that includes:
- **no German plant closures through 2030,**
- **no compulsory redundancies through 2030,**
- **preservation of the collective wage framework,**
- **protection of co-determination,**
- and **binding investment/product commitments.**

Anything less means the workforce is being asked to pay for management’s restructuring twice: once in concessions, and once in jobs. That is unacceptable.

## What we reject
Let me be explicit:
- We reject closures as a bargaining threat.
- We reject forced redundancies as a planning tool.
- We reject employee concessions without reciprocal guarantees.
- We reject “shared sacrifice” that is really one-sided sacrifice.
- We reject any attempt to make the collective agreement framework the casualty of the restructuring.

2. a deep-conversion scenario,

each showing:
