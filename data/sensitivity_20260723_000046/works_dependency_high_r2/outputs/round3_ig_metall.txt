We are now at the point where the discussion must become concrete, not rhetorical.

## To management
I acknowledge one point: the company needs real, durable restructuring. I do **not** deny the cost pressure, the market weakness, or the need for future investment.

But I reject your core framing in one decisive respect: **you still have not shown that plant closures and compulsory redundancies are necessary, rather than simply the fastest and harshest way to cut costs.**

That matters.

If you want workforce support, you need to present:
- the quantified savings gap,
- the contribution of each lever,
- what can be achieved through working-time, productivity, redeployment, and capacity adjustment,
- and what remains after every non-destructive option has been exhausted.

Until then, saying “closures remain on the table” is not a restructuring strategy. It is leverage against the workforce.

And let me be equally clear: **we will not accept a restructuring in which employees bear the irreversible damage while management and owners preserve maximum optionality.**

## To the works council
We are aligned on the essentials:
- no forced redundancies,
- no plant closures as a bargaining starting point,
- and no dismantling of the collective framework.

I support your insistence that the workforce must not be sacrificed to short-term margin repair.

Where I would sharpen the argument is this: we should not only say what we reject, but also define what we can accept in a binding package:
- temporary labour-cost relief,
- working-time flexibility,
- internal redeployment,
- qualification and reskilling,
- voluntary instruments only,
- and capacity reduction through organization and product allocation.

That is the route to a credible alternative. But it only works if the company matches that with binding guarantees.

## To the state government
I appreciate that you do not want closures as a first instinct. But “no unnecessary closures” is too weak if it still leaves closures as the default threat in the background.

If the state wants to play the role of anchor shareholder seriously, then use that position to insist on:
- no compulsory redundancies,
- binding job and site security,
- serious investment commitments,
- and a negotiated industrial plan that preserves the German base.

A weak political caution is not enough. We need a clear line that the company must prove alternatives before it can threaten irreversible damage.

## To the controlling family
You speak of stewardship and long-term value. Then prove it in the settlement structure.

Stewardship does **not** mean shifting the burden first onto the workforce and only later, if at all, asking for contribution from capital and management. A credible settlement must show shared sacrifice:
- management contribution,
- owner contribution,
- and employee contribution,
all within a framework that protects jobs and the collective wage structure.

I also reject the idea that legacy guarantees are simply “dead weight.” They are part of the trust architecture of this company. If you tear them up without a fair replacement, you may win a cost line item but you will lose the basis for future cooperation.

## To investors
You are right that the final package must be financially credible. I agree with that.

But you keep underestimating the value destruction of an aggressive, socially explosive restructuring:
- strikes,
- production loss,
- deteriorating labour relations,
- loss of know-how,
- and long-term instability.

That is not noise. That is economic damage.

So if you want a durable solution, you need to support a package that the workforce can actually live with. A deal that is “clean” on paper but unstable in practice is not value creation.

## What we are prepared to discuss
We remain prepared to discuss a serious employee contribution, including:
- temporary wage moderation or limited concessions,
- working-time adjustments,
- flexibility measures,
- redeployment,
- qualification and reskilling,
- voluntary exit tools,
- and productivity improvements,

**but only if the company provides binding commitments on:**
- no plant closures in Germany,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- preservation of the collective-bargaining framework,
- and clear investment commitments for future products and sites.

That is the minimum basis for a fair exchange.

## What we reject
Let me remove any ambiguity:

- We reject plant closures as a bargaining chip.
- We reject compulsory redundancies.
- We reject “modernization” that is really a cutback of social protections.
- We reject employee contributions that are demanded without reciprocal guarantees.
- We reject a deal where workers carry all the pain and management retains all the options.

## Bottom line
If the company wants a future, it must build it **with** the workforce, not against it.

If management can bring us a quantified, binding package that protects sites and jobs while producing real savings, we are ready to negotiate hard and responsibly.

If not — if closures and forced layoffs remain the starting point — then we will treat that as a refusal to reach a fair settlement, and we will use the tools available to us. The peace obligation has ended, and our members expect us to defend their jobs, wages, and dignity.
