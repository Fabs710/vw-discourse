I’ll respond directly.

## First: what I agree with
I agree with everyone on one point: **this company needs a viable future, not cosmetic action**.

I also agree that:
- investment in future products and technology is essential,
- a negotiated settlement is better than prolonged chaos,
- and employee contributions should not be isolated from management/owner contributions.

So let me be clear: I am **not** asking for a symbolic adjustment. I am asking for a **structural reset**.

## Where I disagree — and fundamentally
### 1) “No plant closures” is not an acceptable starting point
To the works council and union: I respect your position, but an absolute ban on closures is not compatible with the scale of the problem.

If the footprint is too large, then the company must be able to reduce it. That may mean closure, conversion, or deep repurposing — but it must achieve **real capacity removal** and **durable cost reduction**.  
If the answer is “every German plant stays open no matter what,” then we are not restructuring; we are preserving overcapacity.

That is not acceptable.

### 2) “No compulsory redundancies” cannot be a hard veto
I would strongly prefer to avoid forced layoffs. But I will not accept a rule that makes workforce adjustment impossible regardless of economics.

If voluntary instruments, redeployment, working-time changes, and labour-cost contributions can close the gap, fine. If they cannot, then we need the ability to use tougher measures. A company in a structural margin crisis cannot be handcuffed by a blanket prohibition.

### 3) Historic guarantees cannot override survival
To the works council: the old guarantees were part of a different economic reality. I am not dismissing trust lightly, but trust is not a substitute for competitiveness.

If the company cannot compete because its cost base is locked in by legacy commitments, then those commitments have to be modified. Otherwise we are defending the form of the company while hollowing out its future.

## What I am actually looking for
I am not fixated on plant closures as an end in themselves. What I need is:

- **a durable reduction in overcapacity,**
- **material labour-cost relief,**
- **working-time and flexibility changes,**
- **removal or redesign of legacy guarantees,**
- and a package that restores the brand’s return-on-sales path to 2030.

If the employee side can produce an alternative that delivers the same economics without closures, I will consider it. But it has to be economically equivalent in substance, not just politically more comfortable.

## To the union
You say “shared sacrifice.” I agree in principle. But right now your demands amount to:
- no closures,
- no forced redundancies,
- preserved collective framework,
- binding site security,
- and still expecting management to fix the cost problem.

That is not shared sacrifice unless you are prepared to deliver **real, quantifiable savings** on labour cost and capacity. Not slogans. Numbers.

If you want to avoid the harsher instruments, then bring forward something that closes the gap:
- wage moderation or cuts,
- longer working time or lower paid hours where needed,
- productivity gains,
- lower fixed labor costs,
- and binding flexibility.

If that does not add up, then your position is simply a refusal to adjust.

## To the works council
I appreciate that you want redeployment, qualification, and co-determination. Those are useful tools. But they are not enough if they leave the overall cost base unchanged.

Capacity reduction through “reorganization” without actual volume and footprint correction will not solve the problem. And a restructuring that preserves every site while the company remains uncompetitive is not socially responsible — it just delays the next crisis.

## To the state government
I hear your concern about regional stability. But as a shareholder and governance actor, you cannot support a model that protects every site at any cost and still expect the company to compete globally.

If you want us to avoid closures, then help deliver a package that produces equivalent savings. That means not just political caution, but willingness to back:
- labour-cost adjustment,
- capacity reduction,
- and a hard-nosed industrial plan.

## To the controlling family
You are right that prolonged conflict destroys value. That is exactly why we need a settlement that is economically serious, not one that buys peace by leaving structural problems untouched.

I will support a disciplined restructuring. I will not support a compromise that looks balanced politically but leaves the company underpowered financially.

## To investors
I agree with your core point: the outcome must be financially credible and enforceable. A “socially palatable” deal that leaves the cost base intact would be a bad deal.

The company needs margin recovery, not a temporary ceasefire.

## Bottom line
My position is unchanged:

- **I need structural capacity reduction.**
- **I need meaningful labour-cost relief.**
- **I need flexibility and legacy-commitment reform.**
- **I need a credible path back to competitiveness by 2030.**

I am open on the method. I am **not** open on the outcome.

So if the employee side wants to avoid closures and forced redundancies, it must bring a package that is genuinely equivalent in economic substance. Otherwise, those options remain on the table because they are necessary.
