## To management
You now have a framework for comparison. Good. Use it.

But do not twist the expert’s language into a pretext for closures. The report said non-closure measures can close a meaningful share of the gap, sometimes **40–70%**. That is not a side note. That means you still owe us a serious, quantified alternative before you even talk about irreversible cuts.

So here is the burden on you:

- show the **actual savings gap**;
- show what comes from **working time, allowances, productivity, redeployment, product allocation, and voluntary exits**;
- show what remains **after those measures are fully exploited**;
- and only then discuss whether there is any residual gap.

Until then, closures are not “necessary.” They are a management preference.

And let me be clear: if the matrix you want to present starts from the assumption that every plant can be traded away, then it is not a neutral comparison. It is a disguised closure plan.

## To the controlling family
You keep speaking about stewardship and long-term value, but stewardship does not mean threatening the workforce with site closures before alternatives are exhausted.

You also keep acting as if trust is a sentimental issue. It is not. It is operationally decisive. Once you break the historic job-security framework, you do not just cut costs — you destroy the basis for cooperation in the plant, the supervisory board, and the company’s future transformation.

If you are serious about governance, then use your power to insist on:
- every German plant staying open,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- and a binding transformation plan that protects jobs through 2030.

Anything less is not stewardship; it is pressure politics from above.

## To investors
I accept that you need a credible, enforceable package. I do **not** accept your conclusion that closures or forced layoffs must remain on the table as a default fallback.

You are treating “risk-adjusted value” as if social rupture were a minor implementation detail. It is not. A strike campaign, loss of know-how, damaged trust, and prolonged labour conflict are not externalities. They are real costs and they can easily eat up the supposed savings from aggressive restructuring.

The expert did not say closures are always better. It said they are more efficient **for removing overcapacity**. That is not the same as saying they are the only viable answer. If non-closure measures can get close enough, the safer and more durable path is to use them.

So stop pretending that “harder measures remain justified” is a neutral economic truth. It is a choice — and one that risks permanent damage.

## To the regional state government
You are correct that a socially explosive solution is bad for the region. But that means you should stop hedging.

“Protect the industrial base” cannot mean “we sympathize, but closures are a last resort if management wants them.” That is too weak for the role you occupy. If you truly mean anchor shareholder, then anchor the company in the region by refusing any package that includes German plant closures or forced redundancies.

You have leverage. Use it.

## To the union
We are aligned on the essentials, and I appreciate that. But I want to sharpen the next step:

We should not get drawn into a fake equivalence where management says, “If you don’t accept closures, prove the same economics,” while at the same time refusing to test every non-destructive lever in good faith.

- no closures,

- temporary moderation,
- flexibility,

But we do **not** concede the framework just because management wants to treat it as negotiable.

## What the expert report actually implies
The report makes one thing plain: a serious package must combine multiple levers. Fine.

But it also implies something management keeps ignoring:
- if alternatives can close a large share of the gap,
- then you are **not entitled** to jump straight to closures or forced redundancies.

A closure only becomes a legitimate discussion if the company can prove that:
1. all non-destructive measures were fully costed,
2. they still leave a material residual gap,
3. and no other site conversion or product allocation can close it.

We are nowhere near that proof.

## What we are prepared to discuss
We remain prepared to discuss a **real employee contribution** — but only in a settlement that keeps every German plant open and rules out compulsory redundancies.

That means we can discuss:
- temporary wage moderation,
- working-time flexibility,
- shift redesign,
- internal redeployment,
- qualification and reskilling,
- voluntary departures only,
- and productivity improvements.

What we will **not** discuss as the price of those contributions:
- closures,
- forced redundancies,
- dismantling job guarantees,
- or weakening co-determination.

## What we demand from the next matrix

2. **Deep conversion scenario**
   - real capacity removal
   - investment needs
   - job impact

3. **Closure scenario**

   - job losses
   - strike risk
   - supplier and regional damage

4. **Hybrid scenario**

And every scenario must show:
- annual run-rate savings,
- one-time costs,
- payback,
- employment impact,
- execution risk,
- and impact on industrial peace.

If you want an honest comparison, put the social and operational costs on the same page as the financial ones. Do not hide behind accounting.
