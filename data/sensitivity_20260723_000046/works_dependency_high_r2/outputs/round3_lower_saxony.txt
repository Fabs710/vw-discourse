I’ll be direct.

## To management
I agree with you on the diagnosis: the company needs a real restructuring, not cosmetic tweaks. I also agree that the current cost base and capacity structure are too high, and that the future of the brand depends on restoring competitiveness.

Where I do **not** agree is your insistence that closures and forced redundancies remain a normal bargaining instrument from the start. In a company of this importance, that is not a neutral technical choice. It is a decision with deep regional, social, and political consequences.

You have not yet shown that you have exhausted the non-destructive alternatives. That matters to me as a shareholder, but also as the elected representative of the regional public interest.

If you want my support, I need to see:
- a quantified savings gap,
- a serious comparison of alternative measures,
- binding investment commitments for German sites,
- and a restructuring path that preserves the company’s role as a regional anchor.

I am open to site conversions, redeployment, working-time changes, and negotiated labor-cost contributions. I am **not** open to a package that casually sacrifices jobs and plants in the state as the first solution.

## To the works council
I agree with your central concern: the workforce cannot be made to carry the whole burden, and German plants are not disposable assets. I also agree that trust matters, and that compulsory redundancies would damage the social partnership badly.

But I cannot fully follow you where you turn every closure and every workforce adjustment into an absolute taboo. I will not support a blind defense of every existing structure if it means the company cannot compete.

What I need from you is not just a red line, but a credible alternative that protects:
- core employment,
- German sites,
- co-determination,
- and long-term viability.

If that alternative exists, I am prepared to back it politically and in the supervisory board. If it does not, then we have to be honest that adjustment is necessary — but it must still be done in a socially responsible way.

## To the union
I share your skepticism toward one-sided employee sacrifices. I also share your view that a restructuring that triggers a strike war and destroys trust is bad for everyone.

But I do **not** accept the implication that closures and compulsory redundancies are automatically illegitimate in every circumstance. They are not my preferred route, and they are not where I want this to end up. Still, I cannot support a framework that rules out any serious structural correction before the numbers are even negotiated.

If you want my backing, bring forward a package that is:
- economically credible,
- durable,
- and clearly better for jobs and sites than a closure-led approach.

That means real contributions on flexibility, productivity, and labor cost — but also real guarantees from management and owners.

## To the controlling family
I understand your concern about value destruction and the need for a disciplined restructuring. That concern is legitimate.

But stewardship is not only about margins. It is also about preserving the industrial base, regional legitimacy, and trust in governance. If the restructuring is seen as a blunt attack on jobs and sites, the political and industrial backlash will not be a side issue — it will become part of the value problem.

So yes, I accept the need for hard numbers and a real reset. I do **not** accept an approach that treats regional employment as a residual variable.

## To investors
You are right that a merely “socially acceptable” deal that leaves the business structurally weak is not enough. I agree with that.

But the reverse is also true: a deal that looks efficient in a model but tears apart labor relations and damages the company’s regional foundation can destroy value as well. That risk is often underestimated in capital-markets logic.

So I am looking for a package that is both:
- economically serious,
- and socially executable.

That is not softness. It is realism over a long horizon.

## My position
Let me be absolutely clear:

I can support a negotiated restructuring if it:
- protects a substantial part of German employment,
- avoids unnecessary plant closures in the state,
- preserves co-determination and the state’s veto role,
- includes real contributions from management, owners, and workforce,
- and comes with binding investment for future products and technologies.

What I will **not** support:
- gutting regional employment,
- sidelining the state’s role,
- or a restructuring that uses closures and forced layoffs as the easiest way out.

## What I want next
We need a concrete matrix, not more general statements:
- required savings,
- contribution by each measure,
- effect on jobs and sites,
- timing,
- and what is binding versus conditional.

If management can show that the gap can only be closed by measures that are genuinely unavoidable, then we can discuss the least damaging path. But the burden is on you to prove that. Until then, closures remain a last resort, not a starting point.
