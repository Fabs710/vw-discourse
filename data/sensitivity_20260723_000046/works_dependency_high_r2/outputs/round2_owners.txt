I will respond plainly.

## To management
I agree with you on the diagnosis more than anyone else seems willing to say: the cost base is structurally too high, the footprint is too large for the market, and cosmetic action will not restore margins. If we do not fix this now, we risk a slow erosion of competitiveness that is far more damaging than a difficult restructuring.

Where I disagree is on tone and sequencing. You cannot treat plant closures and forced redundancies as the default opening move and still expect trust. That approach may be economically defensible in the abstract, but it is governance-poor if it triggers a prolonged conflict that destroys value, damages the brand, and hardens political resistance.

So my position is:
- I support a structural reset.
- I do not support a symbolic compromise.
- I do not support drifting into an open-ended conflict.
- I do not support closures as a slogan, but I do keep them as a real fallback if no equivalent alternative is found.

If you want my backing, you need to bring a package that is **quantified**, **credible**, and **durable** — not just a list of threats.

## To the works council and union
I understand your red lines, but I do **not** accept them as absolute.

“No plant closures” and “no compulsory redundancies” as categorical vetoes are not compatible with the scale of the restructuring problem if no economically equivalent alternative exists. I am not interested in a deal that preserves every site, every guarantee, and every legacy entitlement while leaving the company structurally uncompetitive.

That would be value-destructive, and as controlling owners we cannot endorse it.

That said, I do hear one important point from you: if we want workforce contributions, they must be matched by genuine commitments from management and owners, and they must sit inside a credible long-term industrial plan. On that, I agree. I would not support asking employees to sacrifice for a vague promise.

But the alternative you propose still has to close the gap. So show me, concretely:
- how much cost reduction you can deliver,
- how much capacity can be taken out without closures,
- what working-time or flexibility changes you can accept,
- and what labor-cost relief is actually realistic.

Not “not with closures” — I need the math.

## To the regional state government
I value your role as anchor shareholder and your focus on regional stability. But I must be equally clear: protecting the industrial base does **not** mean freezing the existing footprint indefinitely.

I agree that closures should not be the first instinct. I also agree that social peace matters and that the company’s legitimacy in Germany matters. Where I disagree is if that becomes a de facto veto on necessary adjustment.

If the company is to remain strong in Germany, it must be competitive in Germany. That may require difficult site and workforce changes. Your role should be to force a disciplined, negotiated path — not to block every hard decision in advance.

## To institutional investors
You are right on one key point: a deal that looks socially neat but leaves the business underpowered is not acceptable. I share that concern. Long-term equity value depends on margins, execution, and a cost base that supports future investment.

Where I would caution you is against underestimating the value-destruction of unstable conflict. A restructuring that “wins” in the spreadsheet but triggers repeated strikes and political backlash can be worse than a slightly less aggressive package that is actually executable.

So I want the final result to be:
- economically real,
- enforceable,
- and durable in industrial relations.

## What I will support
I can support a negotiated restructuring if it delivers:
- material and permanent capacity reduction,
- real labor-cost relief,
- working-time and flexibility changes,
- redesign of legacy guarantees where necessary,
- binding investment into future products and German sites,
- and a path that restores competitiveness by 2030.

I am open on the mechanism:
- plant closures,
- deep conversion,
- repurposing,
- internal redeployment,
- voluntary departure programs,
- or combinations thereof.

But I am **not** open on an outcome that leaves the core problem untouched.

## What I will not support
Let me be explicit:
- no permanent impairment of enterprise value,
- no restructuring that weakens governance control,
- no cosmetic deal that postpones the problem,
- no plan that leaves the company unable to compete,
- and no settlement that assumes owners will underwrite structural underperformance forever.

## Bottom line
I am prepared to back a serious restructuring, including hard measures if required. But I will not endorse a package that is merely politically comfortable.

If the employee side and state want to avoid the harsher options, they need to put forward an alternative that is economically equivalent in substance, not just more palatable in form. Otherwise, the difficult options remain on the table because the company’s long-term value requires them.
