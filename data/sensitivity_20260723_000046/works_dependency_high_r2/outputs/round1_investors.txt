From the perspective of dispersed capital-market shareholders, the central issue is not who “wins” the symbolism of this restructuring, but whether the final outcome restores **credible, durable competitiveness** and protects risk-adjusted equity value.

## Where we agree
We agree with management, the family, and even the state on the basic diagnosis:

- The cost base is too high.
- The current footprint is likely too large for the market environment.
- The brand is not meeting its return-on-sales target.
- Cosmetic measures are not enough.
- A negotiated solution is preferable to a prolonged destructive conflict.

We also agree with the workforce representatives on one important point: a restructuring that destroys trust and triggers open-ended disruption can destroy more value than it creates. Prolonged strikes, repeated uncertainty, and half-finished measures are bad for margins, bad for execution, and bad for the share price.

## Where we disagree
### 1. “No plant closures” as a hard line is not acceptable
We do not support an outcome that simply preserves every site regardless of economics. If some German capacity is structurally uneconomic, then the company needs the ability to close, repurpose, or deeply downsize it. A settlement that rules out closures in principle may protect jobs in the short run, but it risks leaving the cost base uncompetitive and margins structurally impaired.

That would be unacceptable to us.

### 2. Compulsory redundancies are not the point — durable savings are
We are not ideologically attached to layoffs, but we are also not willing to fund a compromise that avoids difficult decisions while leaving the savings gap unfilled. If the alternative to layoffs is a credible package of capacity cuts, working-time changes, labour-cost reductions, and productivity gains that really closes the gap, we can support that.

But if “no redundancies” just means the same fixed cost burden gets pushed into the future, then that is not a solution. It is deferred value destruction.

### 3. Investment commitments do not substitute for restructuring
We welcome investment in future products and German sites, but investment alone does not fix an uncompetitive industrial model. If management asks for capital discipline from shareholders, it must show disciplined cost action first. Future product spending only makes sense if the underlying margin structure can support it.

## What would move us
We would back a settlement that is economically credible and enforceable. Specifically, we would want to see:

- **A clear, quantified path back to competitive margins**
- **Durable capacity reduction**, even if not via full plant closures
- **Material labour-cost relief**
- **Flexible working-time and deployment measures**
- **A finite, time-bound restructuring package**, not a permanent exception regime
- **A credible implementation timetable and accountability**
- **Meaningful owner and management contribution**, but not at the expense of leaving the business structurally impaired

## Our red lines
We will not support:
- a purely cosmetic compromise,
- an arrangement that keeps all sites open while failing to reset the cost base,
- a deal that buys temporary peace at the price of long-term underperformance,
- or a restructuring that leaves shareholders with a lower-margin, lower-return business.

## To management
We are prepared to support you if you can show that the restructuring is real and the savings are durable. But “closures are on the table” is not enough. Bring a package that demonstrates:
- how much savings are needed,
- how much each lever contributes,
- what the fallback is if labour resists,
- and how the company returns to target margins within a credible horizon.

If the final package is weak, we will say so clearly in capital markets and to analysts.

## To the works council and union
We respect the social and political constraints, but the company cannot be asked to carry historic guarantees indefinitely. If you want to avoid closures and compulsory redundancies, then you need to bring something economically equivalent, not just a refusal.

The burden cannot sit entirely with shareholders through lower returns and with management through delayed action. If the workforce wants site security, it has to come with real, lasting cost relief.

## To the state and family
We understand your role as anchors, but the market will judge the outcome on performance, not on the political elegance of the deal. If the final agreement preserves governance stability but leaves the company undercompetitive, that is still a poor outcome for equity holders.

## Bottom line
We are willing to support a negotiated restructuring, but only if it delivers:

- structural cost reduction,
- credible capacity adjustment,
- competitive margins,
- and implementation certainty.

We will not support a settlement that is socially palatable but financially hollow.
