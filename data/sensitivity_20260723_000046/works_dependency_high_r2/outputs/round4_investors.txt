We appreciate the additional legal and technical clarity. It helps. But from a capital-markets perspective, the core test has not changed:

**Does the package credibly restore competitive margins, on a durable basis, with acceptable execution risk?**

That is the only standard we will back.

## Where we agree

### With management
We agree that:
- the business needs a **real** restructuring, not cosmetics;
- the current cost base is too high;
- legacy protections cannot be sacred if they block competitiveness;
- and a deal that merely buys short-term peace while leaving the cost structure broken would destroy value.

We also agree that **closures and compulsory redundancies cannot be ruled out in principle**. If the quantified gap is large enough, then they remain legitimate tools.

### With the family
We agree that:
- endless delay is value-destructive;
- a hollow compromise is unacceptable;
- and governance has to be disciplined and reality-based.

### With the state
We agree that:
- strike escalation and prolonged conflict would damage value;
- execution certainty matters;
- and industrial peace has financial value.

### With labour
We agree that:
- a destructive conflict is costly;
- workforce contributions should not be one-sided;
- and a settlement has to be executable in practice, not just good in theory.

## Where we disagree

### 1) On “no closures” as an absolute
We do **not** accept a blanket no-closure rule.

The expert input was useful precisely because it showed that:
- non-closure measures can close a meaningful share of the gap,
- but they do **not** automatically close it all,
- and whether they are sufficient depends on the size of the structural problem.

So our position is unchanged:  
If a site is structurally uneconomic and alternatives cannot match the savings and capacity reduction, then closure or deep conversion must remain available.

A promise to keep every German plant open regardless of economics would simply lock in overcapacity and defer the problem. That would be a bad outcome for shareholders and for the company’s long-term competitiveness.

### 2) On “no compulsory redundancies” as an immutable rule
We prefer voluntary solutions. But we will **not** support a framework that makes compulsory redundancies impossible under all circumstances.

If voluntary exits, redeployment, working-time changes, and productivity measures are enough, good. If they are not, management must retain the ability to act.

A blanket veto on workforce adjustment is not a restructuring plan. It is a refusal to restructure.

### 3) On sequencing
We do **not** accept the idea that binding job and site guarantees must come first, with economics proven later.

The order has to be:
1. quantify the gap,
2. test alternatives,
3. compare run-rate savings, one-time costs, and execution risk,
4. then bind the package.

Security without economics is not enough. But neither is economics without implementation certainty.

## What the expert input changes
It changes one thing only: it confirms that **management must prove the gap**, not simply assert that tough measures are unavoidable.

That is appropriate.

So we do **not** support pre-emptive closures announced before the comparison is done. But we also do **not** accept the conclusion that a no-closure package is automatically sufficient just because it is socially preferable.

Social acceptability is not the same as risk-adjusted value creation.

## What we want to see next
We support the moderator’s request for a real scenario matrix, but it has to be decision-grade, not rhetorical.

It should compare:
1. **No-closure / no-forced-redundancy**
2. **Deep conversion**
3. **Closure**
4. **Hybrid**

And each scenario must show:
- annual run-rate savings,
- one-time costs,
- payback,
- employment impact,
- timing,
- probability of successful implementation,
- and industrial-relations risk.

If the no-closure scenario truly closes the gap with better execution certainty, we are open to it.  
If it does not, then closure or deep conversion remains justified.

## Our line, plainly stated
We will support **whatever package credibly restores competitive margins and durable value**.

We will **not** support:
- a cosmetic settlement,
- a package that leaves structural overcapacity in place,
- or an arrangement that rules out the tools needed to fix the cost base.

If the numbers show that a protected, negotiated package can genuinely get the company back to target, then fine.
If not, then tougher measures must remain on the table.

That is our position.
