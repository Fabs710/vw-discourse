We are at the point where slogans are no longer enough. The issue is not whether everyone says they want a “viable future.” The issue is **who pays for it, in what form, and with what guarantees**.

## To management
You keep saying the restructuring must deliver “structural, sustainable reduction in cost and capacity.” I do not dispute the need for real savings. I dispute your conclusion that closures and forced redundancies must remain available as a normal bargaining option.

That is where we draw the line.

If management wants employee support for any contribution, then it must show a package that is:
- **economically credible**,
- **durable**,
- and **socially responsible**.

At minimum, that means:
- no plant closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- preservation of the collective-bargaining framework,
- and a binding industrial plan that secures investment, work volumes, and site futures.

If you cannot do that, then what you are asking for is not shared sacrifice. It is the workforce absorbing the downside while management preserves maximum room to maneuver. That is not acceptable.

## To the works council
We are aligned on the essentials: no closures, no forced layoffs, no unilateral demolition of protections. That is the right starting point.

Where I want to sharpen the point is this: “capacity reduction” can only be acceptable if it is done through **work-sharing, redeployment, qualification, and voluntary instruments**, not by making people disappear through backdoor redundancies. If capacity is to come down, then working time, product allocation, and investment must be adjusted accordingly.

I support your insistence on binding guarantees through 2030. That is not an ideological wish; it is the minimum needed to make any employee contribution legitimate.

## To the state government
Your position is closer to ours than management’s, and I acknowledge that. But I want to be very clear: “no unnecessary closures” is not strong enough if it still leaves closures as the default threat in negotiations.

If the state is serious about preserving the industrial base, then it should use its position to push for:
- binding site security,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- real investment commitments,
- and a negotiated transformation with burden-sharing from owners and management as well.

The state should not help normalize plant closures as a first-line instrument.

## To the controlling family
You speak of stewardship and long-term value. Then act like a steward, not a financial sponsor trying to offload risk onto the workforce.

You say historic guarantees cannot remain sacred if they block survival. Fine. But then show us the numbers, show us the real alternatives you tested, and show us what you and management are contributing. Do not ask the workforce to accept pain first and security later. That sequence is exactly what destroys trust.

## To investors
You keep framing this as a question of “credible, durable competitiveness.” On that point, there is no disagreement. But you are underestimating something important: a restructuring that relies on open-ended conflict, strikes, and permanent fear is not durable. It is fragile.

You want implementation certainty? Then support a settlement that the workforce can actually live with.

A deal that tramples jobs and wage standards may look clean in a model, but it is unstable in practice. It will produce resistance, lost production, and lasting damage to labor relations. That is not value creation.

## Our position in plain terms
We are prepared to discuss a serious, negotiated contribution from employees. That can include:
- temporary labour-cost relief,
- flexibility in working time,
- internal redeployment,
- qualification and reskilling,
- and possibly foregoing some payments or bonuses,
**if and only if** the company gives binding guarantees on:
- no closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- preserved collective bargaining,
- and real investment in German sites.

That is the fair sequence.

## What is not on the table
Let me remove any ambiguity:
- No plant closures in Germany.
- No compulsory redundancies.
- No dismantling of the collective wage framework.
- No “transformation” that is really just a euphemism for social liquidation.

If management insists that only closures and layoffs can deliver the savings, then we will treat that as a refusal to negotiate a fair restructuring. And if that is the case, we will use the tools available to us.

The peace obligation has ended. Our members expect us to defend jobs, wages, and dignity. We are willing to contribute to a viable solution, but we will not be the only side paying the bill.
