I’ll be equally direct.

The latest legal and technical input confirms something important: **a binding, enforceable no-closure / no-compulsory-redundancy settlement through 2030 is possible in principle**. That matters. It means this is **not** a situation where closures and forced layoffs are the only serious instruments. It also means management cannot hide behind “we have no choice” without first proving the gap in hard numbers.

## To management
I agree with you on the diagnosis:
- the company needs a real restructuring,
- the current cost base is too high,
- and cosmetic measures will not restore competitiveness.

I also accept that a durable solution must be economically credible.

Where I disagree is your insistence that **closures and compulsory redundancies remain normal fallback tools from the start**.

That is not acceptable to me as an anchor shareholder and political actor in the regional public interest.

Why? Because:
- we have not yet seen a fully quantified comparison proving that non-destructive measures fail;
- the expert has now confirmed that a legally binding protective package is possible;
- and the regional and social costs of closures are not side effects — they are central to the value calculation.

If you want my support for any tougher measures, then you must first show:
1. the exact savings gap,
2. what non-closure measures deliver,
3. what remains after that,
4. and why that residual gap cannot be closed without destroying sites and jobs in the state.

Until then, closures are not “necessary.” They are an unproven option.

## To the works council
I agree with your core line:
- no plant closures as a starting point,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- no dismantling of co-determination,
- and no one-sided burden on the workforce.

You are right that trust is operationally decisive, not sentimental. Once the company breaks the social partnership model, the conflict costs become real and persistent.

Where I would sharpen the argument is this: we should not only defend red lines; we should define the **constructive package** that makes those red lines workable.

That means I am prepared to back:
- temporary labor-cost moderation,
- working-time flexibility,
- shift redesign,
- redeployment,
- training and reskilling,
- voluntary exits,
- and productivity improvements,

if — and only if — they are exchanged for binding guarantees on:
- no German plant closures through 2030,
- no compulsory redundancies through 2030,
- preservation of co-determination,
- and serious investment and product commitments for German sites.

That is a legitimate exchange. It is not surrender. It is a social-partnership restructuring.

## To the union
I appreciate that you are now speaking in concrete terms. That is the right direction.

I agree that employees should contribute to the turnaround. But I strongly reject the idea that the workforce’s contribution should be negotiated under the shadow of closures as a default threat. That is coercive bargaining, not fair restructuring.

The expert input now shows the legally workable route:
- no compulsory redundancies,
- no closures,
- voluntary instruments,
- collective and company-level agreements,
- and binding governance alignment.

So I expect you to support a real package that includes contributions on:
- working time,
- flexibility,
- allowances,
- productivity,
- and voluntary adjustment,

but I will not accept these being traded against the destruction of jobs and sites in our state.

If management says the savings still do not add up, then they must prove it. They do not get to assume closures just because they are easier on the spreadsheet.

## To the controlling family
You talk about stewardship. I take that seriously.

Stewardship is not only about margin repair. It is also about preserving the industrial base, regional legitimacy, and the company’s long-term social license to operate.

The expert has confirmed that we can build a binding protective framework through 2030. That weakens any argument that closures must remain the default leverage point.

So if you are serious about stewardship, use your influence to support:
- a binding no-closure settlement,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- and a credible restructuring built on shared sacrifice.

I do **not** accept a model where the workforce is asked to give up job security while capital keeps every strategic option open. That is not shared responsibility.

## To investors
I agree with you on one central point: a purely politically comfortable deal that leaves the business structurally weak would be bad value.

But the reverse is equally true: a deal that looks tough in the abstract but triggers conflict, execution failure, loss of know-how, and political backlash can also destroy value.

So your standard should not be “harder is better.” Your standard should be:
- what delivers the best risk-adjusted, durable value?

And the legal and industrial-relations input suggests that a protected settlement may actually be the better execution path if it can still close the gap sufficiently.

So I am open to a package that is economically serious, but only if it is also socially executable and does not gut the regional industrial base.

## What I am prepared to support
I am prepared to support a negotiated restructuring that:
- preserves a substantial part of German employment,
- keeps all German plants open through 2030,
- rules out compulsory redundancies through 2030,
- preserves co-determination and the state’s veto role,
- includes real contributions from management, owners, and workforce,
- and comes with binding investment for future products and technologies.

I am also prepared to accept:
- temporary labor-cost relief,
- working-time flexibility,
- redeployment,
- qualification,
- voluntary departure tools,
- and site-specific efficiency measures.

## What I will not support
I will not support:
- plant closures in Germany as a first solution,
- compulsory redundancies as a bargaining instrument,
- a restructuring that guts regional employment,
- or any attempt to sideline the state’s co-determination and veto role.

That is not a tactical preference. It is a substantive limit.

## What needs to happen next
The discussion now has to move from slogans to a **shared scenario matrix**.

I want management to put forward, side by side:
1. **No-closure / no-forced-redundancy scenario**
2. **Deep conversion scenario**
3. **Closure scenario**
4. **Hybrid scenario**

Each should show:
- annual run-rate savings,
- one-time costs,
- payback period,
- employment impact,
- execution risk,
- and industrial-relations risk.

But let me be clear: the burden is not on the workforce alone. If the no-closure scenario can close the gap with reasonable burden-sharing, that is the politically and socially superior path. Closures only become discussable if management proves they are genuinely unavoidable after all alternatives are fully costed.

## Bottom line
I support a real restructuring.
I do **not** support a destructive one.
I support negotiated adjustment.
I do **not** support blackmail through closure threats.
I support competitiveness.
I do **not** support sacrificing the state’s industrial anchor to achieve it.

If the numbers show that a protected, negotiated package can restore competitiveness, I will back it.
If they do not, then management must come back with a much stronger justification before asking for irreversible cuts.

That is my line.
