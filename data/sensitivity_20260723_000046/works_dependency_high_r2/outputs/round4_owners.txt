I will be direct.

## My overall position
I support a **real restructuring**. I do **not** support:
- cosmetic change,
- endless delay,
- or a settlement that leaves the company structurally uncompetitive.

At the same time, I do **not** accept that closures or compulsory redundancies should be the automatic starting point. The legal and operational inputs confirm that they are **options**, not inevitabilities. They may become necessary if the gap cannot otherwise be closed, but that still has to be proven, not assumed.

## To management
You are right on the diagnosis:
- the cost base is too high,
- the footprint may be too large,
- and the company needs durable savings.

Where I disagree is your tendency to treat closures and forced redundancies as the default instruments rather than the fallback after alternatives are tested.

The expert inputs have made one thing clear:
- non-closure measures can be substantial,
- a legally binding protective package through 2030 is possible,
- and the real question is whether the alternative package closes the gap in hard numbers.

So the burden is now on you to show:
1. the actual annual savings gap,
2. what comes from working time, allowances, productivity, redeployment, product allocation, and voluntary exits,
3. what remains after those measures,
4. and why that residual gap cannot be closed without harsher measures.

Until that matrix exists, I will not endorse pre-emptive closure logic.

What I **will** support is a disciplined comparison of:
- no-closure / no-forced-redundancy package,
- deep conversion,
- closure,
- and hybrid scenarios.

If the no-closure package is not enough, then say so with numbers.

## To the works council
I agree with you on one important point: this cannot be a one-sided burden on the workforce.

I also accept that binding job and site security can be made legally real through 2030. That is helpful. But I do **not** accept your attempt to convert that into an absolute veto over any meaningful restructuring outcome.

Your position is too rigid if it assumes:
- every German site must remain untouched,
- no compulsory redundancy can ever be contemplated,
- and every legacy guarantee survives unchanged regardless of economics.

That would risk freezing overcapacity and weakening the enterprise long term. A weak company does not protect jobs either.

So my answer is:
- yes to binding, time-limited protection if it is paired with real employee contributions,
- no to permanent immunization from structural adjustment.

## To the union
You are right that a destructive conflict would damage value. I agree.

You are also right that employees should not carry the burden alone. I agree with that too.

Where I disagree is your conclusion that no closures and no compulsory redundancies should be the fixed outcome from the start. That may be the preference, but it is not yet the economically proven answer.

If you want that route to stand, then it has to produce:
- meaningful labor-cost relief,
- real capacity reduction,
- and a credible return to margin targets.

If it cannot, then I will not pretend it is sufficient simply because it is more comfortable.

## To the regional state government
I understand your regional responsibility. I respect that.

But I cannot support a position that effectively pre-commits the company to protect every site and every structure before the economics are tested. That would turn public stewardship into a de facto veto over restructuring.

You are right that social and political costs matter. But they must be weighed against the cost of preserving an uncompetitive structure.

So I welcome your support for a binding, negotiated package. I do not accept a blanket regional veto on all tough options.

## To investors
You are right to insist on quantified, enforceable savings and on real capacity reduction.

You are also right that a deal which looks socially tidy but leaves the business structurally impaired is not value creation.

Where I would caution you is this: do not overstate closure as if it were automatically the superior answer. The expert input made clear that execution risk, strike risk, and industrial-relations damage are real costs. A deal that is “clean” in a spreadsheet but unstable in practice is not better.

So I support:
- hard numbers,
- scenario comparison,
- and a real assessment of execution risk.

I do **not** support a simplistic “harder is always better” logic.

## What the legal input means for me
The legal assessment did **not** move me toward accepting endless protection. It confirmed that:
- binding no-compulsory-redundancy commitments are possible,
- no-closure commitments are possible with proper drafting,
- but they must be time-limited, conditional, and carefully structured.

That is useful because it gives us a way to negotiate a serious settlement without turning the company into a hostage to permanent rigidity.

But it also means the workforce cannot claim that closures and layoffs are off the table by definition. They remain available if the numbers require them.

## My governance stance
I want management to bring a **real scenario matrix** to the supervisory board and shareholder governance process:
1. no-closure / no-forced-redundancy,
2. deep conversion,
3. closure,
4. hybrid.

Each must include:
- annual run-rate savings,
- one-time costs,
- payback,
- employment impact,
- execution risk,
- and industrial-relations risk.

That is the only serious way to decide this.

## Bottom line
My position remains:
- I support a durable restructuring,
- I support cost discipline,
- I support competitiveness,
- and I support a negotiated settlement if it is economically credible.

I do **not** support:
- a cosmetic compromise,
- a permanent impairment of enterprise value,
- or an automatic rush to closures before alternatives are fully tested.

If the quantified alternative closes the gap, I can support it.
If it does not, then harder measures must stay available.

That is the line I will hold.
