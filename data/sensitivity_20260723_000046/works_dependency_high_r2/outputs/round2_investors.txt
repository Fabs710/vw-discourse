From the perspective of dispersed capital-market shareholders, the discussion is now moving in the right direction in one respect: everyone is finally acknowledging that **the restructuring must be real, durable, and quantified**. That matters to us. We are not here to endorse symbolic compromise.

But I need to be equally clear about where we stand.

## What we agree with
We agree with management, the family, and the state on the core diagnosis:
- the cost base is too high,
- capacity is misaligned with the market,
- and cosmetic measures will not restore margins.

We also agree with labour and the state on one important point:
- **a prolonged strike war or a badly executed restructuring can destroy value fast**.

We are not interested in “winning” an industrial confrontation if the result is lower margins, delayed product execution, and a weaker share price.

## Where we disagree
### 1) An absolute “no closures” line is not acceptable
To the works council and union: we understand why you are taking that position, but from an equity-holder perspective it is not a viable starting point. If there is structurally uneconomic capacity in Germany, the company must be able to remove it. If every site is preserved regardless of economics, then the cost base remains uncompetitive and the burden is simply deferred.

That is not a solution. That is value destruction.

### 2) “No compulsory redundancies” cannot become a blanket veto
We prefer negotiated workforce adjustment. We do **not** prefer forced layoffs. But if all voluntary and flexible measures fail to close the savings gap, then a blanket prohibition becomes a way of blocking restructuring altogether. We cannot support that.

### 3) Social peace is valuable, but only if it is built on real economics
To the state and the union: we do not dismiss industrial peace. In fact, we value it highly. But peace that preserves an uncompetitive structure is only a delay mechanism. Markets will price that in quickly.

## What we need to see
We are prepared to support a negotiated settlement only if it gives us a credible path back to competitive margins. That means:

- **material, durable cost reduction**
- **real capacity reduction**
- **a quantified savings plan**
- **a finite implementation timetable**
- **clear accountability**
- **and no open-ended exception regime**

If alternatives to closures can deliver the same economics, we are open to them. But the bar is high: they must be **economically equivalent**, not just politically more comfortable.

## On the specific arguments raised

### To management
We agree with your insistence that the company needs structural rather than cosmetic change. But we would caution you against framing the issue too narrowly as closures versus nothing. The market does not care about the form; it cares about margins, execution, and cash generation.

So bring the numbers:
- what savings are required,
- what each lever contributes,
- what the fallback is if labour resists,
- and when the margin target is realistically restored.

If the final package is weak, we will say so publicly and in capital-markets channels.

### To the works council and union
You are right that trust matters. You are right that a brutal, one-sided approach would be disruptive. But trust is not an alternative to competitiveness.

If you want to avoid closures and forced layoffs, you need to bring forward a package that actually closes the gap:
- capacity cuts,
- working-time flexibility,
- labour-cost relief,
- redeployment and reskilling,
- productivity improvements.

If that package is real, we can support it. If it is just a refusal to adjust, we cannot.

### To the state
We respect your role as an anchor shareholder and political stabilizer. But the market will judge the final package on performance, not on how socially elegant it sounds. Do not back a settlement that protects every site but leaves shareholders with a structurally impaired business.

### To the controlling family
You are right that prolonged conflict is destructive. We agree. But you must also understand that capital will not fund a compromise that leaves the company underpowered. Governance stability is not enough if the economics are weak.

## Our red lines
We will not support:
- a cosmetic compromise,
- a deal that keeps all sites open while failing to reset the cost base,
- a settlement that buys temporary peace at the price of long-term underperformance,
- or a restructuring that leaves margins structurally impaired.

## What would move us
A package that we could support would need to show:
1. a credible path to competitive margins,
2. durable capacity reduction,
3. meaningful labour-cost relief,
4. enforceable implementation,
5. and a clear industrial plan tied to future investment.

If those conditions are met, we are open to solutions that avoid the most disruptive measures. If they are not, we will not support a deal just because it sounds balanced.

## Bottom line
We are willing to back a negotiated restructuring — but only if it is financially serious.  
We will not support a settlement that is socially acceptable but economically hollow.
