I will respond directly, because we are now well past generalities.

## First: what I agree with
I agree that a viable future matters.
I agree that cosmetic measures are useless.
I agree that strikes, distrust, and open conflict destroy value.
And I agree that any settlement must be durable, not temporary.

I also agree that employee contributions should not be one-sided in the abstract. Management, owners, and the state all have a role in making a restructuring work.

## Where I fundamentally disagree
### 1) “No plant closures” is not an acceptable absolute
To the works council and union: I hear your red line, but I do not accept it.

If German capacity is structurally above what the market can support, then the footprint must come down. If you rule out closures categorically, you are ruling out one of the few instruments that can create durable capacity reduction at the scale required.

That is not a serious restructuring answer. It is a demand to preserve overcapacity indefinitely.

### 2) “No compulsory redundancies” cannot be a blanket veto
I would prefer to avoid forced layoffs. But I will not accept a framework that makes them impossible under any circumstance.

If voluntary departures, redeployment, working-time changes, and labour-cost measures are enough, then fine. If they are not enough, then the company must retain the ability to act. A structural margin crisis cannot be solved with a hard veto on workforce adjustment.

### 3) Historic guarantees cannot outweigh survival
The old guarantees were written for a different market reality. That reality is gone.

I am not dismissing trust lightly, but trust does not substitute for competitiveness. If legacy protections prevent the company from restoring margins, then those protections have to be revised. Otherwise we are defending a structure that cannot finance its own future.

## To the works council
You keep asking for proof that closures are necessary, as if the burden of proof were still open in the abstract.

It is not enough to say “show us every alternative” while rejecting the actual levers that would make those alternatives work. If you want to preserve every site, then you need to deliver equivalent economics. Not sentiment. Economics.

That means quantified contributions in the following areas:
- labour-cost relief,
- working-time flexibility,
- lower fixed staffing cost,
- productivity gains,
- product allocation shifts,
- and real capacity reduction through reorganization.

If those do not close the gap, then your position is simply a refusal to restructure.

## To the union
You say the company has not proven closures are necessary. But you also refuse the very tools that would prove the opposite.

I am willing to discuss a serious alternative if it produces the same outcome in substance:
- durable cost reduction,
- real footprint reduction,
- and a path back to margin targets by 2030.

That alternative must be more than temporary wage moderation and reskilling. It has to fix the structural cost base. If it doesn’t, then it is not an alternative; it is delay.

## To the state government
You want to protect the industrial base. I understand that. But protecting the industrial base does **not** mean freezing the footprint and protecting every site regardless of economics.

If you want me to avoid closures, then help deliver a package with real savings and real flexibility. Don’t simply ask management to absorb the downside while keeping every site politically protected.

## To the controlling family
You are right that an unstable conflict would destroy value. But that cuts both ways.

A compromise that preserves peace but leaves the company structurally uncompetitive also destroys value — just more slowly. I am not interested in buying short-term calm at the price of long-term decline.

So yes, I support a disciplined, negotiated restructuring. I do **not** support a symbolic settlement.

## To investors
You are right to demand quantified, enforceable savings. That is exactly the point.

I need:
- permanent capacity reduction,
- material labour-cost relief,
- working-time and flexibility reform,
- revision of legacy commitments where they block competitiveness,
- and a credible route back to return-on-sales targets.

If I don’t get that, then the restructuring fails, regardless of how well it is packaged politically.

## What management is prepared to consider
I am not fixated on one single mechanism. I am open on the method, not on the outcome.

Possible instruments can include:
- plant closure,
- deep conversion,
- repurposing with true capacity removal,
- voluntary departure programmes,
- working-time changes,
- labour-cost contributions,
- redeployment,
- and productivity measures.

But the package has to add up in hard numbers. If it does not, we will not pretend otherwise.

## What I will not accept
Let me be explicit:
- no cosmetic deal,
- no restructuring that preserves uncompetitive overcapacity,
- no settlement that leaves the brand unable to fund future products and technology,
- no blanket veto on tough measures,
- and no model where management is expected to restore competitiveness without structural workforce contribution.

## Bottom line
The company needs a real reset, not a protected status quo.

If the employee side wants to avoid closures and forced redundancies, it must bring forward a package that is economically equivalent in substance. Until then, those options remain on the table because they are necessary.

I am prepared to negotiate hard on the mechanism. I am not prepared to negotiate away the need for a structurally competitive cost base.
