I will speak plainly.

We are aligned on the objective: **the company must become competitive again, restore margins, and protect the long-term industrial base**. I also agree that a purely symbolic restructuring would be a mistake. If the cost base is structurally out of line, then that has to be addressed.

But I do **not** support a solution that destroys value through a prolonged conflict, damages the brand, or weakens the company’s governance. And I do **not** support any restructuring that turns into a de facto permanent impairment of the enterprise.

## Where I agree
- The company needs **real structural improvement**, not cosmetic adjustments.
- The workforce cannot be the **only** side to contribute.
- A **negotiated settlement** is preferable to open escalation.
- The German sites need a **credible future**, not just cost cutting.
- Investment in future products and technology is necessary.

## Where I disagree

### 1. “No plant closures” cannot be a blanket veto
I understand the political and social reasons for resisting closures. But from an ownership perspective, I cannot accept an absolute red line that makes any structural correction impossible.

If management can show that the current footprint is too large and too expensive, then the answer cannot simply be “keep everything open and hope for the best.” That would be value-destructive.

What I will insist on is this: closures must be a **last resort**, not a slogan. They must be justified by transparent economics, and only pursued if serious alternatives fail to deliver comparable durable savings.

### 2. Compulsory redundancies are not ideal, but they are not automatically off the table
I would strongly prefer a negotiated solution that avoids forced layoffs. But I will not support a framework where employment adjustment is effectively impossible regardless of the business case.

If the employee side wants to avoid that outcome, it needs to help produce a credible alternative that actually closes the cost gap. Good intentions are not enough.

### 3. Legacy guarantees cannot be treated as sacred if they block survival
Historical protections matter. Trust matters. But if every guarantee becomes untouchable, the company loses the ability to adapt. That is not stewardship; that is slow erosion.

I am prepared to accept modifications to legacy arrangements if they are part of a balanced, time-limited, and economically serious settlement.

## What would move us
We would support a solution that is built around **durable competitiveness**, not just conflict avoidance. That means:
- meaningful capacity reduction,
- labor-cost relief,
- working-time and flexibility measures,
- stronger productivity commitments,
- binding investment into future products and German sites,
- and a stable industrial peace that lasts.

What we will **not** support is a package that leaves the footprint, cost base, and legacy obligations essentially intact while calling the result “transformation.”

## To management
You have made the seriousness of the situation clear, and I do not dismiss it. But if you want support from the ownership side, you must present this in a way that is disciplined and credible:
- what savings are required,
- what alternatives were tested,
- what the cost of each path is,
- what the workforce contribution is,
- and how long the result remains competitive.

If the numbers justify deep restructuring, then say so cleanly. If alternatives can deliver the same effect, then they deserve serious consideration. But vague pressure will not be enough.

## To the works council and union
I understand your refusal to accept closures and forced redundancies as a starting point. But if that position becomes absolute, you risk blocking any viable adjustment and pushing the company toward a worse outcome later.

A fair settlement cannot mean that employees contribute nothing while owners and management absorb all the risk. Shared sacrifice is necessary. The question is whether it is negotiated in a controlled way or imposed later under worse conditions.

## Bottom line
My position is this:

- I support a **serious restructuring**.
- I support **negotiated alternatives** to closures if they deliver equivalent savings.
- I do **not** support a blank refusal of any site or workforce adjustment.
- I do **not** support a one-sided attack on the workforce.
- I do **not** support any outcome that permanently damages enterprise value or undermines governance control.

If there is a credible plan that restores competitiveness, preserves industrial peace, and protects the long-term value of the company, I am prepared to back it. If not, then I will not endorse a superficial compromise that simply defers the problem.
