I will speak plainly.

## My position
I am **not** prepared to support a restructuring that relies on **German plant closures** or **compulsory redundancies** as the central solution. That would cross a line for us as regional shareholder and political actor. It would damage regional employment, weaken the industrial base, and undermine the company’s role as an economic anchor in our state.

At the same time, I am **not** defending the status quo. I accept that the company needs a **real, durable competitiveness reset**. But that reset must be **socially responsible**, economically credible, and compatible with co-determination. I will not back a plan that solves a margin problem by hollowing out the region.

## Where I agree

### To management
I agree that cosmetic compromise is worthless. The company needs:
- measurable savings,
- a credible industrial plan,
- investment in future products,
- and a structure that restores competitiveness.

I also agree that the numbers matter. We cannot rely on slogans.

### To the union and works council
You are right that the workforce cannot be treated as the only shock absorber. Any package has to include contributions from:
- management,
- ownership,
- overhead,
- procurement,
- and future product investment.

That is fair, and I support that principle.

### To the family and investors
You are right that a drawn-out stalemate would be damaging. A strike spiral or a weak compromise would hurt the company and the region. I share that concern. We need a solution that is durable, not theatrical.

## Where I disagree

### On closures and forced redundancies
I do **not** accept management’s framing that closures should remain the default fallback while “the numbers are tested.”

Closures are not a neutral tool. Once a German plant is closed, the damage is not limited to capacity:
- skills are lost,
- supplier networks are weakened,
- regional trust is destroyed,
- and the company’s social legitimacy is damaged.

The same is true, politically and socially, for compulsory redundancies. They are highly escalatory and should not be treated as normal bargaining leverage.

### On unconditional red lines from the employee side
I also do **not** accept a blanket refusal to discuss any capacity adjustment at all.

A serious restructuring may require:
- working-time flexibility,
- voluntary departures,
- redeployment,
- qualification,
- temporary wage moderation,
- bonus restraint,
- and management reductions.

But these must be part of a balanced package. I will not support a deal where everyone else contributes and labour is exempt, just as I will not support one where labour pays while ownership and management stay untouched.

### On “review triggers” as a trap
I understand the works council’s concern that review mechanisms can become a delayed closure mandate. That concern is legitimate. But the answer is not to reject any staged solution outright. The answer is to design one that is genuinely fair, transparent, and bounded by clear governance.

A trigger mechanism can only be acceptable if:
- it is jointly defined,
- the alternatives are quantified first,
- and any escalation is subject to the full co-determination and shareholder governance framework.

It must not be a backdoor closure plan.

## What the expert’s numbers tell us
The independent modeler confirmed something important: non-destructive measures can be sufficient **if the gap is moderate**, but may fall short if the structural gap is very large.

That is exactly why I am insisting on **a full, plant-by-plant and function-by-function financial bridge** before anyone claims inevitability.

I want the company to present:
- the recurring savings gap,
- what each measure contributes,
- what is temporary and what is durable,
- one-time costs,
- and the residual gap after all non-destructive measures.

Only then can we judge whether closures are genuinely necessary or whether a negotiated alternative can do the job.

## What I can support
I am prepared to support a package that includes:
- working-time flexibility,
- temporary wage moderation or deferred components,
- bonus restraint,
- internal redeployment,
- qualification and retraining,
- voluntary retirement pathways,
- management overhead reductions,
- procurement and efficiency improvements,
- and capacity reduction **where it does not require plant closure**.

I can also support a **time-limited, jointly governed review** if — and only if — it is genuinely a review of alternatives, not a coded closure decision.

## What I cannot support
I will not support:
- German plant closures as the first serious answer,
- compulsory redundancies as a bargaining instrument,
- a structure that guts regional employment,
- or a governance process that sidelines the state and co-determination.

And let me be equally clear: I will not support a “solution” that simply postpones the problem while leaving the company uncompetitive. That would fail both the company and the region.

## To management
If you want my support, bring a **quantified package** that shows:
- what can be achieved without closures,
- what management and ownership contribute,
- what labour contributes,
- and what residual gap remains.

Do not ask me to endorse closures before proving that negotiated alternatives are insufficient.

## To the union and works council
If you want to avoid closures, then I need to see a package that is not just principled, but **numerically credible**. That means real contributions, not symbolic ones, and a willingness to discuss productivity, flexibility, and internal restructuring.

But the price of my support is also clear: **no plant closures, no forced redundancies, and no erosion of co-determination**.

## To the family and investors
If you want long-term value, then you must understand that regional industrial stability is not a side issue. It is part of the company’s strength. A value-destructive conflict would not be stewardship; it would be self-harm.

## Bottom line
I am prepared to back a **serious negotiated restructuring** that:
- restores competitiveness,
- protects the core German industrial base,
- preserves jobs as far as possible,
- and maintains co-determination and the state’s blocking role.

I am **not** prepared to back a plan built around plant closures and compulsory redundancies as the central mechanism.

If management cannot produce a sufficiently strong alternative, then it will not find the regional state government endorsing social and industrial damage.
