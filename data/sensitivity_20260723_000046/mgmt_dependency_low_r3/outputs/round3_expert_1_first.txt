From a restructuring-finance perspective, the key question is not whether “non-destructive” measures are conceivable in the abstract, but whether they can close the **required recurring cost gap** with enough certainty, speed, and durability to satisfy a lender/owner/investor standard.

## My assessment in one sentence
**If the company’s structural gap is large enough to require several hundred million euros of annual recurring savings, then a package relying only on working-time flexibility, wage moderation, bonus restraint, overhead cuts, and productivity improvements may close part — but often not all — of the gap unless the workforce contribution is unusually deep and management/owner contributions are also material.**

## What can be quantified

Because no detailed plant P&L or cost bridge was provided, I cannot verify the exact required savings. So I’ll use a transparent restructuring model framework:

### 1) Typical savings categories in a non-closure package
These measures usually fall into four buckets:

1. **Labour-cost moderation**
   - temporary wage restraint
   - deferred compensation
   - bonus reduction
   - special-payment deferral
   - working-time flexibility

2. **Headcount reduction without forced layoffs**
   - voluntary retirement
   - attrition
   - internal redeployment
   - partial retirement schemes

3. **Non-labour savings**
   - management overhead cuts
   - procurement and logistics savings
   - travel, external services, IT simplification

4. **Productivity/capacity measures**
   - shift reconfiguration
   - line balancing
   - reduced overtime
   - capacity utilization improvement
   - product allocation changes

### 2) Order-of-magnitude savings ranges
For a large German automotive manufacturing base, reasonable annual recurring savings ranges are often:

- **Temporary wage moderation / bonus restraint:**  
  roughly **1% to 3% of affected wage bill** as recurring-equivalent savings, depending on duration and design.

- **Working-time flexibility / shift redesign:**  
  often **€5k to €20k per affected employee per year** equivalent, but highly site-specific.

- **Voluntary attrition / retirement:**  
  can reduce cost materially, but savings are **delayed** and offset by severance / bridge costs.

- **Overhead and procurement:**  
  often **low-to-mid double-digit millions** in a large plant or business unit, sometimes more if there is obvious duplication.

- **Forced closure of a plant:**  
  can produce the **largest recurring fixed-cost reduction**, but with **very high one-time cash costs**:
  - severance
  - decommissioning
  - environmental / site remediation
  - supply-chain disruption
  - utilization penalties at remaining plants
  - start-up / relocation costs

### 3) Closure vs. non-closure economics
This is the core trade-off:

#### Non-closure alternative
**Pros**
- lower one-time cash outlay
- lower disruption
- lower labour conflict risk
- preserves skills and local supply relationships
- faster to implement if negotiated

**Cons**
- recurring savings are usually smaller
- may not fully remove structural overcapacity
- savings may be back-loaded or conditional
- can be fragile if based on temporary concessions

#### Closure-based scenario
**Pros**
- largest potential fixed-cost reduction
- clearer long-term capacity rationalization
- more credible to capital markets if the gap is severe

**Cons**
- significant one-time cash cost
- major execution risk
- strike / disruption risk
- possible underestimation of lost output, rework, ramp-up, and morale effects
- can destroy value if the plant is not truly excess capacity or if product allocation shifts are imperfect

## Illustrative scenario framework
Since I don’t have the actual gap, here is how the economics usually look.

### Scenario A: “Non-destructive package”
Assume:
- wage moderation + bonus restraint: **€60m–€120m/year**
- working-time flexibility / shift changes: **€30m–€80m/year**
- overhead + procurement savings: **€40m–€100m/year**
- voluntary attrition / redeployment: **€20m–€70m/year** recurring-equivalent after ramp

**Total plausible recurring savings:**  
**€150m–€370m/year**

This is meaningful, but it may still be short if the required gap is in the **€400m+ per year** range.

### Scenario B: Closure / hard structural cut
Assume:
- plant closure or equivalent major capacity removal delivers: **€250m–€500m/year** recurring savings, depending on size and load factor
- one-time cash cost: **€300m–€900m+** or more, depending on severance, decommissioning, contracts, and site issues

This can close a bigger gap, but the payback period depends on:
- annual savings actually realized
- one-time cash cost
- strike/disruption losses
- post-closure output substitution cost

A simple payback test:
- If closure saves **€350m/year** and costs **€700m** one-time, nominal payback is about **2.0 years**.
- If disruption or lost output costs another **€100m–€200m**, payback extends materially.

## What this means for the dispute
The union/works council position is financially plausible **if** the needed gap is moderate and the company is willing to combine:
- real labour concessions,
- management cuts,
- owner contribution,
- and product/investment commitments.

But management/investor skepticism is also financially plausible **if** the structural gap is large, because:
- temporary measures may not be enough,
- “guarantees through 2030” can freeze flexibility,
- and savings from reversible measures may not be durable enough.

## Bottom-line expert view
- If the gap is around **€150m–€300m/year**, a negotiated non-destructive package is often **financially feasible**.
- If the gap is around **€400m–€600m+/year**, then non-destructive measures may be **insufficient unless unusually deep concessions are obtained** and supported by management/owner contributions.
- If management is considering closures, it should compare:
  - **NPV of recurring savings**
  - versus
  - **one-time restructuring cash cost + disruption risk + lost output**

In other words: **closures are not automatically the better financial option, but they become more likely to be value-positive as the structural gap grows and as non-closure measures approach the limits of durability and magnitude.**
