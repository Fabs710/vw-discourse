## First: what the expert confirmed
The independent modeler basically validated the core management point: **non-destructive measures may be enough only if the gap is moderate**. If the structural gap is in the several-hundred-million-euro range, then wage moderation, working-time measures, overhead cuts, and voluntary attrition may still fall short.

That matters. It means the employee side cannot simply repeat “no closures, no redundancies” and assume the numbers will somehow work. They have not shown that their preferred model closes the gap.

## Where I agree
### With the expert
I agree with the expert’s main conclusion: this is a numbers question, not a moral slogan contest. We need to know the recurring savings gap, the one-time costs, and the durability of each measure.

### With the union and works council
I agree that cosmetic compromise is worthless, and I agree that a strike spiral would destroy value. I also agree that workforce contributions must be real, measurable, and not symbolic.

### With the state and family
I agree that a value-destructive conflict would be bad. I also agree that long-term stewardship matters.

## Where I disagree
### 1) “No closures” and “no compulsory redundancies” cannot be absolute
This remains the central issue.

The works council says review triggers are a trap. The union says “first” is not enough. But neither of them has offered a package that demonstrably closes the structural gap without leaving us with a permanently uncompetitive cost base.

If the gap is big enough, then **capacity has to come out of the system**. That is not rhetoric; it is the reality of the cost structure. If that can be achieved without plant closures, fine — I’m open to that. But I will not accept a self-denying rule that rules out the instruments before we know whether the alternative is sufficient.

### 2) Guarantees through 2030 are not credible without hard restructuring
The employee side keeps demanding binding site and job guarantees through 2030. I understand why they ask for that. But I will be blunt: if the savings are insufficient, such guarantees are not a solution, they are a postponement of the problem.

We cannot give unconditional guarantees that preserve an uncompetitive structure. That would be irresponsible to the company, the owners, and the capital base we need for future products and technology.

### 3) “All sides contribute” is correct, but not enough unless the workforce contribution is material
The expert’s ranges are helpful. They suggest that a non-closure package can perhaps deliver **€150m–€370m annually** in a large industrial base, depending on depth and scope. That may be enough in some cases. But if the required gap is above that, then we need more.

So the key question is not whether management, overhead, procurement, and owners should contribute. Of course they should. The question is whether those contributions are enough **without** deeper structural capacity adjustment.

I have not heard a credible answer to that.

## What the expert’s numbers mean for us
The expert’s own model shows exactly why I’m insisting on structural options:
- Non-closure measures can be meaningful.
- But they are often back-loaded, conditional, or temporary.
- Closure or equivalent major capacity reduction gives the clearest recurring savings.

That is why closures remain on the table. Not as a threat for its own sake, but because they may be the only way to close the residual gap if negotiated measures fall short.

## On the moderator’s bridge proposals
### Proposal 1: Two-stage package with trigger-based escalation
I can accept a staged approach **only if stage one is materially meaningful and stage two is not blocked by employee-side vetoes of reality**.

In other words:
- Stage 1: immediate, real cost actions — wage moderation, bonus restraint, working-time flexibility, overhead cuts, voluntary separation.
- Stage 2: if the residual gap remains, then the company must retain the right to implement deeper structural capacity measures.

That is not a trapdoor. That is responsible governance.

### Proposal 2: “No forced redundancies first” with quantified review
I can work with “no forced redundancies first” as a sequencing principle, but not as a permanent ban. Forced redundancies should not be the first resort. Fine. But if there is still a structural gap after negotiated measures, then management cannot be handcuffed.

### Proposal 3: Binding guarantees tied to measurable contributions
This is only workable if the guarantees are conditional on achieving the needed savings. I will not sign up to unconditional site guarantees through 2030 if they prevent the restructuring from actually fixing the business.

## My position, clearly
I am prepared to support a negotiated deal if it achieves all of the following:
1. **durable reduction in the cost base**
2. **real reduction in overcapacity**
3. **material workforce contribution**
4. **management and owner contribution**
5. **credible margin recovery and investment capacity**

That can be achieved through a combination of measures, including closures if needed. If the employee side can prove that a non-closure package is sufficient in the numbers, that is preferable. But I have not seen that proof.

These positions do not work for me:
- a blanket no to closures,
- a blanket no to compulsory redundancies,
- unconditional site guarantees through 2030,
- symbolic concessions,
- or a settlement that preserves the current footprint and hopes the margin problem goes away.
