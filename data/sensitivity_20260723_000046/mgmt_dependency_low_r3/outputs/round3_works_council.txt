Let me be very clear: I do **not** accept a “review-trigger” model if it is just a dressed-up pathway to plant closures and forced layoffs later. The workforce will not sign away its security today in exchange for a promise that the axe may fall after some future accounting exercise.

## Where I agree
I agree with the moderator on one point: we need numbers, not slogans.

I also agree with the union, the state government, and even parts of what management and investors say: this company needs a durable solution, and it is true that temporary patches alone are not enough.

But durability does **not** mean closing plants and breaking the job-security framework. Durability means restoring competitiveness **without** destroying the industrial backbone in Germany.

## Where I disagree

### To Group & Brand Management
You still have not answered the central question: **why are closures and forced redundancies the first serious tools on the table?**

You speak of “structural overcapacity” as if that automatically proves closures are necessary. It does not. Before you demand irreversible damage, you must show, in hard numbers, that you have exhausted:
- working-time measures,
- internal redeployment,
- qualification and retraining,
- management overhead cuts,
- procurement savings,
- executive compensation restraint,
- and a fair owner-side contribution.

You have not done that convincingly.

And I reject your framing that a commitment to keep every German plant open is “preserving an uncompetitive structure.” That is a false dichotomy. There are restructuring paths that reduce costs without destroying sites:
- capacity reductions through shifts and lines, not closures,
- flexible working-time arrangements,
- product allocation changes,
- and cost contributions from capital and management.

If your model cannot close the gap without shutting plants, then the model is too narrow, not the workforce too expensive.

### To the Institutional Investors
You keep saying “the numbers must work,” and I do not disagree. But you are treating closures as if they were neutral instruments. They are not. They carry massive execution risk, labour conflict, supplier disruption, and long-term damage to the company’s reputation and internal trust.

That is not a side issue. It is part of the numbers.

You ask what margin improvement would be enough. My answer is simple: **a package that restores competitiveness without closures or compulsory redundancies is the only package that is socially and industrially acceptable.** If your threshold requires plant closures, then you are not asking for restructuring — you are asking the workforce to pay for management failures.

### To the Controlling Family Holding
You talk about stewardship, but stewardship means more than demanding a sharper margin. It means protecting the company’s industrial base and its social legitimacy.

If you truly care about long-term value, then you should not support a plan that detonates trust with the workforce. Because once that trust is gone, it is not recovered by spreadsheets.

I will also say this plainly: owners cannot insist on “hard choices” while refusing to bear hard consequences themselves. If labour is expected to contribute, then owners must put real skin in the game:
- long-term reinvestment,
- credible product commitments,
- and a binding rejection of closures and forced redundancies.

### To the Regional State Government
Your position is correct in principle, but I need more than political concern. If you are serious, then use the leverage you have in governance to make one thing unmistakable: **German plant closures and compulsory redundancies are not acceptable tools.**

Do not hide behind “review triggers.” If a trigger is written in a way that allows closures later, then it is just a delayed closure decision.

### To the Union
We are aligned on the essentials, and I appreciate that. But let me tighten the point: temporary wage moderation, bonus restraint, and flexibility are only discussable if they are tied to **binding guarantees through 2030** with:
1. no German plant closures,
2. no compulsory redundancies,
3. preserved co-determination,
4. and concrete investment and product allocation.

Without that, workforce concessions are simply a one-way transfer of risk.

## On the moderator’s bridge proposals

### Proposal 1: Two-stage package with trigger-based escalation
I reject this in its current logic. A trigger-based escalation is only a polite way of saying: “concede now, and we reserve the right to break the promise later.” That is not a guarantee. That is conditional insecurity.

### Proposal 2: “No forced redundancies first” with quantified capacity review
This is closer, but still not enough. “First” implies that forced redundancies remain a legitimate fallback. For us, they are not. And a “capacity review” that can end in closure is simply closure by another name if the parameters are loose enough.

### Proposal 3: Binding guarantees tied to measurable owner/management contributions
This is the only direction that even begins to approach seriousness, but only if the guarantees are real and not revocable:
- every German site remains open,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- no closure triggers,
- no erosion of co-determination,
- and all contributions are shared fairly.

If management and owners want labour contributions, then we need a genuine contract, not a trapdoor.
