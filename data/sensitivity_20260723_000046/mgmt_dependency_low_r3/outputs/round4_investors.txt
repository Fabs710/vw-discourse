We agree with management, the family, and the specialists on the central point: **this is a numbers problem, not a slogan problem**. But we are not seeing a numbers case yet that justifies either a blanket no-closure stance or an open-ended “structure reset” that merely shifts pain without restoring margins.

## Our position
We want the deal that delivers the **best risk-adjusted value** for shareholders:
- a **credible return to competitive margins**,
- a **durable reduction in the cost base**,
- and **execution certainty**.

If the company cannot get there without deeper structural measures, then the restructuring must be deep enough to work. If it can get there without closures, fine — but the burden is on the employee side to prove that with hard numbers, not principles.

## Where we agree

### With management
You are right that the current cost base is not competitive enough, and that temporary patchwork alone is not enough. We also agree that a cosmetic compromise would be value-destructive.

### With the family holding
We agree that a prolonged strike spiral or a badly executed confrontation would destroy value. We also agree that closures are not automatically good if they wipe out more value than they save.

### With the state
We agree that social conflict and industrial damage are costly. But regional industrial policy only matters if the business remains viable.

### With the specialists
The experts have made the key point clearly: non-closure measures can work **if the gap is moderate**, but may fall short if the structural gap is large. That is the right framing.

## Where we disagree

### 1) Absolute red lines against closures and compulsory redundancies
We do **not** support turning “no plant closures” and “no compulsory redundancies” into unconditional vetoes before the numbers are tested.

If a non-closure package can close the gap, we would prefer it because it reduces disruption and execution risk. But if it cannot, then ruling out structural options in advance is not responsible capital allocation.

### 2) Binding guarantees through 2030 as a precondition
We understand why labour wants guarantees. But unconditional guarantees that are not supported by a financially sufficient plan simply transfer risk back to shareholders while leaving the margin problem unresolved.

We will not back commitments that lock in an uncompetitive structure.

### 3) Review-trigger arrangements that become delayed closure promises
We are skeptical of trigger-based models if they are just a way to avoid hard decisions now and reopen them later under worse conditions. That only adds uncertainty.

A staged approach can be acceptable only if it is transparent, time-bound, and tied to clearly quantified savings gaps.

## What we need
To support a deal, we need a **fully quantified restructuring bridge**, plant by plant and function by function:
1. current recurring cost gap,
2. savings from labour measures,
3. savings from management overhead and procurement,
4. savings from capacity reduction options,
5. one-time costs of each scenario,
6. disruption and strike risk,
7. and the residual gap after all negotiated measures.

Without that, nobody can credibly claim closures are necessary — or unnecessary.

## How we read the current debate
The works council and union are right that labour should not be the only shock absorber. But their alternative only becomes persuasive if it is **numerically credible**.

A package based on:
- working-time flexibility,
- temporary wage moderation,
- bonus restraint,
- redeployment,
- retraining,
- voluntary exits,
- and management/owner contributions,

could be attractive **if it materially lowers recurring costs**. But if it does not close the gap, then the company remains uncompetitive, and that is not a sustainable outcome for shareholders or employees.

## What would move us
A credible financial bridge showing that the preferred alternative:
- closes the savings gap,
- is durable,
- is executable,
- and avoids a prolonged conflict.

If the non-closure route can do that, we would prefer it.  
If it cannot, then hard structural options remain on the table.

## Bottom line
We are **open to a deep non-closure package**, or a **hybrid package with measured structural reduction**, but only if it is proven to restore competitive margins and durable cost discipline.

We are **not** prepared to support:
- a cosmetic compromise,
- a blanket no-closure rule,
- or a settlement that preserves the current cost base and postpones the reckoning.

If no one can produce the full bridge, then the risk falls back on the business — and we will respond accordingly through capital-market discipline.
