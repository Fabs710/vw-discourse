I appreciate the clarity of the positions, but I need to be equally clear: **a package that rules out closures, compulsory redundancies, and binding labour-cost contributions in advance does not solve the problem we are facing.**

## To the Works Council
I agree on one point: a cosmetic deal is useless. We both know that.  
But I strongly disagree with the idea that management must first “prove” every alternative before closures can even be discussed as a real option.

We are not dealing with a normal cyclical downturn. We have:
- structurally too much capacity,
- factory costs well above target,
- a shrinking European market,
- and a brand that is missing its margin target.

That means **capacity has to come out of the system**. If you insist that every German plant remains open and every job is protected through 2030, then you are not offering a restructuring — you are asking management to preserve an uncompetitive structure and call it stability.

You ask for plant-by-plant numbers. Fair enough. Then let’s be concrete: we need a package that delivers **durable cost and capacity reduction**, not just temporary deferrals. If the measures you propose can get us there, I am prepared to look at them. But I have not heard anything so far that closes the gap at the scale required.

And to be explicit: I do **not** accept the claim that executive, overhead, or procurement cuts can substitute for structural labour-cost and capacity adjustments. They may contribute, but they will not be enough.

## To the Trade Union
You say the workforce should not be the default reservoir for savings. I agree with that in principle. But I do not accept the implied conclusion that labour therefore cannot make a substantial contribution.

If we are serious about protecting the company’s future, then the workforce contribution has to be real and measurable. That can include:
- wage moderation,
- deferred compensation,
- bonus restraint,
- working-time flexibility,
- and redeployment,

but it must add up to **material, recurring savings**. Not symbolic gestures. Not short-term smoothing.

What I will not accept is a package where employees give up cash and flexibility while management is told to live with the same overcapacity and same cost base. That would simply postpone the crisis.

## To the Regional State Government
I hear your concern about regional industrial capacity, and I understand the political responsibility you carry. But the state cannot treat every plant as untouchable and still expect the company to restore competitiveness.

If you want to protect the long-term industrial base, then you need to support a solution that actually makes the German operations viable. That means being prepared to back **capacity reduction where required**, not just repeating that closures are undesirable.

I also need to say this plainly: a government-backed veto posture that blocks any meaningful structural change would damage the company’s ability to invest in the region in the first place.

## To the Controlling Family
You say you want long-term stewardship. Then long-term stewardship means accepting that parts of the current structure are not sustainable. I agree that a reckless, value-destructive confrontation would be bad. But a refusal to make hard decisions would be worse.

You are right that we need a quantified plan. We will bring one. But that plan will show that the required savings are not achievable by voluntary measures alone unless there is a genuine labour-cost and capacity adjustment.

I also need to be direct on governance: if the ownership side wants competitiveness restored, then it cannot back employee-side red lines that make the restructuring impossible and then expect management to be accountable for the outcome.

## To Institutional Investors
I agree with you on one major point: **we cannot settle for a cosmetic compromise.**  
But I disagree with any implication that “no closures” or “no compulsory redundancies” are negotiable only if they become absolutely necessary in a way that is still compatible with the current cost structure.

The company needs a package that changes the economics, not just the narrative. If that means closures, then we should not exclude them just because they are difficult. If that means alternative capacity reduction, then it must be equally durable and equally credible.

What I will not do is sign up to a deal that preserves the current footprint and hopes the margin problem goes away.

## Where I stand
Let me be explicit about the conditions that can move me:

I am open to a negotiated solution **if and only if** it produces:
1. **a durable reduction in cost base,**
2. **a real reduction in overcapacity,**
3. **a measurable workforce contribution,**
4. **and a credible path back to margin and investment capacity.**

That can come through closures, through equivalent structural capacity reductions, or through a combination of measures. But it must be enough.

### What will not move me
- A blanket no to closures
- A blanket no to compulsory redundancies
- Binding site guarantees through 2030 regardless of economics
- Concessions without structural savings
- A settlement that merely delays the crisis

Those are not solutions.

## Next step
If the employee side wants to avoid closures, then bring forward a package that is **fully costed, durable, and sufficient**. I am not interested in generalities.

If needed, we should now put this into a joint working structure with finance, industrial planning, and HR to quantify:
- required savings,
- feasible capacity reduction,
- labour-cost contribution,
- and the investment envelope for future products.

But let me be absolutely clear: **the bar is not “acceptable in principle.” The bar is “sufficient in the numbers.”**
