I will respond directly.

## To the Works Council
I agree with you on the essentials: **no compulsory redundancies, no German plant closures, and no bargaining under threat**. Your point is correct that a “review trigger” can be abused if it is merely a delayed closure decision.

Where I would sharpen the position is this: we should not allow management to smuggle in closures through vague language like “capacity adaptation” unless it is tied to **hard, jointly verified alternatives first**. If there is any review architecture at all, then it must be narrowly framed, transparent, and bound to negotiated alternatives — not a blank cheque for later layoffs.

That said, I will not retreat from the union’s flexibility in principle: if there is a package that truly protects jobs and sites, then labour can discuss temporary sacrifice. But only under **binding guarantees**, not conditional promises.

## To Group & Brand Management
You still have not answered the most important issue: **why should closures and compulsory redundancies remain on the table before you have exhausted real alternatives?**

You say the numbers must work. I agree. But you have not shown that the numbers *cannot* work through a package that includes:
- working-time flexibility,
- temporary wage moderation or deferred compensation,
- bonus restraint,
- internal redeployment,
- qualification/retraining,
- management overhead cuts,
- procurement savings,
- and a fair owner-side contribution.

If you want credibility, then present the model:
- the exact savings gap,
- what each measure contributes,
- what is recurring versus temporary,
- and what residual gap remains **after** all non-destructive measures.

Until then, “closures may be necessary” is not an argument; it is a threat.

And let me be equally clear: I do not accept the implication that labour must prove its package is sufficient while management retains the right to reserve the harshest options. If the burden of proof is asymmetrical, then the bargaining is already distorted.

## To the Institutional Investors
You are right about one thing: a cosmetic compromise is worthless. So I am not offering one.

But I reject your framing that closures are just another neutral efficiency tool. They are not. They carry execution risk, industrial conflict, reputational damage, and the loss of skills and capacity that cannot be recovered quickly. Those are real costs, not externalities.

You ask what improvement would be enough. My answer is simple:
- a deal that delivers **durable savings**,  
- preserves **jobs and collective bargaining standards**,  
- and avoids forced redundancies and plant closures,  
is the kind of deal I can support.

If you are saying only closures can deliver sufficient margins, then I challenge that premise. Show the full cost model first. Do not start from the most destructive option and call it realism.

## To the Controlling Family Holding
If you mean long-term stewardship, then act like an owner, not a prosecutor.

Long-term stewardship means:
- preserving industrial capability,
- avoiding a strike spiral,
- and not breaking the trust that underpins production, quality, and transformation.

You speak of hard choices. Fair enough. But the hard choice cannot always be pushed onto the workforce. If employees are asked for moderation, then owners must also contribute materially:
- reinvestment,
- binding product commitments,
- protection of sites,
- and a rejection of one-sided social dumping.

I will not accept “we support a structural reset” as code for making labour carry all the risk.

## To the Regional State Government
Your position is important, but it must become more than a political statement. If you do not want closures or forced redundancies, then say so in governance in a way that has teeth.

I agree with you that social conflict and industrial destruction are value-destructive. That is precisely why a negotiated alternative is the correct route.

But I also need to be clear: if your support is limited to calling for “balanced” solutions while leaving closures on the table as a fallback, then that does not protect the workforce. It merely delays the blow.

## On the moderator’s bridge proposals

### Proposal 1: Two-stage package with trigger-based escalation
I do **not** accept this as currently framed. A trigger-based escalation model can too easily become a promise today and a closure mechanism tomorrow. The workforce cannot be asked to give up wages and flexibility now in exchange for a future threat.

If there is any staged model, the first stage must come with **binding site and employment guarantees**, not open-ended review rights for management.

### Proposal 2: “No forced redundancies first” with quantified capacity review
This is closer, but I still have concerns. “First” is not enough if “later” means forced redundancies or closures become legitimate. I can only engage with such a model if it clearly states:
- no compulsory redundancies,
- no plant closures,
- and any capacity adjustment must be achieved through negotiated, reversible, socially responsible means.

If that cannot be written plainly, then the proposal is not acceptable.

### Proposal 3: Binding guarantees tied to measurable owner/management contributions
This is the only direction that approaches seriousness. But the guarantees must be real:
1. no German plant closures,
2. no compulsory redundancies,
3. preserved co-determination,
4. binding investment and product commitments,
5. and measurable management/owner contributions.

If those conditions are met, then labour-side contributions can be discussed:
- temporary wage moderation,
- deferred compensation,
- bonus restraint,
- working-time flexibility,
- redeployment,
- qualification,
- and voluntary retirement pathways.

## What I am prepared to discuss
I remain open to a balanced package with:
- temporary wage moderation or deferred components,
- bonus restraint,
- working-time flexibility,
- internal redeployment,
- qualification and retraining,
- voluntary retirement,
- management overhead reductions,
- procurement savings,
- and plant-level productivity improvements.

But I repeat the boundary:
- **no compulsory redundancies,**
- **no German plant closures,**
- **no open-ended erosion of the collective wage framework,**
- **no bargaining under the threat of future layoffs.**

## What would actually move me
Not rhetoric. Not pressure. Not “structural necessity” as a slogan.

What could move the union is a package that shows:
- management and owners are contributing materially,
- labour is not carrying all the pain,
- jobs and sites are protected,
- and the savings are durable enough to secure the future.

That is the condition for a fair settlement.

## Bottom line
We are prepared to negotiate a serious restructuring. We are **not** prepared to negotiate away the workforce’s security, the collective wage framework, or the industrial base of the German operations.

If management wants agreement, it must bring forward a **fully quantified, balanced, and non-destructive** package. If it cannot, then it should not expect the union to bless closures and forced layoffs as “realism.”
