I will be blunt.

## My position
As the regional state government and anchor shareholder, I do **not** support a restructuring built around **German plant closures** or **compulsory redundancies** as the central answer. That is not a credible basis for a socially legitimate, long-term solution in this company, and it would damage the regional economy we are responsible for.

I do support a **serious, quantified restructuring** that restores competitiveness. But it must do so **without gutting regional employment** and **without sidelining our co-determination and veto role**.

---

## Where I agree

### With management
You are right about one thing: this cannot be cosmetic. The company does need durable savings, not symbolism. I also accept the expert’s point that temporary measures alone may be insufficient if the structural gap is very large.

### With the union and works council
You are right that the workforce should not be the only shock absorber. A fair package must include real contributions from:
- management,
- ownership,
- overhead,
- procurement,
- and product investment.

### With the controlling family and investors
You are right that a value-destructive stalemate would be bad. A strike spiral, weak compromise, or prolonged uncertainty would hurt the company and the region.

---

## Where I disagree

### 1) You are still treating closures as a normal fallback
Management keeps saying closures should stay on the table “until the numbers are proven.” I do **not** accept that framing as neutral.

Plant closures are not just another efficiency lever. They create:
- permanent job losses,
- regional damage,
- supplier disruption,
- loss of know-how,
- and lasting political and industrial mistrust.

That is why I will not treat them as the default bargaining lever.

### 2) “No absolute red lines” is not a serious answer
I hear management and investors saying the employee and state side should not make exclusions absolute before the numbers are tested. But that logic is one-sided.

The burden is not on us to prove that closures are impossible. The burden is on management to prove that a **non-destructive package cannot work**. Until then, closures are not justified — they are a threat.

### 3) Review-trigger schemes are not automatically acceptable
I share the works council’s concern here. A “review” that simply postpones the closure decision is not a compromise; it is a delayed trigger for the same outcome.

If there is any review framework, it must be:
- narrowly defined,
- jointly governed,
- transparent,
- and explicitly designed to test alternatives first, not to create a trapdoor later.

---

## On the expert input
The specialists have helped clarify the real question:
- If the recurring gap is moderate, a non-closure package may be sufficient.
- If the gap is very large, deeper structural measures may be needed.

That is fair. But the missing piece is still the same: **the side-by-side comparison**.

I want a **plant-by-plant, function-by-function bridge** showing:
- recurring savings from each non-destructive measure,
- temporary versus durable effects,
- one-time costs of any closure scenario,
- disruption and strike risk,
- lost output,
- and the residual gap after all alternatives.

Without that, no one can honestly claim closures are necessary.

## My answer to management
You say you need “durable structural reduction in cost and capacity.” I accept that. But durability does not automatically mean closing plants.

What I will support is:
- working-time flexibility,
- temporary wage moderation or deferred elements,
- bonus restraint,
- internal redeployment,
- qualification and retraining,
- voluntary retirement,
- management overhead cuts,
- procurement and efficiency savings,
- product allocation adjustments,
- and capacity reduction **without plant closure** where possible.

What I will **not** support is a plan that starts from closures and forced redundancies before exhausting those alternatives.

If your financial model cannot close the gap without closures, then show the full model. Do not ask for political endorsement of an unproven worst-case scenario.

---

## My answer to the works council and union
I support your refusal to accept closures and forced redundancies as first-choice instruments. That position is legitimate and important.

At the same time, I need you to be serious about numbers, not only principle. If the company can be stabilized through:
- flexibility,
- wage moderation,
- redeployment,
- and productivity gains,

then that package needs to be substantial enough to matter. I will back a negotiated solution if it is financially credible and protects jobs and sites.

But I will not support a settlement that merely preserves the status quo and hopes the market improves.

---

## My answer to the controlling family
If you are serious about stewardship, then prove it in the form of:
- reinvestment,
- product commitments,
- restraint from pushing destructive closure logic,
- and a willingness to support a negotiated industrial solution.

Ownership is not just about demanding margin discipline. It is also about preserving the industrial base and the social legitimacy that makes this company durable.

---

## My answer to investors
You are right that a cosmetic deal is worthless. But you should also recognize that closures are not “clean” in the real world. They carry one-time costs, operational risk, and long-term damage that often get understated.

If a non-closure or hybrid package can close most or all of the gap, that is the better value-preserving route. If it cannot, then the company must show that honestly — not use that possibility to pressure the workforce into accepting irreversible harm up front.

---

## What I can support
I can support a package that:
1. restores competitiveness,
2. preserves German sites and jobs as far as possible,
3. avoids compulsory redundancies,
4. keeps co-determination intact,
5. includes measurable management and owner contributions,
6. and secures investment in future products.

I can also support a **time-limited, jointly audited restructuring bridge** — but only if it is genuinely a bridge to a non-destructive solution, not a hidden route to closures.

---

## What I cannot support
I will not support:
- German plant closures as the central mechanism,
- compulsory redundancies,
- a review mechanism that is really a delayed closure mandate,
- or a settlement that sacrifices the regional industrial base to solve a margin problem.

---

## Bottom line
The state will back a **serious negotiated restructuring**, but not one that destroys the company’s regional anchor role.

If management believes closures are unavoidable, then it must prove that with a transparent, comparable model that includes all costs and risks — not just the savings side.

Until then, our position stands:
**protect the state’s industrial base, preserve co-determination, and pursue competitiveness without regional hollowing-out.**
