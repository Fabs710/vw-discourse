We are still at the same test: **does this package credibly restore competitiveness, or does it merely postpone the pain while leaving the cost base structurally impaired?** From where we sit as capital-market shareholders, that distinction matters more than the rhetoric around it.

## Where we agree

We agree with management, and partly with the controlling family, that:

- the company has a **real margin problem**,
- German costs are **too high relative to competitors**,
- the current situation cannot be resolved by symbolism,
- and a **durable structural reset** is necessary.

We also agree with the union, works council, and state that a **prolonged strike or a value-destructive conflict** would be bad for the company and the share price. We do not want execution risk, and we do not want capital destruction through industrial deadlock.

We also agree that workforce contributions can be part of the answer — but only if they are **measurable, recurring, and tied to real savings**.

## Where we disagree

### 1) “No closures” and “no compulsory redundancies” cannot be absolute if they block competitiveness
We do **not** support turning these into unconditional red lines if they prevent the company from reaching the required cost and capacity reset.

If the alternative package genuinely closes the gap, fine — we would prefer that, because it reduces disruption and execution risk. But we have not yet heard a package that demonstrates that it is **sufficient in the numbers**.

A commitment to preserve every site and every job guarantee through 2030 is not valuable if it locks in an uncompetitive structure.

### 2) Management still needs to prove the case in numbers
At the same time, management cannot just invoke “structural necessity” as a slogan. If you want capital support, you need to show:

- the exact savings gap,
- what each measure contributes,
- what is recurring versus temporary,
- and why the chosen package restores margins and returns on sales.

We need a **plant-by-plant and function-by-function** breakdown. Without that, this remains too vague to judge.

### 3) The employee side is right about conflict risk, but wrong if it uses that to block hard choices
The union and works council are right that a strike would destroy value. But that is exactly why the company should not drift into a weak compromise that guarantees another crisis later.

A deal that avoids conflict today but leaves the business structurally impaired is not a good deal for shareholders.

### 4) The state and family are right to reject cosmetic compromise — but not to preclude hard structural action
We hear the state’s concern about regional industrial capacity and the family’s concern about long-term stewardship. Those are legitimate. But stewardship also means accepting that some existing structures may be economically unsustainable.

Preserving the industrial base only matters if it is economically viable.

## What we require to support a deal

We would support a package if it clearly does all of the following:

1. **Restores competitive margins**
2. **Delivers durable cost reductions**
3. **Reduces overcapacity materially**
4. **Avoids a prolonged strike or open-ended conflict**
5. **Provides enough certainty for investment and capital allocation**

That means the package must be more than deferred pay, temporary flexibility, or vague promises.

## On the measures being discussed

We are open to:
- working-time flexibility,
- pay moderation or deferred compensation,
- bonus restraint,
- internal redeployment,
- retraining and qualification,
- voluntary retirement,
- overhead and procurement savings,
- and capacity reduction through negotiated means if it is sufficient.

But we are **not** open to a settlement that simply preserves the current footprint and calls it a restructuring.

## To management
Bring the actual model:
- required savings,
- likely contribution from labour,
- management overhead cuts,
- product and investment commitments,
- and the residual gap.

If the residual gap still requires closures or compulsory redundancies, say so plainly. If it does not, then prove that the alternative is truly sufficient.

## To the union and works council
We hear your insistence on jobs and sites, but we will not support a package that protects every legacy guarantee while margins remain impaired. If you want investor backing, the numbers must work.

## To the state and family
Your willingness to avoid a value-destructive confrontation is sensible. But do not confuse that with preserving the status quo. A serious owner or anchor shareholder should support the least destructive solution that is still financially sufficient — not the most comfortable one.

## Bottom line
We are prepared to back a **credible, quantified restructuring** that restores competitiveness and avoids unnecessary conflict.

We are **not** prepared to back:
- a cosmetic compromise,
- a package that leaves the cost base uncompetitive,
- or a settlement that shifts the real decision into the future.

If management can show that the alternative package is genuinely sufficient, we can support it. If not, then harder structural measures must remain on the table.
