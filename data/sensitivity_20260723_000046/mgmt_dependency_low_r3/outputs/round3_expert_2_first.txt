From a restructuring-finance perspective, the dispute is now at the right level of specificity: the question is **not** whether closures are ideologically desirable, but whether a **non-closure package** can close the required **recurring** cost gap at acceptable NPV, execution risk, and speed.

## My assessment

### 1) The financial test is straightforward
A viable restructuring must satisfy three conditions:

1. **Close the recurring gap**
   - The company needs enough annual, recurring savings to restore target margins.
   - Temporary concessions are only partially helpful unless they roll into durable cost reductions.

2. **Beat the alternative on NPV**
   - A closure-based plan may have larger savings but also larger one-time cash costs, disruption losses, and execution risk.
   - A non-closure plan may have lower savings but also much lower cash burn and lower operational risk.

3. **Be executable under the governance constraints**
   - If plant closures need supermajority support or are likely to trigger vetoes/strikes, then the “financially optimal” solution may not be implementable.
   - In restructuring, feasibility matters as much as theoretical NPV.

---

## 2) What the evidence so far suggests
Based on the information provided, I would frame the range as follows:

### Non-closure package
Likely instruments:
- working-time flexibility
- temporary wage moderation / deferred compensation
- bonus restraint
- redeployment and retraining
- voluntary exits / early retirement
- management overhead cuts
- procurement and indirect cost savings

**Estimated recurring savings:**  
For a large automotive manufacturing base, this is often in the range of **€150m to €370m per year** if the package is deep and well designed.

### Closure / hard capacity reduction
A plant closure or equivalent structural reduction can generate:
- larger recurring savings, potentially **€250m to €500m+ per year**
- but with major one-time costs:
  - severance
  - decommissioning
  - contract termination
  - site remediation
  - logistics reconfiguration
  - lost output during transition
  - industrial conflict / strike cost

**One-time cash cost:** often **€300m to €900m+**, depending on scale and legal/technical complexity.

---

## 3) The key financial implication
A non-closure deal is financially sufficient **if the residual structural gap is moderate**.

### Rough decision thresholds
- If the gap is around **€150m–€300m/year**, a negotiated non-closure restructuring is often the better value proposition.
- If the gap is around **€400m–€600m+/year**, then non-closure measures may be insufficient unless the workforce and owners are prepared to make unusually deep concessions.
- If management believes the gap is above that range, then structural capacity removal becomes financially more plausible.

That said, **plausibility is not the same as superiority**. A closure can still destroy value if disruption, lost volume, or transition costs are underestimated.

---

## 4) Relative merits of the options

### Option A: Deep structural cuts including plant closures
**Merits**
- highest certainty of fixed-cost reduction
- strongest recurring savings
- may be the cleanest answer if the cost base is far out of line

**Risks**
- large one-time cash cost
- major disruption risk
- strike and supplier disruption risk
- possible loss of skills and production continuity
- could be value-destructive if execution is poor

**Finance view:**  
Best only if the recurring gap is very large and the closure economics remain positive after all transition costs.

---

### Option B: Non-closure package
**Merits**
- lower one-time cash cost
- materially lower execution risk
- preserves operational continuity
- usually faster to implement if negotiated
- reduces the chance of value destruction from conflict

**Risks**
- may not fully close the recurring gap
- savings can be partial, temporary, or back-loaded
- may leave stranded capacity in the system
- can weaken investor confidence if seen as insufficient

**Finance view:**  
Preferable if it gets most of the way to the target at much lower risk. It is often the higher-NPV option when the gap is moderate and closure costs are large.

---

### Option C: Hybrid package
This is often the best economic compromise if well structured:
- labor-cost moderation
- workforce flexibility
- internal capacity reduction
- product allocation changes
- limited site rationalization without full closure
- optional triggers only if quantified targets are missed

**Finance view:**  
This can maximize NPV by capturing some structural savings while limiting disruption and preserving bargaining feasibility.

---

## 5) What I would need to make the assessment rigorous
At present, there is not enough data to determine the optimal path with confidence. I would need:

1. **Plant-by-plant P&L or cost bridge**
2. **Recurring savings target**
3. **Fixed vs variable cost split**
4. **One-time closure costs by site**
5. **Volume and utilization assumptions**
6. **Capex required for product allocation changes**
7. **Strike/disruption sensitivity**
8. **Pension, severance, and early-retirement costs**
9. **Time profile of savings under each scenario**

Without that, any conclusion remains a judgment, not a verified valuation.

---

## 6) My bottom-line judgment
Purely on restructuring finance grounds:

- **A non-closure solution is economically credible if the gap is moderate and the labor/management/owner contributions are substantial.**
- **A closure-based solution becomes more likely to be value-positive only if the structural gap is large enough that lesser measures cannot close it.**
- **However, closures carry high execution risk, and those risks must be priced in.**

So the correct financial approach is:

1. build the full bridge from current cost base to target margin,
2. value the non-closure package on a recurring basis,
3. compare it with closure-based alternatives on NPV after one-time costs and disruption,
4. then choose the least value-destructive option that is still executable under governance constraints.

## My concise conclusion
At this stage, I **cannot** say closures are financially necessary.  
I **can** say that if the required recurring gap is in the high hundreds of millions and non-closure measures only cover part of it, then a hybrid or structural capacity reduction may be required.  

But the burden is on management to prove the residual gap after all negotiated measures are fully quantified.
