I’ll be direct.

## My position
I support a **serious structural reset**. I do **not** support a cosmetic compromise that leaves the company uncompetitive.  
But I also do **not** support a rushed closure program or a confrontation that destroys value through strikes, reputational damage, and a broken labour relationship.

The question is not whether change is needed. It is whether the package is **financially sufficient** and **stability-preserving**.

---

## To management
I agree with you on the core diagnosis: German operations are under structural pressure, and the company needs real recurring savings. The expert’s input reinforces that point.

Where I disagree is on the implication that closures and compulsory redundancies must stay available in the background as a default answer. That may be true in a narrow financial model, but ownership does not stop at the spreadsheet. If you trigger a prolonged conflict or destroy long-term industrial capability, you can erase the very value you claim to protect.

So I need more than “the numbers might require it.” I need:
- the savings gap by plant and function,
- the split between recurring and temporary savings,
- the one-time cost and disruption risk of closures,
- and the best non-destructive package you can actually quantify.

If the gap is truly too large, say so plainly. But do not assume the most destructive instrument is the most rational one.

---

## To the works council
I agree with you that a forced-layoff/closure-first approach would be reckless. I also agree that worker trust is an asset, not a nuisance.

Where I disagree is your insistence that plant closures and compulsory redundancies are off the table as absolute red lines **before** the economics are fully tested. That is too rigid.

You are right that “review triggers” can be abused. But you are wrong if you treat any staged or conditional framework as automatically a trap. A staged approach can be legitimate if it is:
- transparent,
- jointly quantified,
- bounded in time,
- and tied to real financial outcomes.

What I cannot accept is a structure that guarantees all legacy positions through 2030 regardless of whether the business case can support them. That would not be stewardship; it would be denial.

---

## To the union
I understand and respect your position that labour should not be the sole reservoir for savings. I agree.

But if the union wants to avoid closures, then the workforce contribution must be **material**, not symbolic. That means a package with real, recurring savings:
- wage moderation,
- bonus restraint,
- working-time flexibility,
- deferred compensation,
- redeployment,
- qualification,
- voluntary exits,
- and productivity gains.

If that package closes the gap, I am interested. If it does not, then insisting on “no closures” is not a solution, it is simply refusing the economics.

I also disagree with the notion that management and ownership can be made to bear all the hard consequences while labour remains fully insulated. That is not balanced, and it will not restore competitiveness.

---

## To the regional state government
I respect the state’s role and the political responsibility it carries. I also agree that a value-destructive conflict would be harmful.

But I do **not** accept a governance posture that effectively makes German capacity untouchable. The state’s interest is not preserved by freezing an uncompetitive structure; it is preserved by ensuring the company remains viable enough to invest, employ, and produce in the region at all.

So I support a solution that protects the industrial base, but not one that pretends every site can remain unchanged regardless of economics.

---

## To institutional investors
I agree with you on one key point: cosmetic compromise is worthless. The numbers must work.

Where I disagree is any suggestion that closures are automatically the cleanest path to value creation. They can be value-destructive if the one-time cost, disruption, and labour conflict outweigh the recurring savings.

Your concern should not just be margin recovery in year one. It should be durable value creation over several years. That means you need to compare:
- the NPV of a non-destructive restructuring,
- against the NPV of closures plus the disruption and execution risk.

That is the right test. Not slogans about discipline.

---

## On the expert input
The modeler’s point is helpful: a non-closure package can be financially feasible if the gap is moderate, but may fall short if the structural gap is large.

That tells me two things:
1. Management must quantify the gap honestly, not rhetorically.
2. The employee side must not assume that any package without closures is automatically sufficient.

So yes, the economics matter. But they must be assessed alongside execution risk and long-term value, not in isolation.

---

## What I would support
I am prepared to support a package if it delivers all of the following:
1. **durable cost reduction**
2. **real capacity adjustment**
3. **material workforce contribution**
4. **management and owner contribution**
5. **credible investment and product commitments**
6. **industrial peace sufficient to protect value**

If that can be achieved without closures, that is preferable. If it cannot, then the company must prove that harder measures are truly necessary and still value-positive after all costs are counted.

---

## What I will not support
I will not support:
- a deal that is merely symbolic,
- a settlement that preserves structural uncompetitiveness,
- a prolonged strike spiral,
- or a closure plan executed in a way that destroys trust and long-term value.
