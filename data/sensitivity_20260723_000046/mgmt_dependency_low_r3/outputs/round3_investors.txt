We agree with the specialists on the central point: **this is a numbers problem, not a slogan problem**. But the numbers have still not been shown to justify either a guaranteed no-closure posture or a vague “structure reset” that simply shifts the burden onto labour.

## Our position
From the capital-markets side, we need a package that **credibly restores margins, reduces overcapacity, and lowers the cost base in a durable way**. If it does not do that, it is not a restructuring; it is postponement.

We are open to a non-closure solution **only if it is financially sufficient**. We are not open to a cosmetic compromise that protects every legacy arrangement while margins remain structurally impaired.

---

## Where we agree

### With management
You are right that the current footprint and cost base are not competitive enough. You are also right that temporary concessions alone are not enough if they do not produce recurring savings.

The specialist input confirms that point: if the gap is large, the package must be deep and durable. We agree that the company needs more than a symbolic deal.

### With the family holding
We agree that long-term value matters and that a rushed, value-destructive confrontation would be bad. We also agree that closures are not automatically the best answer if they destroy more value than they create.

But stewardship is not just about avoiding political pain. It is about choosing the least destructive option that still restores competitiveness.

### With the state
We agree that a strike spiral or social breakdown would be value-destructive. We also agree that the industrial base matters.

But “industrial base” only has value if it is economically sustainable. The state should help secure a viable solution, not freeze an uncompetitive structure.

### With the union and works council
We agree that the workforce should not be the only shock absorber, and that management/ownership must contribute too.

We also agree that a cosmetic compromise would be worthless. A deal that merely defers the problem is not acceptable.

---

## Where we disagree

### 1) Absolute red lines against closures and compulsory redundancies
We do **not** support turning “no plant closures” and “no compulsory redundancies” into unconditional vetoes **before** the numbers are tested.

If the non-closure package closes the gap, fine — we would prefer that because it reduces disruption and execution risk. But if it does not, then excluding structural options in advance is not responsible.

The specialist’s assessment is important here: a non-closure package may plausibly deliver around **€150m–€370m per year** in recurring savings if it is deep. That may be enough in some cases. But if the gap is in the **€400m–€600m+** range, then the company may need stronger structural measures. We are not prepared to pretend otherwise.

### 2) “Binding guarantees through 2030” as a precondition
We understand why the employee side wants guarantees. But guarantees that are not supported by a financially sufficient plan are just a transfer of risk back to shareholders.

We will not support commitments that lock in an uncompetitive structure and leave the share price, margins, and investment capacity impaired.

### 3) A review-trigger model that becomes a delayed closure promise
We are wary of trigger-based arrangements if they are simply a way to avoid hard decisions now and reopen them later under worse conditions.

A staged approach can be acceptable only if it is transparent, time-bound, and tied to clearly quantified savings gaps. Otherwise it just adds uncertainty.

---

## What we require
To support a deal, we need a **fully quantified restructuring bridge** showing:

1. the current recurring cost gap,
2. the savings from labour measures,
3. the savings from management overhead, procurement, and support functions,
4. the savings from capacity reduction options,
5. the one-time costs of each scenario,
6. and the residual gap after negotiated measures.

We need this **plant by plant and function by function**. Without that, nobody can credibly claim that closures are or are not necessary.

---

## Our reading of the specialist input
The expert effectively says two things:

- A non-closure package can work if the gap is moderate and the concessions are real.
- A closure-based package may be financially justified if the gap is large enough, but only after one-time costs and disruption risk are properly counted.

That is exactly the framework we want. What we do **not** accept is jumping straight to absolutes: neither “everything stays open” nor “closures are inevitable” has been proven.

---

## To management
Bring us the actual model. Not rhetoric, not strategic language — the model.

We want to see:
- the recurring savings target,
- the expected contribution from each lever,
- what is temporary versus durable,
- and what residual gap remains.

If the non-closure alternative is sufficient, then that should be the preferred outcome because it preserves operational continuity and reduces strike risk. If it is not sufficient, then say so plainly and explain why.

---

## To the works council and union
You are right that labour should not carry all the pain. But your position only becomes persuasive if your alternative package is **numerically credible**.

A package based on:
- working-time flexibility,
- temporary wage moderation,
- bonus restraint,
- redeployment,
- retraining,
- voluntary exits,
- and management/owner contributions,

is potentially attractive **if it materially lowers recurring costs**. But if it does not close the gap, then the company will still be uncompetitive, and that is not a sustainable outcome for shareholders or employees.

So we need more than principle. We need a quantified bridge.

---

## To the state and family
Your preference for a negotiated, less destructive outcome is sensible. We support that — **if it is financially sufficient**.

But you should not confuse “less painful” with “good enough.” A settlement that avoids immediate conflict but leaves margins structurally impaired will eventually destroy more value.

---

## Bottom line
We are prepared to support:
- a deep non-closure package,
- or a hybrid package with measured structural capacity reduction,

**but only if it is proven to restore competitive margins and durable cost discipline.**

We are **not** prepared to support:
- a cosmetic compromise,
- a blanket no-closure rule,
- or a settlement that preserves the current cost base and postpones the reckoning.

### What would move us
A credible financial bridge showing that the preferred alternative:
- closes the savings gap,
- is durable,
- is executable,
- and avoids a prolonged conflict.

If that cannot be shown, then hard structural options remain on the table.
