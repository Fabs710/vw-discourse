Management keeps speaking about “structural necessity,” but it still has not answered the central question: **why should the workforce pay with plants and jobs for strategic misjudgments made elsewhere?**

I will respond point by point.

## Where I agree
Yes, the company needs a durable solution. Yes, we need transparency, investment, and a credible product plan. And yes, cost pressure is real.

But none of that justifies crossing our red lines.

## Where I disagree
### 1) Plant closures are not an acceptable “tool”
I reject the repeated suggestion that closures are somehow just one option among many. In Germany, plant closures would destroy trust, skills, and regional industrial capacity that cannot simply be rebuilt later. Under co-determination, this is not something management can expect to impose by framing it as “business necessity.”

If management wants a solution, it must first prove that it has exhausted every alternative **without** closures.

### 2) Compulsory redundancies are off the table
I hear the argument that no option should be excluded in advance. I reject that logic. A company of this size does not get to solve competitiveness problems by forcing people out after decades of work and loyalty. That is not restructuring; that is social dumping.

We will not accept forced layoffs as a bargaining instrument.

### 3) “Temporary” concessions without binding guarantees are not enough
The union has already said workforce contributions may be discussed, and I agree — but only if they are matched by **binding site and employment guarantees through 2030**. Without that, workforce concessions simply become a one-way transfer of risk from management to employees.

If labour gives up pay, flexibility, or bonuses, then management must lock in:
- every German site remaining open,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- binding investment commitments,
- and a clear product allocation for the German plants.

## On management’s “savings gap”
Management keeps asking for “comparable durable savings.” Fine. Then bring the numbers in a serious way, plant by plant and function by function:
- What savings come from working-time measures?
- What savings come from redeployment and qualification?
- What savings come from management reductions, procurement, overheads, and executive compensation?
- What savings come from postponing or rephasing non-essential investment elsewhere in the group?

Do not start with closures and layoffs and then call that the benchmark.

If the company truly wants a negotiated solution, it must show that it is prepared to take costs out of **all** parts of the structure, not just labour.

## On the shareholder side
### To the family holding
You say you want long-term stewardship. Then act like it. Long-term stewardship means preserving the industrial base, not threatening sites to force a quick margin fix.

### To the investors
You speak of risk-adjusted value creation. Fine. Then understand the real risk: a strike, a damaged labour relationship, and the destruction of trust in the company’s German backbone. That is not value creation. That is self-inflicted damage.

### To the state
I appreciate your support for site security. But support must be converted into pressure inside the governance structure. We need a clear message that the state will not back any plan involving German plant closures or forced redundancies.

## What I am prepared to discuss
We are not refusing responsibility. We are prepared to discuss:
- working-time flexibility,
- temporary wage moderation,
- bonus restraint,
- internal redeployment,
- qualification and retraining,
- voluntary retirement models,
- and other negotiated, reversible measures.

But these are only acceptable if they are part of a **balanced package** with:
1. **no plant closures in Germany,**
2. **no compulsory redundancies,**
3. **binding job and site guarantees through 2030,**
4. **preserved co-determination rights,**
5. **and concrete investment and product commitments.**

## Bottom line
If management comes back with a plan that still relies on closures or forced layoffs, then we are not negotiating the details of a mistake — we are rejecting the premise.

We are ready to negotiate a serious restructuring.  
We are **not** ready to negotiate away the workforce’s security, the plants, or the co-determination framework.
