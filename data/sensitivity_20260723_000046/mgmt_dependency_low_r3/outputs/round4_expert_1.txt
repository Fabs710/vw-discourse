## Financial logic of the three scenarios

### 1) Non-closure package
Typical elements:
- working-time flexibility
- temporary wage moderation / deferred pay
- bonus restraint
- redeployment
- voluntary exits
- management overhead cuts
- procurement savings
- productivity improvements

**Financial profile**
- Lower one-time cost
- Lower disruption risk
- Savings are partly reversible
- Recurring savings can be meaningful, but usually capped

**Best when**
- the residual gap is moderate
- labor cost is a large share of the problem
- there is some excess labor capacity but not severe excess plant capacity

### 2) Hybrid capacity reduction
Typical elements:
- shift reductions
- line consolidation
- product reallocation
- site specialization
- selective voluntary exits
- targeted asset rationalization

**Financial profile**
- Moderate one-time cost
- Some disruption risk
- More durable savings than purely temporary measures
- Better fit if capacity is structurally too high but closures are politically/operationally costly

**Best when**
- the gap is too large for labor moderation alone
- closures are too destructive
- there is redundant capacity that can be removed without shutting a plant outright

### 3) Closure scenario
Typical elements:
- plant shutdown or major site exit
- workforce reduction
- asset impairment
- relocation of output
- supply-chain reconfiguration

**Financial profile**
- Highest one-time cash cost
- Highest labor conflict / strike risk
- Highest disruption risk
- Potentially the largest recurring savings if a site is structurally unneeded

**Best when**
- overcapacity is severe
- fixed-cost burden is high
- alternative measures cannot close the gap
- the expected recurring savings materially exceed one-time and disruption costs on an NPV basis

## How I would compare them in the model
For each scenario:

### NPV formula
**NPV = PV(recurring savings) – one-time restructuring costs – PV(disruption losses) – PV(strike losses) – PV(extra capex)**

Where:
- recurring savings are annual and durable
- one-time costs happen in year 0–2
- disruption losses depend on ramp-down speed and labor conflict
- strike losses can be material if a closure path escalates conflict

## Practical interpretation for this case
Given the governance constraints described:
- employee side has an effective veto
- state and family have blocking power
- investors want margin repair
- management wants durable cost reduction

That means the **economic hurdle for closures is not just financial, but also executional**.

So the closure option must beat non-closure not only on base savings, but also after:
- strike probability
- delayed implementation
- plant ramp-down inefficiency
- reputational cost
- supplier disruption
- lower productivity during transition

In many German industrial restructurings, those transition costs are large enough that closure needs to produce **substantially higher recurring savings** to justify itself.
