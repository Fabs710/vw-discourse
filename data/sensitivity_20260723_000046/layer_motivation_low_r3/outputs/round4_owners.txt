We are at the point where the discussion has to become economically exact, not emotionally repetitive.

## My position
The controlling family holding supports a **durable restructuring that restores competitiveness and margins**. We do **not** support a cosmetic compromise, and we do **not** support a solution that leaves structural overcapacity untouched.

At the same time, I am **not signing up to plant closures as a reflex**. Closure is a means, not a goal. If a closure-free package can truly close the gap with comparable durability and execution certainty, that is preferable. But that has to be **proven**, not asserted.

## Where I agree
I agree with nearly everyone on the following:
- The current situation is unsustainable.
- A shallow compromise would destroy value.
- Burden-sharing must extend beyond labour.
- Management, owners, and shareholders all have to contribute.
- A quantified, binding package is necessary.

The expert has also clarified the right framework: the real test is recurring savings, one-time costs, timing, utilization, and execution risk.

## Where I disagree
### To the works council and union
You keep repeating “no closures” and “no compulsory redundancies” as absolutes. I understand the political logic, but I do **not** accept those as self-justifying boundaries.

If you want those outcomes excluded, then bring a package that is **economically equivalent**:
- durable capacity reduction,
- real labor-cost relief,
- meaningful headcount effect,
- improved utilization,
- and credible implementation.

So far, I hear red lines and principles. I do **not** yet see a package that closes the structural gap. Voluntary exits, redeployment, working-time flexibility, and productivity gains are useful, but on their own they are often too weak for a problem of this scale.

### To management
I also do not accept management turning “competitiveness” into a blank cheque for the most disruptive option.

If closures are being used as leverage, then they must be justified with plant-by-plant numbers. I want to see:
- the exact savings gap,
- recurring savings by measure,
- one-time costs,
- and the residual gap under each scenario.

Until that is presented, closures remain a possibility, not a demonstrated necessity.

### To the state government
I respect your role and your concerns. But the state cannot simply veto reality. A protected but structurally uncompetitive footprint is not an industrial strategy; it is deferred decline.

Your position is only sustainable if a closure-free package really can restore margins. If it cannot, then insisting on no closures irrespective of the numbers would be value-destructive.

### To investors
You are right that a cosmetic deal is unacceptable. But value certainty does not come from forcing the quickest cut at any price. It comes from a package that actually works and can be implemented without prolonged paralysis.

## What I need now
I support the expert’s request for a **plant-by-plant quantified restructuring model**. That should compare:
1. closure-free restructuring,
2. partial consolidation,
3. plant closure scenarios,

and show:
- recurring savings,
- one-time costs,
- utilization effects,
- timing,
- and the residual margin gap.

## My bottom line
- **No support for a cosmetic deal**
- **No support for preserving structural overcapacity**
- **No support for a weak solution that simply postpones the problem**
- **No automatic endorsement of closures without proof**

If the closure-free route is genuinely equivalent, I can support it.  
If it is not, then deeper structural measures remain on the table, because protecting every structure as it is would be a value-destructive mistake.
