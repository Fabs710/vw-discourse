I appreciate the positions laid out. Let me be equally clear from the ownership side: we support a **credible competitiveness program**, but we will not back a course that simply postpones the hard decisions or preserves an uncompetitive structure at the expense of long-term enterprise value.

### Where I agree
I agree with management on the core diagnosis: **the cost base is too high and structural adjustment is necessary**. If the company’s margins continue to erode, that will eventually hurt every stakeholder — especially the workforce and the long-term owners. I also agree that any solution must be **durable**, not a temporary patch.

I also agree with the works council, the union, and the state government on one important point: **a stable settlement is preferable to open-ended confrontation**. Prolonged conflict would destroy value, damage the brand, and weaken the investment case.

### Where I disagree
I do **not** accept the idea that the only serious restructuring path is immediate plant closure or a blunt program of layoffs.

That is too narrow and too destructive as a first-line approach. It risks:
- irreparable reputational damage,
- industrial escalation,
- political backlash,
- and a weakened company before the restructuring benefits even materialize.

Likewise, I do **not** accept a position that treats “no closures, no compulsory redundancies” as an absolute outcome regardless of the economics. If the company cannot restore competitiveness through alternatives, then avoiding all hard structural change is not stewardship; it is delay.

### What would move us
From the family holding’s perspective, the test is straightforward:

1. **Does the plan materially restore margins and competitiveness?**
2. **Does it preserve the long-term value of the core business?**
3. **Does it avoid destabilizing conflict and protect labour peace?**
4. **Does it maintain governance control and strategic flexibility?**

If the answer to those questions is yes, we can support a tough package. If the answer is no, then we will not endorse a settlement that locks in decline.

### On the shape of a viable solution
We are open to an agreement that achieves savings through a broader combination of measures:
- capacity reduction,
- working-time adjustments,
- voluntary departures,
- redeployment,
- productivity improvements,
- and a negotiated employee contribution.

But that contribution must be **real and durable**, not symbolic. We also expect management to show discipline and ensure that the burden is not pushed solely onto labour. At the same time, we are not prepared to support an arrangement that permanently protects every legacy structure if that leaves the company structurally uncompetitive.

### On closures and layoffs
I am not endorsing plant closures as a political gesture, and I understand why they are highly sensitive under the company’s governance structure. But I also will not declare them taboo if no alternative closes the cost gap. The decisive issue is not a slogan about “no closures”; it is whether the final package restores the company’s economics without causing worse damage elsewhere.

### Bottom line
The ownership position is this:

- **Yes** to a negotiated restructuring.
- **Yes** to alternatives to closures if they are economically equivalent.
- **Yes** to workforce contribution, provided it is matched by management discipline and strategic commitments.
- **No** to a cosmetic compromise that leaves structural overcapacity in place.
- **No** to a prolonged confrontation that erodes value and weakens the company’s future.

So I am not here to cheer on confrontation, and I am not here to protect the status quo. I am here to protect the company’s long-term value. Bring forward a package that genuinely restores competitiveness, and we will consider it seriously. Bring forward a plan that avoids hard decisions but does not solve the problem, and we will not support it.
