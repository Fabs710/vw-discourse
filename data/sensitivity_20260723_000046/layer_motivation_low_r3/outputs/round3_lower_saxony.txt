I will be very clear: the state shareholder is **not** prepared to endorse a restructuring that uses German plant closures or compulsory redundancies as the starting point.

## Where I agree
I agree with everyone on the basic diagnosis:
- the current situation is not sustainable,
- a cosmetic answer would be irresponsible,
- and the company needs a durable restructuring that restores competitiveness.

I also agree that:
- burden-sharing must be broader than labour alone,
- management and owners must contribute,
- and open conflict would damage the company, the workforce, and the region.

So yes: there is a real problem, and yes: it needs a real solution.

## Where I disagree
### 1) Closures are not the default answer
To management, family holding, and investors: you keep repeating that closures may be needed if alternatives are not “economically equivalent.” That is a high bar, and rightly so. But you have not yet shown that this bar has been met.

A plant closure is not a neutral efficiency measure. It is an irreversible decision with major regional, political, and industrial consequences. For a company that is a central economic anchor in Germany, closures cannot be the first move or the fallback slogan.

If you want the state shareholder to consider such a step at all, you need to prove:
- the exact savings gap,
- what closure-free measures were tested,
- why those measures fail,
- and why the damage from closure is unavoidable.

So far, that proof has not been made.

### 2) Forced redundancies are not an acceptable solution
I also reject the idea that compulsory layoffs are simply “part of the package.” They may be convenient from a balance-sheet perspective, but they are not acceptable as the default answer in a company with this governance structure and this regional role.

If headcount has to be reduced, then the priority must be:
- voluntary exits,
- retirement bridges,
- redeployment,
- working-time adjustments,
- qualification and internal transfers.

That is the line the state can support. Forced redundancies as a first-line instrument cross the line.

### 3) The co-determination and statutory role of the state are not bargaining chips
Let me be explicit: the state’s veto and co-determination role are not things to be “worked around” in the name of urgency. They exist because this company is not just a financial asset. It is an industrial institution with public significance.

Any solution that sidelines that role is not a legitimate compromise from our perspective.

## What we can support
We are prepared to support a **closure-free restructuring package**, but only if it is concrete and binding. That means:
- capacity reduction without site closures,
- working-time flexibility,
- voluntary departures only,
- internal redeployment,
- productivity improvements,
- management cost discipline,
- and a real investment commitment for future products and technologies in Germany.

If those measures genuinely close the gap, then the state shareholder will engage constructively. If they do not, then the burden is on management and owners to explain why they are still reaching for the most destructive options.

## To the works council and union
Your red lines are understandable, and on the core issue I stand with you:
- no plant closures as a starting point,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- no dismantling of co-determination,
- no unilateral breaking of the social compact.

At the same time, I want to be pragmatic: a temporary employee contribution can be discussed if it is matched by binding guarantees and genuine investment. That is a negotiable burden-sharing model. But it must not become a one-sided sacrifice.

## To the family holding and investors
You are right about one thing: a weak compromise that leaves the company structurally uncompetitive would fail. I do not support that either.

But I do not accept the implication that the only serious answer is to threaten closures until labour accepts deep cuts. That may be economically neat on paper; it is politically and socially reckless, and potentially self-defeating in this governance setting.

If you want support from the state shareholder, bring forward an alternative that:
1. preserves German sites,
2. protects core employment,
3. restores competitiveness,
4. and is credible over time.

That is the standard. Not “whatever closes fastest.”

## What I need from management now
Since the discussion has moved into package design, I want a concrete proposal with:
- quantified savings,
- quantified employee contribution,
- quantified management contribution,
- quantified owner/shareholder contribution,
- and a clear answer on how capacity is reduced **without** closures.

If management cannot show that, then the case for closures is still unproven.

## Bottom line
The state shareholder will support a serious restructuring **only** if it:
- protects the company as a regional economic anchor,
- avoids plant closures where possible,
- rules out compulsory redundancies,
- preserves co-determination,
- and secures future investment in Germany.

Anything that guts regional employment or weakens the state’s role is unacceptable.

If management wants a different answer, it must bring a credible, quantified, closure-free package first. Until then, I will use the full weight of the state’s position to block a destructive solution.
