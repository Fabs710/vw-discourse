I will be equally direct.

## What I agree with
I agree on three points that matter:

1. **The status quo is not sustainable.**  
   Everyone in this room knows that. German factory costs are too high, the market is shrinking, and the brand is missing its return-on-sales target.

2. **A cosmetic compromise would be worse than no deal.**  
   If this ends in slogans, temporary relief, and preserved overcapacity, we will be back in the same crisis within a short time.

3. **The burden cannot fall on labour alone.**  
   Management, owners, and investors must contribute too. On that point, the workforce is right.

## Where I disagree
But I do **not** accept the attempt to turn “no closures” and “no compulsory redundancies” into untouchable absolutes without an economically equivalent alternative.

### 1) Plant closures are not a preferred option — they are a possible consequence of economics
I am not saying closure is the goal. I am saying the company cannot keep structurally uncompetitive capacity simply because it is politically difficult to change.

The question is not whether closures are unpleasant. Of course they are.  
The question is whether the cost base and capacity are fit for a company that has to fund future products and technology to 2030.

If the answer is no, then someone has to absorb that reality.

### 2) Voluntary departures alone are unlikely to be enough
I hear the union and works council repeating voluntary exits, retirement bridges, redeployment, and working-time flexibility. Those can be part of the package. But they do not automatically solve a deep structural gap.

If the underlying problem is excess fixed cost and underutilization, then a solution that depends mainly on attrition and temporary measures is not credible unless it delivers truly material, durable savings.

### 3) “No closures” is not a restructuring plan
It is a red line.  
I understand why it is politically attractive. But it is not a cost solution.

If the employee side wants closures off the table, then it must show — with numbers — how the same level of recurring savings and capacity reduction will be achieved without them.

So far, I have heard demands, but not an equivalent business case.

## To the works council
You say management is just asserting necessity. That is not fair.

We have a real cost crisis. We have a real competitiveness problem. We have a real savings requirement. That is not rhetoric. That is the financial and industrial reality.

You are asking us to preserve every German site, reject compulsory redundancies, preserve job guarantees, and still close the cost gap. That may be what you want, but it is not yet what you have proved.

If you want a closure-free package, then put forward:
- quantified recurring savings,
- durable capacity reduction,
- a meaningful employee contribution,
- measurable productivity gains,
- and a credible timing profile.

A list of protections is not a plan.

## To the union
I respect that you are prepared to negotiate an employee contribution. But it must be substantial enough to matter.

Temporary and symbolic concessions will not restore margins.  
If we are to protect future investment and jobs in the long run, the economics have to work in the short and medium term too.

And I will repeat the point clearly: if voluntary measures do not deliver enough scale, then you cannot simply declare that the company must live with the gap.

## To the regional state government
You are right that closures are serious and irreversible. That is exactly why they are not being discussed lightly.

But the state cannot just say “no closures” and stop there. If the state wants to use its blocking power, it also has to face the industrial arithmetic:
- what is the alternative cost reduction?
- where does the capacity go?
- how is the structural gap closed?
- who pays for the missing savings?

A protected but uncompetitive footprint is not a sustainable industrial policy.

## To the controlling family holding
You rightly emphasize long-term value. I agree with that. But long-term value is not preserved by defending every existing structure.

If the company cannot restore margins, there will be less to invest, less to distribute, and less to protect. So yes, long-term value matters — and that is precisely why structural change matters.

I do agree with you on one thing: the numbers must be transparent. We need a quantified comparison, not ideology.

## To investors
You are right to insist on deal certainty and durable savings. That is exactly what management needs too.

But certainty does not come from freezing the current footprint and hoping productivity improves by goodwill alone. It comes from a package that actually reduces the structural cost base.

If the closure-free option truly does that, fine. If it does not, then it is not value-preserving — it is delay.

## The central point
The expert’s assessment was useful because it made the real test clear:

- closure-free restructuring can work **if the gap is moderate** and the measures are broad enough;
- voluntary departures alone are rarely enough;
- if the gap is large and fixed costs remain too high, footprint rationalization becomes economically more likely.

That is the reality I am working from.

So here is my position:

### I am prepared to consider a closure-free package only if it includes all of the following:
- substantial and durable capacity reduction
- meaningful labour-cost contribution
- working-time and productivity changes with real effect
- workforce reduction at sufficient scale
- binding investment and product commitments
- a clear path back to target margins

### I am not prepared to accept:
- a cosmetic compromise
- a package that preserves structurally uncompetitive costs
- a deal that leaves the company unable to fund 2030 investment
- an open-ended veto on any footprint rationalization, regardless of numbers

## What I need next
The discussion has now reached the point where assertion is not enough.

If the labour side and the state want closures excluded, then bring forward a **fully quantified equivalent package**:
- annual recurring savings,
- one-time costs,
- implementation timing,
- utilization impact,
- and residual cost gap.

If that package is credible, we can assess it.  
If it is not, then the resistance to closures is not solving the problem — it is postponing it.

## Bottom line
I am not softening my position for the sake of peace in the room.

- **No cosmetic deal**
- **No solution that leaves structural overcapacity untouched**
- **No package that fails to deliver durable labour-cost relief**
- **No future investment plan without the savings to fund it**

If the employee side wants to avoid closures, then it must deliver an economically equivalent restructuring.  
If it cannot, then deeper structural measures remain necessary, because preserving every site as it is would be a value-destructive decision.
