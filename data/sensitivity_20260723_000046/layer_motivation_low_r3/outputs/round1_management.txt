Let me respond very clearly.

I hear the message from the works council and the union: you want to protect jobs, sites, and the collective framework. I understand that. But I need to be equally clear about the company’s position: **we do not have the luxury of preserving every structure exactly as it is** while our cost base remains uncompetitive and our market share comes under pressure.

### Where I agree
I agree on one point: **a cosmetic restructuring is not enough**. If we are going to ask employees to contribute, then the package must be **durable, structural, and credible**. We are not interested in a short-term patch that leaves the same cost problem in place by the next planning cycle.

I also agree that any solution must give the company a viable future: investment in new products, technology, and competitiveness matters. Without that, there will be no secure industrial base to protect.

### Where I disagree
I do **not** accept the red lines as stated.

- **No plant closures in Germany**: that is not a credible starting point when we have structural overcapacity.
- **No compulsory redundancies**: if we are serious about restoring competitiveness, then workforce reduction has to be part of the equation somewhere.
- **No meaningful change to labour costs**: also not acceptable. Our German cost base is too high relative to target and relative to competitors.

We cannot solve a structural cost problem with slogans about preservation. The market is shrinking, competition is intensifying, and we have already lost time. If we do nothing decisive, then the result will be worse later: deeper erosion of margins, weaker investment capacity, and more jobs at risk across the whole system.

### What management needs
Management is prepared to support a negotiated solution, but it must deliver **real capacity and cost reduction**, not just delay the problem. That means:

1. **A substantial reduction in overcapacity**
2. **A durable labour-cost contribution**
3. **A credible reduction in headcount**
4. **A plan that materially improves the brand’s return-on-sales trajectory**

If there are alternatives to closure that achieve the same structural effect, we will examine them. But “alternative” cannot mean “no change” or “temporary concessions with no lasting impact.”

### On burden-sharing
I am open to a balanced package, but balance does not mean symmetry in rhetoric. It means each side contributes to a result that restores competitiveness.

- The workforce will have to contribute.
- Management will have to implement tough restructuring.
- Owners and governance bodies will need to support a strategic reset rather than block it.
- But employee contributions must be **real**, not symbolic.

### Bottom line
We are prepared to negotiate, but not around the question of whether adjustment happens. Adjustment **must** happen. The real question is whether we achieve it through a structurally credible package that secures the company’s future, or whether we drift into confrontation and force the issue later under worse conditions.

So my position is:

- **Yes** to a negotiated settlement.
- **Yes** to alternatives to closures only if they produce comparable, durable savings.
- **Yes** to employee contributions if they are meaningful and structural.
- **No** to a solution that protects every plant and every existing guarantee while leaving the cost base unchanged.
- **No** to a settlement that is merely temporary or cosmetic.

If you want to avoid closures, then bring forward a package that really closes the cost gap. Otherwise, management will continue to pursue deeper structural measures.
