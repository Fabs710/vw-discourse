From an industrial restructuring and plant-utilization perspective, the central question is:

**Can a closure-free package plausibly close the company’s savings gap, and under what conditions?**

## Short answer
**Possibly, but only if the required recurring savings are moderate relative to the current cost gap and if management is prepared to use a full set of measures beyond simple voluntary departures.**  
If the gap is large, persistent, and concentrated in fixed overhead and underutilized capacity, then **closure-free measures alone are often insufficient** unless accompanied by substantial labor-cost reductions, major workforce resizing through attrition, and very strong productivity gains.

The key issue is not whether a closure-free plan is desirable. It is whether it is **arithmetically sufficient** after accounting for:
- recurring savings,
- one-time restructuring costs,
- timing,
- utilization effects,
- and execution risk.

## What can be said with confidence
### 1) Closure-free restructuring can generate real savings
A package built around:
- shift reductions,
- working-time flexibility,
- temporary wage concessions,
- voluntary exits,
- retirement bridges,
- redeployment,
- overhead cuts,
- and procurement/productivity measures

can materially reduce cost. It is not merely symbolic.

### 2) But voluntary exits alone are usually not enough
At a large industrial employer, voluntary departures typically reduce headcount gradually and unevenly. They are useful for smoothing the adjustment, but by themselves they rarely produce:
- fast enough fixed-cost reduction,
- deep enough capacity removal,
- or enough certainty about annual recurring savings.

### 3) Underutilized plants are expensive even before closure
If a plant remains open but runs materially below efficient load, it continues to carry:
- building and maintenance costs,
- utilities,
- plant services,
- engineering support,
- supervision,
- logistics overhead,
- and sometimes under-absorbed depreciation.

In such cases, a “no closure” solution can still leave a significant structural cost burden unless production is consolidated or volume is restored.

## The accounting logic
The comparison is between:

### Option A: closure-free restructuring
Savings come from:
- labor-cost measures,
- reduced shifts,
- lower support overhead,
- productivity gains,
- tighter logistics,
- and some workforce reduction through non-forced means.

Costs include:
- severance/bridging costs,
- retraining,
- transition inefficiencies,
- and possible temporary under-absorption of fixed costs.

### Option B: closure / footprint rationalization
Savings come from:
- removal of plant-level fixed costs,
- better utilization at the remaining sites,
- fewer duplicate support functions,
- and possibly stronger scale efficiency.

Costs include:
- shutdown costs,
- severance,
- remediation,
- asset impairments or disposal losses,
- logistics reconfiguration,
- and transition disruption.

A closure-free package is viable if its **net present value of recurring savings**, after transition cost, is close enough to the closure alternative and sufficiently durable.

## What determines feasibility
A no-closure package is more likely to work if most of the following are true:

- the savings gap is **not dominated by unavoidable plant-level fixed costs**
- there is meaningful scope for **shift reduction and utilization improvement** without destroying efficiency
- the company can secure **multi-year wage and working-time concessions**
- workforce reduction can be achieved substantially through **natural attrition and voluntary exits**
- management overhead and indirect labor can be cut materially
- product allocation or model mix can be improved enough to raise utilization
- the company can tolerate a **slower savings ramp** in exchange for less disruption

A no-closure package is less likely to work if:
- one or more plants are structurally underloaded
- the required savings are very large relative to current operating profit
- the business needs immediate and irreversible fixed-cost removal
- the labor contribution is temporary but the cost gap is permanent
- logistics or product constraints prevent meaningful consolidation without closure

## My technical assessment of the works council’s claim
The works council is correct on one narrow point: **closures are not automatically necessary just because management asserts competitiveness pressure.**  
They must be justified by numbers.

However, from an accounting perspective, the reverse is also true: **a closure-free plan is not automatically sufficient just because it preserves sites.** It must show:
- enough recurring savings,
- enough utilization improvement,
- and enough durability.

So the correct technical test is:

> Can the closure-free package reduce the company’s recurring cost base by at least the amount of the structural gap, on a durable basis, after transition costs?

If yes, closures are not economically necessary.  
If no, then the gap remains and management’s case strengthens.

## Practical conclusion
Based on the general facts you provided, my provisional view is:

- **A closure-free restructuring is possible in principle.**
- **Its success depends on the magnitude of the gap and the extent of underutilized capacity.**
- **If the company needs deep, permanent fixed-cost removal, closure-free measures will likely need to be unusually broad and may still fall short.**
- **If the gap is moderate and much of the problem is labor cost, overhead, and suboptimal utilization rather than irreducible plant fixed cost, then a no-closure deal could be economically credible.**
