I’ll be equally direct.

We are not moving off the essential point: **the workforce will not pay for restructuring alone, and we will not accept plant closures or compulsory redundancies as the default answer.** That position stands.

## Where I agree
I agree with the works council on the core diagnosis:
- the situation is serious,
- a cosmetic fix is useless,
- and burden-sharing has to involve management, owners, and shareholders too.

I also agree with management, the family holding, and investors on one important point: **a durable solution is necessary now**. Delay would make the eventual outcome worse for everyone.

## Where I disagree
### 1) “Structural overcapacity” does not automatically justify closures
Management keeps repeating that closures are “needed.” That is still an assertion, not proof. If you want to claim closure is unavoidable, then show:
- the exact savings gap,
- the exact effect of closure-free alternatives,
- and why those alternatives fail.

Until then, closure is a preferred option, not a demonstrated necessity.

### 2) Forced redundancies are unacceptable
I will not endorse a restructuring where workers are pushed out while management talks about shared responsibility. If headcount must fall, then it must be through:
- voluntary exits,
- retirement bridges,
- redeployment,
- qualification and transfer measures,
- working-time adjustments,
- and negotiated flexibility.

Compulsory redundancies destroy trust and make industrial conflict likely. That is not a technical detail; it is a major bargaining boundary.

### 3) The collective framework is not disposable
We are not accepting a restructuring that uses the crisis to break the collective wage agreement or gut long-standing protections. If employees contribute, that contribution must be part of a **negotiated package**, not a one-sided degradation of standards.

## What I am prepared to discuss
We are prepared to negotiate a serious package built around:
- capacity reduction **without closures**,
- working-time flexibility,
- productivity gains,
- voluntary departures,
- redeployment,
- and a temporary employee contribution.

But that package must be matched by real commitments from the other side:
- **binding site guarantees**
- **no compulsory redundancies**
- **preservation of the collective wage framework**
- **management cost discipline**
- **owner contribution**
- **investment commitments for German sites and future products**

That is the only basis on which we can even discuss an employee contribution.

## To management
You say the company needs decisive structural change. I do not dispute that. What I dispute is the idea that the only serious way to do that is by closing plants or forcing people out.

If you want our cooperation, put the numbers on the table:
- what savings are required,
- what savings each measure delivers,
- what management and owner contributions look like,
- and what closure-free restructuring can realistically achieve.

If closure-free measures cannot close the gap, then explain why with evidence, not rhetoric. But you have not earned the right to demand sacrifices from the workforce before exhausting those alternatives.

## To the works council
Your insistence on plant and job protection is legitimate, and the union stands with you on that. We should stay unified on the red lines:
- no closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- no unilateral dismantling of the collective framework.

Where I would nuance the position is this: we should remain open to **temporary, negotiated employee contributions** if they buy real security and a credible future for the sites. That is not surrender. That is bargaining.

## To the regional state government
Your blocking position matters, and it should be used to prevent destructive solutions. But it is not enough to say “no closures” in principle; the state also has to pressure management and ownership to present a genuine alternative. If they want public and political support, they need to show a viable industrial plan, not just a cost-cutting narrative.

## To the family holding and investors
I hear your concern about margins and competitiveness. That concern is legitimate. But long-term value is not protected by provoking a social conflict that damages production, trust, and the company’s industrial base.

If you want a deal that restores value, then support a package that:
- shares the burden,
- avoids forced layoffs,
- avoids plant closures,
- and delivers durable savings through negotiated measures.

A “strong” solution that destroys labour peace is not strong; it is risky.

## Our concrete line
We are prepared to negotiate:
- temporary employee contribution,
- working-time adjustments,
- capacity reduction without site closures,
- voluntary exits,
- redeployment,
- productivity improvements,
- and investment commitments.

We are **not** prepared to accept:
- German plant closures,
- compulsory redundancies,
- one-sided wage cuts,
- or the dismantling of the collective wage framework.

That is not posturing. It is the boundary of a workable settlement.

## Final point
If management and owners want peace, they need to stop testing whether the workforce will blink first. We have already shown flexibility by putting alternative measures on the table. Now we need a **specific counterpackage** with:
1. quantified savings,
2. quantified employee contribution,
3. binding site guarantees,
4. no forced redundancies,
5. and investment commitments.

Bring that, and we can talk seriously. Bring closures and forced layoffs as the starting point, and you will get resistance, not consent.
