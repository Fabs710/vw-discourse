We align with the expert and with management on the **need for durable, quantified restructuring**. We do **not** align with any position that leaves the cost base structurally uncompetitive or treats “no closures” as a value-preserving outcome in itself.

## Where we agree
- The status quo is unacceptable.
- A cosmetic compromise would destroy value.
- Burden-sharing must extend beyond labour.
- The package must be quantified, binding, and durable.
- Open-ended conflict is value-destructive.

## Where we disagree

### 1) The labour side’s “no closures, no compulsory redundancies” line is not sufficient
We understand the social and political reasons for it, but from a shareholder perspective it is **not a viable starting point unless it is proven economically equivalent**.

Right now, what we see is:
- strong red lines,
- but no plant-by-plant proof that closure-free measures can close the gap,
- and no assurance that voluntary exits, working-time changes, and productivity gains will be large enough or fast enough.

If the closure-free route truly delivers:
- durable recurring savings,
- materially lower fixed costs,
- and a credible path back to target margins,

then we can support it. But if it simply preserves all sites while leaving margins weak, we will not support that. That is just delay.

### 2) Voluntary measures are helpful, but unlikely to be enough on their own
We support:
- voluntary departures,
- retirement bridges,
- redeployment,
- qualification,
- working-time flexibility,
- productivity improvements.

But the expert is right: these measures often underdeliver when the problem is structural overcapacity and high fixed costs. If the savings gap is large, then a voluntary-only approach risks producing too little, too late, and too expensively.

### 3) Guarantees cannot become a shield against necessary restructuring
We support binding safeguards if they buy real savings and implementation certainty.  
We do **not** support guarantees that freeze the footprint and preserve an uncompetitive base.

A package that protects every site, every guarantee, and every current arrangement while asking shareholders to accept weaker margins is not a solution.

## Where management must be clearer
We agree with management that “competitiveness” is not rhetoric; it is the core issue. But management has not yet done enough to prove that closures are necessary.

If closures are being kept on the table, then management must show:
- the exact savings gap,
- what closure-free measures deliver,
- how much of the gap remains,
- and why that remaining gap cannot be closed without structural footprint change.

Without that, closure is still a preferred option, not a demonstrated necessity.

## What would move us
We would support the most value-preserving package, but it must meet these tests:

1. **Recurring savings, not just one-offs**
2. **Durable capacity reduction**
3. **Meaningful labour contribution**
4. **Management and owner contribution**
5. **Implementation certainty and timing discipline**
6. **A credible return to competitive margins**

If those can be achieved without closures, fine. If not, then insisting on no closures is not protecting value; it is impairing it.

## Our position on the current bridge ideas
- **Time-limited closure-free trial**: acceptable only if there is a hard, quantified savings and utilization test, and not an open-ended escape from difficult decisions.
- **Conditional closure trigger**: acceptable in principle as a governance safeguard, but only if the criteria are explicit, objective, and economically rigorous.
- **Shared-burden package with guardrails**: best option if it really reduces cost and does not merely shift pain around.

## Bottom line
We are prepared to back:
- a closure-free deal, **if and only if** it is economically equivalent;
- or a deeper restructuring, if that is the only way to restore margins and competitiveness.

We are **not** prepared to back:
- a cosmetic compromise,
- an outcome that leaves structural overcapacity untouched,
- or a deal that keeps the company uncompetitive in order to preserve every current site.
