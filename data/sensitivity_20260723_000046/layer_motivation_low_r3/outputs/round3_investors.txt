## Where we disagree

### 1) “No closures” as an absolute
We do **not** accept “no German plant closures” as a non-negotiable starting point if the company cannot otherwise close the cost gap.

The expert was clear: closure-free measures may work only if the savings gap is moderate and the fixed-cost problem can be genuinely reduced. If that is not the case, then insisting on no closures risks **preserving structurally uncompetitive margins**. That is not acceptable from a shareholder-value perspective.

We are open to avoiding closures **if** the economics are truly equivalent. But “equivalent” means:
- durable recurring savings,
- real capacity removal,
- and a credible path back to target margins.

Not symbolic reductions. Not postponed pain.

### 2) “No compulsory redundancies” as an absolute
We understand why this is politically and socially demanded, but from a capital-market perspective it is only acceptable if the alternative delivers **sufficient scale and certainty**.

Voluntary exits, retirement bridges, redeployment, and working-time changes are all preferable. But the expert’s assessment matches our concern: **voluntary departures alone are rarely enough**. If the company needs a material headcount reset, then ruling out compulsory measures in all cases may leave the restructuring underpowered.

### 3) Guarantees cannot become a shield against economics
We do support binding safeguards, but not if they freeze the cost structure in place.

A package that preserves all protections while leaving overcapacity untouched would amount to **deferring the problem onto shareholders** through weaker margins. We will not support that.

## What would move us
We could support a closure-free settlement **only if** management can show, with numbers, that it is economically equivalent to deeper structural rationalization. Specifically, we need:

1. **Quantified annual recurring savings**
   - not one-offs, not aspiration.

2. **Clear capacity reduction**
   - enough to lower fixed cost absorption materially.

3. **Meaningful labour contribution**
   - durable, not temporary.

4. **Management and owner contribution**
   - overhead discipline, lower extraction, realistic capital allocation.

5. **A credible implementation path**
   - limited strike risk, limited slippage, and deliverable timing.

6. **A believable path to competitive margins**
   - the return-on-sales target must become achievable, not merely discussed.

If that can be shown without closures, that is preferable. If it cannot, then avoiding closures ceases to be a virtue and becomes a costly delay.
