I will answer directly, because we are now at the point where repetition must be replaced by substance.

## Where I agree
I agree with the expert and with the state shareholder on one crucial point: **this must be quantified**.  
If management claims that closures are “necessary,” then it must show:
- the exact savings gap,
- the recurring annual savings from each measure,
- the one-time restructuring costs,
- the timing,
- and the execution risk.

That is not optional. We cannot bargain over slogans.

I also agree that:
- the status quo is not sustainable,
- a cosmetic package is worthless,
- and the company needs a durable restructuring.

And yes, burden-sharing must involve **management, owners, and shareholders** — not just labour.

## Where I disagree
### 1) “No closures” is not a symbolic red line
It is a real boundary.  
Management keeps speaking as if plant closures are the natural, rational next step. But that has not been proven.

The expert did **not** say closures are automatically needed. The expert said:
- closure-free measures can work if the gap is moderate,
- voluntary exits alone are usually not enough,
- and the answer depends on the numbers.

That means the burden is still on management to prove necessity, not on labour to prove innocence.

So I reject the idea that “we can’t rule out closures” is somehow a neutral position. In practice it is a threat that shifts the burden onto the workforce.

### 2) Compulsory redundancies remain unacceptable
I am not moving on this.

If the company needs headcount reduction, then we are prepared to discuss:
- voluntary exits,
- retirement bridges,
- redeployment,
- qualification,
- internal transfers,
- working-time measures,
- and productivity improvements.

But **forced layoffs are not an acceptable restructuring instrument** in this company as the default or fallback answer.

Why? Because they break trust, destroy commitment, and turn a negotiated restructuring into coercion. That is not “efficiency”; it is social damage.

### 3) A “conditional closure trigger” is not a bridge from our side
The mediator’s second bridge idea is still a closure threat, only delayed.  
From the workforce perspective, that is not security. It is a gun on the table with a countdown.

We will not sign up to a structure that says:
- accept concessions now,
- and if management later decides the numbers are still not good enough, it can close plants.

That is not a durable settlement. That is pressure management.

## What we can discuss
We are prepared to discuss a **serious closure-free restructuring package**, but only if it is genuinely binding and balanced.

### The package we are willing to negotiate includes:
- capacity reduction **without site closures**
- shift reductions and working-time flexibility
- voluntary departures only
- redeployment across plants
- qualification and internal mobility
- productivity improvements
- management overhead reduction
- owner contribution
- binding investment commitments
- clear future product allocation for German sites

### And the guarantees must be explicit:
1. **Every German plant remains open**
2. **No compulsory redundancies**
3. **No unilateral dismantling of the collective framework**
4. **No weakening of co-determination**
5. **Site and employment security through 2030**
6. **Transparent burden-sharing beyond labour**

That is the minimum basis on which an employee contribution becomes thinkable.

## To management

**What exact savings gap remains after a genuine closure-free package, and why can that gap not be closed by serious internal restructuring plus management and owner contribution?**

If your answer is “because closures are cleaner,” then that is not necessity. That is preference.

Bring us the numbers:
- what each plant contributes,
- how much capacity can be reduced without closing sites,
- what overhead and management cuts are possible,
- what owner contribution is on the table,
- what investment is guaranteed in return for labour’s contribution.

Until then, your closure case is not proven.

## To the controlling family holding
You speak about long-term value. Then act like long-term owners.

Long-term value is **not** protected by tearing up the social compact, provoking warning strikes, and destabilizing the industrial base that creates value in the first place.  
If you want value preservation, support a closure-free solution and put real capital discipline and investment behind it.

## To the investors
You say you want deal certainty. So do we. But certainty does not come from threatening closures until the workforce gives in.

A deal that triggers industrial conflict, damages trust, and weakens production stability is not certainty. It is risk dressed up as strategy.

If you want a value-preserving agreement, then back:
- closure-free capacity reduction,
- employee contribution,
- management contribution,
- owner contribution,
- and binding investment.

## To the regional state government
Your line is correct, but it must stay firm.  
Do not let “economic urgency” be used to normalize destructive precedents.

Use the blocking position. Demand proof. Demand the numbers. Demand a closure-free plan before any discussion of irreversible measures.

## What would move us
We are open to a temporary employee contribution if — and only if — it is matched by:
- binding site security,
- no forced redundancies,
- no closures,
- real management restraint,
- owner contribution,
- and credible investment in German sites.

That is a fair, shared-sacrifice package.  
Workers are willing to contribute. They are **not** willing to be the only side paying for the crisis.

Our position remains clear:
