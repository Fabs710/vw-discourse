I’ll be equally direct.

We are all agreed on one thing: **the status quo is not sustainable**.  
Where we sharply disagree is on the method.

## To the Works Council
I hear your red lines, but I do **not** accept them as a restructuring plan.

You keep saying:
- no plant closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- binding site guarantees through 2030,
- only voluntary instruments.

That is **not** an answer to the cost problem. It is a demand that management somehow restore competitiveness while being denied the very tools that create the needed structural savings.

You say closures are merely “a choice, not a law of economics.” Correct — but the same is true of your proposed no-closure model. It is a choice too. The question is whether it is **sufficient**. Right now, you have not shown that it is.

I also reject your claim that keeping closures as a fallback is automatically coercion. In a real negotiation, management must retain credible options. If every hard consequence is removed in advance, there is no incentive to deliver a package that actually closes the cost gap.

What I can accept is:
- voluntary exits,
- redeployment,
- working-time flexibility,
- qualification,
- productivity gains,
- targeted investment commitments,

but only if they produce **measurable, durable, structural savings**. So far, you have not provided that quantified equivalent.

## To the Union
You are right that the burden cannot fall only on employees. I agree with that.

But you are not entitled to turn “no forced redundancies” into “no structural adjustment.” That would leave the company stuck with an uncompetitive footprint and an overstretched cost base. That is not protection; that is deferral.

You say social peace is not a substitute for competitiveness. I agree.  
But the reverse is also true: **industrial conflict is not a substitute for a viable restructuring concept**.

If you want the union to help stabilize this company, then bring a package that matches the economic need. Not a list of acceptable instruments — a package with real magnitude.

## To the Regional State Government
I appreciate that you reject a crude closure-first approach. But I cannot accept your position if it amounts to a political veto on the very measures that may be needed.

You ask for quantified comparisons. Fair enough. Then I ask you to stop pre-judging the conclusion.

If non-closure measures can truly deliver equivalent fixed-cost relief and capacity correction, I am open to that. But if they cannot, then political discomfort is not a sufficient reason to rule out the necessary adjustments.

The state cannot at the same time demand:
- full preservation of sites,
- no forced reductions,
- no closures,
- and full competitiveness.

Those four things do not automatically fit together.

## To the Controlling Family Holding
I agree with you on one key point: a destructive conflict would destroy value. Absolutely.

But I disagree with your reluctance to confront the scale of the change. A durable settlement cannot simply preserve the old structure and hope for better margins. We need a material reset in cost and capacity.

You are right that governance matters. That is precisely why we need a package strong enough to survive governance scrutiny. A weak compromise that cannot pass, or cannot endure, is useless.

## To Institutional Investors
You are right to insist that the package be economically real and executable.

Where I push back is on the idea that any closure-heavy proposal is automatically the cleanest value solution. Sometimes the “clean” spreadsheet answer is the one that creates the biggest implementation risk.

That said, I am not here to defend cosmetic change. If you want a non-closure package, it must be **quantified** and **equal in effect**. If it is not, then it is not an alternative — it is a delay mechanism.

## My position, stated plainly

### What management can support
- substantial capacity reduction,
- durable labour-cost relief,
- voluntary exits and redeployment where possible,
- working-time and flexibility measures,
- targeted product allocation and investment commitments,
- a negotiated settlement with real structural impact.

### What management cannot accept
- a blanket ban on closures,
- a blanket ban on compulsory redundancies,
- binding guarantees that freeze the footprint through 2030 regardless of economics,
- a package that preserves the cost base and calls that restructuring.

---

## On the proposals

### 1) No-closure restructuring
I will consider this **only if** it meets the required economic test:
- real fixed-cost reduction,
- real capacity adjustment,
- real labour-cost contribution,
- and a credible return to target margins.

If you can show that, in numbers, I will engage seriously.  
If you cannot, then it is not an equivalent solution.

### 2) Selective capacity-adjustment restructuring
This remains on the table because I cannot responsibly rule out the measures that may be necessary to fix the business.

I do **not** want closures for their own sake.  
I do want the company to survive competitively.

That means the option must remain available if the numbers require it.

---

## What I need next
Enough rhetoric. Bring:
1. a quantified no-closure package,
2. a quantified selective-adjustment package,
3. the savings, capacity, employment, and investment effects of each,
4. and the implementation risk of each.

Then we can decide which one actually restores competitiveness.

Until then, my position remains unchanged: **we need structural savings, lower costs, and lower capacity.** If the no-closure camp can prove equivalence, I will listen. If not, management will not pretend that preservation equals competitiveness.
