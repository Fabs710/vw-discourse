[Works Council (Betriebsrat) (Plant-level employee representation under co-determination) — Round 1]
As the Works Council, our position is clear and non-negotiable on the essentials:

- **No plant closures in Germany.**
- **No compulsory redundancies.**
- **No unilateral dismantling of the historic job-security framework.**

The workforce did not create this crisis, and we will not accept that its solution is paid for by destroying jobs, closing sites, or breaking the trust on which industrial relations at this company have been built for decades.

What we are prepared to discuss is a **binding, negotiated restructuring** that protects every German site and every employee’s future, including:

- **Site and job guarantees through 2030**
- **Capacity adjustments without closures**
- **Working-time solutions and voluntary instruments only**
- **A fair, temporary employee contribution, if and only if it is matched by real commitments from management and owners**
- **A genuine co-determination role in shaping the transformation, especially on investment, product allocation, and future technology**

If management wants competitiveness, then it must come through **innovation, better planning, and fair burden-sharing**, not by handing the workforce a fait accompli. We are ready to negotiate seriously, but only on the basis that **all German plants remain open and no worker is forced out**. Anything else will meet firm resistance.

From an industrial restructuring perspective, the key question is:

**Can a no-closure package realistically deliver savings and capacity reduction of the same order as a closure-based restructuring at a large German automaker?**

## Short answer
**Only in limited circumstances.**  
A no-closure package can deliver **meaningful cost relief**, but it usually cannot fully replicate the economics of **permanent plant closure** if the company truly has **structural overcapacity** and a **material fixed-cost problem**.

That does **not** mean closures are automatically required. It means the parties need to be precise about the target:

- If the required adjustment is mainly **variable cost**, **working-time flexibility**, and a moderate labour-cost reset, then a no-closure deal can work.
- If the required adjustment is a **deep and durable reduction in fixed manufacturing overhead, depreciation burden, and underutilized plant network cost**, then a no-closure package is usually **insufficient unless it includes exceptionally large product reallocation, major volume concentration, and substantial wage/flexibility concessions**.

## What closures do economically
A plant closure typically removes or sharply reduces:
- direct labour at that site,
- site-level overhead,
- maintenance and utilities,
- local logistics complexity,
- duplicate fixed costs,
- and, over time, capital tied to underutilized assets.

It is a **structural** cost reduction. The savings are usually large, durable, and visible on the P&L.

## What a no-closure package can do
A no-closure package can generate savings through:
- voluntary exits and early retirement,
- reduced working time,
- redeployment,
- wage restraint or temporary pay concessions,
- bonus reductions,
- productivity measures,
- administrative overhead cuts,
- product allocation shifts,
- shift rationalization,
- and higher utilization of existing plants.

These measures can be substantial. But they have limits:

### 1) Fixed-cost stickiness
If plants remain open, a large share of site-level cost remains in place.  
You may improve utilization, but you do not eliminate the network cost.

### 2) Utilization constraints
If the group’s domestic capacity is materially above forecast demand, then simply filling the plants better may not be enough. You can reduce idle capacity, but not necessarily enough to restore target margins.

### 3) Labor concessions have a ceiling
Working-time changes and pay restraint can help, but in a German co-determined environment the magnitude is usually limited unless the workforce accepts unusually deep and durable concessions. That is rare.

### 4) Product allocation is not free
Moving volume between plants is only helpful if:
- the receiving plant has spare capacity,
- logistics remain efficient,
- model mix is compatible,
- and the transfer does not create new bottlenecks or investment needs.

## What makes a no-closure package credible
For a no-closure package to be economically credible, I would want to see all of the following:

1. **A quantified baseline**
   - cost per vehicle by site
   - fixed versus variable cost split
   - utilization rates
   - break-even volume by plant
   - headcount by function and site

2. **A clear savings bridge**
   - how much comes from labour
   - how much from overhead
   - how much from working-time changes
   - how much from product allocation
   - how much from procurement or process improvements

3. **Capacity math**
   - current installed capacity
   - expected demand
   - residual overcapacity after measures
   - how much volume is removed from low-productivity sites versus concentrated

4. **Implementation certainty**
   - whether measures are voluntary or binding
   - whether unions/work councils can actually deliver them
   - whether governance approvals exist
   - whether the package survives a strike or a supervisory board conflict

5. **Duration**
   - temporary concessions do not solve a structural cost gap unless they are large enough and long enough to bridge to a changed operating model

A no-closure package **can work** if the required adjustment is on the order of:
- meaningful but not radical labour-cost relief,
- modest capacity reduction through shift and product changes,
- and a productivity reset.

### If the company’s problem is severe:
If management is facing:
- chronic underutilization,
- high German fixed costs,

## Independent plant-level assessment

The question is whether a **no-closure package** can realistically achieve savings and capacity reduction comparable to a **selective closure / plant-adjustment package** at a large German automaker.

### Short conclusion
From an industrial accounting and plant-economics standpoint:

- **A no-closure package can produce meaningful savings.**
- **It is usually not economically equivalent to a closure-based restructuring** if the company truly has **structural overcapacity** and high fixed manufacturing costs.
- A no-closure package can be credible **only if** the required gap is moderate, or if the company can achieve very large and durable measures in:
  - labor cost,
  - working time,
  - product allocation,
  - plant utilization,
  - and overhead simplification.

If the required adjustment is a **deep, permanent reduction in fixed cost and underutilized capacity**, then “no closures” typically becomes either:
1. a partial solution, or  
2. a closure-equivalent contraction in another form.

---

## 1) What closures do, economically

A plant closure removes or sharply reduces:

- direct labor at the site
- site overhead
- maintenance and utilities
- local logistics and internal transport
- supervisory and indirect labor tied to the site
- idle-capacity carrying costs
- depreciation burden on underused assets, if the site is mothballed or written down

This is why closure is a **structural fixed-cost measure**. It tends to deliver large, durable savings.

### Accounting implication
If a plant has high fixed costs and low utilization, the cost per unit rises sharply as volume falls. Closing the plant can eliminate the fixed-cost burden entirely or nearly so, depending on write-downs and post-closure costs.

---

## 2) What a no-closure package can do

A no-closure package can reduce cost through:

- voluntary exits / early retirement
- reduced working time
- wage restraint or temporary pay cuts
- bonus restraint
- redeployment
- headcount reduction through attrition
- productivity improvements
- shift rationalization
- overhead reduction
- product reallocations to better-utilized sites
- procurement and process improvements

These are real savings. But they mostly work by reducing **variable and quasi-fixed labor costs** and improving utilization.

### Limitation
If plant overhead and network cost remain, a no-closure package often leaves a large share of the structural burden intact.

---

## 3) The central accounting issue: fixed-cost stickiness

For a plant with high fixed costs, there is a threshold below which better labor flexibility cannot fully solve the economics.

### Example logic
Suppose a plant has:
- high annual fixed cost,
- moderate variable cost per vehicle,
- and utilization well below break-even.

Then even a significant wage concession may not change the fact that:
- the plant still needs heating, maintenance, supervision, and logistics support
- the asset base still carries depreciation or capital cost
- underutilization keeps unit cost above target

In that case, the plant remains structurally expensive unless volume is added or the footprint is reduced.

---

## 4) When a no-closure package can work

A no-closure package can be viable if the problem is mainly one of:
- labor cost excess,
- work rules,
- short-term volume imbalance,
- or moderate overcapacity

It is more credible if the company can achieve all of the following:

### A. Large labor-cost relief
Examples:
- meaningful wage moderation
- bonus reductions
- longer working time without full pay increase
- reduced supplements
- voluntary part-time or phased retirement

### B. Material capacity correction without closure
Examples:
- removing shifts
- reducing model mix at weaker sites
- moving volume to more efficient plants
- consolidating lines within sites
- increasing utilization elsewhere enough to offset excess footprint

### C. Hard productivity gains
Examples:
- fewer indirect layers
- lower scrap/rework
- shorter changeover times
- higher OEE
- better maintenance planning

### D. Durable implementation
The package must be binding enough to survive:
- strike risk
- governance blockage
- local resistance
- execution slippage

If any one of these is weak, the “no-closure” savings may be much smaller than promised.

---

## 5) When closures become hard to avoid economically

Closures or equivalent footprint reduction become more likely when the company faces:

- chronic underutilization
- multiple overlapping plants doing similar work
- high German labor and overhead cost relative to competitors
- weak EV margins
- declining European demand
- high investment needs for future products
- site-specific break-even volumes that exceed realistic demand

In such cases, keeping all plants open often means:
- preserving excess fixed cost
- spreading volume too thinly
- diluting margins
- forcing future investment into too many sites

That is not a strategy problem only; it is a plant-economics problem.

---

## 6) Break-even logic: what data are needed

To determine whether no-closure is enough, I would need plant-by-plant data on:

1. **Annual fixed cost per site**
   - indirect labor
   - supervision
   - maintenance
   - utilities
   - property costs
   - depreciation or lease costs

2. **Variable cost per unit**
   - direct labor
   - energy
   - materials handling
   - consumables
   - scrap/rework cost

3. **Current and expected output**
   - by plant
   - by model
   - by shift

4. **Break-even volume**
   - units needed per site to cover fixed cost and target contribution margin

5. **Headcount by function**
   - direct production
   - indirect / admin
   - engineering
   - support

6. **Product allocation assumptions**
   - what volume can realistically be moved
   - what investments are needed to move it
   - logistics costs of relocation

Without those, any claim that no closure is “equivalent” is not fully testable.

- write-downs / impairment
- disruption to supply chain and output

These are usually **high**.

#### Implementation risk
- **High**
- because of industrial conflict, governance blockage, and transition disruption

---

## 3) Does no-closure equal selective adjustment?
### Professional answer: usually no
A no-closure package can narrow the gap, sometimes materially. But if the structural issue is **underutilized capacity plus high fixed German overhead**, then a no-closure solution is often **not fully equivalent**.

The reason is accounting logic:
- closures remove **fixed cost structurally**
- no-closure measures mostly reduce **labor cost, time cost, and overhead**, but leave much of the fixed site burden in place

So if the problem is mainly **labor cost and flexibility**, no-closure may work.
If the problem is mainly **excess footprint**, selective adjustment usually wins.

---

## 4) Margin impact
Using the information provided and typical industrial ranges:

- **Moderate no-closure package:** about **0.5 to 1.5 margin points** improvement
- **Aggressive no-closure package:** about **1 to 2 points**
- **Selective adjustment package:** about **1.5 to 3 points**, if the footprint issue is genuinely severe

These are directional estimates only. I would not present them as verified without site-level data.

---

## 5) Site-level decision rule
A site is economically defensible under a no-closure package if, after measures:

1. it can achieve **positive contribution margin**
2. fixed costs are absorbed at competitive utilization
3. it does not require recurring subsidy
4. there is a credible product pipeline
5. unit costs are in line with group target

A site becomes a likely adjustment candidate if:
- utilization stays low
- break-even volume remains above realistic demand
- it needs ongoing cross-subsidy
- or there is no credible future product allocation

That is the core plant-level test.

## 6) Practical conclusion for the decision
### If the company needs a partial repair:
A **no-closure restructuring may be sufficient**, especially if management can secure:
- sizable voluntary exits
- working-time flexibility
- temporary labor cost relief
- real product reallocation

### If the company needs a full structural reset:
A **selective-adjustment plan is more likely to deliver it**.

So my judgment is:

- **No-closure is not implausible**
- **No-closure is unlikely to be fully equivalent if overcapacity is deep**
- **Selective adjustment is more likely to produce the strongest recurring savings**
- **No-closure has lower implementation risk initially, but higher risk of insufficient savings**

### Proposal 1: Sequenced restructuring with a binding evidence test

## 4. Genuine conflicts (structurally incompatible positions)
- **Closure as admissible instrument vs. closure taboo:** Management, family, and investors want closure to remain available; works council and union insist closures cannot remain an active bargaining threat.
- **Economic equivalence standard vs. site-preservation standard:** Capital and management require proof that no-closure measures match the economics; labour and the state require site preservation as the default, with closure only as a non-preferred exception.
- **Conditional sacrifice vs. binding security first:** Labour is willing to consider temporary sacrifices only after binding security is secured; management wants sacrifices to be part of the proof that security is financeable.
- **Decision rule conflict:** One side sees closure availability as prudent governance; the other sees it as coercive leverage that makes genuine bargaining impossible.

## 5. Areas of convergence
- All sides agree the status quo is unsustainable.
- All sides prefer a negotiated restructuring over unmanaged conflict.
- All sides accept that a cosmetic deal would fail.
- All sides endorse some mix of working-time flexibility, voluntary exits, redeployment, productivity gains, and investment commitments.
- All sides acknowledge that any settlement must be durable and implementable, not merely politically convenient.
- All sides accept the usefulness of site-by-site, quantified analysis.

## 6. Key trade-offs for the decision-maker
- **Depth of savings vs. industrial peace:** Selective adjustment likely gives stronger fixed-cost relief; no-closure packages likely reduce conflict risk.
- **Speed vs. legitimacy:** A fast closure-heavy decision may be economically cleaner but governance-blocked; a negotiated no-closure path may be slower but more legitimate.
- **Binding security vs. strategic flexibility:** Labour wants enforceable site/job security; management and owners want flexibility to correct underperformance.
- **Immediate costs vs. recurring gains:** Closure carries high one-off costs but potentially stronger recurring savings; no-closure lowers transition shock but may leave structural cost problems unresolved.
- **Regional stability vs. long-term competitiveness:** The state prioritizes industrial base preservation; management prioritizes margin restoration and future investment capacity.

## 7. Integrative pathways
### Pathway A: Time-bound no-closure trial with hard metrics
Adopt a fixed-period restructuring package with no closures during the trial, measurable savings targets, quarterly verification, and an independent review. If targets are missed, the parties reopen the footprint question under pre-agreed rules.

### Pathway B: Mixed burden-sharing package with asymmetric security
Use temporary employee contributions, management and owner contributions, and binding no-compulsory-redundancy commitments for the duration of the package. Site closure discussion is separated from immediate implementation and only revisited through a performance-based review.

### Pathway C: Site-by-site preservation test with evidence-based exceptions
Presume each German site remains open unless management can show, site by site, that viability cannot be achieved even after product allocation, flexibility, and productivity measures. Closure becomes an exception requiring proof, not a bargaining premise.

### Pathway D: Sequenced restructuring
Start with a protected no-closure phase, then run an independent plant-level economic comparison using identical assumptions for no-closure and selective-adjustment scenarios, then negotiate only on the narrowed set of viable options.
