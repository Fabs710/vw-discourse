As dispersed institutional investors, our position is straightforward: **we care about the company’s risk-adjusted equity value, not preserving every legacy structure**. That means we are sympathetic to management’s diagnosis, but we will judge this on whether it produces a **credible, durable return to competitive margins**.

Where we **agree**:
- The status quo is not acceptable.
- German costs and capacity appear out of line with the market.
- A cosmetic agreement that simply postpones the problem would destroy value.
- The company needs structural savings, not a temporary pause.

Where we **disagree** with the workforce-side positions:
- A blanket “no closures, no compulsory redundancies, no rollback of protections” is not a business plan.
- If those red lines make it impossible to achieve the required cost base, then they amount to asking shareholders to fund permanent underperformance.
- We will not support an outcome that leaves the company structurally uncompetitive or keeps margins impaired.

Where we **disagree** with management if it means escalation for its own sake:
- A confrontation that triggers prolonged strikes, board paralysis, or repeated public conflict would also destroy value.
- Closing a plant may be economically justified in principle, but if the process becomes disorderly and drags on, the financial benefit can evaporate.
- So management needs not just a hard target, but a **deal that is executable and enforceable**.

Where we **agree** with the controlling family and, to a degree, the state:
- Governance reality matters. If there are blocking rights, the company needs a settlement that can actually pass.
- A durable compromise is worth more than a technically optimal plan that cannot be implemented.

Our core test is this:

1. **Does the package materially reduce fixed costs and capacity?**
2. **Does it restore competitiveness fast enough to protect margins and investment capacity?**
3. **Is it credible enough to avoid another restructuring in two years?**
4. **Does it minimize the risk of prolonged industrial conflict?**

On that basis, we would support:
- plant closure or capacity reduction **if necessary** to achieve the required savings,
- labor-cost reductions and working-time flexibility,
- voluntary exits and redeployment where they are real and sufficient,
- and targeted investment commitments only if they are tied to measurable restructuring gains.

What we will **not** support:
- a deal that preserves all sites and jobs while offering only symbolic savings,
- open-ended guarantees that hard-code uncompetitive structures,
- or a plan that leaves the market believing management lacks the will to act.

So our message to all parties is: **we want a settlement, but it must be economically real**. If a no-closure alternative can demonstrably deliver the same structural margin improvement, we would view that positively. But if it cannot, then shareholders should not be expected to absorb the cost of political comfort or industrial stalemate.

We are watching the numbers, not the rhetoric.
