## Independent plant-level restructuring assessment

I have been asked, in effect, to test whether a **no-closure package** can realistically deliver savings and capacity reduction comparable to a **selective-adjustment** plan, and to estimate the recurring margin impact and implementation risk.

### Bottom line
A **credible no-closure package is possible only in a narrow to moderate overcapacity case**.  
If the German footprint is **structurally oversized** and the cost gap is material, then a no-closure package can reduce the gap, but it is **unlikely to be fully equivalent** to selective adjustment in recurring savings unless the employee contribution is unusually large, durable, and accompanied by meaningful product/volume reallocation.

In plain terms:

- **No-closure can work as a bridge**
- **No-closure is not automatically a substitute for footprint reduction**
- **Selective adjustment generally wins on fixed-cost removal**
- **No-closure generally wins on implementation risk, at least at first**
- **The decisive question is not ideology but whether the recurring savings bridge is big enough**

---

# 1) What a defensible comparison needs
To make this judgment properly, I would need site-level data on:

1. **Capacity and utilization by plant**
   - rated capacity
   - current output
   - expected demand by model/program

2. **Fixed cost base by site**
   - depreciation/amortization
   - plant overhead
   - utilities and maintenance
   - salaried labor
   - logistics and indirect support

3. **Variable conversion cost**
   - direct labor
   - scrap and yield
   - energy
   - line efficiency

4. **Restructuring options by site**
   - shift reduction
   - line consolidation
   - product reassignment
   - voluntary exit potential
   - early retirement eligibility
   - relocation feasibility

5. **Transition costs**
   - severance
   - site shutdown costs
   - equipment relocation
   - redundancy of overhead functions
   - write-downs / impairments

6. **Demand assumptions**
   - volume by model
   - EV launch timing
   - export allocation
   - make/buy decisions

Without these, any assessment is necessarily scenario-based rather than forensic.

---

# 2) Practical scenario framework

I would test three cases.

## Case A: No-closure, moderate restructuring
Assumptions:
- no plant closures
- no compulsory redundancies
- voluntary exits, working-time reduction, redeployment
- modest wage restraint / temporary employee contribution
- overhead cuts and product allocation changes
- some capacity reduction via shifts and line balancing

### Likely economic effect
- **Recurring savings:** material, but limited
- **One-off costs:** moderate
- **Implementation risk:** moderate, because labor peace may be achievable
- **Net effect:** often enough to improve margin, but not enough if overcapacity is deep

### Order-of-magnitude estimate
For a large German automotive footprint, a credible no-closure package of this kind often yields recurring savings in the range of:

- **~€300m to €800m annually** in a substantial program
- potentially more if wage concessions are unusually deep
- but usually **not full structural equivalence** to shutting a materially underutilized plant

This is a rough range, not a prediction.

---

## Case B: No-closure, aggressive restructuring
Assumptions:
- no closures
- very large voluntary exit program
- significant working-time cuts and flexibility
- temporary pay/bonus restraint
- more aggressive redeployment and product shifting
- deep administrative overhead reduction
- possibly some outsourcing / insourcing changes

### Likely economic effect
- **Recurring savings:** larger, potentially material enough to approximate a closure case in a moderate overcapacity situation
- **One-off costs:** high, because voluntary exits and redeployment are expensive
- **Implementation risk:** still lower than closures, but rising sharply because the package becomes harder to sustain politically and socially

### Order-of-magnitude estimate
This kind of package might reach:
- **~€500m to €1.2bn annually** recurring, depending on scale and wage terms

But to be candid:
- getting to the upper end requires **real labor-cost reset**, not symbolic concessions
- and it still may fall short if the real problem is **excess physical footprint**

---

## Case C: Selective adjustment / selective closure
Assumptions:
- one or more plant closures or major footprint reductions
- associated overhead removal
- workforce reduction including compulsory elements where voluntary exits are insufficient
- product and volume concentration on fewer sites

### Likely economic effect
- **Recurring savings:** highest, because fixed cost comes out structurally
- **One-off costs:** highest
- **Implementation risk:** highest in the short term due to industrial action and governance friction

### Order-of-magnitude estimate
Recurring savings can easily exceed:
- **~€700m to €1.5bn+ annually**
depending on plant size, overhead structure, and how much of the network is actually removed.

That range is broad because a plant closure can eliminate:
- direct site overhead,
- some indirect labor,
- maintenance and energy,
- local logistics complexity,
- and duplicated support functions.

---

# 3) Is “no-closure” economically equivalent?
## Short answer: usually not, unless the overcapacity is moderate and the employee contribution is substantial.

A no-closure package can be close to equivalent if:
- the plant network is only **mildly oversized**
- there are multiple opportunities for **product reallocation**
- utilization can be improved without major capital spending
- wage and time flexibility changes are durable
- and the company can sustain a large voluntary exit program

But if:
- one or more plants are materially underutilized,
- fixed costs are high,
- and demand is structurally lower in Europe,
then no-closure measures often **reduce the pain** but do not **fully remove the structural cost**.

So the realistic conclusion is:

- **No-closure may close part of the gap**
- **Selective adjustment is more likely to fully close it**

---

# 4) Recurring annual margin impact
Because the company’s exact baseline is not provided, I’ll express this in operating terms.

If the current problem is a structural margin shortfall of, say, **2 to 4 percentage points** on a large car division with heavy German cost exposure, then:

- a **moderate no-closure package** might recover roughly **0.5 to 1.5 points**
- an **aggressive no-closure package** might recover **1 to 2 points**
- a **selective-adjustment package** could plausibly recover **1.5 to 3 points**, depending on the severity of the footprint issue

Again, those are rough ranges.

### Economic interpretation
- If management needs only a **partial margin repair**, no-closure may be enough.
- If management needs a **full structural reset**, no-closure is less likely to suffice unless concessions are unusually deep and durable.

---

# 5) Implementation risk comparison

## No-closure package
### Advantages
- lower immediate industrial disruption
- lower litigation/governance conflict
- easier to maintain output continuity
- more likely to preserve know-how and local operational stability

### Risks
- savings may be **too shallow**
- risk of “temporary” concessions becoming permanent burdens without solving the problem
- may leave a structurally expensive footprint intact
- risk of delayed strategic correction

### Overall implementation risk: **moderate**
Lower in the first 6–12 months, but potentially higher over a 3–5 year horizon if the economics remain weak.

---

## Selective adjustment package
### Advantages
- strongest fixed-cost relief
- best chance of restoring structural competitiveness
- clearer long-term footprint rationalization

### Risks
- severe industrial conflict risk
- governance blockage risk
- higher one-off cash costs
- potential execution disruption
- reputational and morale impact can hurt output and quality

### Overall implementation risk: **high**
Especially in a co-determined setting with effective vetoes.

---

# 6) Site-by-site analytical logic
A plant should be considered viable under a no-closure package only if, after measures, it can plausibly achieve:

1. **positive contribution margin**
2. **adequate fixed-cost absorption**
3. **utilization consistent with competitive unit cost**
4. **no persistent dependence on cross-subsidy**
5. **a credible product pipeline**

A site likely becomes a closure candidate if:
- it remains below efficient utilization even after volume reallocation
- it cannot absorb fixed cost at realistic demand
- it requires recurring subsidy to stay open
- or it has no credible next product

That is the technical test.

---

# 7) My defensible conclusion
## Can a no-closure package realistically achieve the stated savings and capacity reduction?
### Yes, but only under conditions.
It can be realistic if:
- the overcapacity problem is moderate rather than severe
- employee concessions are substantial and durable
- product allocation changes are real
- voluntary exits are large enough
- and management accepts that some savings will come from labor and overhead rather than site elimination

### No, if the current footprint is structurally too large.
If one or more plants are underutilized to the point that fixed costs cannot be absorbed, then no-closure is **unlikely to be economically equivalent** to selective adjustment.

---

# 8) What I would recommend as the decision rule
The parties should not argue closures in the abstract. They should require:

- a **plant-by-plant break-even statement**
- a **no-closure savings bridge**
- a **selective-adjustment savings bridge**
- one-off cost estimates for both
- and a 3-year margin trajectory for each

Then compare:
- **net recurring savings**
- **implementation risk**
- **cash cost**
- **time to realize savings**

Only that comparison can tell you whether no-closure is a true substitute or merely a delay mechanism.

---

# 9) Final judgment
My professional judgment, given the facts provided, is:

- A **no-closure package is not implausible**
- A **fully equivalent no-closure package is unlikely unless concessions are large**
- A **selective-adjustment package is more likely to deliver the full economic reset**
- A **no-closure package likely offers lower near-term execution risk but higher risk of insufficient savings**
