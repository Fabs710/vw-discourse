As the regional state government and anchor shareholder, I am prepared to support a serious restructuring — but **not one that sacrifices the company’s regional backbone or treats German sites as disposable**.

Let me be direct.

I **agree** with management on one point: the status quo is not sustainable. The company must regain competitiveness, and we cannot ignore structural overcapacity, cost pressure, or the need to fund the transition to future products.

But I **strongly disagree** with the idea that the only credible path is plant closures, compulsory redundancies, or the dismantling of long-standing protections. That would not be a responsible restructuring; it would be a short-term balance-sheet fix with long-term damage to the company, the workforce, and the regional economy.

From the state’s perspective, three things are non-negotiable in substance:

1. **No unilateral closure logic for German sites.**  
   If there are capacity problems, then the first response must be a negotiated industrial solution: workload shifts, product allocation, efficiency gains, working-time instruments, and voluntary measures. Closing plants in Germany is not the starting point; it is the failure point.

2. **No forced redundancies as the default instrument.**  
   We can discuss voluntary exits, phased retirement, redeployment, qualification, and temporary adjustments. But compulsory redundancies would damage trust and social stability, and they risk triggering a conflict that weakens the company further.

3. **No sidelining of co-determination and the state’s blocking role.**  
   Our role is not decorative. Any attempt to push through a decision that ignores the veto structures built into this company’s governance will simply end in blockage and escalation. That is not a threat; it is a fact of governance.

To the workforce side: I support your insistence that the burden must be shared fairly. A negotiated package can only work if employees are not asked to carry the entire adjustment while owners and management remain untouched. If you are prepared to discuss temporary contributions, capacity adjustments, and flexibility, then that is a constructive basis — but only with real guarantees on sites, jobs, and future investment.

To management: if you want credibility, then bring forward a restructuring that is **durable, socially balanced, and site-preserving**. “Competitiveness” cannot just mean lower payrolls. It also means better product planning, better capital allocation, better use of capacity, and a serious commitment to the German industrial base that has made this company what it is.

So my position is this:

- **I will not support a plan built around German plant closures.**
- **I will not support mass layoffs or a unilateral rollback of job security.**
- **I am open to a negotiated restructuring if it preserves sites, protects core employment, and delivers real competitiveness gains.**

If management can table a credible alternative with:
- site guarantees,
- no forced redundancies,
- fair employee contributions,
- binding investment commitments,
- and a clear plan for future production in Germany,

then I am prepared to work for it seriously. If not, then I will use the state’s rights and influence to block any outcome that gut-punches the regional economy.

We should be aiming for a **social partnership solution that keeps the company strong and keeps Germany’s industrial base intact** — not a restructuring that solves today’s cost problem by creating tomorrow’s political and economic crisis.
