As the controlling family holding, I want to be very clear: **I support a restructuring that restores competitiveness, but I do not support a value-destructive confrontation or a cosmetic compromise that leaves the company structurally unfit.**

I agree with management on the core diagnosis: **the current cost base and capacity are not sustainable**. If we avoid hard decisions entirely, we will damage margins, investment capacity, and ultimately the long-term value of the enterprise. That is unacceptable for us as long-term owners.

At the same time, I agree with the others on one crucial point: **a solution that triggers prolonged industrial conflict would also destroy value**. So the goal must be a durable settlement, not a public showdown.

Where I disagree is with the idea that the only credible route is either full closures and forced redundancies, or a blanket preservation of all sites and guarantees to 2030. **Neither extreme is acceptable as a governance outcome.**

To the Works Council and union:
- I understand the refusal to accept arbitrary closures and mass layoffs.
- But I do **not** accept a position that effectively freezes the company’s industrial footprint regardless of economics.
- If the alternative merely preserves every site, every structure, and every legacy entitlement while asking ownership and management to absorb the cost, then it is not a restructuring.

To management:
- I support the need for real savings, not symbolic ones.
- But I will not back a plan that ignores the governance reality here or assumes that the workforce will simply yield under pressure.
- If you table a proposal that depends on maximal confrontation, you should expect it to fail in governance even if it is economically coherent on paper.

To the state government:
- I respect the role of the state and its legitimate concern for regional stability.
- But the company cannot be run as a regional employment preservation scheme.
- If the state’s position is effectively “no closures under any circumstances,” that risks turning necessary restructuring into deferred decline.

My position is therefore this:

1. **There must be a credible, durable reduction in the cost base and capacity.**
2. **There must be labour peace and a negotiated settlement.**
3. **There should be no automatic assumption of plant closures, but neither can they be ruled out in advance if no equivalent solution is available.**
4. **Any package must protect the long-term value of the enterprise, not just short-term employment figures.**

What would move me:
- a package that achieves substantial fixed-cost reduction,
- real capacity alignment,
- a meaningful employee contribution,
- voluntary and socially managed workforce reduction where possible,
- and binding commitments on future product allocation and investment.

What would not move me:
- a deal that preserves everything and merely postpones the crisis,
- or a plan that destroys labour peace and reputation while only pretending to solve the economics.

So let me be explicit: **I am open to a negotiated restructuring without closures, but only if it delivers the same structural economic effect.** If that cannot be achieved, then I will not block necessary hard decisions simply to preserve appearances. But I will also not support an outcome that permanently weakens the company through conflict or half-measures.

We need a settlement that is economically real, socially manageable, and governance-proof. Anything else is not stewardship.
