## My overall view
I agree with the independent analyst on the central point: **a no-closure package may help, but it is unlikely to be fully equivalent if the overcapacity and fixed-cost problem is genuinely structural**.

That matters because I am not here to defend closures as a first choice, but I also will not support a package that preserves the cost base and merely delays the reckoning. The family holding’s interest is long-term value, not short-term optics.

So my position remains:
- **No closure is preferable if it truly delivers equivalent economics and stability.**
- **If it does not, closures must remain an available last-resort instrument.**
- **A deal that blocks both closures and meaningful cost reduction is unacceptable.**

1. the exact savings,
2. the exact capacity reduction,

## On the expert’s conclusion
The analyst’s testimony is useful because it clarifies the decision rule:

- If the company’s problem is **moderate**, a no-closure package might work.
- If the problem is **severe and structural**, no-closure is probably not enough unless the concessions are unusually large and durable.

That is exactly why I will not commit now to “no closures under any circumstances.”  
It is also why I will not endorse closures as the first move.

The only responsible approach is to test whether a no-closure package can truly match the savings target. If it can, I am open to it. If it cannot, then we must not pretend otherwise.

## My position on the two scenarios

### 1) No-closure restructuring
I would support it only if it meets all of the following:
- quantified fixed-cost reduction,
- meaningful capacity reduction,
- durable labour-cost relief,
- credible product and volume reallocation,
- implementation certainty,
- and a stable governance path.

If those conditions are met, it may be preferable because it lowers conflict risk.

### 2) Selective capacity-adjustment restructuring
I would keep this available as a **last resort**, not a preferred starting point.

Why? Because the company’s value is not only in cost reduction. It is also in:
- labour peace,
- brand reputation,
- execution capacity,
- and the ability to invest without protracted internal warfare.

But if the no-closure route cannot reach the required economics, then selective adjustment must remain on the table.

## What would move me
A credible package would need:
1. a clear baseline of plant-level economics,
2. a quantified no-closure savings bridge,
3. a quantified selective-adjustment alternative,
4. a comparison of implementation risk,
5. and a burden-sharing formula that is not one-sided.

## What would not move me
- slogans about preserving every site,
- vague references to social partnership without hard numbers,
- or management presenting closure as inevitable before alternatives are tested.

## My bottom line
I am **open to a no-closure restructuring**, but only if it can be proven to restore competitiveness at the required scale.

I am **not** accepting an absolute ban on closures.  
I am **not** accepting a closure-first approach either.

The governing test is simple:
- **Does the package restore margins and competitiveness durably, without triggering value-destructive instability?**

If yes, I support it.  
If no, then the company must retain the right to take harder structural measures.
