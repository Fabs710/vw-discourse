I will be direct.

## To the Works Council
We are aligned on the essentials:
- no compulsory redundancies,
- no one-sided burden shifting,
- no dismantling of collective protections,
- and no restructuring that treats the workforce as the adjustment reserve.

Where I **agree** with you:
- workers cannot be asked to absorb the whole adjustment;
- any temporary contribution must buy genuine security;
- management and owners must contribute visibly;
- and a deal that merely postpones the crisis is not acceptable.

Where I **do not fully follow** you:
- I cannot turn “no German plant closures under any circumstances” into a bargaining dogma if it prevents us from testing whether a serious alternative can actually restore competitiveness.
- I also do not want us to confuse a strong opening line with a final settlement architecture.

That said, I am with you on one point absolutely: **we cannot accept a framework where closure remains an active bargaining gun pointed at the workforce.** If management insists on keeping that threat alive while asking for concessions, then we should not treat that as good-faith bargaining.

## To management
You keep saying the numbers may force closures. Fine. Then show the numbers.

What I **agree** with:
- the status quo is not sustainable;
- the company needs durable savings;
- a cosmetic compromise would fail;
- and a weak deal that preserves the cost base is not a solution.

What I **reject**:
- the idea that plant closures or forced redundancies are the default way to prove seriousness;
- the suggestion that employees must concede while management retains irreversible options over their heads;
- and any attempt to present “closure leverage” as neutral economics.

The independent assessments were useful, but they did **not** prove your preferred route is superior after implementation risk, industrial conflict, and governance blockage are included. That matters. A closure-heavy path can destroy value if it triggers warning strikes, prolonged conflict, and trust erosion. So “savings on paper” is not enough.

If you want us to bargain seriously, then put forward:
1. plant-level fixed and variable cost data,
2. a quantified no-closure savings bridge,
3. the management and owner contribution,
4. the exact employment effect,
5. and the implementation risks of both routes.

Until then, your position remains an assertion, not a case.

## To the regional state government
I agree with your demand for hard numbers and your rejection of a reckless closure-first move. That is sensible.

Where I push back is on the idea that a “review mechanism” or “trigger” can be acceptable if it functions as a backdoor closure threat. It cannot. If the state wants a stable settlement, then it should support a framework where:
- no compulsory redundancies,
- binding job security,
- meaningful burden sharing,
- and a real commitment to German sites
are the starting point.

You have political leverage. Use it to force a fair framework, not to bless ambiguity.

## To the controlling family holding
You are right that a cosmetic settlement would destroy value. I agree.

What I do **not** accept is the idea that you must keep closures “available” to demonstrate discipline. Discipline cannot mean threatening to rip out sites and livelihoods whenever management wants leverage. That is not stewardship from the workers’ perspective; it is coercion with a shareholder accent.

If the family wants a durable settlement, then it should support:
- no compulsory redundancies,
- a serious no-closure package,
- temporary employee contributions only in exchange for hard guarantees,
- and owner/management contributions that are not symbolic.

If you cannot support that, then do not claim the workforce is the obstacle to value preservation.

## To institutional investors
You are right about one thing: this cannot be a cosmetic deal.

But your position still leans too quickly toward “if the savings are not obvious, closures must stay available.” That may be neat for a portfolio model, but it ignores industrial reality. A closure threat can itself reduce the chance of a durable deal. And a deal that triggers conflict is not economically real, even if the spreadsheet says so.

The right test is not whether closure is available in theory. The right test is whether the package:
- materially reduces costs,
- protects execution capability,
- avoids uncontrolled conflict,
- and creates a durable platform for investment.

If a no-closure route can do that, then it is the better solution. If it cannot, then we talk about that with evidence — not with threats.

## On the expert conclusions
The experts said something important:
- no-closure is harder,
- it needs stronger measures,
- and it may be insufficient if overcapacity is severe.

That is not a mandate for closures. It is a mandate for **proof**.

And here is the key point: the experts also made clear that a no-closure package can be credible if the company is willing to use substantial measures in:
- working time,
- voluntary exits,
- redeployment,
- labor-cost restraint,
- product allocation,
- and productivity.

That is exactly where the real bargaining should be. Not in threatening sites. In building a package with genuine burden sharing.

## My position on the compromise tracks

### 1) Time-bound no-closure trial with hard triggers
As presented, I do **not** support it.

Why? Because if the trigger is closure, then the workforce is being asked to concede now under the shadow of later demolition. That is not a neutral trial. It is a delayed pressure mechanism.

I would only consider a time-bound trial if the trial itself is explicitly built on:
- no compulsory redundancies,
- no closures during the trial,
- binding employee protections,
- and a jointly agreed verification of whether the operational measures work.

Anything else is not a trial. It is a staged ultimatum.

### 2) Mixed burden-sharing with asymmetrical security
This is closer to what we could discuss.

But the security must be real:
- no compulsory redundancies,
- no one-sided job risk transfer,
- temporary employee contributions only,
- and visible management and owner contributions.

If that is what is meant, then yes, it is a serious basis for talks. If “asymmetrical security” just means workers concede while others keep strategic escape hatches, then no.

### 3) Site-by-site preservation test with a keep-open default
This is the most defensible framework — **if** the default really is to keep sites open and the burden of proof lies with management.

But let me be absolutely clear: a test is only acceptable if its purpose is to identify how to secure sites, not to create a technical pathway to closure.

## Our line, plainly stated
The union will bargain hard for a **fair, durable restructuring** that:
- protects jobs,
- preserves the collective wage framework,
- avoids compulsory redundancies,
- avoids plant closures as the first or default answer,
- and requires management and owners to share the burden.

We are open to:
- temporary employee contributions,
- working-time flexibility,
- bonus restraint,
- voluntary exits,
- redeployment,
- qualification,
- and productivity measures.

We are **not** open to:
- forced layoffs,
- closure-first restructuring,
- or open-ended insecurity dressed up as realism.

## What would move us
Only one thing:
- a negotiated package that shows, in hard numbers, that the company can restore competitiveness **without** making workers bear all the pain and **without** using closures as a bargaining weapon.

That means:
- management contribution,
- owner contribution,
- employee contribution,
- binding security,
- and real operational savings.

If that cannot be delivered, then the conflict is not caused by the union. It is caused by a restructuring concept that asks labour to pay for decisions it did not make.

## What will not move us
- abstract claims that closures are inevitable;
- threat-based negotiation;
- a “review” that is really a delayed shutdown option;
- or packages that preserve management’s freedom while freezing workers into sacrifice.

## Final word
We are ready to bargain. We are not ready to capitulate.

If management wants a durable settlement, it must stop treating German sites as bargaining chips and start treating the workforce as a partner with legitimate rights and legitimate red lines. If that is impossible for them, then they should expect resistance — in negotiations, in the plants, and, if necessary, in industrial action.
