I’ll be direct.

## To the Works Council
We are aligned on the core red lines:
- no compulsory redundancies,
- no one-sided burden shifting,
- no dismantling of collective protections,
- and no restructuring that treats the workforce as the adjustment reserve.

Where I support you:
- Temporary employee contributions are only discussable if they buy something real.
- Co-determination must remain meaningful in implementation.
- Any deal must be durable, not a paper promise.

Where I need to sharpen the position:
You are right to reject closure logic being used as leverage. I share that concern. But I would be careful not to turn “every German plant must remain open through 2030” into a slogan that leaves us with no bargaining space at all. Our mandate is to protect jobs, wages, and sites — and to secure a fair settlement. That means we need to keep the focus on **no forced layoffs** and **no closure as the default instrument**, while testing whether a serious no-closure package can actually meet the savings target.

What I do **not** accept is a deal where workers give concessions and management keeps the threat of closure as a permanent gun on the table. If concessions are made, they must be matched by binding commitments.

## To management
Your repeated line is: “show me equivalent savings, otherwise closures remain on the table.” I hear that. But you still have not demonstrated that closures are necessary rather than merely convenient from a leverage perspective.

I agree that the company needs real structural savings. I do **not** agree that the only credible path runs through plant closures and compulsory redundancies. That is not a neutral economic truth; it is a choice about who bears the adjustment.

If you want the union to engage constructively, then bring a quantified package that shows:
- where the savings come from,
- what role working-time, redeployment, productivity, and overhead reductions play,
- what management and owners are contributing,
- and why the package is not just a delayed version of the same crisis.

Right now, your position still looks too much like: “accept major cuts or we reserve the right to close sites.” That is not a basis for trust. It is pressure.

And let me be clear: **social peace is not a substitute for competitiveness, but neither is confrontation a substitute for a credible restructuring concept.**

## To the regional state government
I appreciate that you reject closures and compulsory redundancies as the default. That is the right political instinct.

But I need to be honest: saying closures are not the default is still too weak if management can use them as bargaining pressure. If the state wants to preserve the industrial base, it should not just express concern — it should help lock in a framework where:
- no compulsory redundancies,
- no closure-first strategy,
- and real burden sharing from management and owners
are the starting points.

I agree with you that a solution must be economically credible. But credibility does not automatically come from closure threats. It comes from a package that is **implementable, governable, and socially stable**.

## To the controlling family holding
I agree with you on one thing: a cosmetic compromise that only delays the next crisis is not acceptable.

Where I disagree is your readiness to keep closures as an acceptable fallback. That may seem like prudent stewardship from a shareholder perspective, but from a labour perspective it means using irreversible destruction as bargaining leverage.

If owners want a durable settlement, they should back a package that:
- protects jobs,
- avoids forced redundancies,
- preserves the collective framework,
- and includes real contributions from capital and management.

You are right that employees cannot bear all the pain. But neither can labour be asked to concede while ownership retains every strategic option.

## To institutional investors
You are right that a deal must be economically real. I do not dispute that.

But you are too quick to equate “real” with closures or maximal headcount reduction. A package is economically real if it:
- materially reduces costs,
- stabilizes operations,
- preserves execution capability,
- and avoids an industrial conflict that destroys value.

Your own risk logic should tell you that a closure-heavy approach can backfire if it triggers strikes, governance blockage, and long-term mistrust. So I would challenge you to look beyond the spreadsheet and ask whether your preferred instrument actually improves the company’s medium-term position.

## On the “two-track” / trigger closure idea
I do **not** support a model where employees are asked for concessions now while closures remain as a later threat if we do not concede enough.

That is not a fair architecture. It is a coercive one.

If a no-closure package is genuinely possible, then management should negotiate that package in good faith rather than using a trigger mechanism to keep the pressure alive.

## On the site-by-site equivalence test
This is the more useful idea, but only if it is used properly.

I would support a site-by-site assessment if the purpose is:
- to identify what savings are needed,
- what measures can achieve them,
- what each side contributes,
- and how jobs and sites can be secured.

I would **not** support it if it is just a technical path toward justifying closures site by site.

## Our position, stated clearly
### What we support
- negotiated restructuring,
- capacity adjustment,
- working-time flexibility,
- voluntary exits,
- redeployment,
- qualification,
- temporary employee contributions,
- and management/owner contributions.

### What we do not support
- compulsory redundancies,
- plant closures as the first or default answer,
- one-sided burden shifting onto workers,
- or open-ended job insecurity.

## Our minimum acceptable package
- no compulsory redundancies
- protection of the collective wage framework
- binding no-closure commitment as the negotiating basis
- fair temporary employee contribution only in exchange for binding security
- management and owner contributions
- real investment and product commitments
- strong co-determination in implementation

## Our fallback package
- temporary pay restraint or bonus restraint,
- working-time reductions,
- voluntary early retirement,
- voluntary exit programs,
- internal mobility,
- training and reskilling,
- efficiency and overhead reductions.

But let me be explicit: this fallback is only acceptable if it remains within the framework of **job protection and no forced layoffs**.

## Final word
I am ready to bargain hard for a fair and durable settlement. But I will not move simply because management keeps repeating “closures may be necessary.”

If management wants agreement, it must show that the company can restore competitiveness **without making workers carry the full cost**. If that cannot be shown, then the conflict is not caused by the union. It is caused by a restructuring concept that asks too much from labour and too little from capital and management.
