We are aligned on the diagnosis that **the status quo is not sustainable**. That matters. I also agree that a settlement that triggers prolonged industrial conflict would destroy value and weaken the company’s ability to invest. So yes: **we need a durable, governance-proof restructuring**.

But I need to be equally clear: I do **not** accept a solution that merely preserves everything and calls that a compromise. The company has a real competitiveness problem, and if we avoid structural change entirely, we will damage margins, investment capacity, and ultimately the enterprise itself.

## To the Works Council
I hear your red lines:
- no plant closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- no dismantling of job security.

I understand why you are putting those forward. But I do **not** accept them as absolute conditions if they make it impossible to restore competitiveness. A package that keeps every site open, freezes the footprint, and relies only on working-time measures and voluntary tools may sound attractive, but it does not yet show me **where the required fixed-cost reduction comes from at the necessary scale**.

So where I agree with you:
- capacity must be reduced in a socially manageable way where possible,
- voluntary instruments should be used first,
- redeployment, qualification, and working-time flexibility are preferable to forced measures,
- and a negotiated solution is far better than confrontation.

Where I disagree:
- I will not treat “no closures under any circumstances” as a governance rule.
- I will not support a package that preserves the old structure while postponing the adjustment.
- I will not back a deal that asks owners to absorb the cost of uncompetitive operations indefinitely.

If you want a no-closure solution, it has to be **economically equivalent**, not just politically preferable.

## To the union
I agree with your point that the burden cannot fall only on employees. That is correct. Owners, management, and capital must also bear responsibility for getting this right.

But I disagree with the implication that industrial peace alone can substitute for structural savings. It cannot. Social stability matters, but it does not by itself restore margins or fund future products.

So yes to:
- temporary employee contributions if they are meaningful,
- voluntary exits,
- working-time tools,
- qualification and redeployment,
- a fair sharing of pain.

No to:
- a one-sided burden shift onto workers,
- open-ended protections that freeze the cost base,
- or a deal that looks balanced socially but remains economically weak.

## To management
I support your insistence that the company needs **real, durable savings**. On that point, you are right.

Where I am not aligned is any framing that says closures or forced redundancies are the only serious tools. They are not the only tools, and if they become the default mindset, you will push this into a conflict that reduces value even if the spreadsheet looks cleaner.

I need you to present:
- a quantified no-closure scenario,
- a quantified selective-adjustment scenario,
- the fixed-cost impact of each,
- and the implementation risk attached to each.

Without that, “closures may be necessary” is not yet a decision case; it is a bargaining position.

## To the regional state government
I respect the state’s role and the importance of the regional industrial base. I also agree with your view that a crude closure-led approach would be damaging if it became the first and only lever.

But I disagree with the idea that closures should be effectively precluded by political preference. That would leave the company with no credible restructuring option if non-closure measures do not deliver enough savings.

The right test is not whether closures are politically comfortable. The test is whether there is a **credible alternative that restores competitiveness and can actually pass governance**.

## To institutional investors
You are right to focus on risk-adjusted value and not to romanticize legacy structures. I agree that a cosmetic compromise would destroy value.

Where I differ is that “structural savings” do not automatically mean a closure-heavy solution. They mean a solution that:
- materially improves the cost base,
- restores margins,
- is executable,
- and avoids a destructive governance and labour conflict.

If a no-closure package can truly deliver that, it deserves support. If it cannot, then we should not pretend it can.

## What would move me
A credible package would need to show:
- substantial fixed-cost reduction,
- meaningful capacity alignment,
- a real employee contribution,
- voluntary and socially managed workforce reduction where possible,
- and binding commitments on future product allocation and investment.

## What would not move me
- a package that preserves all sites regardless of economics,
- a deal that postpones the crisis,
- or a confrontation strategy that destroys labour peace without improving the economics enough to justify it.

## My position
I am **open to a negotiated restructuring without closures**, but only if it delivers the same structural economic effect. If it does not, then I will not support pretending that preservation equals competitiveness.

At the same time, I am **not** endorsing a closure-first strategy. That would be unnecessarily destructive if alternatives can do the job.

So the task now is simple:
- show me the numbers for a no-closure package,
- show me the numbers for selective capacity adjustment,
- and show me which one actually restores the company’s competitiveness while remaining governable.

That is the standard I will use.
