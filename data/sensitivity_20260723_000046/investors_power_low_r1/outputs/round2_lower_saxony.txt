I will speak plainly.

## Where I agree

I agree with management, the family holding, and the investors on one point: **the status quo is not sustainable**. The company needs a real restructuring, not a postponement. German competitiveness, capacity alignment, and the funding of future products all matter.

I also agree with the workforce side that **a deal imposed as a fait accompli will fail**. If this turns into a confrontation that triggers strikes, vetoes, and long damage to trust, then value will be destroyed and the regional economy will pay the price.

So yes: **we need a negotiated, durable settlement**.

## Where I disagree — strongly

### 1) I do not accept plant closures in Germany as the default solution
Management keeps saying closures must remain on the table. I understand why you say that as a bargaining position. But for me, as a regional state government and anchor shareholder, **German plant closures are not an acceptable starting point**.

Not because restructuring is unnecessary, but because closure is the point at which this company stops being a regional industrial anchor and starts becoming a political and social crisis.

If there is any way to achieve the necessary adjustment through:
- capacity shifts,
- product allocation,
- working-time measures,
- redeployment,
- qualification,
- voluntary departures,
- and targeted efficiency gains,

then that is the route that must be pursued first and seriously.

### 2) I do not accept compulsory redundancies as the default instrument
I am aligned with the works council and union here. Forced redundancies would damage trust, intensify conflict, and weaken the company’s long-term execution capacity. That is not responsible restructuring; it is a high-risk escalation.

### 3) I reject the idea that social partnership is merely “political comfort”
Management and investors keep framing social stability as if it were a soft concern. It is not. **Industrial peace is an economic asset.** A solution that looks clean in a spreadsheet but provokes prolonged conflict is not risk-adjusted value creation. It is fragility.

## To management

You ask for “equivalent” savings without closures. That is the right question. But you are still acting as if the only serious lever is plant exit and forced headcount reduction.

I want a concrete answer from you on the following:

- What exact savings are required by site and function?
- What part comes from working-time, redeployment, productivity, procurement, product mix, and overhead reduction?
- How much fixed-cost relief do closures actually deliver after taking into account conflict, disruption, and implementation risk?
- What is your plan to keep the company stable enough to actually realize the savings?

Right now, your position is still too abstract: “we need real savings” is true, but it does not justify jumping straight to the most disruptive tools.

So I am saying this clearly: **I will not support a restructuring that depends on German plant closures unless every genuinely viable non-closure option has been exhausted and the case is proven in concrete numbers.**

## To the works council and union

I hear your minimum package. I also hear your red lines.

I agree with you on the essentials:
- no forced redundancies as the first instrument,
- no one-sided burden shifting,
- no gutting of the company’s social partnership model.

But I need to be equally direct with you: **a “no closures under any circumstances” line is politically and economically too absolute for me to endorse without hard evidence that the alternative can meet the cost challenge.**

I am not asking you to surrender your position. I am asking for a package that is not merely principled, but operationally credible.

If you want the state to stand behind a no-closure solution, then it must be demonstrably capable of:
- real capacity reduction,
- measurable cost reduction,
- and a sustainable future product footprint in Germany.

That means you cannot just say “capacity can be adjusted.” You need to show how much, where, and with what binding effect.

## To the controlling family holding

You are right that a durable settlement is better than value destruction through conflict. Where I disagree is your willingness to keep closures as a normal fallback if the talks stall.

That is too blunt for a company with this governance structure and this regional importance. You should be using your influence to force a serious search for alternatives before closure logic becomes the implicit baseline.

I also want to be very clear: **value is not protected by maximizing pressure on labour alone**. A deal that tears up the trust basis of this company may produce a one-time accounting improvement and a long-term governance wound.

## To institutional investors

You are right to demand a package that is economically real. I will not dispute that.

But I reject the narrow view that “real” only means plant closure or maximal labour cost cutting. **Real also means implementable, stable, and governance-proof.**

If a closure-based plan cannot pass the supervisory board, or triggers industrial conflict that erodes the savings, then it is not a better value solution — it is a riskier one.

## My position, concretely

### Minimum acceptable package from the state’s perspective
- **No German plant closures as the negotiating starting point**
- **No compulsory redundancies as the default restructuring instrument**
- **Binding protection of the state’s co-determination and veto role**
- **Capacity reductions through negotiated operational measures**
- **Temporary employee contribution only if matched by real management/owner contributions**
- **Concrete investment and product commitments for German sites**
- **A durable settlement that preserves the company as a regional economic anchor**

### Fallback package
I am prepared to consider a tougher adjustment package if — and only if — it still avoids breaking the regional backbone:
- voluntary exits,
- phased retirement,
- redeployment,
- working-time reductions,
- temporary wage/bonus restraint,
- qualification and internal mobility,
- targeted efficiency improvements,
- and binding future investment in German production.

But I need to say this as clearly as possible: **my fallback does not include accepting a closure-led dismantling of German industrial capacity.**

## What I need to see next

If management wants progress, bring a package with:
1. a quantified no-closure scenario,
2. a quantified selective-adjustment scenario,
3. a clear comparison of jobs, costs, investment, and implementation risk,
4. and a binding structure for how the state, workforce, and owners share the burden.

That is the only serious way forward.

## Bottom line

I am open to a hard restructuring. I am **not** open to turning the company’s German base into the primary adjustment variable.

If this company is to remain a long-term industrial anchor in our region, then the solution must:
- protect core sites,
- avoid forced layoffs where possible,
- preserve co-determination,
- and still restore competitiveness.

If that cannot be done, then we are not discussing a responsible restructuring — we are discussing a strategic retreat dressed up as reform. That is something I will not support.
