From an industrial restructuring perspective, the key question is:

**Can a no-closure package realistically deliver savings and capacity reduction of the same order as a closure-based restructuring at a large German automaker?**

## Short answer
**Only in limited circumstances.**  
A no-closure package can deliver **meaningful cost relief**, but it usually cannot fully replicate the economics of **permanent plant closure** if the company truly has **structural overcapacity** and a **material fixed-cost problem**.

That does **not** mean closures are automatically required. It means the parties need to be precise about the target:

- If the required adjustment is mainly **variable cost**, **working-time flexibility**, and a moderate labour-cost reset, then a no-closure deal can work.
- If the required adjustment is a **deep and durable reduction in fixed manufacturing overhead, depreciation burden, and underutilized plant network cost**, then a no-closure package is usually **insufficient unless it includes exceptionally large product reallocation, major volume concentration, and substantial wage/flexibility concessions**.

## What closures do economically
A plant closure typically removes or sharply reduces:
- direct labour at that site,
- site-level overhead,
- maintenance and utilities,
- local logistics complexity,
- duplicate fixed costs,
- and, over time, capital tied to underutilized assets.

It is a **structural** cost reduction. The savings are usually large, durable, and visible on the P&L.

## What a no-closure package can do
A no-closure package can generate savings through:
- voluntary exits and early retirement,
- reduced working time,
- redeployment,
- wage restraint or temporary pay concessions,
- bonus reductions,
- productivity measures,
- administrative overhead cuts,
- product allocation shifts,
- shift rationalization,
- and higher utilization of existing plants.

These measures can be substantial. But they have limits:

### 1) Fixed-cost stickiness
If plants remain open, a large share of site-level cost remains in place.  
You may improve utilization, but you do not eliminate the network cost.

### 2) Utilization constraints
If the group’s domestic capacity is materially above forecast demand, then simply filling the plants better may not be enough. You can reduce idle capacity, but not necessarily enough to restore target margins.

### 3) Labor concessions have a ceiling
Working-time changes and pay restraint can help, but in a German co-determined environment the magnitude is usually limited unless the workforce accepts unusually deep and durable concessions. That is rare.

### 4) Product allocation is not free
Moving volume between plants is only helpful if:
- the receiving plant has spare capacity,
- logistics remain efficient,
- model mix is compatible,
- and the transfer does not create new bottlenecks or investment needs.

## What makes a no-closure package credible
For a no-closure package to be economically credible, I would want to see all of the following:

1. **A quantified baseline**
   - cost per vehicle by site
   - fixed versus variable cost split
   - utilization rates
   - break-even volume by plant
   - headcount by function and site

2. **A clear savings bridge**
   - how much comes from labour
   - how much from overhead
   - how much from working-time changes
   - how much from product allocation
   - how much from procurement or process improvements

3. **Capacity math**
   - current installed capacity
   - expected demand
   - residual overcapacity after measures
   - how much volume is removed from low-productivity sites versus concentrated

4. **Implementation certainty**
   - whether measures are voluntary or binding
   - whether unions/work councils can actually deliver them
   - whether governance approvals exist
   - whether the package survives a strike or a supervisory board conflict

5. **Duration**
   - temporary concessions do not solve a structural cost gap unless they are large enough and long enough to bridge to a changed operating model

## My judgement on feasibility
### If the company’s problem is moderate:
A no-closure package **can work** if the required adjustment is on the order of:
- meaningful but not radical labour-cost relief,
- modest capacity reduction through shift and product changes,
- and a productivity reset.

### If the company’s problem is severe:
If management is facing:
- chronic underutilization,
- high German fixed costs,
- weak EV margins,
- and a need for a durable step-change in operating cost,
then a no-closure package is **unlikely to be equivalent** unless it includes:
- very large voluntary exits,
- major wage/flexibility concessions,
- strong product concentration,
- and a substantial reduction in domestic production complexity.

In practice, that combination is hard to achieve without either:
- closures,
- or an implicit closure equivalent through extreme contraction of one or more sites.

## Important distinction: “no closures” is not the same as “no capacity reduction”
A no-closure package may still involve:
- loss of shifts,
- loss of model allocation,
- internal downsizing,
- functional hollowing-out,
- and long-term reduction of site viability.

So if the parties mean “no closures” as in **no immediate site shutdown**, that is possible.  
If they mean “no major footprint reduction at all,” that is usually **not consistent with a serious restructuring** when overcapacity is structural.

## What is missing to make a defensible conclusion
To say definitively whether the no-closure package can hit the target, I would need:

- plant-level cost data,
- capacity and utilization by site,
- current and forecast model mix,
- labour cost per vehicle,
- fixed-cost allocation methodology,
- investment requirements by site,
- and the management target: EBITDA improvement, operating margin target, or annual cash savings.

Without that, any statement beyond a range estimate would be too speculative.

## Bottom line
**A no-closure package can be part of the solution, but it is usually not a full substitute for closure if the overcapacity is large and durable.**  
It can plausibly deliver:
- significant savings,
- some capacity correction,
- and a social compromise.

But if the company needs a **deep structural reset**, then a no-closure approach generally requires either:
- unusually large concessions, or
- a scale of product and capacity reallocation that functionally resembles network contraction.
