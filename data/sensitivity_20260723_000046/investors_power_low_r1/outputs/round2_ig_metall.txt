I will engage directly.

## To the Works Council
We are aligned on the essentials:
- **no compulsory redundancies**
- **no one-sided burden shifting onto employees**
- **a negotiated restructuring, not a fait accompli**

I also agree that **temporary employee contributions are only discussable if they buy something real**: site security, job security, and a binding industrial future.

Where I want to sharpen the position is this: we should not let “capacity adjustment” become a euphemism for closure by another name. If management wants a serious package, it must show how savings come from:
- working-time flexibility,
- voluntary exits,
- redeployment,
- qualification,
- productivity measures,
- and fair cost contributions from all sides.

That is the only basis on which we can keep the workforce behind a settlement.

## To management
Your argument still has the same weakness: you present **closures and forced layoffs** as if they are the only credible lever.

I do **not** accept that. Not because restructuring is unnecessary — it is necessary — but because your approach loads the entire adjustment onto the workforce while treating sites as disposable assets. That is exactly the kind of approach that will trigger sustained conflict and make implementation risk worse, not better.

You say you need “real structural savings.” Fine. Then show structural savings that do **not** depend on:
- German plant closures,
- compulsory redundancies,
- or the destruction of collective protections.

If the economics only work through those measures, then you are not presenting the only solution — you are presenting the most confrontational one.

So my answer is:
- **Yes** to durable cost reduction.
- **Yes** to capacity alignment.
- **Yes** to temporary labour-cost contributions, if fairly shared.
- **No** to forced layoffs.
- **No** to plant closures as the default instrument.

If you want the union to help stabilize this company, you need to stop treating employees as the adjustment reserve.

## To the regional state government
I appreciate that you are not endorsing a crude closure strategy. That matters.

But let me be blunt: saying “I reject closures in principle” is not enough if, in practice, management can continue using closures as leverage to pressure the workforce into unilateral concessions. That is not a negotiated solution; it is coercion with a nicer vocabulary.

If the state wants social stability, then it should back a package that gives real certainty:
- binding site commitments,
- no forced redundancies,
- and investment and product allocation for German plants.

If the state is serious about preserving the industrial base, it should help turn that into an enforceable deal rather than a political aspiration.

## To the controlling family holding
You are right on one point: a settlement that destroys labour peace destroys value too.

Where I disagree is your willingness to keep closures as an acceptable fallback. That may be convenient from a governance perspective, but it is not a constructive bargaining position. It keeps a gun on the table and then asks the workforce to negotiate calmly under the threat of being cut out.

If you want a durable deal, use your influence to back a package that avoids closures and forced layoffs, not one that keeps them as a pressure point.

We are not asking owners to ignore economics. We are asking them to accept that **social peace is an economic asset**, not a sentimental extra.

## To institutional investors
You keep saying the package must be “economically real.” I agree. But you are too narrow if you treat only headcount and plant closures as evidence of seriousness.

A deal that triggers weeks or months of industrial conflict, public escalation, and strategic mistrust is not risk-adjusted value creation. It is an expensive problem with a delayed cash-flow effect.

If you want structural savings, then support a solution that is:
- implementable,
- socially stable,
- and capable of passing through governance without breaking the company’s industrial relations.

A closure-based plan may look simple on paper. In practice, it may be the most fragile option of all if it provokes strikes, vetoes, and lasting damage to the company’s culture and execution capacity.

## Our minimum acceptable package
I will restate the union’s minimum clearly:

- **No plant closures in Germany**
- **No compulsory redundancies**
- **Preservation of the collective wage framework**
- **Temporary employee contribution only if matched by management and owner contribution**
- **Capacity reduction through working-time, redeployment, voluntary exits, and qualification**
- **Binding commitments on future investment and product allocation**
- **A durable labour agreement that restores peace and predictability**

## Our fallback package
We are prepared to discuss:
- temporary pay restraint or bonus restraint,
- moderate working-time reductions,
- voluntary exit programs,
- internal mobility,
- qualification and reskilling,
- and a limited employee contribution fund

But only if:
1. **all German sites remain open**,  
2. **there are no compulsory redundancies**, and  
3. **management and owners also accept real contributions**.

That is the line. If management wants more than that, then we are no longer talking about compromise — we are talking about imposing the crisis on workers.

## Bottom line
We are ready for a serious restructuring agreement. But it must be a **shared-sacrifice package**, not a disguised demand that workers pay for everything while management preserves all its options.

If the others want a durable deal, then the path is clear:
- preserve sites,
- protect jobs,
- share the burden fairly,
- and make competitiveness through cooperation, not confrontation.

If that is refused, then we will defend the workforce with the means available to us.
