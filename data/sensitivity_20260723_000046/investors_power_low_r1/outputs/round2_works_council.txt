Let me respond point by point, because this is not a rhetorical exercise — it is about real jobs, real sites, and real commitments.

## To management
You say the status quo is not an option. I agree on one thing: **doing nothing is not acceptable**. But your conclusion that therefore German plants must be put on the chopping block is **not** something I accept.

You are still framing the crisis as if the only serious solution is to destroy capacity and threaten livelihoods. That is exactly the kind of logic that triggers conflict and destroys trust. We will not accept a restructuring that starts from closures and layoffs and then asks the workforce to “understand.”

If you want to talk about a real package, then talk about:
- **capacity adjustment without closures**
- **working-time flexibility**
- **redeployment and qualification**
- **voluntary instruments only**
- **product allocation and investment commitments**
- **temporary, fair employee contributions**
- **management and owners contributing as well**

What I do **not** accept:
- plant closures in Germany,
- compulsory redundancies,
- or the dismantling of the job-security framework.

If your numbers only work by cutting into those red lines, then the numbers are not the only issue — **your restructuring concept is wrong**.

## To the union
We are aligned on the essentials. The workforce must not be made to pay for management’s strategic failures alone. I support the union’s position that any burden must be shared fairly and that industrial action remains on the table if we are handed a fait accompli.

But I want to be very precise: **temporary concessions from employees are only discussable if they are matched by binding guarantees**. Not vague promises. Not press releases. Binding commitments on:
- all German sites remaining open,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- and a genuine role for co-determination in future industrial planning.

Without that, there is nothing to negotiate.

## To the regional state government
I appreciate your clear stance that German sites are not disposable. That is correct. But let me be equally clear: support for “a negotiated solution” is not enough if management is still pushing a closure logic behind closed doors.

If the state wants a socially stable and economically sound solution, then it must use its influence to stop the escalation before it becomes irreversible. The state cannot simply say it rejects closures in principle while allowing management to keep them as a permanent threat in negotiations.

We need **binding site guarantees**, not just political concern.

## To the controlling family holding
You say you support restructuring, but not value destruction. On that point, we agree. A company that tears up its social contract and provokes long industrial conflict destroys value too.

But I reject the idea that “if no equivalent solution is available, closures may be necessary” as a legitimate starting point. That is management’s language, not a social partnership approach. The company has alternatives:
- better utilization of existing capacity,
- shifting product allocation,
- delayed or redesigned investments,
- voluntary reductions,
- internal mobility,
- shorter working time,
- and fair temporary contributions.

If the owners want a durable settlement, they should back a solution that protects the German industrial base instead of treating closure as an acceptable pressure tool.

## To institutional investors
You are very focused on “structural savings.” So are we — but not at any price. A settlement that destroys labour peace, ignores co-determination, and breaks the job-security framework will not be structurally sound. It will just create the next crisis faster.

Your test should not be only:
1. cost reduction,
2. margin improvement,
3. speed.

It must also be:
4. implementation risk,
5. industrial peace,
6. long-term site viability.

A closure-based solution may look clean in a spreadsheet. In reality, it risks massive conflict, reputational damage, and a weakened company culture. That is not “risk-adjusted value creation” — that is short-termism.

## Our minimum acceptable package
This is the minimum we can even discuss:

- **No plant closures in Germany**
- **No compulsory redundancies**
- **Binding site guarantees through 2030**
- **Capacity reductions through reallocation, working-time measures, and voluntary instruments**
- **A negotiated, temporary employee contribution only in exchange for binding commitments**
- **Concrete investment and product allocation promises for German sites**
- **Strengthened co-determination in the restructuring implementation**

## Our fallback package
If management insists on deeper savings, then the only fallback is still within the same red lines:
- temporary wage or bonus restraint,
- shorter working time,
- voluntary early retirement and exit programs,
- internal redeployment,
- training and qualification,
- and targeted efficiency measures.

But let me be absolutely clear: **there is no fallback for closures or forced redundancies**. That is where my mandate ends.

## The testable comparison
### 1) No-closure restructuring
This can work if management and owners are serious about:
- reallocating production,
- reducing costs by process and footprint optimization,
- using voluntary instruments,
- and securing future model allocation in Germany.

That is the only route that preserves social peace and governance stability.

### 2) Selective capacity-adjustment restructuring with closures
I reject this as a path in Germany. It may be what management prefers, but it will face resistance from us, from the union, and from the governance structures that make unilateral action impossible. It is not a realistic “solution” if it cannot pass.

So I am not moving from the core position. If management wants a deal, it must come back with a package that keeps **every German plant open** and protects employees from forced exits. Anything else is a confrontation, not a settlement.
