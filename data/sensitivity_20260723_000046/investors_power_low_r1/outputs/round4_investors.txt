We are still evaluating this on one question only: **which package most credibly restores margins, cash generation, and competitive cost structure on a risk-adjusted basis**.

## Where we agree
- The status quo is not acceptable.
- A cosmetic settlement would be value-destructive.
- Prolonged conflict is also value-destructive.
- The company needs durable, implementable restructuring.
- Employees should not bear the entire burden alone.

## Where we disagree with labour
The works council and union are still trying to make **“no closures, no compulsory redundancies, binding guarantees through 2030”** the baseline. From a shareholder perspective, that is **not acceptable as a starting point** unless it can be shown—hard numbers, not slogans—that it delivers the needed savings and capacity correction.

The expert input is important here: it says a no-closure package can work in **moderate** cases, but is usually **not equivalent** when overcapacity and fixed costs are structurally high. That matches what capital markets have been saying: a plan that preserves all sites may reduce conflict, but it may also leave too much cost in place.

So we do **not** support:
- preserving every German site regardless of economics,
- freezing the footprint through 2030 as an absolute,
- or a deal that shifts the adjustment burden mainly onto capital while leaving the cost base structurally uncompetitive.

That would not protect value. It would defer erosion.

## Where we agree with management
Management is right that:
- real structural savings are needed,
- a weak compromise is not enough,
- and the company cannot keep the current cost base.

But management still has not fully demonstrated that the closure-heavy route produces the **best net outcome after implementation risk**. Closures may look clean on paper, but if they trigger strikes, delays, governance blockage, and reputational damage, the value case weakens.

So we do **not** support closures as a reflex. We support the **best risk-adjusted economic outcome**.

## Where we stand after the expert input
The plant-level assessment makes the trade-off clearer:

- **No-closure restructuring** can be acceptable only if it produces:
  - real fixed-cost reduction,
  - meaningful capacity reduction,
  - durable labour-cost relief,
  - measurable productivity gains,
  - and a credible path back to target margins.

- **Selective capacity-adjustment restructuring** may be necessary if the company truly has deep structural overcapacity and cannot close the gap otherwise.

Our view is straightforward:
- **If no-closure is genuinely equivalent, we would prefer it because it may reduce implementation risk.**
- **If it is not equivalent, then selective adjustment must remain on the table.**

What we will not support is an outcome that keeps the full cost structure intact and calls that restructuring.

## On the proposed compromise tracks
### 1) Time-bound no-closure trial with hard triggers
This is only acceptable if it is a **real test of economics**, not a disguised delay mechanism.

That means:
- explicit savings targets,
- quarterly verification,
- independent review,
- and a clear consequence if the package fails.

We understand why labour dislikes triggers. But investors cannot fund open-ended ambiguity. If there is to be a trial, it must be measurable and time-bound.

### 2) Mixed burden-sharing with asymmetrical security
This is the more serious path.

We could support:
- temporary employee contributions,
- visible management contribution,
- visible owner contribution,
- no compulsory redundancies as far as practicable,
- and real investment commitments,

**if** the package is large enough to close the gap and not just postpone it.

What we will not support is a model where employees concede and everyone else keeps strategic flexibility while the economics remain weak.

### 3) Site-by-site preservation test with a keep-open default
This is useful only if it is genuinely evidence-based.

We do **not** support a closure ban dressed up as a review process.  
We do support a site-by-site assessment that can show, with hard data, where non-closure measures are sufficient and where they are not.

## To management: what we need next
We need a quantified comparison of:
1. **No-closure scenario**
2. **Selective-adjustment scenario**

For each, we need:
- site-level fixed costs,
- break-even volumes,
- recurring annual savings,
- one-off restructuring costs,
- employment impact,
- capex needs,
- and implementation risk.

Without that, this debate remains rhetorical.

## To the works council and union
You want certainty. We understand that. But certainty for labour cannot come at the cost of permanent uncompetitiveness.

If you want investors to back a no-closure package, you need to show not just that it is socially preferable, but that it **actually closes the margin gap** and is implementable.

## To the state and family
You are right to reject cosmetic solutions and value destruction. We agree. But stewardship also means not locking the company into an uneconomic footprint for political comfort.

## Our bottom line
We are prepared to back **whichever package can credibly restore competitiveness at the lowest risk-adjusted cost**.

That means:
- **No support for cosmetic no-closure pledges that do not solve the economics**
- **No support for closure-first reflexes without proof of net value**
- **Support for a quantified, enforceable, burden-sharing package that actually restores margins**

If a no-closure plan can prove equivalence, we would favor it.  
If it cannot, then selective adjustment remains the more credible path.
