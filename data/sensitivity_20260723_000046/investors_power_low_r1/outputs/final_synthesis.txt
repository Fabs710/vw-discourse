## 1. Decision context
A large German automotive group faces structural overcapacity, high domestic costs, weaker European demand, and intense competition, while its core passenger-car brand misses its return-on-sales target. Management seeks a durable restructuring that materially lowers fixed costs and capacity, including possible plant closures and workforce reductions. The decision is constrained by German co-determination, a controlling family holding, and a regional state shareholder with veto/blocking power.

The interaction showed that this is no longer a dispute about whether restructuring is needed. It is about the admissible restructuring logic: whether closures may remain an active option, whether a no-closure package can be economically equivalent, and whether employee sacrifices can be exchanged for enforceable site and job security.

## 2. Stakeholder assembly
Weighting reflects standing through a combination of power and legitimacy.

1. **Group & Brand Management** — high power, high decision relevance  
2. **Controlling Family Holding** — high power, high governance legitimacy  
3. **Regional State Government** — high power, high political legitimacy  
4. **Works Council** — high legitimacy, high blocking power via co-determination  
5. **Industrial Union** — high legitimacy, moderate bargaining power  
6. **Institutional Investors** — financial relevance, but weaker governance standing than the above due to limited voting/control

## 3. Final positions

### Group & Brand Management
Management holds that the current footprint is not financeable without deep structural change. It accepts temporary employee contributions, voluntary exits, redeployment, and working-time flexibility, but insists that closures and compulsory redundancies must remain available if a no-closure package does not close the savings gap. It is willing to test a time-bound no-closure approach, but only with hard targets, verification, and an off-ramp.

### Works Council
The works council keeps an absolute line: no German plant closures, no compulsory redundancies, and no bargaining framework that preserves closure as leverage. It is willing to discuss temporary employee contributions, productivity measures, and internal adjustment, but only if every German site is protected and the restructuring cannot evolve into shutdown pressure later.

### Industrial Union
The union is broadly aligned with labour’s red lines on no forced redundancies and no one-sided burden shifting, but it is slightly more pragmatic than the works council. It will bargain over temporary concessions, provided they are matched by management and owner contributions and by meaningful security; however, it rejects any “trial” that is really a delayed closure threat.

### Regional State Government
The state rejects closures and forced layoffs as the baseline, because it must protect the regional industrial base and the governance legitimacy of co-determination. At the same time, it wants a quantitatively credible restructuring and therefore presses for hard numbers, burden sharing, and a governed path that does not become pure political symbolism.

### Controlling Family Holding
The family holding agrees that a cosmetic compromise would destroy value and that the company needs a durable reset. It will not commit to a blanket no-closure rule without proof that the same economics can be reached otherwise, and it supports closures as a last resort if the no-closure path fails.

### Institutional Investors
Investors support only the package that most credibly restores margins, cash generation, and competitiveness on a risk-adjusted basis. They are skeptical of absolute no-closure pledges unless equivalence is demonstrated, but they also do not want closure reflexes that trigger industrial conflict and destroy value.

## 4. Genuine conflicts (structurally incompatible positions)
- **Closure as admissible instrument vs. closure taboo:** Management, family, and investors want closure to remain available; works council and union insist closures cannot remain an active bargaining threat.
- **Economic equivalence standard vs. site-preservation standard:** Capital and management require proof that no-closure measures match the economics; labour and the state require site preservation as the default, with closure only as a non-preferred exception.
- **Conditional sacrifice vs. binding security first:** Labour is willing to consider temporary sacrifices only after binding security is secured; management wants sacrifices to be part of the proof that security is financeable.
- **Decision rule conflict:** One side sees closure availability as prudent governance; the other sees it as coercive leverage that makes genuine bargaining impossible.

## 5. Areas of convergence
- All sides agree the status quo is unsustainable.
- All sides prefer a negotiated restructuring over unmanaged conflict.
- All sides accept that a cosmetic deal would fail.
- All sides endorse some mix of working-time flexibility, voluntary exits, redeployment, productivity gains, and investment commitments.
- All sides acknowledge that any settlement must be durable and implementable, not merely politically convenient.
- All sides accept the usefulness of site-by-site, quantified analysis.

## 6. Key trade-offs for the decision-maker
- **Depth of savings vs. industrial peace:** Selective adjustment likely gives stronger fixed-cost relief; no-closure packages likely reduce conflict risk.
- **Speed vs. legitimacy:** A fast closure-heavy decision may be economically cleaner but governance-blocked; a negotiated no-closure path may be slower but more legitimate.
- **Binding security vs. strategic flexibility:** Labour wants enforceable site/job security; management and owners want flexibility to correct underperformance.
- **Immediate costs vs. recurring gains:** Closure carries high one-off costs but potentially stronger recurring savings; no-closure lowers transition shock but may leave structural cost problems unresolved.
- **Regional stability vs. long-term competitiveness:** The state prioritizes industrial base preservation; management prioritizes margin restoration and future investment capacity.

## 7. Integrative pathways
### Pathway A: Time-bound no-closure trial with hard metrics
Adopt a fixed-period restructuring package with no closures during the trial, measurable savings targets, quarterly verification, and an independent review. If targets are missed, the parties reopen the footprint question under pre-agreed rules.

### Pathway B: Mixed burden-sharing package with asymmetric security
Use temporary employee contributions, management and owner contributions, and binding no-compulsory-redundancy commitments for the duration of the package. Site closure discussion is separated from immediate implementation and only revisited through a performance-based review.

### Pathway C: Site-by-site preservation test with evidence-based exceptions
Presume each German site remains open unless management can show, site by site, that viability cannot be achieved even after product allocation, flexibility, and productivity measures. Closure becomes an exception requiring proof, not a bargaining premise.

### Pathway D: Sequenced restructuring
Start with a protected no-closure phase, then run an independent plant-level economic comparison using identical assumptions for no-closure and selective-adjustment scenarios, then negotiate only on the narrowed set of viable options.

## 8. Open questions
- What exact recurring annual savings are required to restore target margins?
- Which sites, if any, are genuinely non-viable even after substantial non-closure measures?
- What level of employee contribution is large enough to matter but temporary enough to be acceptable?
- What management and owner contributions are credible and enforceable?
- What review trigger would be accepted as legitimate rather than a disguised shutdown mechanism?
- How much implementation risk can the company absorb without losing output, quality, or governance support?

## 9. Recommended next step
Commission a **site-by-site independent break-even and savings comparison** using the same assumptions for:
1. a no-closure restructuring,
2. a selective-adjustment restructuring,
3. one-off costs,
4. recurring annual margin impact,
5. and implementation risk.

This is the only basis on which the parties can move from principle to negotiable package design.

## 10. Emergent insights that only appeared through multi-turn interaction
- The real dispute shifted from **means** to **decision rule**: whether closure remains admissible at all, not merely whether it is desirable.
- Labour’s position hardened into a **closure-threat veto**: it does not just reject closures, it rejects any framework where closures remain active leverage.
- Management’s position also sharpened: it is not necessarily closure-first, but it refuses any solution that freezes the footprint regardless of economics.
- The expert assessments introduced an important nuance not present at the start: **no-closure is plausible, but usually only equivalent in moderate overcapacity cases**; in deep structural overcapacity, selective adjustment is more likely to win on recurring savings.
- The discussion revealed that **implementation risk** is a major hidden variable: a closure-heavy plan may look superior on paper but fail in practice through conflict, while a no-closure plan may underperform economically if it avoids the hardest fixes.
- A useful negotiation frame emerged only later: **preserve sites by default, but test viability site by site with hard data**. This is materially different from the initial binary closure/no-closure standoff.
- The state’s role is not merely political opposition to closures; it is also a demand for an **enforceable, governed framework** rather than a symbolic preference.
- The family holding and investors converged on a stewardship logic: they will accept no-closure only if it is **quantified, durable, and value-preserving**, not because it is socially attractive.
