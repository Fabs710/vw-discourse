I appreciate the clarity from the works council and the union, but I need to be equally clear: **your minimum packages do not solve the actual problem**.

## To the Works Council
I agree with you on one point: doing nothing is not an option. But I **do not** agree that the only acceptable answer is to keep **every German plant open**, preserve all guarantees, and rely on working-time measures and redeployment alone.

That is not a restructuring. That is a postponement.

You keep saying capacity can be adjusted without closures. I am asking a simple question: **where, exactly, do the required fixed-cost reductions come from, and in what amount?** If the answer is only:
- temporary wage restraint,
- voluntary exits,
- internal mobility,
- and process optimization,

then I am telling you plainly: **that is not enough** to restore competitiveness at the scale we need.

I am willing to discuss:
- working-time flexibility,
- redeployment,
- qualification,
- voluntary instruments,
- and targeted investment commitments,

but only if they produce **real structural savings**. Not just a softer headline. Not just a deferral. Not a package that leaves us back here in two years with the same cost base and less credibility.

So I reject your “no closures under any circumstances” line. If a site is structurally uncompetitive, management has to retain the ability to act. I am not committing to closures as the first choice, but I am also **not ruling them out in advance**.

## To the Union
You say “no forced redundancies” and “no plant closures” are the line. I understand that is your negotiating position. But it is not a viable business solution if it prevents the company from making a durable adjustment.

I agree that the burden cannot fall only on employees. I have said that already. But I **disagree** with your implication that because industrial conflict is costly, we should therefore avoid the hard measures that create the savings. Conflict is costly — yes. But so is preserving an uncompetitive cost structure and watching margins continue to erode.

You ask me to show structural savings without closures or compulsory redundancies. Fine: if you can put on the table a package that **mathematically matches the required cost reduction**, I will engage seriously. But right now, what you are offering is a list of instruments, not a complete answer.

And let me be direct: **social peace is not a substitute for competitiveness**. It matters, but it does not fund future products, battery technology, software, or model renewal.

## To the Regional State Government
I respect your governance role, and I agree that this has to be politically and socially manageable. But I do **not** accept the idea that because closures are difficult, they should effectively be ruled out by political pressure.

If the state’s position is “no closures in principle,” then it is not balancing interests — it is pre-judging the outcome. That would leave management with no credible leverage to secure the savings the company needs.

I also need to be frank about the limits of your approach: if we protect every site regardless of economics, we risk turning a competitiveness crisis into a prolonged decline. That would not preserve the regional industrial base; it would weaken it over time.

What I can support is:
- a negotiated package,
- social mitigation,
- redeployment,
- voluntary reductions,
- and investment commitments,

but only if it also delivers **meaningful footprint and cost adjustment**. Otherwise it is not a restructuring, it is political delay.

## To the Controlling Family Holding
I agree with you on the need for a durable settlement and on the fact that a prolonged conflict would destroy value. That is exactly why I am pressing for a solution that is structurally sufficient.

Where I disagree is your reluctance to confront the scale of the adjustment required. You say you support hard decisions, but you also want labour peace. Those goals are compatible only if the package is economically real.

A compromise that avoids all closures and all compulsory reductions may sound governance-friendly, but if it does not close the gap on costs and capacity, it will simply postpone the next crisis and force another round of restructuring later. That is not stewardship either.

So yes: I want a negotiated settlement. But no: I will not endorse a settlement that preserves the cost base and calls it a solution.

## To Institutional Investors
You are right to focus on risk-adjusted value. On that point, we agree more than the labour side seems to want to admit.

Where I disagree is with the idea that implementation risk automatically argues against hard measures. Sometimes the bigger risk is **not acting decisively enough**. A deal that looks gentler but leaves the economics broken can be the most expensive outcome of all.

I also reject the suggestion that management is seeking confrontation for its own sake. We are seeking a durable fix. If that requires difficult measures, then so be it. The real danger to shareholder value is not insisting on structural change — it is accepting a cosmetic compromise that fails to restore margins.

## My position, stated plainly
Here is what management is prepared to pursue:
- **Meaningful capacity reduction**
- **Substantial labour-cost relief**
- **Voluntary exits and redeployment where possible**
- **Working-time and flexibility measures**
- **Targeted investment commitments tied to restructuring**
- **A negotiated settlement that is executable and durable**

Here is what management cannot accept:
- **A blanket ban on closures**
- **A blanket ban on compulsory redundancies**
- **Long-term guarantees that freeze the cost base through 2030 regardless of market reality**
- **A package that is socially framed as a compromise but economically functions as delay**

## On the testable comparison
### 1) No-closure restructuring
I will only consider this if it delivers:
- equivalent capacity reduction,
- equivalent fixed-cost reduction,
- measurable labour-cost contribution,
- and a credible margin improvement sufficient to restore competitiveness.

If those conditions are met, I am open to it. If not, it is not equivalent.

### 2) Selective capacity-adjustment restructuring with closures
This remains on the table because I cannot rule out the measures necessary to fix the business. I would prefer a negotiated solution that minimizes disruption, but I will not withdraw the option simply because it is uncomfortable.

## Bottom line
I am not moved by general appeals to social partnership if the economic result is inadequate.

**We need structural savings, lower costs, and lower capacity.**  
**We need a settlement that restores margins and frees funds for 2030 investment.**  
**We do not need a cosmetic deal that preserves the old structure under a new name.**

If the other side wants a no-closure solution, then bring forward a package that genuinely equals the economic effect of closures and workforce reduction. Until then, management cannot and will not pretend that preservation equals competitiveness.
