We agree on the diagnosis more than the labour side wants to admit: **the current cost base is not competitive, and a cosmetic settlement is not acceptable**. The market will not reward a deal that preserves structural underperformance, kicks the can down the road, or leaves margins impaired.

That said, we also agree with the family holding and, to a lesser extent, the state on one critical point: **a destructive confrontation destroys value too**. Prolonged strikes, governance paralysis, and public escalation can wipe out whatever savings management hopes to extract. So the question is not whether restructuring is needed — it clearly is — but whether the package is economically real and executable.

## Where we agree
- **Status quo is unacceptable.**
- **Structural savings are required.**
- **A temporary, negotiated solution is better than unmanaged conflict.**
- **Any package must be durable and governance-proof.**
- **Employees should not bear the entire burden alone.**

## Where we disagree with the workforce side
The works council and union keep repeating “no closures, no compulsory redundancies, no rollback of protections” as if that were sufficient. It is not.

That position may be understandable politically, but financially it is only acceptable if it still delivers:
- materially lower fixed costs,
- real capacity reduction,
- and a credible return to target margins.

If it does not, then it amounts to asking shareholders to tolerate permanent underperformance in order to preserve legacy structures. We will not support that.

Also, “social peace” is not a substitute for competitiveness. A peaceful, uncompetitive company is still destroying shareholder value, just more quietly.

## Where we disagree with management
Management is right to insist that real savings are needed, but it still has to prove that the chosen toolset is not just the most confrontational one. If the company pushes too hard into closures and forced reductions, the implementation risk may outweigh the savings.

So we are not giving management a blank cheque for escalation. We want **credible economics plus credible execution**.

## Where we agree with the family and state
The governance reality is not optional. If there are blocking rights, then the only valuable plan is one that can pass. A technically elegant plan that cannot be implemented is worthless. We also agree that an approach that permanently damages trust and labour relations can be value-destructive.

## Our position on the two scenarios

### 1) No-closure restructuring
We would support this **only if it is quantitatively equivalent** to a harder plan.

That means:
- clear, measurable capacity reductions,
- substantial and durable fixed-cost reductions,
- labour-cost relief that is real, not symbolic,
- voluntary exits and redeployment at sufficient scale,
- and firm implementation milestones.

If those conditions are met, a no-closure deal could be attractive because it lowers implementation risk.

But if “no closures” just means softer cuts and delayed pain, then it is not a solution. It is a delay strategy, and we will not support it.

### 2) Selective capacity-adjustment restructuring
We would support selective closures or footprint reduction **if necessary** to deliver the required savings and if the process is controlled, time-bound, and governance-feasible.

That is not a preference for closures. It is a recognition that shareholders should not be forced to fund uncompetitive capacity indefinitely.

But closures only help if they are:
- economically justified,
- limited to what is required,
- and not so conflict-heavy that they erase the benefit.

## What would move us
We need a package with:
1. **A quantified path to competitive margins**
2. **A durable fixed-cost reduction**
3. **A credible capacity correction**
4. **Shared sacrifice, not just employee sacrifice**
5. **Low implementation risk relative to the savings**
6. **A clear line of sight to reinvestment and future product funding**

## What would not move us
- A settlement that preserves all sites and all structures while calling it restructuring
- Open-ended guarantees that freeze the cost base
- A prolonged industrial conflict that burns cash and credibility
- Any plan that leaves the company structurally unable to hit margin targets

## Bottom line
We are open to either route — no-closure or selective adjustment — **but only if the economics are real**. The deciding question is not who sounds most socially responsible. It is whether the package restores competitiveness fast enough to protect equity value.

If the next round cannot show a quantified, credible comparison between the two options, then management and the other stakeholders are still operating in rhetoric, not restructuring.
