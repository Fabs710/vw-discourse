I will be direct.

I agree with several points made by management, the investors, and the experts: the status quo is not sustainable, a cosmetic deal would be value-destructive, and a durable restructuring must actually improve margins and competitiveness. I also agree with the state that conflict and governance blockage would be costly. That is exactly why I do **not** support an outcome that freezes the company into an uncompetitive structure.

Where I disagree is with the labour side’s insistence that **no closures and no compulsory redundancies** must be the starting premise, and that closures should be removed as a negotiating instrument entirely. From an ownership and stewardship perspective, that is not a credible governance position unless it is proven that the same economics can be achieved without them. So far, that proof has not been delivered.

The independent assessments were useful. They confirmed what I suspected: a no-closure package can help, but it is usually **not equivalent** if the overcapacity and fixed-cost problem is structural. That matters. It means I cannot responsibly commit the family holding to a blanket no-closure line, because doing so could lock in value erosion and simply postpone a harder correction.

## To the Works Council
I respect the focus on labour peace and the concern about irreversible decisions. But your position still treats all closures as unacceptable by definition. I cannot accept that as a governance principle.

You say closures are just a choice, not an economic law. True. But the same is true of keeping all sites open. The question is whether the alternative can actually deliver the needed savings and capacity reduction. At present, you have not shown that it can at the required scale.

What I agree with:
- doing nothing is not an option,
- forced redundancies should not be the first instrument,
- voluntary exits, redeployment, qualification, working-time flexibility, and temporary employee contributions should be used first,
- management and ownership should also contribute visibly.

What I do **not** accept:
- a permanent ban on closures,
- binding site guarantees through 2030 regardless of economics,
- or a structure where capital is expected to fund uncompetitive capacity indefinitely.

If you want serious support for a no-closure solution, then bring a quantified bridge:
1. exact savings by measure,
2. exact capacity reduction,
3. exact product and volume reallocation,
4. and a credible path back to target margins.

Without that, your position is a preference, not a solution.

## To the Industrial Union
You are more balanced than the works council, and I appreciate that. You accept that competitiveness matters and that a cosmetic compromise is not enough. That is the right starting point.

But I disagree with any attempt to turn “no forced redundancies” into an implicit veto on deeper structural change. If the company truly has a fixed-cost and overcapacity problem, then the burden has to be large enough to matter.

What I can support:
- temporary employee contributions,
- management and owner contributions,
- no forced redundancies as far as possible,
- strong co-determination in implementation,
- and a serious test of a no-closure package.

What I cannot support:
- concessions that are too small to close the gap,
- or an arrangement where closures are politically taboo but no one is willing to specify how the economics work.

## To Group & Brand Management
I agree with you on the need for a structural reset. I also agree that a weak compromise would fail.

Where I push back is on the tendency to speak as if closures are already the answer before the alternatives have been tested in sufficient detail. The expert input does not give management a blank cheque. It says: if the no-closure route is to be credible, it must be quantified, durable, and large enough.

That is exactly the standard I would insist on as controlling shareholder.

So my message to management is:
- produce the plant-level economics,
- quantify the savings bridge,
- quantify the implementation risk,
- and show where a no-closure route genuinely fails.

If you can show that, then closures remain a last resort, not a first move. If you cannot, then you are asking owners to accept unnecessary destruction.

## To the Regional State Government
I understand your political and regional responsibility. I agree that a destructive conflict would damage the company and the region.

But I do not accept the idea that the state can effectively pre-rule out closures as a baseline principle. That would turn a governance actor into an economic veto-holder over necessary adaptation.

What I can support:
- a no-closure-first test,
- no forced redundancies as a default,
- binding burden sharing,
- and strong investment commitments for the German footprint.

What I cannot support:
- a political line that prevents the company from taking structurally necessary action if the numbers demand it.

The state should use its leverage to force proof, not to impose an outcome before the evidence is in.

## To Institutional Investors
You are right to insist that the package be economically real and executable. I support that. I also share your concern that a cosmetic compromise would destroy value.

Where I would caution you is against assuming that closure automatically equals discipline and value creation. It does not, if it triggers strikes, governance deadlock, and lasting trust erosion.

I support the best risk-adjusted outcome, not the most aggressive one. That means:
- if a no-closure package can truly restore competitiveness, I am open to it;
- if it cannot, then selective adjustment must remain available.

That is not indecision. That is stewardship.

## On the expert findings
The experts were helpful because they narrowed the real issue:
- a no-closure package can work in a moderate case,
- it is usually not equivalent in a severe structural overcapacity case,
- and if it is to work, it needs substantial, durable measures and a credible implementation path.

That confirms my position: I will **not** support a blanket no-closure commitment. But I also will **not** support closures as the first answer.

The correct order is:
1. prove the no-closure package,
2. test whether it reaches the required economics,
3. only then consider harder structural measures if it fails.

## My position on the compromise tracks

### 1) Time-bound no-closure trial with hard triggers
I can consider a time-bound trial only if it is genuinely a test of economic equivalence, not a delayed shutdown mechanism.

That means:
- explicit savings targets,
- quarterly verification,
- independent review,
- and predefined consequences if the package fails.

I know labour dislikes that. But from an ownership perspective, an open-ended promise is not enough. If the trial is serious, it must be measurable.

### 2) Mixed burden-sharing with asymmetrical security
This is the most promising direction, but only if the security is real and the burden-sharing is real.

I would support:
- temporary employee concessions,
- management contribution,
- owner contribution,
- no forced redundancies as a principle for the period,
- and meaningful investment commitments.

But I will not support a model where workers pay while management and owners keep all strategic flexibility and the economics never improve.

### 3) Site-by-site preservation test with a keep-open default
I support a site-by-site test if it is evidence-based and not ideologically preset.

I do **not** support a closure ban disguised as a “keep-open default.”  
If the default is too rigid, then it becomes a political instrument rather than an economic review.

## What would actually move me
Only a credible package with:
- site-level fixed cost data,
- break-even volumes,
- quantified savings from non-closure measures,
- quantified savings from selective adjustment,
- implementation risk analysis,
- and a burden-sharing formula that is not one-sided.

Until then, my position remains:
- no closure-only dogma,
- no forced redundancy-first logic,
- but also no permanent immunity from structural change.

## Bottom line
I am prepared to back a **time-limited, quantified no-closure restructuring** if it can prove that it restores competitiveness and margins at the necessary scale.

I am **not** prepared to back an absolute ban on closures or a commitment to keep every German plant open regardless of economics.

That is the line. It is not about cruelty or ideology. It is about protecting the long-term value of the company, the family holding’s core asset, and avoiding a slow-motion erosion of competitiveness that would hurt everyone in the end.
