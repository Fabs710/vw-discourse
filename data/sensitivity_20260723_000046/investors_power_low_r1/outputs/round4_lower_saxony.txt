## My position remains unchanged
I **do not accept German plant closures or compulsory redundancies as the baseline for restructuring**.

That is not a symbolic preference. It is a governance and regional-policy line. The company is a regional economic anchor, and the state is not here to rubber-stamp an adjustment strategy that destroys jobs, weakens the industrial base, and turns our co-determination role into decoration.

At the same time, I do **not** support inertia or denial. The company needs a real restructuring. But it must be one that preserves the German footprint as far as economically possible, shares the burden fairly, and remains consistent with the company’s special governance structure.

## To management
I agree with you on one thing: the status quo is not sustainable.

I **do not** agree that the only serious route is to keep closures and forced redundancies available as leverage. The independent assessments did not prove that closures are necessary; they proved only that a no-closure package must be **more disciplined, more quantified, and more binding** than vague assurances.

That matters.

What you still have not shown me is:
- a **plant-by-plant** savings bridge,
- a credible **no-closure scenario** with real capacity correction,
- the extent of **management and owner contribution**,
- and why a negotiated restructuring with no forced layoffs cannot deliver the needed result.

You say closures must remain available if the economics demand it. I do not accept that as a negotiating starting point. It is exactly what destabilizes the process and escalates conflict. If management wants a settlement, it must stop treating closures as the default instrument and present a **serious non-closure package** in hard numbers.

## To the Works Council and Union
I agree with you on the essentials:
- no compulsory redundancies,
- no one-sided burden shifting,
- no treating the workforce as the adjustment reserve,
- and no cosmetic deal.

You are right to insist that employees should only contribute temporarily if they receive something real in return.

That said, the one thing I will not accept is a bargaining architecture where closures remain an active threat over the workforce. The “trial with triggers” idea becomes unacceptable if it is just a delayed closure weapon. I share your concern there.

## To the controlling family holding
I agree with you that a cosmetic compromise would be harmful.

I disagree with the idea that stewardship requires keeping closures permanently available as discipline. Stewardship also means recognizing the company’s social legitimacy and regional role. If you insist on preserving long-term value, then you should back a solution that protects the core German footprint unless and until it is proven impossible to do so.

So I ask you directly:
- Will you back a **time-limited no-closure restructuring** if it is quantified and binding?
- Will you support a package that makes management and owners visibly share the burden?
- Will you accept a solution that prioritizes site preservation and only revisits footprint questions if a serious independent review shows failure?

That is a more legitimate test than keeping closure leverage alive from day one.

## To institutional investors
I understand your insistence that the package be economically real.

But you are too quick to equate “real” with “closure-capable.” A deal is economically real if it:
- materially reduces costs,
- improves utilization,
- preserves execution capability,
- avoids destructive industrial conflict,
- and delivers a durable margin path.

A closure-heavy approach may look neat in financial models, but it can also destroy value through strikes, governance blockage, reputational damage, and long-term mistrust. That risk is not a footnote; it is central.

So I will not support a package that sacrifices German sites simply because that is the cleanest spreadsheet answer.

## On the expert input
The experts were useful because they clarified the actual issue:
- a no-closure package is harder,
- it needs larger concessions and tighter execution,
- and it may fail if the overcapacity problem is severe.

Fine. That is a challenge to construct a better package, not a mandate for closures.

What the experts did **not** establish is that closures are the only legitimate route. They established only that a no-closure deal must be robust enough to withstand reality. That is exactly the kind of package I want tested first.

## What I would support
I could support a negotiated package if it includes all of the following:

- **No compulsory redundancies**
- **No immediate plant closures**
- **Temporary employee contribution only**
- **Management and owner contribution**
- **Capacity reduction through working-time, redeployment, qualification, voluntary exits, and productivity gains**
- **Concrete investment and product commitments for German sites**
- **A time-limited review mechanism focused on performance of the package, not on preloaded closure threats**
- **Protection of the state’s co-determination and veto role**

That is a serious restructuring framework. It is not a refusal to adapt. It is adaptation without surrendering the company’s regional anchor function.

---

## What I will not support
- Plant closures in Germany as the starting premise
- Compulsory redundancies
- A restructuring architecture that uses shutdown threats to force concessions
- Open-ended site insecurity
- Any plan that shifts the whole burden onto workers and the region

---

## My bottom line
I am open to a **time-bound, quantified no-closure restructuring** with hard monitoring and binding burden sharing.

I am **not** open to a closure-first logic disguised as pragmatism.

If management can prove, with hard numbers, that the German footprint can be stabilized without closures or forced layoffs, then I will support that path. If not, then we still have not exhausted the alternatives — and I will not let irreversible damage be treated as the easy answer.
