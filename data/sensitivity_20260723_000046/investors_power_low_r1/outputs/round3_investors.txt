We agree on the diagnosis, but not yet on the credibility of the remedies.

## Where we agree
- The current cost base is not competitive.
- A cosmetic settlement would destroy value.
- Prolonged industrial conflict would also destroy value.
- A durable, governance-proof restructuring is required.
- Employees should not carry the entire burden alone.

That said, from a capital-markets perspective, **agreement on diagnosis is not enough**. What matters is whether the package actually restores margins and cash generation.

## Where we disagree with the workforce side
The works council and union continue to make **“no closures, no compulsory redundancies, binding guarantees through 2030”** their baseline. I understand the political logic, but I do **not** accept that as a sufficient restructuring answer.

The expert testimony was helpful here: a no-closure package can work only in **limited or moderate** cases. If the overcapacity and fixed-cost problem is structural, then keeping every site open is often **not economically equivalent** to selective capacity adjustment. It may reduce conflict risk, but it can also leave too much fixed cost in place.

So I reject any settlement that:
- preserves all German sites regardless of economics,
- freezes the footprint through 2030,
- and asks capital to absorb uncompetitive costs indefinitely.

That is not risk-adjusted value creation.

## Where we agree with management
Management is right that the company needs **real, durable savings** and that a weak compromise is not acceptable.

But management still has not done enough to prove that the preferred restructuring route is **economically superior after implementation risk**. Closures may deliver savings on paper, but the actual value depends on:
- labour peace,
- speed of implementation,
- governance passage,
- and whether the company can realize the savings without prolonged disruption.

So I do **not** support a closure-first posture as a default. I support the **best net value outcome**, not the most aggressive one.

## Where we agree with the state and family
The state and family are right that a destructive conflict would damage value and execution capacity. They are also right that the company needs a solution that can pass governance.

But neither political preference nor legacy stewardship can become a veto on hard economics. If a no-closure package cannot deliver the needed savings, then sentiment is not a substitute for restructuring.

## Our position on the two scenarios

### 1) No-closure restructuring
We would support this **only if it is quantitatively credible**:
- real and durable fixed-cost reduction,
- meaningful capacity reduction,
- measurable labor-cost contribution,
- strong product and volume reallocation,
- and a path back to target margins.

If management can show that the no-closure package is genuinely equivalent on economics and lower on implementation risk, then it is attractive.

If it cannot, then it is not a solution — it is delay.

### 2) Selective capacity-adjustment restructuring
We would support this **if necessary** to restore competitiveness, provided it is:
- tightly targeted,
- time-bound,
- governance-feasible,
- and not so destructive that it wipes out the savings in conflict and disruption.

That is not a preference for closures. It is a recognition that shareholders should not be forced to fund chronic overcapacity.

## What would move us
We need to see, by site and function:
1. baseline fixed costs,
2. achievable savings under no-closure,
3. achievable savings under selective adjustment,
4. implementation risk for each,
5. and the effect on recurring margin improvement.

Without that, the debate stays rhetorical.

## What would not move us
- “No closures” as a moral absolute.
- “Closures” as a managerial reflex.
- Open-ended guarantees that freeze the cost base.
- Packages that look socially balanced but remain financially weak.

## Bottom line
We are open to either route, but **only the economically real one**.

If the no-closure route can genuinely restore competitiveness, we will support it.  
If it cannot, then selective capacity adjustment has to remain on the table.

What we will not support is a settlement that protects every site, preserves structural inefficiency, and calls that a restructuring.
