I will be equally clear in this round: **my position stands.**  
No German plant closures. No compulsory redundancies. No bargaining framework that keeps closure as a leverage weapon over the workforce.

The expert contributions have confirmed what we already knew from the plants: **a closure-heavy route may look cleaner on paper, but it is not automatically the stronger solution once you account for trust, implementation, industrial peace, and long-term site stability.**

## To management
You keep asking for “equivalent savings,” but you still have not shown that the business case for closures is stronger than the social and operational damage they would create.

The plant-level analysis says something important:
- yes, closures can remove fixed cost,
- yes, a no-closure package has limits,
- but the real question is whether management has **actually exhausted the alternatives**.

That is where your case remains weak. You are still presenting closure as the measure of seriousness instead of proving that it is truly unavoidable. That is not the same thing.

If you want the workforce to accept any sacrifices at all, then bring:
- plant-by-plant economics,
- transparent break-even data,
- concrete product allocation plans,
- management and owner contributions,
- and a binding guarantee that there will be **no closures and no forced redundancies**.

Without that, you are not negotiating a restructuring. You are trying to convert uncertainty into pressure.

## To the union
I support your core line:
- no compulsory redundancies,
- no one-sided burden shifting,
- no dismantling of protections.

Where I go further is this: I do **not** want a “time-limited no-closure trial” if that trial is just a polite way of saying, “accept cuts now and maybe we close later.” That would simply repackage the same threat.

A trial is only acceptable if it starts from a binding basis:
- German sites remain open,
- job security is protected,
- and the review is about how to make the sites stronger, not whether to shut them down later.

## To the regional state government
You are right to ask for hard numbers. But let me be blunt: **the state cannot act as if closures are a neutral technical option.** They are not. They would hit the regional industrial base, and everyone in this room knows it.

If you want an enforceable framework, then use your leverage to support:
- a binding no-closure commitment,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- and a restructuring package built on investment, redeployment, working-time flexibility, and shared burden.

A “review mechanism” that keeps closures alive in the background is not enough. The company needs a clear line, not a permanent threat.

## To the controlling family holding
You speak the language of stewardship, but stewardship is not the same as preserving strategic latitude at the expense of the workforce.

I do not accept the idea that you must keep closures “available” to show discipline. If the only way you can demonstrate seriousness is by threatening to destroy sites, then the restructuring logic is flawed.

If you want a durable settlement, then publicly back:
- no German closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- and real contributions from capital and management.

If you will not do that, then do not pretend the workforce is the problem for resisting your version of “discipline.”

## To institutional investors
You are right about one thing: a cosmetic deal is worthless.  
But your error is to assume that a closure option is what makes a deal “economically real.”

A deal is economically real if it:
- can actually be implemented,
- preserves execution capability,
- maintains industrial peace,
- secures future products and investments,
- and does not blow up in conflict and mistrust.

The plant-level assessment also shows that a no-closure package is not automatically weak; it depends on the size and quality of the measures. That means the honest question is not “which sites can we close?” but “which package can deliver durable savings without destroying the company’s social and operational foundation?”

## On the expert findings
The experts were useful, but they did **not** give management a free pass.

What they said was:
- no-closure is harder,
- it needs stronger measures,
- it requires real product and capacity reallocation,
- and it may be insufficient if the overcapacity problem is severe.

Fine. That is a challenge to management to do better, not a license to close plants.

What they did **not** say is:
- that closures are inevitable,
- that forced layoffs are necessary,
- or that the only credible restructuring is one that sacrifices German sites.

That interpretation would be a misuse of the expert input.

## My position on the proposed compromise tracks

### 1) Time-bound no-closure trial with hard triggers
I reject it in the form presented.  
A trigger-based model is only “temporary” in name if the trigger is plant closure. That means the workforce is asked to concede now while the threat remains alive.

If management wants a trial, then the trial must be:
- no closures,
- no forced redundancies,
- binding job security,
- and verification of whether operational measures are enough.

Anything else is a disguised closure mechanism.

### 2) Mixed burden-sharing with asymmetrical security
This is closer, but only if the security is real:
- binding no-compulsory-redundancy commitment,
- binding site protection,
- and employee contributions that are strictly temporary and reversible.

If the “separate delayed review” is really a backdoor closure discussion, then I do not accept it.

### 3) Site-by-site preservation test with a keep-open default
This is the only framework that is remotely acceptable — **if** the default is truly to keep each site open and the burden is on management to prove otherwise with hard evidence.

But even then, my line remains:
- no closures in Germany,
- no compulsory redundancies.

A test must not become a pretext for demolition.

## My minimum acceptable package
This remains unchanged:

- **No German plant closures**
- **No compulsory redundancies**
- **Binding site guarantees through 2030**
- **Temporary employee contribution only in exchange for real security**
- **Management and owner contributions**
- **Capacity adjustment through working-time, redeployment, qualification, voluntary exits, and productivity measures**
- **Strong co-determination in implementation**
- **Concrete investment and product commitments for German sites**

## My fallback package
Still the same:
- temporary pay restraint,
- bonus restraint,
- working-time flexibility,
- voluntary early retirement,
- voluntary exit programs,
- internal mobility,
- training and reskilling,
- overhead and process efficiency.

But I will repeat: **this is not a fallback to closures.**  
It is a fallback to negotiated adjustment without destroying the footprint.

## What would move me
Only one thing: a binding package that preserves every German plant and excludes compulsory redundancies, backed by real burden sharing from management and owners.

Anything less means the company is asking labour to pay for a strategic failure it did not create.

## Bottom line
I will not endorse any round-4 compromise that keeps closures alive as a threat.  
If management truly wants a settlement, it must stop treating German plants as negotiable collateral and start treating the workforce as a partner with a legitimate veto over irreversible destruction.

If that cannot be accepted, then the conflict is not about our rigidity. It is about management’s refusal to build a future without sacrificing the workforce first.
