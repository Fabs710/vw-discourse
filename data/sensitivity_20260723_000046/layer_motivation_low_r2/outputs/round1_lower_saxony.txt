The state government shares the diagnosis that the company cannot simply continue as before. A structurally weaker market, high costs, and intense competition are real. But I want to be equally clear: **a restructuring that destroys German sites and treats the workforce as the adjustment buffer is not acceptable**.

We are not here to defend the status quo at any price. We are here to protect the company as a **long-term industrial anchor** in the region. That means competitiveness, yes — but also employment, industrial capacity, and social stability.

Where I agree with management:
- The current cost base must come down.
- Capacity needs to be adjusted to market reality.
- A negotiated restructuring is preferable to a public escalation.
- The company needs a durable solution, not a temporary patch.

Where I disagree:
- **Plant closures in Germany are not a first resort, and not something we can casually put on the table.**
- **Compulsory redundancies are a drastic step that must remain an absolute last resort, if they are considered at all.**
- Any “solution” that permanently weakens the state’s industrial base would be politically and economically damaging.

I also want to respond to the union and works council: your insistence that there be no closures and no forced redundancies is understandable and legitimate from your side. But I need to be honest as a shareholder and political actor: **we will not support a deal that preserves every structure unchanged if that makes the company unviable.** The company must remain competitive or we all lose — workers, owners, and the region.

That said, I do see a middle path worth pursuing, but it has to be real and binding:
- site guarantees where economically feasible,
- no forced redundancies if alternative instruments can deliver the savings,
- capacity reductions through redeployment, working-time measures, and voluntary programs,
- qualification and internal transfer,
- and a shared contribution from all sides, not only employees.

If management wants the state’s support, it will need to demonstrate:
1. that closures are truly unavoidable, not just convenient;
2. that every possible alternative has been exhausted;
3. that investment in future products and technologies is secured;
4. and that the burden-sharing is balanced.

To be direct: **the state’s support for any restructuring is conditional on preserving jobs and sites as far as possible.** We have statutory leverage, and we will use it if necessary. I would rather broker a responsible compromise than block progress, but I will not be sidelined into rubber-stamping a plan that hollows out the regional economy.

So my position is:
- yes to a negotiated restructuring,
- yes to real savings and capacity adjustment,
- no to using closures as the default answer,
- no to a deal that strips the state of its co-determination and veto role,
- and no to an outcome that leaves the region carrying the social cost while others preserve their positions.

If management comes forward with a serious alternative that protects sites and jobs while restoring competitiveness, the state will engage constructively. If not, we will resist.
