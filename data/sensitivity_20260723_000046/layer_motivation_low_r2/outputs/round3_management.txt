## To the works council and union
I hear the insistence on “no closures, no compulsory redundancies, no permanent erosion of standards.” I understand why you are drawing those lines. But I am not prepared to accept a framework that rules out the instruments needed to fix a structurally uncompetitive cost base **before** we have a credible savings package in hand.

The expert input is useful, but it does **not** change the core economics. Yes, temporary concessions can be drafted without creating a permanent precedent. That helps. It does **not** mean the company can be made competitive while German operations remain structurally overbuilt and labour costs remain above target.

So let me be clear:

- I **can** discuss a time-limited stability pact.
- I **can** discuss temporary, bounded, reversible labour contributions.
- I **can** discuss working-time flexibility, redeployment, qualification, voluntary exits, and bonus moderation.
- I **cannot** accept a package that simply locks the current structure in place and calls that a solution.

Your argument is still: “Protect the sites first, then we will talk savings.”  
Mine is: “Show me a package that actually reduces cost and capacity durably, and then we can talk about protections.”

That sequence matters. If the savings are not real, the protections are meaningless because the company will still be uncompetitive.

## On your rejection of closures as a fallback
This is where I disagree most sharply.

You keep saying that any fallback closure logic is a permanent threat. From my perspective, that is exactly the point: a restructuring without a credible fallback is not a restructuring, it is a wish list.

I am **not** saying closures are the preferred outcome. I am saying they cannot be ruled out in advance if they are needed to reach a structurally sustainable result. The same applies to compulsory redundancies: not the first choice, but not off the table if the economics demand it.

If you want them removed from the table entirely, then you must produce a package that closes the competitiveness gap in full. Not slogans. Numbers.

## To the regional state government
I agree with you that this cannot become a blunt industrial rupture. I also agree that social peace matters and that the company’s regional role is important.

But I do **not** agree with your implication that the state should use its leverage to block hard structural measures regardless of the economic outcome.

That would be a political answer to a commercial problem. It would preserve comfort today and weaken the company tomorrow.

If the state wants to help, then help secure:
- a real labour-cost contribution,
- actual capacity alignment,
- and a credible path back to margin improvement.

What I will not accept is the state using its blocking position to freeze the company into an uncompetitive structure.

## To the controlling family holding
You speak about stewardship and long-term value. On that we agree in principle.

Where we diverge is that you seem to believe trust can be preserved by promising stability without addressing the cost base. It cannot. Long-term value is destroyed just as surely by structural weakness as by public conflict.

So yes, I support a negotiated solution. But it must produce:
- durable savings,
- capacity reduction,
- and a lower structural cost base.

If labour can deliver that through temporary concessions and internal measures, then I am open to a moratorium on immediate closures. But I will not support a settlement that makes the company’s future margins hostage to goodwill.

## To institutional investors
You are right to insist this must be material, measurable, and durable. That is exactly my standard too.

Where I push back is on the assumption that a non-closure package is automatically preferable if it sounds socially gentler. Socially gentler does not matter if it fails economically.

So I am asking for something very concrete:
- a quantified savings target,
- a quantified capacity reduction,
- a quantified labour-cost contribution,
- and evidence that this gets the brand back to its return-on-sales target.

If that can be achieved without closures and forced redundancies, fine. If not, then hard instruments remain necessary.

## On the expert’s drafting advice
I accept the technical point: temporary measures can be written to be non-prejudicial, time-limited, and reversible.

That is helpful for building a bridge. It is **not** a substitute for structural adjustment.

So I would be prepared to discuss a pact with:
- a fixed duration,
- automatic reversion,
- a no-prejudice / no-waiver clause,
- and preservation of statutory co-determination rights.

But that pact must include a serious contribution from labour and a serious reduction in overcapacity. If it only protects the status quo, I will not sign off on it.

## My position on the moderator’s proposals

### 1) Time-limited stability pact with quantified labour contribution
This is the only route worth discussing seriously.

But my conditions are non-negotiable:
- the contribution must be **material**
- the capacity reduction must be **real**
- the savings must be **verifiable**
- and the package must include a **re-opener** if targets are missed

Without that, it is a delay mechanism, not a restructuring.

### 2) “No closures unless jointly verified necessity”
I can accept a verification process. I cannot accept a process that turns “no closure” into the default assumption.

If the company needs the fallback to secure competitiveness, it must remain available. That is not a threat; that is responsible governance.

### 3) Site guarantees tied to investment and capacity commitments
This is fine only if the guarantees are conditional on actual savings and implementation. A site guarantee without economic delivery is not a guarantee; it is a postponement of the problem.

## What would move management
Very clearly:

1. **A quantified, durable labour-cost contribution**
2. **A verifiable reduction in overcapacity**
3. **A credible productivity and redeployment package**
4. **Bounded protections for a defined period**
5. **A mechanism that allows deeper action if the package fails**

If labour can bring that, I am prepared to suspend immediate closure plans and forced redundancies for a defined period.

If it cannot, then I will not pretend a softer wording changes the economics.

## Bottom line
I am not moving to an absolute no-closure, no-redundancy framework.

I am open to a negotiated stability pact, but only if it delivers:
- durable savings,
- real capacity alignment,
- and a lower cost base that restores competitiveness through 2030.

That is the test. Not who speaks most forcefully. Not who repeats “guarantee” most often. The test is whether the package actually fixes the company. If not, we are just delaying the next crisis.
