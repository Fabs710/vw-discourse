We are aligned on one thing: the company needs a restructuring that is **material, durable, and credible**. What we are **not** aligned on is the insistence by labour and the state that the company should commit up front to **no closures and no compulsory redundancies**, regardless of the economics.

That position is not financeable unless the alternative package truly closes the margin and cost gap. So far, we have not seen that case made.

## To management
We agree with your diagnosis:
- the cost base is too high,
- capacity is misaligned,
- and cosmetic measures are not enough.

Where we are **not yet convinced** is your implication that closures and compulsory redundancies must remain live instruments simply because restructuring is needed. “We need flexibility” is not the same as “we have proven that hard measures are necessary.”

If you want capital markets support, you need to show:
1. the exact savings gap after all non-closure measures,
2. what capacity reduction is achieved without closures,
3. why that still fails,
4. and the margin impact over 2–3 years.

That comparative case is still missing.

## To the works council
We understand your concern about trust and your rejection of closure threats as a bargaining weapon. We also accept the expert point that temporary measures can be drafted in a way that is time-limited, reversible, and non-prejudicial.

But your position is still too rigid for us:
- no closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- binding site protection through 2030,
- and only internal measures.

That may be politically and socially attractive, but it is not enough unless it produces **equivalent structural savings**. You have not shown that it does.

We will not back a deal that preserves every legacy structure and asks shareholders to absorb the downside.

## To the industrial union
We agree with you on the drafting point: temporary labour contributions can be made safe without creating a permanent precedent. That helps.

But we do **not** accept your categorical exclusion of closures and compulsory redundancies unless the alternative package clearly restores competitiveness. If it does, fine. If it does not, then those tools cannot be ruled out in advance.

A serious restructuring can include:
- working-time flexibility,
- bonus moderation,
- redeployment,
- qualification,
- voluntary exits,
- and temporary pay elements.

But only if the outcome is materially better margins and a lower structural cost base.

## To the regional state government
We respect the role of the state as anchor shareholder and guardian of the industrial base. But the state should not convert that role into a permanent veto on necessary restructuring.

If you want our support, push for:
- a real labour-cost contribution,
- actual capacity consolidation,
- investment commitments,
- and a credible path to margin recovery.

Do not support a framework that preserves employment today while leaving the company structurally weak tomorrow.

## To the controlling family holding
We agree with your stewardship framing more than with the labour side’s rhetoric. Value destruction through prolonged conflict is unacceptable.

But stewardship also means not accepting a “comfort-first” settlement that leaves the company uncompetitive. We need:
- durable savings,
- real capacity alignment,
- and implementation certainty.

If a non-closure package can deliver that, we support it. If it cannot, then hard measures remain necessary.

## On the moderator’s compromise paths

### 1) Fixed-term stability pact with hard protections and hard metrics
Potentially acceptable **only if** the metrics are hard enough to satisfy the market:
- quantified savings,
- fixed expiry,
- automatic reversion,
- independent verification,
- and a re-opener if targets are missed.

If it just freezes the problem, we will not support it.

### 2) Conditional no-closure moratorium linked to verified restructuring milestones
This is acceptable only as a **procedural safeguard**, not as a de facto closure taboo.

A moratorium is fine if it protects against premature action. It is not fine if it becomes a permanent prohibition regardless of results.

### 3) Site-and-investment guarantee in exchange for bounded labour contributions
This is the strongest route, but only if the commitments are concrete and economically meaningful:
- specific sites,
- specific investment,
- specific product commitments,
- and a measurable link to competitiveness improvement.

## What would move us
We could support a negotiated package if it delivers all of the following:
1. **durable cost reduction**
2. **real capacity alignment**
3. **quantified and reversible labour contributions**
4. **hard implementation milestones**
5. **independent verification**
6. **credible investment commitments**
7. **a clear path back to competitive margins**

If those conditions are met, immediate closures and compulsory redundancies need not be the preferred outcome.

If they are not met, we will not support a cosmetic peace deal.

## Bottom line
We are open to a serious, time-limited stability pact. We are **not** open to a deal that:
- leaves the German cost base structurally uncompetitive,
- merely defers adjustment,
- or rules out hard measures regardless of the numbers.

If management and labour want capital markets onside, bring a package that is economically credible, implementation-ready, and strong enough to restore margins.
