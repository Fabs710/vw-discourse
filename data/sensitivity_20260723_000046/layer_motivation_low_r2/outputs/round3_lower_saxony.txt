I will be direct.

## To management
I agree that the status quo is not sustainable, and I agree that the company needs real restructuring, not symbolism. I also agree that a deal has to be economically credible.

What I do **not** accept is your insistence that plant closures and compulsory redundancies must remain normal instruments at the outset of the negotiation. That is not “responsible governance” from my perspective; it is a choice to keep the most destructive tools on the table before you have proven that less damaging measures are insufficient.

You have still not demonstrated, with a convincing comparative case, that:
- internal redeployment,
- working-time flexibility,
- qualification,
- voluntary exits,
- temporary pay moderation,
- capacity consolidation without closures,
- and product/investment commitments

cannot close the gap to a serious degree.

If you want my support, you need to show me a restructuring path that protects the regional industrial base, not just a path that is administratively clean for management.

## To the works council and union
I agree with your core red lines:
- no plant closures as a default answer,
- no compulsory redundancies as an easy option,
- no hidden dismantling of sites through outsourcing or relocation,
- and no erosion of co-determination.

That is not “inflexibility.” That is the minimum required to preserve trust and industrial peace.

I also take seriously the expert’s point: temporary concessions can be drafted in a way that is clearly time-limited, non-prejudicial, and without waiving statutory rights. That matters. It means there is a serious compromise route available **without** making closures a standing threat.

So yes, I am prepared to support a **temporary, bounded, reversible** contribution from labour if — and only if — it is paired with:
- binding site protection,
- no compulsory redundancies for a defined period,
- clear investment commitments,
- and explicit preservation of co-determination.

What I will not support is a package that asks the workforce to pay now while management reserves the right to come back later and close plants anyway.

## To the controlling family holding
I agree that value destruction through prolonged conflict is unacceptable. I also agree that the company cannot be frozen into an uncompetitive structure.

But I do **not** accept the premise that preserving German sites and competitiveness are mutually exclusive. That is too crude. A negotiated restructuring can and should protect the regional anchor role while still improving the cost base.

What I expect from the family, as a controlling shareholder, is stewardship:
- no rush to closure logic,
- no using the workforce as the first adjustment variable,
- and no settlement that damages the company’s legitimacy in its home region.

If you want long-term value, then support a settlement that keeps the company rooted here, not one that treats regional industrial capacity as expendable.

## To institutional investors
You are right that this cannot be a cosmetic deal. A mere pause will not do.

But you are too quick to imply that only closures or forced redundancies can produce a credible restructuring. That is not established. Credibility comes from:
- measurable savings,
- verifiable capacity adjustment,
- implementation certainty,
- and industrial peace.

A negotiated package with temporary labour contributions and serious internal restructuring can be credible if it is properly designed. What makes it fail is not the absence of closures; it is the absence of hard numbers, binding commitments, and enforcement.

## On the moderator’s compromise paths

### 1) Time-limited stability pact with quantified labour contribution
This is the only route I see as realistic — but only with strict conditions:
- no plant closures during the pact period,
- no compulsory redundancies during the pact period,
- binding site guarantees,
- temporary and reversible labour contributions,
- clear investment commitments,
- and preservation of co-determination.

If those elements are missing, then it is not a stability pact; it is a staged retreat.

### 2) “No closures unless jointly verified necessity”
I am **not** prepared to endorse a framework that leaves closures as a standing negotiation threat. The expert has shown that temporary concessions can be structured without surrendering core rights. That opens a better path than one where the workforce is constantly told that plants may still be shut later.

So my answer is: **not acceptable as drafted**. If the issue is merely a procedural trigger, it still keeps the pressure point on workers. That is not a genuine partnership model.

### 3) Site guarantees tied to investment and capacity commitments
This is the strongest basis for agreement. But the guarantee must be real:
- concrete sites,
- concrete duration,
- concrete production and product commitments,
- concrete enforcement,
- and no backdoor relocation or outsourcing.

Anything less is political decoration.

## What would move the state government
I would support a deal only if it:
1. protects jobs and sites in the state,
2. preserves the state’s co-determination and veto role,
3. delivers real, measurable restructuring,
4. and keeps the company a regional economic anchor.

That means I need:
- a binding no-closure period,
- a binding no-compulsory-redundancy period,
- temporary, quantified labour contributions that are explicitly reversible,
- and a credible investment program for future products and technologies.

If management refuses that, then it should not expect the state to help legitimize a plan that shifts the burden onto the region.

## My answer to the expert input
The expert confirmed what I needed to hear: temporary concessions can be legally and industrial-relationally designed without undermining co-determination or creating a permanent precedent.

That is useful because it gives us a serious middle path:
- labour contributes temporarily,
- management gets measurable savings,
- sites and jobs are protected,
- and the agreement expires cleanly.

That is the kind of arrangement I can support.

What I will **not** accept is using “temporary” as a polite label for a permanent weakening of protection. If the text is vague, if the expiry is soft, or if the company keeps reopening the same concessions, then the package loses legitimacy immediately.

## My line in plain language
I can support a negotiated restructuring.
I cannot support a restructuring that:
- closes German plants,
- normalizes compulsory redundancies,
- sidelines co-determination,
- or sacrifices the state’s industrial anchor role.

So if management wants my vote and my political support, bring me a package that:
- protects the state’s plants and jobs,
- includes a real but temporary labour contribution,
- commits to investment and product renewal,
- and is strong enough to restore competitiveness without breaking the social compact.

That is the condition. Anything else is not acceptable.
