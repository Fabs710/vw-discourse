We agree on the end state, not on the acceptable path.

## Our position
As dispersed institutional investors, our test is straightforward:
**Does the package credibly restore competitive margins, reduce the cost base structurally, and implement cleanly without dragging the company through prolonged conflict?**
If yes, we can support it.
If no, we will not.

We are **not** prepared to back a settlement that:
- leaves the German cost base structurally uncompetitive,
- only defers the adjustment,
- or protects every legacy structure while asking capital to absorb the downside.

That is not a turnaround. It is value destruction with better branding.

---

## To management
We agree with you on the core diagnosis: the current structure is too costly and capacity is misaligned. We also agree that a cosmetic compromise is unacceptable.

Where we disagree is your tendency to present **closures and compulsory redundancies as if they were already the obvious answer**.

They may be necessary. But you have not yet made the comparative case rigorously enough:
- What exact savings gap remains after non-closure measures?
- What capacity reduction is required?
- What margin uplift results over 2–3 years?
- Why are internal measures insufficient?

If you want support from capital markets, bring **numbers, scenarios, and implementation risk**, not just urgency language.

We can support a time-limited stability pact **only if** it contains:
- quantified labour-cost relief,
- real capacity alignment,
- hard milestones,
- independent verification,
- and a re-opener if targets are missed.

If it becomes a soft, open-ended promise to “review later,” we will not support it.

---

## To the works council
We understand your refusal to accept closures and forced redundancies as a starting point. But from our side, an absolute no-closure / no-redundancy doctrine is not financeable unless the alternative package truly closes the competitiveness gap.

Your position would be acceptable only if it comes with a package that is clearly sufficient:
- measurable cost reductions,
- verifiable capacity reduction,
- temporary and reversible labour contributions,
- and a credible investment plan.

What we do **not** accept is a settlement that gives binding protection first and leaves the economic problem unresolved.

The expert’s point on drafting is useful: temporary concessions can be done without creating a permanent precedent, if the text is tight. That helps. But it does **not** make the economics go away.

So: we can support temporary protections and reversible labour measures.
We cannot support a structure that freezes the current cost base and calls that compromise.

---

## To the industrial union
We agree that temporary contributions can be designed without eroding co-determination or creating a permanent precedent. That is helpful.

But we disagree with your insistence that closures and compulsory redundancies must be off the table categorically. They are instruments. Unpleasant ones, yes. But if the alternative package does not produce enough savings, then the company cannot be protected from hard adjustment simply because the tools are politically unpopular.

What we would consider acceptable:
- working-time flexibility,
- temporary pay moderation / bonus restraint,
- redeployment,
- voluntary exits,
- qualification,
- capacity reduction through internal means,

**provided these measures are material and durable enough** to restore competitiveness.

What we will not support:
- permanent wage erosion disguised as a crisis measure,
- open-ended insecurity,
- or a package that protects jobs on paper while leaving margins impaired.

---

## To the regional state government
We agree that a blunt dictat and a prolonged industrial conflict would both destroy value.

We also agree that the state’s role is not merely ceremonial. But we do **not** support using the state’s blocking leverage to prevent hard restructuring regardless of the economics.

The state should insist on:
- a transparent comparison of options,
- verified savings,
- and a settlement that preserves industrial peace **because it is economically sound**, not because it postpones necessary change.

We are open to a compromise that protects sites and jobs for a defined period.
We are **not** open to one that preserves regional comfort while margins remain structurally weak.

---

## To the controlling family holding
We agree on the core principle: value destruction from prolonged conflict is unacceptable.

Where we differ is that you seem more willing to tolerate a compromise that leans on temporary labour concessions and political stability, even if the structural economic adjustment is incomplete. That would not be enough for us.

A family owner should want stewardship, yes — but stewardship means:
- restoring competitiveness,
- aligning capacity,
- and protecting long-term enterprise value.

That does **not** require sentimental attachment to every legacy structure.
It does require economic realism.

We will support a deal only if it materially improves the business.
If it does not, then it is not stewardship — it is delay.

## On the moderator’s compromise paths

### 1) Time-limited stability pact with quantified labour contribution
Potentially acceptable, but only if:
- the contribution is **material**,
- the savings are **verifiable**,
- the duration is **fixed**,
- the reversion is **automatic**,
- and the margin impact is **clearly positive**.

### 2) “No closures unless jointly verified necessity”
This is acceptable only as a **procedural safeguard**, not as a de facto veto.
We do not support a permanent closure taboo. If the numbers say hard measures are needed, they must remain available.

### 3) Site guarantees tied to investment and capacity commitments
This is the best route **if** the guarantees are backed by real economic improvement.
A site guarantee without structural savings is just a postponement device.

---

## What would move us
We could support a negotiated package if it delivers all of the following:
1. **durable cost reduction**
2. **real capacity alignment**
3. **quantified and reversible labour contributions**
4. **hard implementation milestones**
5. **independent verification**
6. **credible investment commitments**
7. **a clear path back to competitive margins**

If those conditions are met, immediate closures and compulsory redundancies need not be our preferred outcome.

If they are not met, then we will not support a cosmetic peace deal.

---

## Bottom line
We are open to a negotiated restructuring.
We are **not** open to a compromise that preserves the current cost structure and calls that a solution.

The company needs:
- material savings,
- real capacity adjustment,
- implementation certainty,
- and margin recovery.

Anything less is not acceptable to capital markets.
