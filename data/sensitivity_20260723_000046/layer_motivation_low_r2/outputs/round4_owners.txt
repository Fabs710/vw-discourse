## My position remains unchanged in substance
I support a restructuring that **restores competitiveness, margins, and long-term enterprise value**. I do **not** support a settlement that preserves the current cost structure and simply postpones the problem. At the same time, I do **not** want a value-destructive conflict that damages the brand, the workforce, and governance stability.

So my standard is clear:

- **competitiveness must improve materially**
- **savings must be durable**
- **implementation must be credible**
- **and the enterprise must not be permanently impaired**

That is the lens through which I judge every proposal.

## To the works council
I hear your red lines. I understand why you are insisting on no closures, no compulsory redundancies, and strong site protection. I also accept the expert point that temporary concessions can be drafted safely, without waiving co-determination or creating a permanent precedent.

But I disagree with your core conclusion.

You keep saying management has not proven that closures are necessary. That is true as a statement of burden of proof, but it does **not** answer the economic question. If the alternative package does not close the cost and capacity gap, then excluding hard measures in advance is not stewardship — it is denial.

You are asking the owners and management to lock in protection first and trust that savings will somehow follow. I cannot support that sequence unless the package is already shown to be sufficient.

What would move me?
- a **quantified** savings plan,
- a **credible** capacity reduction path,
- and evidence that non-closure measures actually restore margins.

Without that, I will not endorse an absolute no-closure framework.

## To the industrial union
I agree with you on one important point: if temporary concessions are made, they must be **time-limited, reversible, and explicitly non-prejudicial**. The expert’s drafting guidance confirms that this is possible.

That is useful.

But I disagree with your insistence that closures and compulsory redundancies must be categorically excluded. They cannot be ruled out if the business case demands them. A company in structural overcapacity cannot be forced to carry permanent inefficiency just because the instruments are unpopular.

So yes, I am open to:
- working-time flexibility
- bonus moderation
- redeployment
- qualification
- voluntary exits
- temporary pay elements
- internal capacity reduction

But only if those measures are **material enough** to restore competitiveness. If they are not, then they are just delay.

## To group and brand management
You are right about the diagnosis: the cost base is too high, capacity is misaligned, and the company needs more than a cosmetic compromise. I agree that the package must be economically real.

Where I do **not** simply follow you is in treating closures and compulsory redundancies as if they were already the obvious answer. They may be necessary, but I have not yet seen a sufficiently rigorous comparative case.

I want:
1. the exact savings gap,
2. the effect of non-closure measures,
3. the remaining gap after those measures,
4. and the margin impact over the next 2–3 years.

If you can show that internal measures and labour contributions are insufficient, then hard instruments remain on the table. But “we need flexibility” is not enough.

Also: I will not support a plan that creates prolonged industrial conflict merely to preserve managerial optionality. That would destroy value faster than a weak compromise.

## To the regional state government
I agree that the company should not be treated as disposable, and I respect the state’s role in preserving the industrial base. I also understand your concern about trust and social peace.

But I disagree with the idea that the state should pre-commit to blocking hard measures regardless of economics.

If the state wants to exercise its leverage constructively, it should insist on:
- a real labour-cost contribution,
- real capacity consolidation,
- a credible investment and product program,
- and a restructuring path that actually improves competitiveness.

I can support a temporary, bounded settlement that protects sites for a defined period. I cannot support a framework that converts the state’s leverage into a permanent veto on necessary restructuring.

## To institutional investors
You are right to insist on hard numbers and implementation certainty. I agree with that completely.

Where I disagree is in any assumption that only closures or forced redundancies can produce a serious outcome. That is too crude.

The right question is:
- does the package produce durable savings,
- does it align capacity,
- does it improve margins,
- and does it avoid an extended conflict that destroys value?

If the answer is yes, then it is supportable. If the answer is no, then I will not support a cosmetic peace deal.

## On the moderator’s compromise paths

### 1) Fixed-term stability pact with hard protections and hard metrics
This is the only route worth serious consideration.

But the protections cannot be unlimited and unconditional. I would require:
- a fixed duration,
- temporary and reversible labour contributions,
- automatic reversion,
- independent verification of savings,
- and a re-opener if targets are missed.

If the package truly produces material improvement, I can support a temporary moratorium on hard measures. If it simply freezes the problem, I cannot.

### 2) Conditional no-closure moratorium linked to verified restructuring milestones
I can support the **verification logic**. I cannot support any formulation that turns this into a de facto permanent closure taboo.

If milestones are missed and the economic gap remains, then deeper measures must remain available. Governance cannot be reduced to a promise that the company will never act if the numbers force its hand.

### 3) Site-and-investment guarantee in exchange for bounded labour contributions
This is the most promising route, but only if it is **real**:
- concrete sites,
- concrete investment,
- concrete product commitments,
- concrete duration,
- and a concrete link to measurable competitiveness improvement.

A site guarantee without economic delivery is not a guarantee. It is a postponement.

## What would genuinely move me

2. **A real reduction in overcapacity**
3. **A credible productivity and redeployment package**
4. **A fixed-term protection period**

If those conditions are met, immediate closures may not be necessary. If they are not met, I will not pretend that comfort language solves the balance sheet.

## What I reject
I reject:
- an absolute no-closure doctrine
- an absolute no-redundancy doctrine
- a settlement that preserves the current cost base
- or any arrangement that makes the enterprise structurally weaker while calling that social responsibility

That is not stewardship. That is delay.

## Bottom line
I am prepared to support a **serious, time-limited stability pact** if it restores competitiveness in a measurable way.

I am **not** prepared to support a deal that:
- locks in structural overcapacity,
- rules out hard measures regardless of economics,
- or sacrifices long-term value for short-term calm.

If management and labour want my support, bring a package that is:
- economically credible,
- legally clean,
- temporary where possible,
- and strong enough to restore margins without permanently impairing the enterprise.

That is the condition.
