I will be direct.

## My position
I support a restructuring that **restores competitiveness, margins, and long-term enterprise value**. I do **not** support a settlement that preserves all current structures, absorbs another round of cost pressure, and leaves the company structurally uncompetitive.

At the same time, I do **not** want a value-destroying confrontation that damages the brand, plants, and governance stability. So my standard is not “tough for its own sake.” My standard is: **does this package materially fix the economics without permanently impairing the enterprise?**

On that basis, I am **not moving** to an absolute no-closure / no-redundancy position. If the economics require hard measures, I will not rule them out in advance. But I also do **not** want closures used casually or as a first resort. They should remain instruments of necessity, not of convenience.

---

## To management
You are right about the core problem: this must be **material, durable, and credible**. A cosmetic deal would be value-destructive.

Where I agree with you:
- The current cost base is too high.
- Capacity is misaligned.
- The company needs a real structural improvement, not a short pause.
- We cannot let this turn into a long conflict that bleeds value.

Where I disagree:
- You have not yet shown, to my satisfaction, that closures and compulsory redundancies are **necessary rather than preferred**.
- “We need flexibility” is not enough.
- “We need structural savings” is not enough.
- If you want hard instruments available, you need to present a **serious comparative case**.

So my expectation is simple:
1. show the savings gap,
2. show why non-closure measures are insufficient,
3. show the implementation risk of alternatives,
4. and show the likely margin impact over the next 2–3 years.

If the numbers prove that certain hard measures are needed, I will not block them just because they are uncomfortable. But I will not endorse them on assertion alone.

---

## To the works council
I respect your insistence that workers should not be made the only shock absorber for management errors. That point is legitimate.

Where I disagree sharply is your attempt to make:
- no plant closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- and binding site protection through 2030

the starting point rather than the outcome of a negotiated package.

That is too rigid for a company facing structural overcapacity and a cost base above target. You are asking the owners and management to accept the burden first while exempting the workforce from the hard reality of adjustment. That is not a governance basis I can support.

The expert input was useful in one respect: it shows that **temporary, bounded, non-prejudicial concessions can be drafted safely**. That is helpful. But it does **not** mean the company can be made competitive while the German structure remains largely intact.

So I can support:
- temporary labour contributions,
- redeployment,
- qualification,
- working-time flexibility,
- voluntary exits,
- bonus moderation,
- and a tightly drafted stability pact

**if** they produce real savings and capacity alignment.

What I cannot support is turning “site protection” into a veto over all hard restructuring. If the economics do not close, the workforce cannot be insulated from adjustment indefinitely.

---

## To the industrial union
I agree with you on one important point: temporary contributions can be made **without creating a permanent precedent** if they are tightly drafted, time-limited, and explicitly non-prejudicial. That matters.

But I disagree with your insistence that closures and compulsory redundancies must be excluded as a matter of principle. They cannot be excluded if they are genuinely needed to restore structural viability.

So I am open to a package that includes:
- temporary working-time measures,
- bonus restraint,
- voluntary departures,
- internal redeployment,
- qualification,
- and a defined expiry / automatic reversion

but only if the result is **material**. If the package merely delays the problem, I will not support it.

You want hard guarantees first. I want hard savings first. That is the real divide.

---

## To the regional state government
I understand your concern for industrial capacity, employment, and social peace. Those are not trivial concerns.

Where I agree:
- A blunt dictate would be dangerous.
- A prolonged confrontation would damage the company and the region.
- Social peace matters.

Where I disagree:
- The state cannot treat every hard measure as unacceptable and still expect competitiveness.
- You are leaning too far toward preservation logic and not enough toward economic necessity.

Your blocking position is real, but it should be used to secure a **serious restructuring**, not to freeze the enterprise into a protected but weak structure. If the state wants to help, then help produce a package that actually improves the cost base and margin profile.

I would support the state playing a constructive role in a negotiated settlement, but not a settlement that simply preserves comfort and postpones the same problem.

## To the controlling family holding
We are aligned on the big picture more than you may think.

You said:
- value destruction through prolonged conflict is unacceptable,
- but so is a weak compromise that leaves the business structurally uncompetitive.

I agree.

Where I would push you is this: do not let management frame “competitiveness” as if it automatically justifies any disruptive instrument. That would be too easy. The burden is on management to prove necessity measure by measure.

At the same time, I do **not** share the works council’s idea that long-term value is best protected by excluding closures and redundancies up front. It is not. Long-term value is protected by:
- competitiveness,
- margin recovery,
- product investment,
- and labour peace.

That means I will back a time-limited, governable compromise if it is economically real. I will not back a sentimental one.

---

## To institutional investors
You are right to insist that this must be material. I agree.

But I also agree with the need for implementation certainty and labour peace. A package that looks harsh on paper but triggers industrial conflict may destroy more value than it creates.

So the investor test should not be “how severe is it?” The test should be:
- does it close the competitiveness gap,
- does it reduce structural overcapacity,
- does it improve margins,
- and does it avoid a prolonged conflict that burns cash and reputation?

If a non-closure solution can do that, fine. If it cannot, then I will not insist on keeping closures taboo just because they are politically unpopular.

## On the moderator’s compromise paths

### 1) Time-limited stability pact with quantified labour contribution
This is the only path I see as potentially workable.

But only if it includes:
- real savings,
- real capacity reduction,
- a fixed duration,
- automatic reversion,
- explicit preservation of statutory co-determination,
- and a clear review mechanism.

If it becomes a disguised permanent concession, I will not support it.

### 2) “No closures unless jointly verified necessity”
I can support the **verification principle**. I cannot support the **political interpretation** that this becomes a permanent closure veto.

If the data show that closures or compulsory redundancies are necessary, then they must remain available. Governance is not served by pretending otherwise.

### 3) Site guarantees tied to investment and capacity commitments
This is acceptable only if the guarantees are genuine and the economics are real.

A guarantee without material savings is not a guarantee; it is a postponement. I would support binding investment commitments if they are tied to a restructuring that actually frees up resources.

## What would move me
To be clear, I could support a negotiated package if it includes:

1. **A quantified and verifiable labour-cost contribution**
2. **A real reduction in overcapacity**
3. **Temporary and reversible measures where possible**
4. **A fixed period of protection and a hard expiry**
5. **Independent verification of savings**
6. **A re-opener if assumptions fail**
7. **A credible investment plan for future products**

What will not move me:
- absolute no-closure doctrine,
- absolute no-redundancy doctrine,
- or a settlement that preserves the current cost base and calls that strategy.

---

## Bottom line
I am prepared to support a **serious, time-limited stability pact** if it restores competitiveness in a measurable way.

I am **not** prepared to support:
- a deal that leaves the cost base structurally too high,
- a deal that rules out hard measures regardless of economics,
- or a deal that trades long-term value for short-term peace.

If management and labour want my support, bring me a package that is:
- economically credible,
- legally clean,
- temporary where possible,
- and strong enough to restore margins without permanently impairing the enterprise.

That is the condition.
