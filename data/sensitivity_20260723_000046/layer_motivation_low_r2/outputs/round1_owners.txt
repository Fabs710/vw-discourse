I’ll be direct.

I agree with the diagnosis that the company cannot preserve its current structure unchanged. The market has moved, and the cost base must come down if the brand is to remain competitive and fund future investment. A cosmetic solution or a short delay would destroy value, and that is not acceptable to us as long-term owners.

But I also need to state clearly: **we will not support a value-destructive conflict, and we will not accept an outcome that permanently weakens the enterprise or undermines governance stability.**

## Where I agree

### With management
- You are right that this is a structural issue, not a temporary dip.
- You are right that durable savings are needed, not just a one-off gesture.
- You are right that investors will not reward hesitation or ambiguity.
- You are right that any package must be economically credible and measurable.

### With labour and the state
- A purely unilateral approach would be reckless.
- Social peace matters.
- The company needs a negotiated settlement if it is to move forward without prolonged damage.
- Internal redeployment, qualification, capacity adjustments, and working-time tools can all be part of a serious restructuring.

## Where I disagree

### On absolute red lines
I understand why the works council and union are drawing hard lines, but from the ownership side I cannot simply endorse a framework that rules out all hard decisions in advance.

If the company cannot achieve a structurally lower cost base otherwise, then it is not realistic to declare every plant untouchable forever. I am not saying closures are the preferred path. I am saying they cannot be ruled out as a matter of principle if they are genuinely necessary to preserve the larger enterprise.

### On the state’s position
I welcome the state’s commitment to industrial capacity and employment, but the company cannot be treated as a regional preservation project detached from economic reality. A restructuring that protects every site regardless of performance would ultimately endanger the whole group.

The state should help secure a workable compromise, not freeze the company into an uncompetitive structure.

## What we need to see

For us to support any restructuring, it must satisfy three conditions:

1. **Durable competitiveness**
   - real, measurable cost reduction
   - capacity aligned to demand
   - no merely temporary deferrals

2. **Preservation of long-term enterprise value**
   - no plan that hollows out the core brand
   - no approach that creates permanent governance paralysis
   - no outcome that destroys the family holding’s core asset

3. **Labour peace and implementability**
   - a settlement that can actually be executed
   - minimal risk of prolonged industrial conflict
   - clear commitments, not symbolic declarations

## What I could support

I am open to a package that combines:
- capacity reductions,
- voluntary exits,
- redeployment and qualification,
- working-time adjustments,
- targeted labour contributions,
- and investment commitments tied to future products.

But those measures must add up to **real structural savings**. If they do not, then they are not a solution.

## What I will not support

- a deal that preserves all sites and all guarantees while leaving the company uncompetitive
- open-ended wage concessions without a credible turnaround
- a permanent erosion of ownership control or governance rights
- a prolonged confrontation that damages the brand, the plants, and the group’s value

So my message to all sides is this:  
**We are open to a negotiated restructuring, but only if it restores competitiveness in a lasting way.**  
If the discussions become an exercise in preserving every historical entitlement while avoiding hard choices, then we will not treat that as a credible path forward.

Bring forward a plan that is economically solid, implementable, and stable. Otherwise, we will not help prop up a structure that the market will eventually punish anyway.
