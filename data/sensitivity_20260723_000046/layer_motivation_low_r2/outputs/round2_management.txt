I will respond directly.

## First: what I agree with
I agree with everyone on one point: **this cannot be a cosmetic exercise**.

A weak, temporary patch that preserves the current cost base is not an answer. A long strike campaign is also not an answer. The company needs a durable restructuring that restores competitiveness and creates room to invest in future products and technology through 2030.

I also agree that a unilateral management diktat will not work. We need a negotiated settlement because the governance structure and co-determination rules make that reality unavoidable.

## Where I disagree sharply
What I cannot accept is the repeated attempt to make **“no closures” and “no compulsory redundancies” absolute principles** before we even test whether they are economically compatible with survival.

That is not a serious restructuring position. It is a demand that management carry all the risk while labour rules out the main instruments that can deliver structural change.

### To the works council
You say alternatives exist. Maybe they do. But you have not shown that they are sufficient.

You keep insisting on:
- no plant closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- no hidden closures,
- binding guarantees through 2030.

That may sound protective, but from where I sit it risks freezing a cost structure that the market will punish. If the package does not produce **real and lasting capacity reduction and labour-cost relief**, then it does not solve the problem.

I am not prepared to commit to a framework that says in advance that German plants are untouchable regardless of economics.

### To the industrial union
You are trying to draw a bright line around the collective agreement and call that a solution. It is not.

Temporary contributions, bonus restraint, working-time flexibility, and voluntary exits are useful only if they add up to a **material structural improvement**. So far you are offering instruments without accepting the scale of the problem.

A “no closures, no redundancies, no permanent wage effect” package only works if it delivers enough savings. If it does not, then it is just delay.

### To the regional state government
You are right to say the company cannot be reduced to a regional preservation project. But you are also not being honest if you imply that the state can defend all sites and still expect the company to become competitive.

If the state wants to help secure a deal, then it must help secure **economic reality**, not just political comfort. That means accepting that some capacity consolidation may be unavoidable.

### To the controlling family holding
I agree with your point that value destruction through prolonged conflict is unacceptable. But value is also destroyed by pretending that all historical structures can remain intact.

If we want to preserve the long-term enterprise value, we need a package that materially reduces the cost base. I do not need public lectures about “governance stability” if the result is a structurally uncompetitive business.

### To institutional investors
You are right to insist on measurable outcomes. But you also need to be clear that:
- temporary gestures,
- symbolic labour contributions,
- and soft promises of future productivity

do not count as structural improvement unless they are sufficient in scale.

I will not support a deal that merely postpones the hard choices and then asks investors to accept another round of disappointment later.

## On the moderator’s compromise options

### 1) “No forced redundancies, closures only as ultima ratio”
I can work with the **principle** that forced redundancies are a last resort, but only if that principle is not turned into a de facto veto.

What I will not accept is language that makes closures impossible in practice even if the numbers do not work. If a fallback is needed to preserve competitiveness, it must remain on the table.

So: **I can accept no immediate forced redundancies if there is a credible, jointly verified path to equivalent savings. I cannot accept an absolute exclusion.**

### 2) “Site guarantees in exchange for quantified labour contributions”
This is closer to a serious basis for talks.

If labour is prepared to make a **material, measurable, time-bound contribution**, then I am prepared to discuss a moratorium on closures and compulsory redundancies for a defined period.

But that only works if the package includes:
- a **real reduction in labour cost**, not a symbolic one,
- **capacity reduction**, not just redeployment on paper,
- **productivity gains**, not just promises,
- and a **clear re-opener mechanism** if savings targets are missed.

### 3) “Two-track restructuring”
This is the most realistic approach, but only if track two is not a stalling tactic.

Track one must deliver immediate, verifiable savings:
- working-time flexibility,
- bonus moderation,
- voluntary departures,
- redeployment,
- qualification,
- and concrete efficiency gains.

Track two must preserve the ability to take deeper structural measures if track one does not close the gap. I am not interested in a process that replaces action with permanent review.

## What would move management
Let me be explicit about what can move us.

We would consider suspending immediate closure plans and forced redundancies if we receive a package that includes all of the following:

1. **Quantified, durable labour-cost relief**
   - not just temporary optics
   - not just one-off concessions
   - something that meaningfully changes the cost base

2. **Real capacity reduction**
   - not merely reshuffling production
   - not hidden overcapacity
   - a verifiable step-down in structural output capacity

3. **Implementable productivity measures**
   - working-time flexibility
   - process changes
   - redeployment and qualification
   - voluntary exit tools

4. **Binding governance and timing**
   - a clear timetable
   - independent verification
   - a re-opener if assumptions fail

5. **Investment certainty**
   - yes, there must be commitments on future products and technology
   - but those commitments have to be tied to a restructuring that actually frees up funds

If those conditions are not met, then a “no-closure” promise is just a costly illusion.

## My answer to labour’s central claim
You keep saying: “Do not make workers pay for strategic failures.”

I understand the political argument, but from a corporate-responsibility perspective it is incomplete.

The company has a duty to all stakeholders, including the owners, investors, and the workforce as a whole. If German operations remain structurally uncompetitive, then refusing deep adjustment does not protect workers — it stores up a worse crisis later.

So yes, the burden must be shared. But shared sacrifice does not mean equal sacrifice in form. It means everyone contributes in a way that restores the enterprise.

Right now, labour is asking for binding protection first and structural adjustment second. Management’s position is the reverse: structural adjustment first, then we can lock in protection where the economics allow it.

That is the real divide.

---

## Bottom line
I am not moving to an agreement on the basis of:
- absolute no-closure demands,
- absolute no-redundancy demands,
- or a package that leaves the cost base broadly intact.

What I am prepared to consider is a **serious, measurable, durable restructuring package** that may avoid closures and forced redundancies **if** it delivers equivalent structural savings.

If the other side wants to prevent closures, then it must produce the economics that make closures unnecessary. Otherwise, it is asking management to sign up to a future competitiveness problem we already know we have.

That is where I stand.
