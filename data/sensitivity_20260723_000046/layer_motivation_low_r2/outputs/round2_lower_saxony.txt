I will respond plainly.

## Where I agree
I agree with management, investors, and the family on one point: **the status quo is not sustainable**. The company needs a durable restructuring, not a cosmetic pause. The cost base has to come down, and the company must regain competitiveness so it can invest in future products and technologies.

I also agree with the works council and union that **a unilateral “dictate” solution would be wrong and dangerous**. A prolonged confrontation would damage the company, the plants, and the region. Social peace matters.

## Where I disagree
What I do **not** accept is management’s framing that the only credible route is to keep closures and compulsory redundancies on the table as normal instruments.

That is too blunt, too risky, and too destructive.

As the regional state shareholder, I cannot support an outcome that:
- guts employment in the state,
- weakens industrial capacity for the long term,
- or sidelines our co-determination and blocking role.

Those are not bargaining chips for me. They are core interests.

### To management
You have not yet made the case that plant closures in Germany are truly unavoidable rather than the quickest way to force concessions.

If you want the state to support a restructuring, then you need to prove:
1. that every serious alternative has been exhausted,
2. that site protection and competitiveness can be reconciled,
3. and that the burden is not being shifted mainly onto the workforce.

I will not endorse a plan that treats German sites as disposable.

### To the works council and union
I understand — and in large part share — your refusal to accept closures and forced redundancies as a starting point. I also agree that workers should not be made the sole shock absorber for strategic missteps.

But I need to be equally direct: **a blanket refusal to adjust anything is not enough either**. The company does need structural savings. So if labour wants strong protection, it must also be ready for a **real, measurable contribution** that is temporary, bounded, and reversible.

That could include:
- working-time flexibility,
- redeployment,
- qualification,
- voluntary exits,
- moderation of bonuses or variable pay,
- and capacity adjustments that preserve sites.

What it cannot mean is a permanent lowering of standards or an open-ended erosion of the collective framework.

### To the family holding
You are right that value destruction through endless conflict would be unacceptable. But you are too quick to accept the logic that “competitiveness” automatically justifies deep cuts that weaken the regional base.

Long-term enterprise value depends on:
- productive sites,
- a skilled and motivated workforce,
- social peace,
- and stable governance.

If the restructuring destroys trust and regional legitimacy, it may save a line item and lose the industrial anchor.

### To investors
I hear your insistence on measurable, structural improvement. That is legitimate. But I would caution against reducing this to a purely numerical test in which plant closures are seen as the cleanest answer.

The political and execution costs of closures are enormous, especially here. A package that preserves sites while still delivering real savings may be more valuable than a harsh plan that triggers strikes, legal conflict, and public backlash.

## On the proposed compromise paths
The moderator’s ideas are not wrong, but I will set a clear condition:

### 1) “Closures as ultima ratio”
This can only be discussed if it is truly a last resort after a verified, negotiated process. It cannot become a standing threat that pressures labour into surrender.

### 2) “Site guarantees in exchange for quantified labour contributions”
This is the most realistic basis for talks.

I could support a package if it includes:
- binding site guarantees for a defined period,
- no compulsory redundancies during that period,
- a temporary, quantified labour contribution,
- investment commitments,
- and transparent review points.

### 3) “Two-track restructuring”
This can work only if track one is real restructuring, not just a delay tactic, and track two is not a coded route to closures.

## My position
The state will support a negotiated restructuring only if it:
- **protects jobs and sites as far as possible,**
- **preserves co-determination and our veto role,**
- **delivers real structural savings,**
- and **keeps the company a regional industrial anchor**.

I am open to difficult measures. I am **not** open to a solution that makes the state merely witness the dismantling of our industrial base.

So my answer is:
- yes to restructuring,
- yes to shared contribution,
- no to plant closures as a default,
- no to forced redundancies as an easy option,
- and no to any deal that weakens the state’s governance position.

If management wants our support, bring a serious plan that protects sites and jobs while restoring competitiveness. If not, do not expect us to wave it through.
