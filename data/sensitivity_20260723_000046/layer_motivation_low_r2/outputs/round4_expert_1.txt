From a German labour-law and industrial-relations perspective, the key issue is not whether a compromise is possible — it is **how to draft it so that temporary concessions do not become a precedent, do not erode co-determination, and do not silently weaken site guarantees**.

## 1) Core legal frame

In this scenario, several different legal regimes are in play:

- **Collective bargaining law**: the union’s wage agreement can regulate pay, working time, bonuses, and related concessions.
- **Works constitution law (BetrVG)**: the works council has information, consultation, and co-determination rights on many operational measures.
- **Company co-determination / supervisory board level**: major structural decisions may require board majorities and, in this scenario, are politically and governance-wise veto-sensitive.
- **Site guarantee / Standortschutz**: if contractually agreed, this becomes a binding promise, but only within the scope and wording actually chosen.
- **Industrial peace and strike law**: once the peace obligation expires, industrial action may resume unless constrained by a valid collective settlement.

The practical problem is that **temporary concessions are legally possible**, but if poorly drafted they can:
1. create an implied acceptance of restructuring logic,
2. dilute future bargaining positions,
3. blur the distinction between temporary burden-sharing and permanent rights erosion,
4. or create ambiguity that management later uses as precedent.

## 2) Can “temporary” measures be drafted safely?

Yes — **in principle**. The key is to draft them as:

- **time-limited**
- **expressly exceptional**
- **without prejudice**
- **without waiver of rights**
- **without precedent**
- **automatically expiring**
- **subject to reversion to the status quo ante**

That said, “temporary” is not magic. If the commercial context shows that a concession is obviously intended as the first step toward permanent cuts, then a court or the industrial-relations actors will treat it as such in practice, even if the wording is polished.

## 3) What must be included to avoid precedent risk

A robust drafting package would normally include:

### A. Express no-precedent clause
State clearly that:
- the agreement is **exceptional**
- it is concluded **in light of the present crisis only**
- it does **not establish a general principle**
- it does **not constitute recognition** that similar measures are permissible outside the specific term and context

This is important industrial-relations hygiene, though it cannot eliminate all future argument.

### B. No-waiver / no-rights-erosion clause
The agreement should state that:
- works-council rights under the BetrVG remain unchanged,
- collective-bargaining rights remain intact,
- the union and works council do not waive objections to future restructuring measures,
- and no silence or consent under the pact may be treated as general approval for later closures, relocations, or outsourcing.

### C. Automatic expiry and reversion
The concessions should:
- end on a fixed date,
- revert automatically without need for notice,
- and not require a separate revocation exercise to restore the baseline.

This avoids “temporary” becoming sticky.

### D. Narrow purpose clause
Define the measure as being:
- solely for bridging the current crisis,
- solely for preserving employment and investment capacity during the defined period,
- and not for permanently redesigning the collective-bargaining structure.

### E. Non-transferability / non-cascade language
Avoid language that allows the measure to be:
- applied to other sites by analogy,
- extended to other plants as a template,
- or used as a reference point in future rounds.

That matters in large groups where one site’s concession tends to become another site’s benchmark.

## 4) Protecting co-determination rights

This is the most sensitive point.

A temporary labour contribution or site pact must **not** be drafted so that management gains a de facto unilateral room to maneuver later. Specifically:

- The works council’s **information, consultation, and co-determination rights** must remain untouched.
- The agreement should explicitly state that it does not replace statutory co-determination procedures.
- For structural measures — reorganization, transfer, workforce reductions, plant rationalization — the normal statutory and company-level procedures still apply.
- If a restructuring agreement is concluded, it should **supplement** but not **supersede** codetermination rights except to the extent legally permissible and expressly agreed.

A common drafting mistake is to create a “stability pact” that is so broad it appears to pre-approve future restructuring steps. That should be avoided.

## 5) Protecting binding site guarantees

A site guarantee is only as strong as its wording.

### If the goal is a genuine protection clause:
It should specify:
- exact site or sites covered,
- exact duration,
- whether only core production or also functions are protected,
- whether relocation within the group is excluded,
- whether outsourcing is excluded,
- whether partial closures or shrinkage are included,
- and what counts as a breach.

### Watch the loopholes
The usual weak points are:
- “site remains operational” but capacity is cut to a token level,
- “no closure” but major functions are transferred out,
- “no compulsory redundancies” but voluntary exit pressure is used to hollow out the site,
- “no relocation” but procurement or engineering are shifted elsewhere,
- “investment commitment” without minimum amounts or project milestones.

If the parties want a meaningful guarantee, they need **operational definitions**, not only headline language.

## 6) Avoiding hidden erosion through “temporary contributions”

Temporary concessions often include:
- bonus moderation,
- wage deferral,
- working-time extensions,
- reduced supplements,
- more flexible shifts,
- temporary suspension of components of pay.

Legally and industrial-relations-wise, the danger is that management later argues:
- the company has learned to operate at the lower level,
- the concession shows comparability,
- or the temporary cut proves the old level was not essential.

That is why it is important to include:
- **explicit reversion**
- **no admission of permanent affordability**
- **no use as precedent in future bargaining**
- **no linkage to individual performance discipline or site selection**

## 7) Re-opener clauses: useful, but risky if too broad

A re-opener can be sensible if truly exceptional events occur. But draft it carefully.

### Good practice
Tie re-opener rights to:
- objectively defined macroeconomic shocks,
- a severe and measurable deviation in sales or production,
- or a jointly verified breach of assumptions.

### Bad practice
Avoid clauses that allow management to reopen merely because:
- it later wants deeper cuts,
- forecasts were “less favorable than expected” without objective threshold,
- or competitive pressure generally persists.

Otherwise, the re-opener becomes a back door to permanent insecurity.

## 8) Verification mechanisms

If the package is meant to reassure capital while protecting labour, it should include:
- independent verification of savings,
- a joint monitoring committee,
- clear KPI definitions,
- and agreed accounting treatment of the measures.

But beware: verification should not become a weapon for retrospectively re-litigating the bargain. It should verify whether the agreed package works, not whether one side can extract more by claiming disappointment.

## 9) On plant closures as “ultima ratio”

Legally, parties can agree that certain steps occur only after defined conditions. Industrial-relations-wise, however, if you write “closure as ultima ratio,” you should be aware of two consequences:

- It may preserve management’s fallback leverage.
- It may also reassure investors that the option is not abolished.

For the workforce, this is often unacceptable because it keeps the threat alive. For management, an absolute ban may be unacceptable because it removes contingency. The drafting compromise is usually:

- closures are **not ruled out in principle**,
- but **cannot be initiated within the pact period**,
- except under tightly defined, jointly verified trigger events,
- and only after exhaustion of internal measures and formal consultation/co-determination steps.

That is the legally cleaner route if a full ban is not politically feasible.

## 10) Practical judgment

My assessment is:

- **Yes**, temporary concessions can be drafted so they do not automatically erode co-determination rights.
- **Yes**, they can be structured so they do not create a permanent precedent.
- **Yes**, site guarantees can be made binding, but only if the scope, duration, and exceptions are precise.
- **No**, wording alone cannot eliminate the industrial-relations risk if the substance is clearly a stepping stone to future cuts.

So the legal drafting must be matched by industrial-relations credibility.

## 11) Minimum drafting essentials

If I were reviewing such a package, I would insist on at least these elements:

1. **Fixed term**
2. **Automatic expiry**
3. **No-precedent clause**
4. **No-waiver clause**
5. **Express preservation of statutory co-determination**
6. **Precise site scope**
7. **Precise exclusions on outsourcing/relocation**
8. **Defined contribution measures**
9. **Objective trigger criteria for any re-opener**
10. **Independent verification**
11. **No use of the temporary concession as an interpretive benchmark later**
