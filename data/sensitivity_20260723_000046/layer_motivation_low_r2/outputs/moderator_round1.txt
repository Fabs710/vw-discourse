### Core impasse

The main blockage is not about whether change is needed; all sides broadly accept that it is. The impasse is about **how far restructuring may go and what can be ruled out in advance**.

More specifically, the deadlock sits on three linked questions:

1. **Plant closures and forced redundancies**
   - Labour side: rejects both categorically.
   - Management and investors: will not rule them out if needed for structural savings.
   - State and family: prefer avoidance, but do not fully endorse absolute prohibitions.

2. **What counts as an acceptable alternative**
   - Labour offers alternatives only if jobs/sites are guaranteed.
   - Management says alternatives are fine only if they deliver equivalent, durable savings.
   - Investors insist the numbers must clearly restore competitiveness.

3. **Burden-sharing versus competitiveness**
   - Labour wants shared sacrifice with binding site/job protection.
   - Management and capital want structural cost reduction.
   - State and family want a compromise that avoids both economic failure and social dislocation.

So the core impasse is: **labour seeks guaranteed protection first, while management and capital seek guaranteed savings first**.

---

### Possible compromise positions

#### 1) “No forced redundancies, closures only as an ultima ratio, with binding trigger criteria”
A possible bridge is to agree that:
- **compulsory redundancies are excluded for a defined period**, and
- **plant closures are not ruled out in principle, but only as a last resort**
- after a jointly verified process showing that agreed alternatives cannot deliver the required savings.

This would require:
- a clear list of alternative measures to be exhausted first,
- an independent verification mechanism,
- predefined economic trigger conditions,
- and a binding consultation / co-determination process before any closure step.

This may bridge:
- **labour’s need for protection**
- with **management and investors’ need for credible fallback options**.

---

#### 2) “Site guarantees in exchange for quantified labour and capacity contributions”
A second compromise is a package with:
- **site guarantees for a fixed period**,
- **no compulsory redundancies during that period**,
- **capacity reductions via voluntary exits, redeployment, working-time reduction, and qualification**,
- **temporary labour contributions** such as bonus restraint, working-time flexibility, or moderated pay elements,
- and **binding investment commitments** tied to product programs.

This would require:
- exact savings targets,
- a review point after a defined period,
- and a re-opener clause if market assumptions materially change.

This may bridge:
- **works council/union demands for protection**
- with **management/investor demands for measurable structural improvement**.

---

#### 3) “Two-track restructuring: immediate efficiency measures plus safeguarded review of structural options”
A third option is a two-track approach:
- **Track 1:** immediate non-disruptive measures  
  - internal redeployment  
  - qualification  
  - working-time tools  
  - temporary cost moderation  
  - accelerated productivity measures
- **Track 2:** a jointly governed review of deeper structural measures  
  - including consolidation scenarios  
  - with closures treated as contingent, not pre-decided

This would include:
- a joint steering committee,
- state participation,
- transparent financial benchmarks,
- and a date by which the parties must either confirm the first package worked or move to the second track.

This may bridge:
- **the need to avoid immediate escalation**
- while preserving **management’s insistence on structural fallback options**.
