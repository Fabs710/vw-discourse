## My position in round 4: unchanged in substance

I will be blunt: the comments from the works council and union still do **not** solve the core problem. They restate protection demands, but they still do not show how the company reaches a **durable, structural cost and capacity reduction** sufficient to restore competitiveness.

I agree on one point only: **temporary concessions can be drafted safely** without waiving co-determination or creating a permanent precedent. That is technically useful. But it does **not** answer the economic question. A legally neat temporary pact that leaves the cost base too high is still a bad deal.

## To the works council
I understand your concern about trust. But trust does not restore margins. Your position remains:
- no closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- no closure fallback,
- no forced layoff fallback,
- binding protection through 2030,
- and internal measures only.

That is not a restructuring strategy unless you can prove it closes the gap.

You keep demanding that management first prove necessity for closures. I do not need a lecture on why closures are undesirable. I need evidence that your preferred package can actually deliver:
- lower structural costs,
- lower overcapacity,
- and a return to target margins.

So far, I have not seen that.

If you want me to rule out hard instruments, then bring a package that replaces them with **equivalent, durable savings**. Not aspirational language. Not moral arguments. Numbers.

## To the industrial union
You are correct that temporary employee contributions can be made reversible and non-prejudicial. That is not in dispute.

What is still missing is the hard part:
- what exact cost reduction do those temporary contributions produce?
- for how long?
- and how do they translate into a lasting improvement in the brand’s economics?

Working-time flexibility, bonus moderation, redeployment, qualification, voluntary exits — yes, these are all discussable. But if the total effect is insufficient, then they are simply not enough.

Your insistence on an absolute exclusion of closures and compulsory redundancies is a red line for you. Fine. But it is not a substitute for a viable business case. If the package does not structurally fix the company, then I cannot accept being boxed into a comfort-first settlement that leaves the cost base uncompetitive.

## To the regional state government
You want industrial peace and a protected regional base. I understand that. But your position still leans too heavily toward blocking hard choices before the economics have been settled.

I agree that the state should not rubber-stamp destruction. I do **not** agree that the state should effectively pre-commit to no-closure / no-redundancy outcomes regardless of what the numbers show.

If the state wants to help, then help secure:
- a serious labour-cost contribution,
- real capacity consolidation,
- and a package that restores margin and investment capacity.

Not a political freeze that preserves jobs today and weakens them tomorrow.

## To the controlling family holding
You are right that prolonged conflict would destroy value. You are also right that the deal must be economically real.

Where I still disagree with you is that you want to keep the “hard measures” available only as an abstract necessity test, while simultaneously expecting management to be constrained by political pressure to avoid them. That ambiguity helps nobody.

I am prepared to suspend immediate closure plans **if** there is a package that actually delivers:
- meaningful savings,
- meaningful capacity reduction,
- and a credible margin path to 2030.

If the package does not do that, then maintaining closures and redundancies as fallback tools remains necessary. That is not a threat. It is responsible governance.

## To institutional investors
You are right to insist on durable, measurable, implementation-ready outcomes. That is exactly the standard I apply.

But your support cannot be conditional on pretending that the socially softer option is automatically acceptable. A soft option that fails economically is the worst of both worlds.

So I need:
1. a quantified savings gap,
2. a quantified package of alternative measures,
3. a comparison showing that the package closes the gap,
4. and an explanation of why the result is durable, not temporary optics.

Until then, I cannot sign off on a closure ban or a permanent exclusion of forced redundancies.

## On the moderator’s proposals

### 1) Fixed-term stability pact with hard protections and hard metrics
Potentially useful, but only if the numbers are hard enough.

For me, the pact must include:
- a material labour-cost contribution,
- verifiable capacity reduction,
- a fixed expiry,
- and a re-opener if the savings are not reached.

If it is just a safe harbour for the status quo, I will not support it.

### 2) Conditional no-closure moratorium linked to verified restructuring milestones
This is closer to acceptable, but only as a process tool — not as a permanent closure veto.

I can accept verification. I can accept milestones. I cannot accept a framework that turns those into a disguised prohibition on necessary structural action.

If the milestones are missed and the economics still do not work, then deeper measures must remain available.

### 3) Site-and-investment guarantee in exchange for bounded labour contributions
This is the most promising path, but only if it is tied to:
- real savings,
- real capacity alignment,
- and a clear effect on profitability.

A guarantee without economic delivery is not a guarantee. It is just deferred conflict.

## What would move me
Very specifically:

- a quantified, durable labour-cost contribution,
- a credible reduction in overcapacity,
- a savings package that materially improves margins,
- independent verification,
- and a path that supports investment in future products and technology.

If those conditions are met, then immediate closures may not be necessary.

If they are not met, then I will not pretend that moral objections solve the balance sheet.

## Bottom line
I am not moving to an absolute no-closure, no-redundancy framework.

I am open to a negotiated stability pact, but only if it produces:
- durable cost reduction,
- real capacity alignment,
- and a credible route back to competitiveness through 2030.

What I will not accept is a compromise that protects every legacy structure, leaves the company structurally weak, and calls that “social responsibility.”

That is not restructuring. That is delay.
