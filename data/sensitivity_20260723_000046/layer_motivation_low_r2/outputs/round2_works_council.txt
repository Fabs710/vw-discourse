I will respond directly.

First: the moderator’s framing confirms the real issue. The impasse is not whether the company needs change. It is whether management and capital are trying to make the workforce pay for strategic errors by destroying sites, jobs, and the job-security framework. On that point, my position remains unchanged.

## To management
You say you need “durable savings.” We do not dispute that the company needs a credible plan. What we dispute is your attempt to define “credible” as closures and forced redundancies.

That is not the only structurally effective route, and you know it. You have not shown that every alternative has been exhausted. You have not shown that the German plants are the only way to deliver savings. And you have not shown why workers must accept a permanent breach of trust after years of flexibility and restraint.

So let me be clear:
- **No plant closures in Germany.**
- **No compulsory redundancies.**
- **No unilateral dismantling of the job-security framework.**

If your plan cannot work without those measures, then it is not a turnaround plan — it is a transfer of risk onto employees.

## To the industrial union
We are aligned on the red lines. That matters.

I also agree with your point that “shared sacrifice” cannot mean an open-ended wage cut or the erosion of collective standards. But I want to sharpen this: any labour contribution must be **strictly temporary, bounded, and reversible**, and only in exchange for hard guarantees.

So yes, we can discuss:
- working-time flexibility,
- redeployment,
- qualification,
- voluntary exit schemes,
- temporary moderation of bonuses or special payments,
- productivity measures,
- internal capacity adjustments.

But only under conditions that are not negotiable from our side:
- **binding site guarantees through 2030,**
- **no compulsory redundancies,**
- **real co-determination in shaping the restructuring,**
- and **no hidden closure by outsourcing or relocation.**

If management wants a contribution from labour, then labour must get something real in return. Not promises. Guarantees.

## To the regional state government
You say you prefer avoiding closures and forced redundancies, but you are still leaving the door open to them “if necessary.” That is exactly the problem.

If the state wants to be taken seriously as an employee-friendly anchor shareholder, then it must use its blocking position to stop a false choice between “closures” and “failure.” There are alternative restructuring paths, but management only comes under pressure if the state and owners make it clear that plants and jobs are not bargaining chips.

I will support a compromise only if the state helps secure:
- a binding no-closure commitment,
- a binding no-compulsory-redundancy commitment,
- and a governance process in which employee representatives shape the solution, not just react to it.

Anything weaker means the state is merely managing the optics while leaving workers exposed.

## To the controlling family holding
You say you will not accept a value-destructive conflict. Neither will we.

But I reject your implication that preserving every German plant is somehow equivalent to “freezing the company into an uncompetitive structure.” That is a false dichotomy. You are using “competitiveness” as a blanket justification for measures that would permanently weaken industrial capacity and destroy trust.

Your real concern is enterprise value. Ours is jobs, sites, and long-term industrial presence. Those are not mutually exclusive if management stops treating closures as the default answer.

If you want stability, then support:
- binding site protection,
- no forced layoffs,
- internal restructuring first,
- and a genuine long-term product and investment commitment.

If you refuse those, then do not pretend the problem is labour resistance. The real problem will be the decision to break the historic compact.

## To institutional investors
You keep saying the package must be “material” and “structural.” Fine. But you are also ignoring that a plant closure is not some abstract efficiency measure. It is a permanent destruction of productive capacity, skilled labour, and social trust — and it creates major execution risk.

So do not oversimplify this into margin math. If you want a stable turnaround, you need industrial peace, know-how retention, and a workforce that is committed to the future of the company. That will not happen if you impose closures and redundancies and call it credibility.

A package can be structural without being destructive:
- capacity reduction through redeployment and voluntary tools,
- shift and working-time adjustments,
- qualification and conversion,
- selective productivity improvements,
- temporary labour contributions,
- and long-term investment commitments.

What we will not accept is a package that is “credible” only because it breaks the workforce.

## On the moderator’s compromise ideas
Let me be explicit: I am **not** accepting any model in which closures remain a standing option and forced redundancies are merely delayed until some later trigger.

That is not a compromise. That is a threat with a timetable.

### Option 1: “Closures as ultima ratio”
Rejected in its current form.  
Why? Because once you build closures into the logic of the agreement, the pressure point remains on the workforce. The company will always be able to say: “If you do not concede enough now, the fallback is closure later.” That is unacceptable.

### Option 2: “Site guarantees in exchange for quantified labour contributions”
This is closer to a basis for discussion, but only if the guarantees are real and enforceable. I stress:
- no forced redundancies,
- no plant closures,
- no outsourcing disguised as restructuring,
- no blank cheque on labour cost cuts.

If management wants a labour contribution, it must be **time-limited and paired with hard guarantees through 2030**.

### Option 3: “Two-track restructuring”
We can discuss a two-track process only if track two is not a coded route to closures. The first track must be the real restructuring: internal redeployment, working-time tools, qualification, product and investment commitment. The second track cannot be a backdoor for shutting plants if management later decides it wants more savings.

## What would move us
Very little, unless the following are on the table in binding form:
1. **No plant closures in Germany through 2030**
2. **No compulsory redundancies through 2030**
3. **Preservation of co-determination in every restructuring step**
4. **Meaningful investment commitments for future products**
5. **Capacity reduction only through voluntary, internal, and reversible measures**
6. **A negotiated labour contribution that is temporary and clearly limited**

Without that, the works council will not soften. We will resist in the supervisory board, in the plants, and publicly.

## My bottom line
The workforce will not be blackmailed into accepting closures and layoffs as the price of “competitiveness.” If the company wants a real solution, then it must stop asking employees to pay for a crisis they did not create and for a strategy they did not control.

We are ready to discuss a serious restructuring. We are not ready to accept plant closures or compulsory redundancies.
