I will respond plainly and from my position as the regional state government and anchor shareholder.

## To management
I agree with you on one point: this company needs real restructuring, not a cosmetic pause.  
But I **do not** accept your repeated insistence that plant closures and compulsory redundancies must remain live instruments from the outset of the process.

You still have not made the comparative case that less destructive measures are insufficient. What I have heard is:
- “the cost base is too high,”
- “capacity is misaligned,”
- “we need credibility,”

but not a convincing demonstration that:
- internal redeployment,
- qualification,
- working-time tools,
- voluntary exits,
- temporary labour contributions,
- product and investment commitments,
- and capacity consolidation without closures

cannot close the gap to a serious degree.

That matters because my mandate is not abstract competitiveness at any cost. It is to protect jobs, plants, and the company’s role as a regional industrial anchor. If you want my support, show me a restructuring path that does that and still improves competitiveness.

I am open to **temporary, bounded, reversible** measures. I am **not** open to a framework that normalizes closure threats or treats the workforce as the first adjustment variable.

## To the works council
I agree with your core red lines:
- no plant closures as the default answer,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- no backdoor relocation or outsourcing,
- and preservation of co-determination.

That is not rigidity; it is the minimum basis for trust.

I also take seriously the expert point that temporary concessions can be drafted without eroding statutory rights or creating a permanent precedent, if they are:
- time-limited,
- expressly non-prejudicial,
- tied to clear expiry,
- and automatically reversible.

That opens a serious compromise route. It does **not** mean the company can be made competitive while nothing changes. It means labour can contribute temporarily without surrendering the industrial base.

So yes: I can support a **temporary stability pact** if it includes:
- binding site protection,
- no compulsory redundancies for a defined period,
- preservation of co-determination,
- clear investment commitments,
- and explicit reversion to the baseline after expiry.

What I will not support is a package that asks workers to concede now while management keeps the right to come back later and close plants anyway.

## To the industrial union
Your discipline on the drafting issues is useful. The expert confirmed what matters legally: temporary concessions can be made safe if they are tightly written and non-prejudicial.

That is precisely why I think a negotiated package is possible **without** keeping closures as a standing threat.

But I also want to be clear: a temporary labour contribution only makes sense if it is matched by a serious commitment from management and owners on:
- job security,
- site protection,
- investment,
- and transparent restructuring goals.

I am not interested in a symbolic peace agreement. I am interested in a package that actually preserves the industrial base and gives the company room to recover.

## To the controlling family holding
I agree with you that value destruction through prolonged conflict is unacceptable. I also agree that the deal must be economically credible.

Where I differ is this: you are still too comfortable with the idea that hard measures remain necessary before the case for alternatives has been fully tested.

That is not stewardship. Stewardship means protecting the productive base, preserving legitimacy in the home region, and restoring the company’s strength without unnecessary damage.

I can support a time-limited compromise if it:
- protects German sites,
- preserves jobs,
- improves competitiveness,
- and avoids a public rupture.

I will not support a settlement that makes the workforce carry the burden while the company retains the option to dismantle the regional anchor later.

## To institutional investors
I understand your demand for durable, measurable improvements. That demand is legitimate.

But you are too quick to assume that only closures or compulsory redundancies can produce credibility. A deal can be credible if it includes:
- quantified labour-cost relief,
- real capacity alignment,
- temporary and reversible concessions,
- binding investment commitments,
- and implementation certainty.

What I need from you is not a slogan about toughness. I need a comparative case showing why a non-closure package cannot work. Until then, I will not support treating closures as an ordinary fallback.

## My position on the moderator’s compromise paths

### 1) Fixed-term stability pact with hard protections and hard metrics
This is the only path I see as workable, **if** the protections are real.

For the state, that means:
- no plant closures during the pact period,
- no compulsory redundancies during the pact period,
- binding site guarantees,
- temporary and reversible labour contributions,
- and independent monitoring of whether the package is actually delivering savings.

This is the kind of settlement I could defend politically and economically.

### 2) Conditional no-closure moratorium linked to verified restructuring milestones
I am **not** interested in a moratorium that merely preserves the closure threat as a bargaining weapon.

If there are milestones, they must be used to verify whether the agreed restructuring works — not to keep workers permanently under threat. The state will not help institutionalize insecurity as the default governance model.

### 3) Site-and-investment guarantee in exchange for bounded labour contributions
This is the strongest route.

If labour is prepared to make temporary and quantifiable contributions, then management and owners must give something equally concrete in return:
- binding site commitments,
- investment commitments,
- product commitments,
- no forced redundancies for a defined period,
- and no closure speculation during the agreement.

That is the only exchange that looks like partnership rather than pressure.

## What would move the state government
I will support a binding compromise only if it protects:
1. jobs in the state,
2. sites in the state,
3. the company’s long-term viability,
4. and the state’s co-determination and veto role.

More concretely, I need:
- a no-closure period,
- a no-compulsory-redundancy period,
- temporary employee contributions that are reversible,
- verifiable investment and product commitments,
- and a restructuring path that preserves the company as a regional economic anchor.

If management refuses that, then I will use the leverage I have.

## Bottom line
I am **not** moving to an “closures and redundancies first” framework.

I am prepared to support a serious, time-limited stability pact, but only if it:
- protects German sites,
- protects employment,
- preserves co-determination,
- and still delivers measurable restructuring.

That is the line. Anything that strips out those protections is not a compromise I can back.
