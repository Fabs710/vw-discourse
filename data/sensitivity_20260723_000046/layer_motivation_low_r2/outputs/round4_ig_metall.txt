I will respond plainly.

## To the works council
We are aligned on the red lines:
- no plant closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- no erosion of co-determination,
- no outsourcing or relocation by the back door.

I support that line.

But I also want to be disciplined about what we are actually negotiating. The works council is right that temporary concessions must not become permanent cuts. That is exactly why any employee contribution must be:
- strictly time-limited,
- clearly reversible,
- explicitly non-prejudicial,
- and tied to automatic reversion.

If management wants labour to contribute, then it must accept that the burden is temporary and that the baseline returns. I will not support a package that quietly normalizes wage erosion.

Where I slightly differ from your framing is this: I do not object to **any** procedural language about verification or milestones if it is used to protect workers from unilateral abuse. What I reject is a structure where closures remain a standing threat. That part is non-negotiable.

## To management
You are still not answering the central point.

You keep saying “material restructuring first,” but you are not proving why that requires keeping closures and compulsory redundancies alive as fallback instruments. Repeating “the numbers matter” is not the same as showing that the only credible path runs through plant closure logic.

If you want the union to discuss concessions, bring:
1. a quantified savings gap,
2. a comparison of non-closure measures,
3. a binding investment and product commitment,
4. and a written agreement that excludes compulsory redundancies and plant closures.

Without that, your argument is just pressure.

And let me be clear: I do not accept the idea that competitiveness can only be restored by making workers fear closure. That is not a serious industrial strategy; it is leverage.

## On the moderator’s fixed-term stability pact
This is only acceptable if the package is built around the following:
- **no plant closures**
- **no compulsory redundancies**
- **binding site guarantees through 2030**
- **temporary, bounded labour contributions**
- **preservation of co-determination**
- **automatic reversion**
- **no precedent / no waiver clauses**

If management cannot accept that, then there is no pact worth discussing.

## To the regional state government
Your leverage matters only if you actually use it to block destructive outcomes. If you want a compromise, then your position must not drift toward “closures if needed later.”

We need the state to support:
- a binding no-closure settlement,
- no forced layoffs,
- and a restructuring path that preserves industrial capacity in Germany.

The state should not be helping management preserve a closure threat as a bargaining instrument.

## To the controlling family holding
Stewardship means more than insisting on margins. It means protecting the productive base and the workforce that makes those margins possible.

I agree with you on one point: a purely cosmetic deal would be value-destructive. But your conclusion is too quick if it leads you toward keeping closures on the table as a default fallback.

I am open to a serious negotiated package because I believe there is a better route:
- temporary employee contributions,
- internal capacity adjustment,
- investment commitments,
- no closures,
- no forced redundancies.

That is how you restore value without destroying trust.

## To institutional investors
You are right that a cosmetic settlement would be unacceptable. But you are wrong if you assume that only hard measures like closures or layoffs are “serious.”

Seriousness is measured by:
- durable savings,
- implementation certainty,
- industrial peace,
- and future competitiveness.

A stable workforce with temporary, reversible contributions can deliver that. A closure-driven conflict can just as easily destroy it.

So I am not offering you symbolism. I am offering you a different restructuring path that avoids self-inflicted damage.

## On the expert input
The expert confirmed something important: temporary concessions can be drafted safely if they are:
- time-limited,
- non-prejudicial,
- and clearly reversible,
while preserving statutory co-determination.

That supports my position, not management’s. It means labour can contribute without surrendering the core protections.

But the same expert point also reinforces why I reject closure threats: once you keep plant closure as a fallback, the agreement is no longer a clean temporary pact. It becomes a pressure mechanism.

## What I am prepared to discuss
I am prepared to discuss:
- bonus moderation,
- working-time flexibility,
- redeployment,
- qualification,
- voluntary exits,
- temporary pay elements,
- capacity reduction through internal tools,

if and only if they are exchanged for:
- no plant closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- binding site protection,
- and a clear expiry with automatic reversion.

## What I still reject
I reject:
- “closures as ultima ratio”
- “no immediate redundancies, but later if needed”
- “site protection only if economics permit”
- “temporary” measures that are not truly reversible
- and any arrangement that leaves workers carrying all the pain while management keeps the right to escalate later.

## Bottom line
There is a deal to be made, but only if it is a **real stability pact**, not a disguised threat.

My position remains:
- protect jobs,
- protect sites,
- protect co-determination,
- preserve the collective wage framework,
- and allow temporary employee contributions only in exchange for binding guarantees.

If management refuses that, then the conflict will continue.
