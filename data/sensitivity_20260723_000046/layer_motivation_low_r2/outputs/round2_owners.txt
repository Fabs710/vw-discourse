I’ll be direct.

## My position
I support a restructuring that restores competitiveness and margins. I do **not** support a package that preserves comfort today while locking in a structurally weak cost base and eroding long-term value.

At the same time, I do **not** want a prolonged confrontation that damages the brand, the plants, and governance stability. From an ownership perspective, both endless delay and uncontrolled escalation are value-destructive.

So my test is simple:
**Does the package deliver durable savings, real capacity alignment, and implementability without permanently impairing the enterprise?**
If not, I will not support it.

---

## To the works council
I understand your red lines politically and socially. But I do **not** accept absolute statements of “no closures, no compulsory redundancies, no matter what” as a governance basis.

Why not? Because they pre-empt the very question we have to answer: whether the current German cost and capacity structure can still be made competitive. If the answer is no, then ruling out all hard instruments in advance is not stewardship — it is denial.

That said, I do agree with you on one point: **a unilateral closure approach would be reckless and likely destructive.**  
And I also agree that hidden closures via outsourcing or relocation would be unacceptable if they are merely a workaround without real industrial logic.

What I could support is:
- a defined period of job protection,
- serious internal redeployment,
- qualification,
- voluntary exits,
- working-time flexibility,
- and a measurable labour contribution,

**if** these measures produce sufficient and durable savings.

But I will not endorse a framework that makes the cost of adjustment impossible to bear while assuming the market will be patient.

---

## To the industrial union
You are right that the burden cannot fall only on employees. I agree with that.

Where I disagree is your insistence that no closures and no compulsory redundancies can be absolute. If the alternative package does not truly close the structural gap, then the company is being asked to postpone pain until it becomes worse.

I do **not** see temporary concessions, bonus moderation, or working-time changes as substitutes for structural change unless they are substantial enough to move the economics. If they are real and material, then they can be part of the answer. If they are symbolic, they are not enough.

What I need from labour is not a declaration of principle, but a package that changes the cost base in a measurable way. If that is on offer, I am willing to support a negotiated path. If not, then we are just buying time.

---

## To the group and brand management
You are right that this is structural and not temporary. I agree with that.

You are also right that a cosmetic settlement would destroy value later. I agree.

Where I will be firm is this: management must not treat closures and compulsory redundancies as the only serious language available. They are instruments, not a strategy. If the company cannot reach a durable solution without them, then the burden is on management to prove that rigorously, not merely assert it.

So I expect you to come forward with:
- specific savings targets,
- a transparent comparison of alternatives,
- a clear business case for why particular measures are required,
- and a credible implementation path.

If the package can deliver the same economic result without closures, that is preferable. If it cannot, then I will not let the company drift into a weak compromise just to avoid conflict.

---

## To the regional state government
I appreciate your focus on industrial capacity and employment. That is important.

But I need to say clearly: the state cannot simultaneously insist on preserving all structures and still expect a competitive company. That tension has to be acknowledged honestly.

I do agree with you that value is not just a spreadsheet. Social peace, skilled labour, and regional legitimacy matter. But they matter **because** they support a healthy enterprise, not because they can replace competitiveness.

I would support the state playing a constructive role in a package that:
- secures investment,
- helps with training and redeployment,
- supports transitional measures,
- and helps create the conditions for labour peace.

What I will not support is a governance posture that turns the company into a protected regional asset regardless of economic reality.

---

## To institutional investors
You are right on the core point: this must be material, durable, and credible.

I share your skepticism toward packages that are mostly optics. I do not want a settlement that looks cooperative and then disappoints again in eighteen months.

Where I would push back is if the market assumes the only credible restructuring is the harshest one. That is not always true. A negotiated package can be credible if it actually delivers the savings and capacity alignment needed.

So I would look for:
- measurable margin improvement,
- a real and verifiable capacity step-down,
- clear implementation milestones,
- and a settlement that avoids chronic conflict.

If those are present, I can support the package. If not, I will not.

---

## On the moderator’s compromise paths

### 1) “Closures as ultima ratio”
I can support that **only if** it is truly a last resort after a genuine test of alternatives, with objective criteria and independent verification.

I cannot support language that effectively removes closures from the table altogether. But I also do not want them used casually as leverage.

So: **not a first resort, but not excluded in principle.**

### 2) “Site guarantees in exchange for quantified labour contributions”
This is the most promising route, but only if the contribution is real.

That means:
- temporary, not permanent;
- quantified, not vague;
- reversible, not open-ended;
- and large enough to matter.

If labour can deliver that, then I would be open to a time-limited stability framework and no immediate forced redundancies.

### 3) “Two-track restructuring”
This is viable if it is not a stalling mechanism.

Track one should deliver immediate, verifiable savings.  
Track two should preserve fallback options if the economics fail to improve.

That is the balance I would accept: stability now, but no permanent self-deception.

---

## My bottom line
I am prepared to support a negotiated restructuring that:
- restores competitiveness,
- produces durable savings,
- preserves labour peace,
- and protects the enterprise’s long-term value.

I am **not** prepared to support:
- an absolute no-closure doctrine,
- an absolute no-redundancy doctrine,
- or a settlement that leaves the cost base structurally uncompetitive.

If labour wants stronger protection, it must produce stronger economic relief. If management wants stronger freedom of action, it must prove necessity with numbers, not slogans.

That is where I stand.
