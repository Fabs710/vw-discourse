I’ll respond directly and without ambiguity.

## My position remains unchanged on the core points
- **No plant closures**
- **No compulsory redundancies**
- **No dismantling of the collective wage framework**
- **No solution where employees carry the main burden while management and owners protect themselves**

If any proposal depends on one of those, then it is not acceptable.

---

## To management
You keep saying you need “durable savings.” I do not dispute the need for a credible restructuring. I dispute your implied conclusion that closures and forced layoffs are the only serious route.

That is simply not established.

You have not shown:
- that every alternative has been exhausted,
- that site-specific restructuring cannot work,
- that internal redeployment and working-time tools are insufficient,
- or that the burden must be shifted onto the workforce through permanent cuts.

And let me be blunt: if the plan only works by breaking the collective agreement or threatening jobs, then the plan is not balanced — it is coercive.

### What we could discuss
We are prepared to discuss:
- temporary working-time reductions,
- redeployment across sites,
- qualification and upskilling,
- voluntary exit programs,
- a temporary waiver or reduction of bonuses,
- a time-limited contribution to a restructuring fund,
- productivity and process improvements.

### What we will not discuss
- forced redundancies,
- plant closures,
- permanent wage cuts,
- hidden closures through outsourcing or relocation,
- open-ended insecurity dressed up as “flexibility.”

If management wants labour to contribute, then management must stop treating labour as the only adjustable cost item.

---

## To the works council
We are aligned on the main red lines, and that matters.

Your insistence on site and job protection is legitimate. I also agree that any labour contribution must be **temporary, bounded, and reversible**. That is the only kind of contribution we can credibly ask members to accept.

But I want to sharpen one point: a “shared-sacrifice” package must not quietly become a permanent lowering of standards. If we concede bonuses or working-time flexibility, then there must be a **clear end point** and a **binding return path** to the collective framework.

So yes, we can work together on:
- internal capacity adjustments,
- voluntary departures,
- transfer and qualification,
- shift and working-time solutions,
- temporary moderation of variable pay.

But only if the result is:
- **binding site protection,**
- **binding job security,**
- and **real co-determination in the restructuring process**.

---

## To the regional state government
You are trying to stand between the sides, and I understand that. But you cannot keep closures and redundancies on the table “just in case” and simultaneously claim to defend the industrial base.

If the state wants credibility with the workforce, then it must use its leverage to stop the company from turning German plants into bargaining chips.

We would welcome the state backing a deal that includes:
- no plant closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- secure investment commitments,
- and a genuine industrial plan for the German sites.

If the state remains vague, then it is effectively enabling management to pressure the workforce into conceding too much.

---

## To the controlling family holding
You say you want enterprise value preserved. Fine — but you are treating that as if it were compatible with forcing structural pain mainly onto employees. That is exactly what will destroy trust and trigger conflict.

A stable long-term enterprise value does **not** come from:
- threatening closures,
- eroding the collective framework,
- or using the market as a blanket excuse for one-sided adjustments.

It comes from:
- a credible product strategy,
- investment,
- a motivated workforce,
- and social peace.

If you want long-term value, then back a deal that protects jobs and sites instead of treating them as expendable.

---

## To institutional investors
You are right about one thing: the company needs a real structural improvement, not symbolism.

But you are wrong if you think a plant closure is automatically the most “credible” form of restructuring. It can also be the most destructive, the most conflictual, and the most expensive in execution terms.

A credible package can include:
- capacity reduction through voluntary tools,
- redeployment,
- working-time changes,
- temporary labour contributions,
- investment commitments,
- and measurable productivity gains.

What makes a package credible is not how harsh it is. It is whether it is **implementable, socially stable, and durable**.

If you want margins improved, then support a settlement that preserves industrial peace and avoids a prolonged labour conflict. If you push too hard for closures and redundancies, you will get the opposite: strikes, resistance, and delay.

---

## On the moderator’s compromise options

### 1) “Closures as ultima ratio”
**Rejected.**

Why? Because once closures are built into the framework, the workforce is negotiating under a permanent threat. That is not a fair bargaining basis. It is a pressure mechanism.

If management insists on keeping closures as a standing fallback, then I must be equally clear: the workforce will treat that as a hostile move and respond accordingly.

### 2) “Site guarantees in exchange for quantified labour contributions”
This is the only route that sounds like a real basis for negotiation — **if** the guarantees are binding and meaningful.

From the union side, we could consider a package that includes:
- temporary restraint on bonuses or special payments,
- a time-limited crisis contribution,
- working-time flexibility,
- qualification and transfer measures,
- voluntary exit options,
- and perhaps a restructuring fund.

But only in exchange for:
- **no compulsory redundancies,**
- **no plant closures,**
- **site protection for a defined period,**
- and **a binding investment and product commitment**.

Anything weaker is not acceptable.

### 3) “Two-track restructuring”
A two-track approach is only acceptable if track two is not a hidden route to layoffs later.

If we do this, then track one must deliver real results:
- redeployment,
- qualification,
- productivity measures,
- capacity adjustments,
- labour contribution,
- and industrial investment.

Track two cannot be a coded threat that says, “If you don’t concede enough now, we’ll close sites later.” That is not a negotiation; that is blackmail with a timetable.

---

## What would genuinely move us
To be clear, we are not inflexible on every detail. We are flexible on the **means**, not on the **red lines**.

What could move us:
1. **Binding no-closure commitment**
2. **Binding no-compulsory-redundancy commitment**
3. **Temporary, limited labour contribution**
4. **Strong investment commitments**
5. **Real co-determination in implementation**
6. **Internal capacity reduction instead of job destruction**
7. **Time-limited review with automatic end to concessions once targets are met**

What will not move us:
- vague assurances,
- “ultima ratio” language that keeps the threat alive,
- permanent wage erosion,
- or any arrangement that leaves workers alone with the pain.

---

## My bottom line
If the company wants a durable settlement, it must stop asking the workforce to pay for strategic failures through closures and compulsory redundancies.

We are ready for a serious, negotiated solution.

But that solution must be built on:
- **jobs protected**
- **sites protected**
- **collective standards protected**
- **and sacrifice shared fairly**

If management and capital refuse that, then conflict is not caused by labour’s rigidity. It is caused by their refusal to accept a fair settlement.
