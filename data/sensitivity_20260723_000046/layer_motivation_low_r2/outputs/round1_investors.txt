We share the central point that this cannot be a cosmetic exercise. From the capital markets side, that is the only useful starting point.

Where I agree:
- Management is right that the current cost base and capacity structure are not competitive.
- The family holding is right that investors will not reward delay, ambiguity, or a deal that merely kicks the problem into the future.
- The state is right that a prolonged public confrontation would destroy value.
- The works council and union are right that a purely unilateral solution would be slow, risky, and likely value-destructive.

Where I disagree:
- I do **not** accept a restructuring framed mainly around preserving all sites and all legacy guarantees if that leaves the business structurally weak.
- I also do **not** support an outcome where “no closures” becomes a permanent veto regardless of economics.
- At the same time, I am not persuaded that compulsory redundancies or plant closures should be treated as the default answer if a credible alternative can achieve the same savings.

My position is simple: **the company needs durable, measurable cost reduction and capacity alignment, not symbolism.** If the final package does not credibly restore margins and competitiveness, then it is not acceptable from an investor perspective.

That means:
- any labour contribution must be **material**, not marginal;
- any capacity reduction must be **real**, not hidden in accounting tricks or deferred attrition;
- any job or site guarantees must be **conditional and economically defensible**, not open-ended promises that freeze the cost base;
- and any investment commitment must be tied to a restructuring that actually improves returns.

To management: you need to show that the proposed savings are sufficient and executable. If they are not, then you are asking shareholders to fund uncertainty.

To labour: if you want to avoid closures and forced layoffs, then the alternative must still produce a structural step-down in costs. “We will talk about alternatives” is not enough unless the numbers work and the savings are durable.

To the state and family owners: investors will support a serious turnaround, but not a politically convenient compromise that preserves headline peace while leaving margins impaired. The market will see through that very quickly.

So from our side, the test is this:
**Does the package restore competitive returns on a credible timeline, with lasting savings and clear implementation risk?**
If yes, we can support it.
If no, we will not back a deal that simply postpones the problem.
