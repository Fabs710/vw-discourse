From the perspective of dispersed institutional investors, the key test is not who “wins” the social argument, but whether the company gets back to **competitive, sustainable margins** with a cost base the market can believe in.

So let me be direct.

### Where we agree
We agree with management, and partly with the controlling family, on the core economic diagnosis:

- The current German cost base is too high.
- The company cannot keep carrying structural overcapacity.
- A cosmetic deal that avoids real change would be value-destructive.
- Prolonged uncertainty and strikes would damage earnings, confidence, and the share price.

We also agree that any package should be **durable**, not just a short-term truce.

### Where we disagree
We do **not** support a settlement that leaves the company uncompetitive in order to preserve every existing structure.

That means:

- We do **not** accept “no closures” as an absolute principle if closures are genuinely needed to restore margins.
- We do **not** accept guarantees that simply delay the day of reckoning while the cost base remains structurally impaired.
- We do **not** accept employee or regional claims that ignore the economics of the business.

At the same time, we are also not interested in management pursuing a symbolic hard line if a **credible alternative** can achieve comparable savings with less disruption. If the same economics can be delivered through capacity reductions, working-time flexibility, voluntary exits, and labour-cost adjustments, that is clearly preferable to a long confrontation.

### Our position
We want the outcome that best restores risk-adjusted value:

1. **A quantified, credible savings package**
2. **Durable reduction in cost and/or capacity**
3. **A clear path back to target margins / return on sales**
4. **Limited disruption and deal certainty**
5. **No vague promises without enforceable delivery**

### What would move us
We would support a negotiated alternative to closures if it is backed by:
- specific and measurable savings,
- clear implementation timing,
- real capacity reduction,
- durable labour-cost relief,
- and credible governance commitment that the restructuring will actually be executed.

### What we will not support
We will not support:
- a cosmetic settlement,
- a strike-driven drift into delay,
- or a package that preserves too much excess cost and therefore leaves margins structurally impaired.

### To management
If plant closures are necessary, say so and justify them with hard numbers. If there is a credible no-closure route, present it clearly and prove that it closes the gap.

### To the works council, union, and state government
If you want to avoid closures, you need to show us how the economics still work. “Protect every plant” is not, by itself, a financial strategy. The burden of proof is on the side proposing preservation.

### Bottom line
We are **not attached to closures as a principle**. We are attached to **value creation**. If the final settlement credibly restores competitiveness, we can support it. If it does not, then we will view it as value-destroying, regardless of how socially pleasant it sounds.
