Let me respond directly, because we are now at the point where slogans have to give way to economics.

## To the Works Council and the Union

I hear your insistence on protecting jobs and sites. I understand why that is your position. But I reject the idea that “no closures, no forced redundancies, no binding flexibility limits” can be the starting point of a serious restructuring discussion.

That is not a solution. It is a request that management somehow fix a structural cost crisis while keeping the existing structure largely intact. That is not credible.

### Where I agree
I agree that:
- the company needs a **durable** solution, not a cosmetic one;
- future products and investment matter;
- employee contribution must be part of a broader package;
- a negotiated outcome is preferable to a prolonged breakdown.

### Where I disagree
I do **not** accept:
- that every German plant must remain open;
- that compulsory redundancies are ruled out in all circumstances;
- that binding site guarantees through 2030 are compatible with restoring competitiveness if they freeze the cost base;
- that “reversible” measures alone can close the gap we face.

You keep asking management to prove that closures are unavoidable. Fair enough — here is the proof standard I am applying:

If the non-closure route can deliver **the same structural savings**, then it is acceptable.  
If it cannot, then closures and more difficult measures remain necessary.

That is the only economically responsible standard.

## To the union specifically

You say you are prepared to contribute. Good. But a contribution must be **material, durable, and measurable**. Not symbolic. Not temporary. Not a short pause before the same problem returns.

If you want a no-closure path to be considered seriously, then it has to include:
- a quantified labour-cost contribution,
- real capacity reductions,
- changes to work rules and flexibility,
- productivity gains that are actually enforceable,
- and a level of job security that does not prevent the company from acting if the numbers do not work.

What I will not accept is a package that:
1. preserves every site,
2. preserves the cost structure,
3. and asks management to pretend this restores competitiveness.

That would be a deception, not a settlement.

## To the Works Council

You repeatedly say closures are just “pressure politics.” They are not. They are a real option because the business is under real pressure.

Management is not asking the workforce to pay for strategic failure in a moral sense. We are asking all sides to confront the fact that:
- the European market is shrinking,
- competitors are leaner,
- German costs are too high,
- and the current structure does not meet our 2030 investment needs.

If you want to keep every plant open, then explain how you will permanently remove enough cost and capacity to hit the return-on-sales target. Not with generalities — with numbers.

If you cannot do that, then your position is not a restructuring alternative. It is a refusal to confront reality.

## To the regional state government

I appreciate your concern for the industrial base. But political pressure does not change the balance sheet.

You say closures would damage the region. I do not dispute that. But letting the company remain structurally uncompetitive would also damage the region — only more slowly, through lost investment, weaker margins, and eventual erosion of the industrial footprint anyway.

A serious state-backed solution would not be to block difficult choices indefinitely. It would be to support a package that genuinely restores competitiveness and future viability.

If the state wants a strong industrial anchor, then it should support the measures that make that anchor sustainable — not just the measures that preserve the status quo.

## To the controlling family

You are right on one point: value creation matters. But value is not protected by hesitation.

I agree that a cosmetic deal is unacceptable. I agree that labour peace matters. I agree that we need future product and investment commitments.

Where I disagree is your apparent assumption that there is always a no-closure path that can deliver sufficient savings if only management negotiates long enough. That may be true in theory; it is not proven in practice.

I am open to avoiding closures **if** the numbers close. But I will not accept an arrangement that leaves us with the same overcapacity, the same cost pressure, and a weaker ability to invest.

## What management is prepared to do

Let me be specific.

We are prepared to discuss a **non-closure alternative** only if it delivers:
- **a genuine structural cost reduction**,
- **a real capacity reduction**,
- **a sustained labour-cost contribution**,
- **implementation certainty**,
- and **no return to the old cost base after a short interval**.

That means the package must be stronger than:
- temporary wage restraint,
- voluntary exits alone,
- redeployment alone,
- or working-time adjustments without corresponding structural change.

Those measures may help, but they are not enough by themselves.

## What remains on the table

If a credible no-closure package cannot close the gap, then we will have to proceed with more difficult restructuring options, including:
- plant closures,
- headcount reductions,
- and permanent changes to the cost base.

I am not saying that because I want confrontation for its own sake. I am saying it because I will not preside over a settlement that leaves the brand uncompetitive and underinvested.

## Bottom line

So my position is unchanged:

- **We want a durable deal.**
- **We are open on the means.**
- **We are not open on the result.**

The result must be:
- materially lower cost,
- lower structural capacity,
- and a business that can hit its return-on-sales target and fund 2030 investment.

If the non-closure route can deliver that, bring it forward with numbers.  
If it cannot, then the difficult measures stay on the table.

And to be absolutely clear: I will not sign off on a cosmetic compromise that leaves the company in the same position a year from now.
