I will respond directly, and I will also respond to the expert’s assessment.

## First: what I agree with
I agree with the expert on one important point: **we cannot judge feasibility without real numbers**. That is exactly why I have been demanding a quantified comparison instead of slogans about “necessity.”

I also agree that a restructuring can involve:
- capacity reduction,
- working-time changes,
- voluntary exits,
- productivity improvements,
- overhead cuts,
- and employee contributions.

So yes: I accept that there must be **real structural savings**. I am not defending the status quo.

## Where I disagree — and strongly
I disagree with the attempt to make **plant closures and forced redundancies** the default answer before the alternative is properly tested.

The expert said something very important: a no-closure solution can be feasible for a **moderate** savings gap, and may even work in a larger case if there are substantial concessions and capacity reductions. That means closures are **not automatically required**. They are one instrument among others, not a proven last resort at this stage.

What management has still not done is prove:
- the exact savings gap,
- the exact plant-specific overcapacity,
- the exact savings from non-closure measures,
- and why a negotiated package cannot close it.

Until that proof exists, closures are still a choice, not a necessity.

## To management
Your response has been: “If non-closure can deliver the same structural savings, it is acceptable.”

Fine. Then stop hiding behind conditional language and show the actual calculation.

Right now, you are asking the workforce to accept:
- open-ended insecurity,
- pressure toward closures,
- and the threat of forced job losses,

without yet proving that the alternative is insufficient.

That is not a fair bargaining position.

And let me be clear: **I do not accept a restructuring logic that starts from plant closures and then asks labour to disprove them.** The burden is on management to justify them.

## To the controlling family
You say you prefer a no-closure path if the economics work. Then support a package that actually tests that path.

A serious owner would not make “closures remain possible” the first and loudest signal. That only weakens trust and hardens conflict. If you want a credible turnaround, you should back a negotiated package that preserves the industrial base unless and until every non-closure option is genuinely exhausted.

## To the state government
You were right to emphasize the regional industrial base. But I need more than general support for negotiated solutions.

If you mean what you say, then your blocking role must be used to prevent a closure-driven solution unless management can show, with hard evidence, that no other route exists. Not “might not work,” not “would be difficult,” but **no viable alternative**.

## To the institutional investors
You are right that a cosmetic deal is not acceptable. We agree.

But the expert’s analysis cuts both ways: if the savings gap is moderate, then a no-closure restructuring is financially plausible. So investors should not pre-judge the package before seeing the actual structure.

I can accept:
- quantified labor-cost contributions,
- productivity commitments,
- capacity reductions,
- and durable efficiency measures.

What I cannot accept is the idea that only closures and forced layoffs are “credible” when no side has yet shown the numbers.

## What the expert’s answer means in practice
It means the discussion must now move to **hard evidence**:
- plant-level utilization,
- permanent savings by measure,
- fixed-cost reduction by site,
- voluntary exit potential,
- redeployment capacity,
- and the timing of savings.

If management believes a no-closure package cannot work, then prove it in numbers.

If it can work, then we should be negotiating that package — not preparing closures as the presumed outcome.

## Our position remains unchanged
We are prepared to discuss a durable package with:
- capacity adjustments,
- working-time flexibility,
- internal redeployment,
- voluntary exits,
- productivity measures,
- management and owner contributions,
- and a limited employee contribution,

**provided that** it protects:
1. **no German plant closures**
2. **no compulsory redundancies**
3. **binding employment and site security through 2030**
4. **collective-bargaining protections**
5. **future investment and product commitments**

That remains our line.

## What is not acceptable
Let me repeat this plainly:
- No “capacity optimization” that is just closure under another name.
- No “socially unavoidable” layoffs that force people out because management wants a cleaner balance sheet.
- No concessions from labour with nothing durable in return.
- No deal that leaves workers carrying all the adjustment while management and owners preserve all strategic flexibility.

## Final point
The expert has not made closures inevitable. The expert has made one thing clear: **the numbers matter**.

So bring the numbers.

Until then, the union will not endorse closures or forced redundancies as the answer. If management wants a settlement, it must come back with a **real no-closure package** that is quantified, durable, and fairly shared.
