As dispersed institutional investors, our position is still driven by one thing: **risk-adjusted value creation through a credible return to competitive margins**. We are not here to defend any particular plant map, and we are not here to endorse confrontation for its own sake. But we also will not support a settlement that leaves the company structurally weak and then calls that “peace.”

## Where we agree

### With management
We agree that:
- the cost base is too high,
- overcapacity is real,
- temporary or symbolic measures will not be enough,
- and a cosmetic deal would be value-destructive.

We also agree that the company needs **durable savings**, not a one-year pause before the same problem returns.

### With the controlling family
We agree that:
- enterprise value must be protected,
- long-term competitiveness matters more than short-term optics,
- and any settlement must be durable and enforceable.

### With the state government
We agree that a prolonged, uncontrolled breakdown would be bad for value and for the industrial base.

## Where we disagree

### With the Works Council and union
We do **not** accept the idea that “no closures, no compulsory redundancies, no binding flexibility” can be the baseline if it prevents a real restructuring.

That position is not a value-preserving strategy. It is a claim that the market, the cost base, and the competitive gap can be ignored because the social consequences are difficult. We cannot support that.

We also do **not** accept guarantees through 2030 if they freeze the company into an uncompetitive structure or materially impair management’s ability to execute later.

### With management
We also do **not** accept a stance that jumps too quickly to closures without proving that a serious no-closure alternative has been properly tested.

If there is a credible route to the same economics through:
- capacity reduction,
- working-time flexibility,
- voluntary exits,
- labor-cost relief,
- and productivity gains,

then that is preferable to destructive plant closures and a long strike. Value is not created by conflict.

## Our bottom line

We need a package that does all of the following:

1. **Delivers quantified, durable cost reduction**
2. **Restores the path to target margins / return on sales**
3. **Has implementation certainty**
4. **Avoids prolonged industrial conflict if possible**
5. **Does not leave the company structurally impaired**

That means we are open to a **no-closure solution only if the numbers are real**. Not aspirational, not political, not contingent on goodwill.

## What would move us

A credible package would need to show, site by site and measure by measure:
- the exact savings achieved,
- the timing of those savings,
- the permanence of the cost reduction,
- how much capacity is actually removed,
- and how the company will regain pricing and margin discipline.

We would also need clarity on:
- what employee concessions are permanent versus temporary,
- what management is giving up,
- what investment is being committed,
- and how the restructuring is protected from being unwound later.

## What we will not support

We will not support:
- a cosmetic compromise,
- a strike-driven drift into delay,
- a preservation of all existing structures regardless of economics,
- or a deal that protects jobs today while damaging shareholder value and future viability tomorrow.

## Our challenge to the other sides

### To management
Bring the comparison in hard numbers:
- closure-based plan vs. no-closure plan,
- same accounting horizon,
- same savings definition,
- same margin impact.

If the no-closure route truly works, prove it. If it does not, say so plainly.

### To labour
If you want to avoid closures, you need to demonstrate that the alternative actually closes the gap. “We will not accept closures” is not enough. The burden of proof is on the side rejecting the most direct cost measure.

### To the family and state
Do not let political or governance constraints become an excuse for a weak deal. If the restructuring does not restore competitiveness, everyone loses value — including anchor shareholders.

## Our current stance

We are **not attached to closures as a principle**.  
We are attached to **credible economics**.

So:
- if a no-closure package can genuinely restore margins, we can support it;
- if it cannot, then harder measures are justified and should not be ruled out by political slogans.

What we need now is not more principle statements, but the numbers. If management wants backing from capital markets, it must show a package that investors can underwrite with confidence.
