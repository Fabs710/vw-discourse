We appreciate the push toward a quantified comparison, and we agree with the direction of the discussion: **this has to be judged on durable economics, not on slogans, not on sentiment, and not on the assumption that conflict itself creates value**.

## Where we agree

### With management
- The current cost base and capacity are not acceptable.
- A cosmetic settlement would be value-destructive.
- Temporary or reversible measures alone are unlikely to be enough if the structural gap is large.
- The package must be **durable**, **measurable**, and **implementable**.

### With the controlling family
- Long-term enterprise value matters more than short-term optics.
- A weak compromise that preserves an uncompetitive structure would destroy value for all shareholders, including us.
- The company cannot be frozen into inaction.

### With the state government
- A prolonged breakdown or strike would damage value, the industrial base, and investor confidence.
- Negotiation is preferable to open-ended conflict.

### With the expert
- The expert’s key point is exactly right: **without actual numbers, nobody can credibly claim that a no-closure path works or fails**.
- A no-closure package may be feasible for a moderate gap, but feasibility collapses if the required savings are large, structural, and immediate.

## Where we disagree

### With the Works Council and union
We do **not** accept the position that:
- no closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- and binding job security through 2030

can be the baseline if those red lines prevent a real restructuring.

That is not a value-preserving stance. It is a demand to protect the existing structure first and hope competitiveness follows later. That is not acceptable.

We also do **not** accept any settlement that would leave the company with:
- structurally high labor and overhead costs,
- excess capacity,
- or insufficient flexibility to adapt.

If that is the outcome, then the settlement fails shareholders even if it avoids immediate conflict.

### With management
At the same time, we do **not** accept a default leap to closures without proving that a serious non-closure option has been fully tested.

A closure-based plan can be economically rational, but it is not automatically the correct first move unless the numbers show that the alternative cannot close the gap.

## Our position

We are **not ideologically attached to closures**. We are attached to:
1. durable cost reduction,
2. credible margin restoration,
3. implementation certainty,
4. and preservation of long-term value.

That means:
- If a **no-closure package** can truly deliver equivalent, durable savings, we can support it.
- If it cannot, then more difficult measures, including closures and job reductions, remain justified.

## What we need next
We want management to provide a **side-by-side, quantified comparison** of:

### 1) Closure-based scenario
- annual savings
- one-off restructuring costs
- timing
- site and job impact
- effect on investment capacity
- durability of savings

### 2) No-closure scenario
- savings by measure
- permanency of each measure
- likely take-up of voluntary exits
- working-time and productivity effects
- overhead and procurement savings
- residual capacity imbalance
- impact on margins and ROS target

Without that, this discussion remains political positioning.

## What would move us
A no-closure deal would need to show:
- real and permanent capacity reduction,
- meaningful labor-cost relief,
- enforceable productivity gains,
- credible implementation timing,
- and no hidden return to the old cost structure after a short transition.

If the package is built mainly on temporary concessions, symbolic commitments, or managerial optimism, we would not back it.

## What we will not support
We will not support:
- a cosmetic compromise,
- a settlement that leaves the company structurally impaired,
- a labor deal that only postpones the problem,
- or a plan that preserves every site regardless of economics.

## Bottom line
Our support is available for a **credible, durable restructuring** that restores competitive margins.

That support is **not** available for a deal that simply buys peace while leaving the cost base uncompetitive.

So we align with the demand for numbers, and we urge management to produce them quickly. If the no-closure route can close the gap, prove it. If it cannot, then the harder measures should not be ruled out by politics alone.
