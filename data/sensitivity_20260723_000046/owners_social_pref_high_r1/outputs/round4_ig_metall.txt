I will be equally direct in round 4.

## Where I agree
I agree with the core diagnosis from everyone: **this company needs a durable restructuring**. I also agree that:
- a cosmetic pause would be unacceptable,
- future product allocation and investment matter,
- productivity gains and capacity adjustment are legitimate topics,
- and the discussion must now be **quantified**, not rhetorical.

I also acknowledge that employees must contribute. We are not defending the status quo at any price.

## Where I disagree — and strongly
What I do **not** accept is the attempt to turn **plant closures and compulsory redundancies** into the default solution and then ask labour to prove why that is wrong.

That reverses the burden of proof.

The works council is right on one central point: management has still not demonstrated, with real site-by-site numbers, that the same durable savings cannot be achieved through a no-closure package. Until that proof exists, closures are **not necessity**; they are a management choice.

And I need to be clear: **no German plant closures and no compulsory redundancies are not a bargaining chip for us.** They are a line we cannot cross.

## To management
You say “no closures” is just a constraint. No — it is the condition for labour peace and a legitimate restructuring. If management wants cooperation, it must present a serious alternative, not threaten the workforce into accepting the first closure plan on the table.

You also keep suggesting that temporary or voluntary measures are inherently inadequate. That is not proven. The expert said a no-closure solution can be feasible if the savings gap is manageable and if the package includes durable measures. So your task is not to repeat that closures are possible; it is to prove that they are necessary.

Bring the figures:
- savings needed,
- site-by-site fixed costs,
- savings from working-time changes,
- voluntary exit potential,
- overhead cuts,
- redeployment capacity,
- and the duration of each effect.

Without that, “overcapacity” is just a slogan for downsizing workers.

## To the works council
I support your rejection of closures and forced redundancies. That said, I also need to be precise: job and site security through 2030 is only sustainable if the package actually delivers durable savings.

So I am not asking for a blank cheque. I am saying that a legitimate no-closure deal must include:
- capacity reduction through internal reorganization,
- working-time flexibility,
- voluntary departures only,
- productivity gains,
- internal redeployment,
- management and owner contributions,
- and a limited employee contribution.

That is not weakness. That is a serious restructuring path.

## To the controlling family
You say you value long-term enterprise value. Then support the industrial base that creates it. A family owner should not treat workforce insecurity as a normal instrument of governance.

If you want durable value, back a package that preserves every German plant and avoids forced layoffs. A restructuring that destroys trust, skill retention, and industrial peace is not value-preserving.

## To the state government
Your blocking role matters, but it must be used decisively. If you oppose closures as a starting point, then say so clearly: **no support for a closure-driven plan unless management proves there is literally no viable alternative**.

“Negotiation” is not enough if closures remain the assumed default. Use your leverage to force a genuine no-closure option onto the table.

## To the institutional investors
You are right that a cosmetic deal would be unacceptable. I agree.

But you should not treat closures as automatically the only credible path. The expert did not say that. The expert said the answer depends on the size and durability of the savings gap. That means the no-closure package must be tested properly, not dismissed because it is politically harder.

## What the union can accept
We are prepared to discuss a real package with:
- capacity adjustments,
- working-time changes,
- productivity improvements,
- voluntary exits,
- redeployment,
- procurement and overhead reductions,
- and a fair, limited employee contribution.

But only if it comes with binding safeguards:
1. **No German plant closures**
2. **No compulsory redundancies**
3. **Binding site and employment security through 2030**
4. **Enforceable future investment commitments**
5. **Real co-determination over implementation**

That is the minimum for a fair settlement.

## What we will not accept
Let me be blunt:

- “Capacity optimization” that closes plants is closure.
- “Socially unavoidable” layoffs are compulsory redundancies.
- “Flexibility” that erodes collective bargaining standards is not compromise.
- Labour contributing while management and owners preserve all strategic options is not burden-sharing; it is one-sided sacrifice.

We will not accept that.

## On the expert
The expert has not made closures inevitable. The expert has said the numbers matter and that no-closure solutions can work in some cases. That is exactly why management must stop relying on fear and start presenting evidence.

If the gap is moderate, the no-closure route may well be feasible.  
If the gap is huge, then management still has to prove that before asking for concessions that break the social contract.

## My bottom line
The union’s position remains:

**No plant closures. No compulsory redundancies.  
Fair burden-sharing. Durable savings. Binding guarantees.**

If management wants an agreement, it must come forward with a real no-closure package that shows, in numbers, how the company can restructure without sacrificing the workforce first.

If it cannot do that, then the conflict is not caused by labour’s refusal — it is caused by management’s refusal to restructure responsibly.
