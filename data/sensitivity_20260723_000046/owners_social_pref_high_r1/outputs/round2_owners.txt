I will respond directly.

## Where I agree

I agree with management, the investors, and the state government on the central economic point: **this company needs a durable restructuring that restores competitiveness and margins**. A cosmetic deal that merely postpones the problem would destroy value for all shareholders, including the family holding.

I also agree that:
- the current German cost base is too high,
- overcapacity cannot simply be wished away,
- future products and investment matter,
- and a prolonged strike would be value-destructive.

## Where I disagree

I do **not** accept the idea that plant closures or compulsory redundancies should be treated as the default answer.

That is not because I am opposed to hard decisions in principle. It is because the burden of proof has not yet been met. If a **credible no-closure package** can deliver comparable, durable savings, that is preferable. If it cannot, then stronger measures remain necessary. But we are not yet at the point where closures are proven unavoidable.

I also do **not** accept a settlement that freezes the company in place through rigid guarantees if that would permanently impair flexibility or governance control. Long-term value matters more than short-term comfort.

## To management

You are right to insist on structural savings. I support that. But I want the numbers, not just the rhetoric.

What I still do not see is a clear, quantified comparison between:
1. a closure-based package, and
2. a serious no-closure alternative.

If the no-closure route can produce:
- permanent capacity reduction,
- meaningful labour-cost relief,
- enforceable productivity improvements,
- and reliable implementation timing,

then that is the better route from an ownership perspective because it may preserve more industrial value and reduce conflict risk.

But if the alternative is merely:
- temporary wage restraint,
- voluntary exits only,
- and promises about future flexibility,

then I agree that it will not be enough.

So my message to management is simple: **bring the hard numbers and show what actually closes the gap**.

## To the Works Council and the union

I hear your red lines. I understand why they are there. But I have to be equally clear: **“no closures, no compulsory redundancies, no binding flexibility” cannot be the starting framework for a serious turnaround** if those conditions make it impossible to restore competitiveness.

Protecting every existing structure is not the same as protecting the company.

What I will support is a negotiated package that includes:
- capacity adjustments,
- working-time changes,
- voluntary instruments,
- redeployment,
- productivity measures,
- and a fair employee contribution,

**provided that** the package is durable and produces real savings.

What I will **not** support is a deal that preserves every plant and every guarantee while leaving the cost base essentially unchanged. That would simply delay the crisis and weaken the enterprise later.

## To the regional state government

I respect the importance of the regional industrial base. I also recognize the state’s governance role. But I have to say clearly: **a blocking position cannot become a veto on necessary restructuring**.

If the state wants to protect jobs in the long run, it must support a package that makes the company viable in the long run. That means accepting that some existing structures may have to change.

I do agree with you on one point: if there is a serious no-closure path with enforceable economics, it should be preferred. But the state should not pre-commit to preserving all sites regardless of economics. That would be a recipe for value erosion.

## To institutional investors

I agree with you on the core value test: we need a credible route back to target margins. I also agree that a cosmetic compromise is unacceptable.

Where I would caution you is against assuming that the only credible route is closures. That may be true, but it must be demonstrated, not asserted. If management can show that a negotiated alternative delivers the same economics with less instability, that should be seriously considered.

## My position as controlling shareholder

From the family holding’s perspective, the priorities are straightforward:
- restore competitiveness,
- protect long-term enterprise value,
- avoid value-destructive instability,
- and keep governance control intact.

That means:
1. **We support a real restructuring.**
2. **We do not support mere delay.**
3. **We are open to avoiding closures if the economics work.**
4. **We will not support a deal that permanently weakens the company’s ability to act.**

## What I want next

I would like management to present, side by side:
- **one closure-based scenario**, and
- **one no-closure scenario**,

with:
- quantified savings,
- timing,
- impact on sites and jobs,
- impact on investment capacity,
- and the durability of the cost reduction.

That is the right basis for a serious governance decision.

## Bottom line

My support is available for a package that restores competitiveness and margins in a durable way.  
My support is **not** available for:
- a cosmetic compromise,
- indefinite preservation of all structures,
- or a deal that leaves the company uncompetitive and unstable.

If the alternative to closures is genuinely capable of delivering the same economic result, I am open to it. If not, then harder measures must remain available.
