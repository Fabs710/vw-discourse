I will be very direct.

## What I agree with
I agree with all parties on one point: **the company needs a durable restructuring and future investment**. A cosmetic pause would be irresponsible. We also accept that there must be **capacity adjustment, productivity gains, and some form of employee contribution**.

But agreement on the need for change does **not** mean agreement on the method.

## What I disagree with
I reject the attempt to make **plant closures and compulsory redundancies** sound like the only serious option.

That is not a proven necessity. It is a choice — and one that shifts the burden onto the workforce and the regions that have built this company. I will not accept that as a starting point, and certainly not as the “logical” default.

## To management
You keep repeating “durable savings,” “implementation certainty,” and “competitive cost base.” Fine. But you still have **not** shown that these require:
- German plant closures,
- forced layoffs,
- or tearing up the job-security framework.

You say reversible measures are insufficient. Then stop speaking in generalities and bring the **site-by-site numbers**:
- exact savings needed,
- exact savings from each measure,
- which parts are truly permanent,
- and why the same result cannot be achieved without closures.

Until then, closure is not proof of responsibility. It is proof that management wants the workforce to pay first.

And let me be clear: **we will not sign off on any package that includes closures or compulsory redundancies in Germany.**

## To the controlling family
You talk about long-term value and governance control. Then act like a responsible owner, not a pressure channel for management.

A company that breaks historic job-security commitments and destroys trust with its workforce is not creating value — it is burning it. Industrial peace, production stability, and skilled labour are assets. You cannot strip them out and call the result competitiveness.

If you want a credible restructuring, then support a package that keeps every German plant open and avoids forced layoffs. Anything less would be a fundamental breach of trust.

## To the institutional investors
You are right that a cosmetic deal is unacceptable. I agree.

But you are wrong if you assume the only credible route to margins is closures and job cuts. A serious alternative exists if management is willing to negotiate honestly:
- capacity reductions without plant closure,
- working-time changes,
- voluntary exits only,
- internal redeployment,
- productivity improvements,
- and a limited employee contribution.

That can be a real restructuring. What it cannot be is a disguise for dismantling.

## To the regional state government
Your line against closures and forced redundancies is the right one. But it must mean more than concern in words.

If the state is serious about its blocking role, it should make clear now: **there will be no support for a closure-driven plan**. Not “as a last resort,” not “if the numbers are difficult,” but only if management proves — with hard evidence — that there is literally no viable alternative. So far, that proof has not been provided.

## On the “no-closure” alternative
Let me be equally precise here: we are not asking for the status quo. We are prepared to discuss:
- productivity improvements,
- capacity reduction through internal reorganization,
- working-time flexibility,
- voluntary departures,
- redeployment,
- and a fair, limited workforce contribution.

But these are only acceptable if they come with binding safeguards:
1. **No German plant closures**
2. **No compulsory redundancies**
3. **Binding employment and site security through 2030**
4. **Real co-determination over implementation**
5. **Investment and future product commitments that are enforceable**

That is not a “wish list.” It is the minimum for any legitimate settlement.

## What remains unacceptable
I want to say this plainly because the discussion keeps drifting toward euphemisms:

- “Capacity optimization” that closes plants is closure.
- “Socially unavoidable reductions” that force people out is compulsory redundancy.
- “Flexibility” that strips away job security is not a compromise; it is erosion.

We do not accept any of that.

## Bottom line
The burden of proof is still on management and the owners.

If they want labour cooperation, they must stop treating closures and forced layoffs as the baseline and present a **real no-closure plan** with numbers that stand up. Until they do, our position remains unchanged:

**No plant closures. No compulsory redundancies. No surrender of co-determination or job security.**

If management cannot operate within those limits, then it is not the workforce that is being unrealistic — it is management that is refusing to do the restructuring properly.
