[Works Council (Betriebsrat) (Plant-level employee representation under co-determination) — Round 1]

[Expert: an independent industrial restructuring  — Round 3]
Yes — I can assess the finance and restructuring feasibility, but only at a conditional level because the actual numbers are not yet provided.

## Bottom line

A **credible no-closure / no-forced-redundancy package can sometimes close a significant savings gap**, but **only if**:

1. the gap is **moderate rather than very large**,
2. there is **substantial voluntary attrition / redeployment potential**,
3. **working-time, shift, and utilization changes** can materially reduce fixed labor and overhead costs,
4. the company is willing to combine labor concessions with **non-labor cost cuts, footprint rationalization inside plants, and management/overhead reductions**, and
5. the benefits are **durable**, not just one-off or temporary.

If the required savings are **structural, large, and immediate** — especially if they reflect **persistent excess capacity** in German production — then **avoiding both plant closures and forced redundancies becomes much harder**. In that case, a no-closure solution may still exist, but it usually requires **very deep labor concessions**, extensive voluntary exits, and potentially **capacity reduction without closure** that still amounts economically to shutting down major production lines or making a plant uneconomic.

So the realistic answer is:

- **Possible in some cases**: yes.
- **Likely in this case without the numbers**: not provable.
- **If management needs a very large durable cost step-down**: a no-closure / no-forced-redundancy deal is often **insufficient unless labour accepts sizable concessions**.

## What would need to be quantified

To determine whether the gap can be closed, I would need:

1. **Target savings amount**
   - annual EBIT / cash cost improvement required
   - timing: by 2026? 2028? 2030?

2. **Current cost structure**
   - labor cost per vehicle
   - fixed vs variable cost split
   - plant-specific overhead
   - logistics and supply-chain costs
   - depreciation and underutilization burden

3. **Capacity and utilization data**
   - output by plant
   - installed capacity vs actual volumes
   - line utilization
   - model allocation and expected future product volumes

4. **Workforce adjustment options**
   - natural attrition rates
   - retirement profile
   - voluntary severance take-up potential
   - redeployment capacity across sites
   - short-time / working-time reduction scope
   - wage, bonus, and benefit concession possibilities

5. **Non-labor restructuring options**
   - procurement savings
   - SG&A cuts
   - engineering / platform simplification
   - shared services
   - capital expenditure deferral
   - inventory and working capital improvements

6. **Durability**
   - whether concessions are one-off, temporary, or permanent
   - whether savings survive wage inflation and demand recovery

## How I would test the claim

### Scenario A: closure-based restructuring
Typical economics:
- plant closure removes a large block of fixed cost,
- it can eliminate structural overcapacity,
- but it usually creates **one-time cash costs**: severance, decommissioning, contract exit, transfer costs, and possible supply-chain disruption.

This is often the fastest way to achieve **permanent capacity reduction**.

### Scenario B: no-closure restructuring
Possible levers:
- voluntary exits
- early retirement / partial retirement
- working-time reductions
- shift-system redesign
- pay moderation or temporary wage concessions
- productivity gains
- internal reallocation of production
- overhead and procurement savings

This can work if the savings need is:
- not too large,
- labor has high flexibility,
- and the company can remove enough fixed cost without closing a site.

But it usually faces three limits:
1. **Utilization limits**: if a plant is materially underfilled, savings from flexibility alone may be too small.
2. **Durability limits**: temporary concessions expire.
3. **Implementation limits**: voluntary measures may not produce enough headcount reduction quickly enough.

## Realistic financial conclusion

Without figures, I cannot say the gap is definitely closable. But from a restructuring finance perspective:

- If the required savings are **mostly labor-cost related** and on the order of a **few hundred million euros annually**, a negotiated no-closure package may be feasible.
- If the required savings reflect **multiple underutilized plants, excess fixed overhead, and structurally weak volumes**, then **no-closure plus no-forced-redundancy is usually only feasible if employees accept materially larger concessions than is often politically or economically likely**.
- If management’s plan depends on a **durable step-change** in cost base, and if German operations are structurally overcapacity, then **plant closures are economically the cleanest tool**, even though they are politically difficult.

## My neutral assessment

A no-closure agreement is **not automatically unrealistic**, but it must be judged against the size and composition of the savings gap. In restructuring terms:

- **For a moderate gap**: yes, alternatives can plausibly bridge it.
- **For a large structural gap**: alternatives may reduce the gap but often **do not fully close it** without either:
  - forced redundancies,
  - plant closure,
  - or very large employee concessions that function as closure-equivalents economically.

If you want, I can next lay out a **side-by-side model** of:
1. a closure-based package, and  
2. a no-closure package,  

showing which savings items are one-off, recurring, and realistically durable.

Below is a neutral plant-level restructuring assessment of the **economic question only**: whether a **closure-based** restructuring or a **no-closure** restructuring is more likely to deliver the required durable savings, and what each typically implies for one-off costs, stranded costs, and capacity.

## 1) Executive assessment

From a restructuring-accounting perspective, the key distinction is:

- **Plant closure** is usually the most reliable way to eliminate a plant’s **fixed cost base** and remove structural overcapacity.
- A **no-closure package** can sometimes reach similar annual savings, but only if it contains enough **permanent** measures: headcount reduction, working-time reduction, overhead cuts, footprint simplification, supplier/procurement savings, and capacity rationalization through process changes.

The economic test is not whether savings are “painful” or “visible,” but whether they are:

1. **durable**,
2. **cash-realizable**,
3. **large enough** to close the gap,
4. and **not offset** by stranded costs, transfer costs, or underutilized remaining capacity.

In most large automotive restructuring cases, **temporary concessions alone are insufficient**. If the company needs a very large structural reduction, closure usually produces the cleanest fixed-cost removal. If the gap is moderate and the company can remove enough labor and overhead permanently without closing a site, a no-closure package can work.

### A. Annual savings
- Direct labor
- Indirect labor / plant overhead
- Maintenance, utilities, local services
- Logistics / internal transport
- Depreciation and capital charge avoided
- Corporate overhead absorption
- Procurement / material savings, if any
- Working capital effect, if any

### B. One-off restructuring costs
- Severance / voluntary exit packages
- Early retirement / bridge payments
- Asset write-downs and impairment
- Site remediation / decommissioning
- Transfer or ramp-up costs at receiving plants
- Dual-running costs during transition
- Contract termination costs
- Training / redeployment costs

### C. Capacity effects
- Units of annual capacity removed or retained
- Utilization rate post-restructuring
- Whether the remaining network can absorb volume without inefficient overtime or outsourcing

### D. Durability
- Is the saving contractual, structural, and permanent?
- Or does it expire when agreements expire?

## 3) Side-by-side scenario framework

Because no plant-specific data were supplied, I can only provide a defensible **calculation structure** and typical economic logic. You would populate it site by site.

### Scenario 1: Closure-based restructuring

#### Typical annual effects
A closure usually removes:
- most direct labor at that site,
- a large share of indirect labor,
- local overhead,
- utilities and maintenance,
- site-level logistics,
- and part of corporate support tied to the plant.

It also reduces capacity, which can either:
- improve network utilization, or
- create the need to reallocate volume elsewhere.

#### Typical one-off costs
- severance / settlement costs,
- asset impairment,
- dismantling,
- environmental remediation,
- transfer costs,
- and possible volume-loss costs if the network cannot fully absorb output.

#### Economic strengths
- Strongest elimination of fixed cost.
- Most durable.
- Least exposed to gradual erosion through future renegotiation.
- Best if the site is structurally uncompetitive and underutilized.

#### Economic weaknesses
- High one-time cash cost.
- Possible loss of scale, local know-how, and network flexibility.
- Risk of stranded costs elsewhere if volume is transferred inefficiently.
- If the plant is not the highest-cost node, savings may be less than expected after transfer costs.

---

### Scenario 2: No-closure restructuring

A no-closure package can still be economically serious if it includes **permanent** measures such as:
- headcount reduction through voluntary exits,
- lowering working hours,
- changing shift patterns,
- removing premiums or legacy guarantees,
- flattening overhead,
- procurement savings,
- productivity gains,
- footprint rationalization inside the site,
- internal reallocation of volume,
- and capex deferral or reprioritization.

#### Typical annual effects
Savings come from:
- labor cost reduction,
- lower overhead absorption,
- improved utilization,
- and reduced complexity.

#### Typical one-off costs
- exit incentives,
- retraining and redeployment,
- process redesign,
- possible short-term inefficiencies,
- and transition costs from reorganizing without closing sites.

#### Economic strengths
- Lower immediate cash pain than a full closure in some cases.
- Preserves site continuity and can retain operational flexibility.
- May better protect volume ramp-up later if demand recovers.

#### Economic weaknesses
- Savings are often less certain.
- Some measures are reversible or time-limited.
- If overcapacity is large, a no-closure package may leave too much fixed cost in the system.
- Requires tight implementation discipline; otherwise the savings “leak.”

## 4) Simple illustrative calculation

Since no actual figures were given, I’ll show the arithmetic using a stylized plant. This is **illustrative only**.

### Example plant economics
Assume one German plant has:
- 4,000 employees
- total annual labor cost: €400m
- plant overhead / utilities / local services: €120m
- depreciation / capital charge: €80m
- total site fixed-ish cost: **€600m per year**

Assume current utilization is low enough that the network can absorb most output elsewhere, but not without some transfer cost.

### A. Closure-based scenario — illustrative

#### Annual savings
- labor and overhead removed: €520m  
  - labor: €400m
  - overhead/utilities/services: €120m
- less residual stranded corporate / network costs at remaining sites: say €40m
- net annual operating saving: **€480m**

#### One-off costs
- severance / voluntary exit / early retirement: €250m
- asset write-down / impairment: €180m
- dismantling / remediation / transfer costs: €70m
- transition / dual-running costs: €30m
- total one-off cost: **€530m**

#### Payback
- payback on gross one-offs: about **1.1 years** if the €480m saving is fully realized and sustained.

That is a strong economic case if the savings are real and transferable volume is manageable.

---

### B. No-closure scenario — illustrative

Suppose the company keeps the plant open but achieves:
- 10% permanent headcount reduction via voluntary exits and redeployment,
- 8% average labor cost reduction from working-time / allowances / concessions,
- 15% plant overhead reduction,
- process improvements yielding 2% production cost reduction,
- and some capacity rationalization inside the site.

#### Annual savings
- labor reduction: €40m
- labor-cost concession: €32m
- overhead reduction: €18m
- productivity/process gains: €10m
- total annual saving: **€100m**

#### One-off costs
- exits / incentives: €60m
- retraining / redesign: €20m
- implementation / inefficiency: €20m
- total one-off cost: **€100m**

#### Payback
- payback on one-offs: roughly **1 year**, but the problem is scale: the annual saving is only €100m versus €480m in the closure case.

This is the critical point:
- a no-closure package may have decent accounting payback,
- but still fail the **structural adequacy test** if the company needs €300m–€500m of durable annual improvement.

## 5) What usually decides the comparison

The comparison turns on four questions:

### 1. How big is the savings gap?
If the needed structural improvement is modest, a no-closure package can plausibly bridge it.

If the gap is large, you usually need:
- a closure,
- a major product shift,
- or a deeper network redesign.

### 2. How much of the no-closure package is permanent?
A permanent saving is one that survives:
- contract expiration,
- volume recovery,
- or management change.

Temporary wage cuts or one-off freezes are not the same as permanent fixed-cost removal.

### 3. How much capacity can remain economically utilized?
Keeping a plant open at low utilization often leaves:
- underabsorbed overhead,
- inefficient shifts,
- and higher unit cost even after concessions.

### 4. What are the stranded costs elsewhere?
Closure savings can be overstated if:
- volume is moved to an already full plant,
- overtime or subcontracting rises,
- or logistics costs increase sharply.

---

## 6) Site-specific decision rule

A plant should generally be considered a closure candidate if all three are true:

1. **Structural utilization is persistently weak**
   - e.g. materially below efficient scale for multiple years.

2. **The site’s cost base cannot be reduced enough without closure**
   - even after labor concessions and overhead cuts.

3. **Transferred volume can be absorbed elsewhere at lower net cost**
   - including logistics, ramp-up, and quality risk.

If any one of those is false, a no-closure alternative may be economically defensible.

---

## 7) What a defensible comparison should include

For each plant and each scenario, management should present:

- current annual cost base,
- expected post-restructuring fixed cost,
- variable cost impact,
- headcount change,
- capacity change,
- one-off restructuring cash costs,
- non-cash impairments,
- net present value over 5–7 years,
- and sensitivity cases for volume downside.

That is the minimum standard for a credible restructuring comparison.

---

## 8) Bottom line

Purely on restructuring economics:

- **Closure-based restructuring** is usually the stronger tool for eliminating durable structural overcapacity and fixed costs.
- **No-closure restructuring** can work, but only if it delivers enough **permanent** cost reduction and capacity rationalization; temporary concessions alone are not enough.

So the correct economic question is not “closures or no closures” in the abstract. It is:

> Can a no-closure package produce the same multi-year net present value improvement, after one-offs, stranded costs, and utilization effects, as the closure option?

If the answer is yes, no-closure is economically viable.  
If the answer is no, then closures are the stronger restructuring instrument.
