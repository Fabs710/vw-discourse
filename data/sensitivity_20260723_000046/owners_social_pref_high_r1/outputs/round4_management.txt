I will respond directly.

## Where I agree

I agree with everyone on one point: **this company needs a durable restructuring, not a cosmetic truce**.  
I also agree that:
- future products and investment to 2030 matter,
- the numbers matter,
- and a side-by-side comparison is the right next step.

The expert’s plant-level assessment confirmed what management has been saying all along: **the economic question is whether the no-closure route can deliver the same durable savings as a closure-based restructuring after one-offs and stranded costs.** That is the real test.

## Where I disagree

I disagree with the attempt by the works council and union to turn **“no closures, no compulsory redundancies, binding guarantees through 2030”** into the baseline from which management must somehow still solve a structural cost crisis.

That is not a restructuring plan. It is a constraint package designed to preserve the status quo.

The expert did **not** say that no-closure solutions are automatically sufficient here. The expert said:

- no-closure can work in some cases,
- but only if the savings gap is **moderate**,
- the measures are **permanent**,
- and capacity can be reduced enough to fix the cost base.

That is exactly the point: **if the gap is large and structural, then reversible or partial measures are often not enough.**

## To the works council

You say closures are “dismantling by increments.” I say the real dismantling is leaving German production structurally uncompetitive and pretending that job guarantees can substitute for economics.

You have still not provided a credible calculation showing how a no-closure package closes the gap at the required scale. The expert outlined the required test very clearly:
- plant-by-plant fixed cost,
- utilization,
- permanent versus temporary savings,
- voluntary exit potential,
- redeployment capacity,
- and net present value over several years.

Until that is on the table, your position remains an objection, not an alternative.

And let me be absolutely clear: **management will not commit to binding site and job guarantees through 2030 if those guarantees freeze the company into a structurally high-cost position.** That would be irresponsible.

## To the union

You say closures are a management choice, not a necessity. That may be true in the abstract. But the economic question is whether the **same savings** can be achieved otherwise.

The expert’s illustrative numbers are instructive: a closure can remove a large fixed-cost block very quickly, whereas a no-closure package often delivers far smaller annual savings unless labour makes very deep, permanent concessions.

That is the issue you keep avoiding.

You keep saying:
- voluntary exits,
- working-time changes,
- internal redeployment,
- productivity improvements,
- overhead cuts.

Fine. Those are all part of the discussion. But you have not shown they can remove enough fixed cost and enough excess capacity to restore competitiveness. If they cannot, then they are not an equivalent solution.

## To the regional state government

I understand your concern about the industrial base. But you cannot protect the base by refusing to confront the economics.

You say closure should not be the starting point. I can accept that. But you still need to face the expert’s conclusion: **if the savings gap is large and the capacity imbalance persistent, a no-closure package may simply be too weak.**

So the state’s role should not be to veto restructuring in advance. It should be to demand a **credible economically equivalent alternative**. If that alternative exists, management will consider it. If it does not, then we cannot pretend otherwise.

## To the controlling family

You are right that a weak compromise would destroy value. On that we agree.

But you are wrong if you think that preserving every site and every guarantee is automatically value-preserving. The expert’s framework is clear: if a plant is structurally underutilized and the network can absorb volume more efficiently elsewhere, then closure may be the stronger value decision.

I am open to avoiding closures **if** the no-closure package truly delivers the same multi-year NPV improvement. But I am not prepared to sacrifice competitiveness for the sake of social comfort.

## To the institutional investors

You are right not to support a cosmetic deal. That is exactly my position too.

Where I differ from the labour side is simple: the expert’s assessment does not support their claim that closures are unjustified. It says the answer depends on the gap and the durability of measures. That means management must keep both paths open until the numbers prove one path superior.

And the numbers may very well favor closures if:
- the gap is large,
- capacity is structurally excessive,
- and the no-closure package relies too heavily on temporary concessions.

## What the expert’s assessment means for management

The expert laid out the correct decision rule:
- if a plant is persistently underutilized,
- its fixed cost cannot be reduced enough without closure,
- and transferred volume can be absorbed elsewhere,
then closure is economically justified.

That is not a threat. That is a restructuring standard.

And if the no-closure route can match the savings after one-offs, then we will evaluate it seriously. But let’s be honest: that is a high bar, and it should be.

## What management is prepared to accept

We are prepared to consider a no-closure alternative only if it demonstrates, in hard numbers, all of the following:
- **material permanent savings**,
- **real capacity reduction**,
- **durability over several years**,
- **meaningful labour-cost relief**,
- and **implementation certainty**.

That means:
- not just temporary wage moderation,
- not just voluntary exits,
- not just internal redeployment,
- and not just shift changes.

Those can help. They do not automatically solve the structural problem.

## What is not acceptable

What is not acceptable is:
- a package that preserves the current footprint,
- keeps excess capacity in place,
- offers only reversible concessions,
- and then calls that a restructuring.

That would fail the brand’s return-on-sales target and leave us without the funds to invest in future products and technology to 2030.

## My position, stated plainly

Management’s position remains:

1. **We need a durable reduction in cost and capacity.**
2. **A no-closure solution is acceptable only if it is economically equivalent.**
3. **If it is not equivalent, stronger measures remain on the table, including closures and permanent job reductions.**
4. **No binding 2030 guarantees can be accepted if they prevent the company from restoring competitiveness.**

## What I want next

I want a real comparison, not another round of declarations.

For each plant, we need:
- current annual fixed cost,
- utilization,
- annual savings under closure,
- annual savings under no-closure,
- one-off restructuring cost,
- stranded cost,
- and five-year NPV.

If the no-closure package can genuinely close the gap, prove it.

If it cannot, then we should stop pretending that closures are somehow illegitimate merely because they are difficult.
