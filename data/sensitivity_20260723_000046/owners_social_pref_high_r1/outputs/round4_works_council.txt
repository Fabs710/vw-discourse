I will respond just as directly.

## First: what is still non-negotiable

Let me be clear: **no German plant closures and no compulsory redundancies**.  
That is not a negotiating preference. It is the line.

If management or the owners want labour cooperation, then they must operate inside that framework. I will not treat closures as a “possible tool” and then bargain over which plant, which site, or how many jobs are sacrificed. That is not restructuring; that is dismantling by increments.

## What I agree with

I agree with all parties on one point: the company needs **durable restructuring** and real future investment. I also accept that the package must include:
- productivity gains,
- capacity adjustment,
- working-time flexibility,
- voluntary exits,
- redeployment,
- overhead and procurement savings,
- and a fair employee contribution.

So no, I am not defending a status quo that ignores the crisis. But restructuring can and must happen **without closing plants or forcing people out**.

## Where I disagree

### 1) With management
Management keeps framing this as if the choice were:
- closures and layoffs, or
- an unserious, temporary compromise.

That is false.

The expert already said a no-closure package can be feasible, especially if the gap is not huge and if the company is willing to use multiple levers. What management has **not** done is prove that closures are required. You keep asking labour to prove the alternative works, while refusing to publish the numbers in a way that can actually be tested.

So here is the burden:  
If you want anyone to even consider your argument, bring:
- plant-by-plant utilization,
- fixed vs. variable cost by site,
- exact savings from each proposed measure,
- voluntary exit potential,
- redeployment capacity,
- and the durability of each saving.

Until then, “overcapacity” is just a slogan used to justify sacrificing jobs.

### 2) With the controlling family
You say you support long-term value, but long-term value is destroyed when management breaks trust with the workforce and tears up job security. Skilled labour, industrial peace, and predictable site commitments are not sentimental extras. They are part of the company’s operating strength.

So I reject the idea that the family can stay above the conflict while treating closure pressure as a normal governance instrument. If you want the company’s long-term value protected, then support a solution that keeps every German plant open and preserves job security.

### 3) With the institutional investors
You keep saying a cosmetic deal is unacceptable. On that, I agree.

But you then leap to closures as if they are the only serious answer. That is not proven. The expert did **not** say closures are automatically necessary. The expert said the answer depends on the size of the gap and the durability of measures. That means investors should not pre-judge the outcome before the comparison is on the table.

A serious no-closure package can still be economically real:
- capacity reduction without closure,
- shift and working-time redesign,
- voluntary departures only,
- productivity improvements,
- internal reassignment,
- management and overhead cuts,
- and limited employee contribution.

That is restructuring. What it is not is a blank cheque for plant closures.

### 4) With the regional state government
You are right to oppose closures as a starting point. But I need more than “we prefer negotiation.”

If you are serious about your blocking role, then use it to prevent any closure-driven plan from becoming the default outcome. Not “if necessary later,” not “as a fallback,” but now: no support for closures and no support for compulsory layoffs. Anything less weakens the only leverage that protects this industrial base.

## On the expert’s position
The expert’s answer was useful, but it did **not** make closures inevitable. It said:
- a no-closure package can work in some cases,
- it depends on the size and nature of the gap,
- and if the company needs very large durable savings, the challenge grows.

Exactly. That is why management must show the numbers.  
But it does **not** give management a license to treat closures as the baseline.

## What I am prepared to discuss
We are prepared to discuss a serious restructuring package that includes:
- productivity measures,
- capacity reduction through internal reorganization,
- working-time changes,
- voluntary exits,
- redeployment,
- procurement and overhead cuts,
- and a limited employee contribution,

**provided that** it comes with binding protections:
1. **No German plant closures**
2. **No compulsory redundancies**
3. **Binding site and job security through 2030**
4. **Strong co-determination over implementation**
5. **Investment and future product commitments that are enforceable**

That is the minimum for a legitimate settlement.

## What is not acceptable
I need to say this plainly because the discussion keeps drifting toward euphemism:

- “Capacity reduction” that closes plants is closure.
- “Socially unavoidable reductions” that force workers out are compulsory redundancies.
- “Flexibility” that destroys job security is not compromise.
- A package that asks labour to concede first and promises investment later is not a settlement; it is a transfer of risk.

We do **not** accept that.

## What needs to happen next
The next step must be a **real comparison**, but not on management’s terms alone. I want a side-by-side comparison based on **actual figures**, with the same accounting horizon, showing:
- the savings needed,
- the savings from each measure,
- which savings are permanent,
- where capacity reduction comes from,
- how many jobs are affected,
- and how investment capacity to 2030 changes.

If management wants to argue that a no-closure deal cannot work, then prove it with facts, not pressure.

## My bottom line
My position has not moved:

**No plant closures. No compulsory redundancies.  
No weakening of co-determination or job-security guarantees.**

If management can operate within those limits, then we will talk about the structure of the restructuring. If not, then the company is choosing confrontation over responsible turnaround.

So my challenge remains unchanged:  
Bring the numbers, but bring them in a way that respects the workforce and the law.
