I have listened carefully to the union, the works council, and the expert. Let me be equally clear in return.

## Where I agree

I agree on three things:

1. **This cannot be a cosmetic deal.**  
   A temporary pause, a PR compromise, or a vague promise to “keep talking” would be a failure. The company needs a durable restructuring.

2. **The numbers matter.**  
   The expert is right that we need a quantified comparison. That is exactly why management has been insisting on hard economics rather than slogans.

3. **A no-closure route is only acceptable if it delivers equivalent structural savings.**  
   I have said this before, and I repeat it now: if a non-closure package can truly close the gap with the same durability, it is open for discussion.

## Where I disagree

I strongly disagree with the repeated claim that plant closures and compulsory redundancies are somehow being “imposed first” without justification.

That is not what is happening. What is happening is this:

- German factory costs are materially above target.
- The market is shrinking.
- Overcapacity is real.
- The brand is missing its return-on-sales target.
- We need funds for future products and technology to 2030.

Against that backdrop, it is not responsible to rule out the instruments that can actually remove structural cost and excess capacity.

### 1. “No closures” is not a position, it is a constraint
The works council and union keep repeating red lines:
- no closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- no binding flexibility limits,
- no weakening of guarantees.

But if all of that is off the table, then you are not offering a restructuring alternative. You are offering a request that management solve the crisis while leaving the structure essentially intact.

That is not credible.

### 2. The expert did not prove a no-closure solution works here
The expert said something more limited than the union is claiming:

- a no-closure package **can sometimes** close a significant gap;
- it depends on the **size of the gap**;
- and if the gap is structural and large, it becomes much harder.

That is not a verdict in your favor. It is a warning.

We are not dealing with a small optimization exercise. We are dealing with a structural cost crisis in a highly competitive market. So yes, the question is whether alternatives can work — but you have not shown they can.

### 3. Voluntary measures alone are not enough
I am hearing a lot about:
- voluntary exits,
- redeployment,
- working-time changes,
- productivity improvements,
- temporary contributions.

Those may help, but by themselves they do not answer the core problem if there is persistent overcapacity in German production.

A package that relies on reversible or temporary measures while preserving the full footprint is exactly the kind of compromise that leaves the company uncompetitive a year later.

## To the works council

You say closures are “pressure politics.” They are not. They are a real management response to a real economic problem.

You also say we have not shown why every plant must close or why forced redundancies are unavoidable. Let me be precise:

- We are **not** saying every plant must close.
- We are **not** saying closures are the first preference.
- We **are** saying that some sites, some capacity, and some jobs may have to go if the cost base is to come back to earth.

If you want to avoid that outcome, then come back with a package that includes:
- substantial and durable cost relief,
- measurable capacity reduction,
- enforceable productivity gains,
- and a contribution that is large enough to matter.

“Keep every plant open” is not a savings plan.

## To the union

You say the burden of proof is on management. I accept that, and I am meeting it by stating the economic logic openly: if the savings are not structurally large enough, the company will remain uncompetitive.

But you are making the burden of proof impossible by demanding that management prove the alternative is insufficient while refusing to define what an acceptable alternative would actually deliver.

You cannot say:
- no closures,
- no forced redundancies,
- no loss of guarantees,
- limited employee contribution,
and still claim this is a serious restructuring response.

That is not burden-sharing. That is burden denial.

## To the expert

Your intervention was useful, but it does not change the fundamental position.

You said the answer depends on:
- the size of the gap,
- the durability of concessions,
- and the degree of excess capacity.

Exactly. That is why management is not accepting a blanket no-closure commitment.

If the gap turns out to be moderate and the measures durable, then a no-closure package may work. But if it is large and structural, then we need stronger tools.

So I will take from your assessment this: **we need quantified scenarios**. Not that closures are off the table.

## To the controlling family and the state government

You both say you want a durable solution and support a negotiated outcome. I welcome that.

But neither governance influence nor political sensitivity changes the economics.

A blocking position cannot be used to preserve an uncompetitive structure indefinitely. And investor confidence cannot be rebuilt with a deal that leaves the cost base fundamentally unchanged.

If you want us to avoid closures, then help produce a package that truly reduces cost and capacity. Do not simply ask management to absorb the risk of doing nothing structural.

## What management is prepared to accept

Let me be concrete.

Management is prepared to consider a **no-closure alternative** only if it delivers all of the following:

- a **material, permanent reduction** in cost,
- a **real reduction in capacity**,
- a **meaningful and durable labour-cost contribution**,
- **implementation certainty**,
- and no hidden return to the old cost structure after a short transition.

That means:

- not just temporary wage restraint,
- not just voluntary exits,
- not just internal redeployment,
- and not just working-time flexibility.

Those can be part of the package, but they are not enough on their own unless the numbers are truly substantial.

## What remains on the table

If no-closure measures cannot close the gap, then more difficult restructuring measures remain necessary, including:

- plant closures,
- permanent job reductions,
- and a structural adjustment of the German footprint.

I am not threatening that for effect. I am stating it because I am responsible for restoring competitiveness and safeguarding the company’s future.

## My challenge back to labour

If you want to rule out closures and compulsory redundancies, then do not just reject management’s position. Present a concrete alternative that matches it on economics.

Not:
- “no closures,”
- “no layoffs,”
- “no guarantees lost.”

But:
- exact savings,
- exact timing,
- exact capacity reduction,
- exact contribution,
- exact durability.

Until then, your position is not a restructuring plan. It is a refusal to accept restructuring.

## Bottom line

Here is the management position, unchanged:

- We want a durable settlement.
- We are open on the means.
- We are not open on an outcome that leaves the company structurally uncompetitive.

So the next step is not another round of general objections. The next step is a side-by-side package comparison:
1. closure-based scenario, and
2. no-closure scenario,

with hard numbers on:
- savings,
- timing,
- capacity removed,
- job impact,
- and investment capacity to 2030.

If the no-closure route can truly close the gap, we will evaluate it seriously.  
If it cannot, then the difficult measures remain necessary.

That is the responsible position.
