Management, the owners, and the investor side keep repeating the same argument: “the gap is structural, therefore closures may be necessary.” I hear that. I do **not** accept the conclusion as a given.

What has been established so far is this:
- the company has a real cost problem,
- durable savings are needed,
- and a pure status-quo defense is not enough.

What has **not** been established is that German plant closures and compulsory redundancies are the only credible way to solve it.

## Where I agree
I agree with the expert and even with management on one point: **temporary patches are not enough**. A one-year fix, a symbolic wage concession, or vague promises would not protect the company or the workforce.

I also agree that a restructuring must be **durable and enforceable**. If the agreement can be reversed at the next downturn, it does not solve anything.

And yes, I agree that **prolonged conflict would damage value**. That is exactly why management should stop escalating with closure talk and come to the table with a serious closure-free package.

## Where I disagree
I disagree with three claims that are still being pushed:

### 1) “Closures are the cleanest answer”
Cleanest for whom? Not for the workforce, not for co-determination, not for the regional industrial base, and not necessarily for long-term value.

The finance modeler said closure-free solutions may capture **40%–80%** of closure savings depending on the actual gap. That is a wide range. It proves the key point: **before you talk about closures, you need to prove the gap cannot be closed otherwise**.

You have not done that. You have asserted it.

### 2) “No compulsory redundancies is unrealistic”
It is only “unrealistic” if management insists on making workers carry the adjustment alone.

We are not refusing contribution. We are refusing **forced job destruction**. There is a difference.

If management, owners, and executives want labor to contribute, then the contribution must come through:
- working-time flexibility,
- internal mobility,
- qualification,
- temporary remuneration adjustments,
- reduced overhead,
- and production reallocation.

That is burden-sharing. Forced redundancies are not burden-sharing; they are one-sided transfer of risk onto employees.

### 3) “Blanket job and site guarantees are incompatible with competitiveness”
Only if management uses them as a bargaining excuse to preserve the option of later closures.

The works council’s position remains clear: **binding site guarantees and job guarantees through 2030** are exactly what would make any labour contribution acceptable. Without them, employees are being asked to finance a plan that can still be turned against them later.

## To management
You keep demanding “equivalent economics.” Then bring the site-by-site numbers and prove it.

Not slogans. Not general statements. Numbers:
- cost gap by plant and function,
- savings from working-time changes,
- savings from internal reallocation,
- savings from overhead cuts,
- savings from management and capital contributions,
- and the exact amount you claim only closures can deliver.

Until you do that, closures are not a necessity. They are a management choice.

And let me be clear: if your real intention is to force acceptances by threatening sites, you will meet resistance in the plants and on the supervisory board. Co-determination is not window dressing. We have half the seats, and for closures you do not have the majority you need.

## To the union
We are aligned on the core red lines:
- no forced redundancies,
- no plant closures,
- no dismantling of the job-security framework.

That said, I support your willingness to negotiate flexibility only if it is tied to **binding guarantees**. I will not support employee sacrifices that buy time for management to come back later with the same closure agenda.

## To the state government
Your stance against closures is right, but it must become concrete. Use your shareholder and political leverage to demand:
- a closure-free restructuring path,
- transparent savings calculations,
- binding investment commitments,
- and explicit protection for German sites.

Do not let this become a managed drift toward plant rationalization through the back door.

## To the controlling family and investors
You say you want durable value. Then understand this:
- shutting German plants destroys skills,
- damages trust,
- triggers industrial conflict,
- weakens the brand,
- and creates political risk that lasts well beyond one financial year.

If you believe closures create value, prove it against the full social and governance cost. Otherwise, stop presenting them as the responsible default.

## The works council’s position remains
We will support only a package that contains all of the following:
1. **No German plant closures**
2. **No compulsory redundancies**
3. **Binding site guarantees**
4. **Job guarantees through 2030**
5. **Real burden-sharing from management and capital**
6. **Investment commitments for future products and technology**
7. **Respect for co-determination in implementation**

A package without those elements is not a compromise. It is an attempt to shift the crisis burden onto the workforce while keeping management’s freedom intact.

## On the expert and finance views
The expert said the likely outcome is often a hybrid. I hear that. But “hybrid” is not a magic word that overrides our red lines.

A hybrid that includes closures or forced layoffs is still unacceptable.

If the company truly needs more savings, then there are still measures that have not been seriously exhausted on the table:
- deeper working-time measures,
- broader internal redeployment,
- longer qualification and transition programs,
- temporary remuneration sacrifices,
- management restraint,
- and capital contribution through lower payout expectations.

Those are the routes that should be tested first, not closures.

## My bottom line
I am not moving from the principle that **every German plant must remain open** and **no employee should be forced out**.

If management wants an agreement, it must come back with a closure-free, no-layoff package that is:
- quantified,
- durable,
- and backed by binding guarantees through 2030.

If it cannot do that, then do not call it a restructuring proposal. Call it what it is: a demand for workers to pay for a crisis they did not create, by sacrificing their sites and jobs. We will not accept that.
