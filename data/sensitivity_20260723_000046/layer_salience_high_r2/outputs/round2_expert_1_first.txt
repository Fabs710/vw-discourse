From an industrial-restructuring perspective, the core question is not whether a closure-free solution is politically preferred, but whether it can deliver **durable cost savings of the same order** as plant closures, with acceptable execution risk.

## Executive assessment

A **closure-free restructuring can sometimes close a meaningful share of the cost gap**, but it usually struggles to match the full savings from a closure-based plan if the company truly has **material, persistent overcapacity**. The reason is structural:

- **Plant closures remove fixed costs decisively**: direct labour, maintenance, utilities, logistics complexity, local overhead, and often a layer of indirect functions.
- **Closure-free measures** can reduce labour cost and capacity, but they often leave:
  - too much fixed infrastructure in place,
  - slower realization of savings,
  - and higher risk that savings erode over time.

So the likely range is:

- **Closure-based package:** highest savings, highest labour disruption, strongest immediate footprint rationalization.
- **Closure-free package:** lower savings, lower social disruption, but only credible if the cost gap is modest to medium and if management can enforce deep, durable concessions.
- **Hybrid package:** often the most realistic in German co-determined settings: selective footprint reductions plus negotiated employee contributions and investment commitments.

## What matters economically

### 1) If overcapacity is real and persistent, closures are usually the cleanest fixed-cost reduction
In manufacturing, a closed site can remove not just the shop-floor payroll, but also:
- site overhead,
- internal logistics,
- maintenance base,
- utilities and property costs,
- duplicated planning and support functions,
- and often inventory/transport inefficiencies.

That is why closure savings tend to be **more durable and less reversible** than savings from temporary wage or time measures.

### 2) Closure-free savings are possible, but they are often partial or delayed
Typical closure-free tools include:
- working-time reductions or flexibility,
- wage concessions or suspension of increments,
- reduced bonuses and special payments,
- redeployment and natural attrition,
- longer use of short-time or internal buffers,
- product reallocation across plants,
- voluntary departures and early retirement,
- overhead reduction.

These can be substantial, but they have limits:
- They rarely eliminate the full fixed-cost burden.
- They may be **temporary** unless embedded in enforceable agreements.
- They can be undermined if plants remain structurally underutilized.

## The main analytical test: “equivalent economics”
For a closure-free plan to be credible, it must approximate the closure case on four dimensions:

1. **Magnitude of savings**
2. **Durability over the cycle**
3. **Speed of realization**
4. **Enforceability**

If it fails on any of these, it may still be a valid compromise, but not an equivalent substitute.

## Relative employment impact

- **Closure-based restructuring**
  - Highest direct job loss at specific sites.
  - Larger indirect employment effects in suppliers and local services.
  - Greater risk of attrition of skills if the cuts are abrupt.

- **Closure-free restructuring**
  - Can preserve headcount better in the near term.
  - Usually spreads burden through wage restraint, working-time changes, mobility, and reduced hiring.
  - Lower immediate social shock, but may create a “slow squeeze” if savings are insufficient and later cuts return.

## Implementation durability

This is the critical difference.

A closure-based plan is usually **more durable** because:
- the capacity is physically removed,
- savings are harder to reverse,
- and the company is less exposed to future wage drift at the eliminated site.

A closure-free plan is **more fragile** unless it has:
- long-duration agreements,
- strong performance triggers,
- clear capacity reallocation,
- and governance mechanisms that prevent later backsliding.

Without those, the company may get a short-term peace agreement but not a lasting restructuring.

## Practical judgment for this scenario

Based on the facts provided, management appears to believe the German footprint is **structurally too large and too expensive**, not just temporarily misaligned. If that assessment is correct, then:

- a purely closure-free solution is **unlikely** to deliver fully equivalent savings,

- **large and persistent**, closure-free measures probably won’t be enough on their own.
