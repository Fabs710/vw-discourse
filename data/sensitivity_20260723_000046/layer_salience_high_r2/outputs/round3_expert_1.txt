## Industrial logic on the three broad options

### 1) Closure-based restructuring
This is usually the **cleanest way** to remove fixed cost and excess capacity when a domestic footprint is structurally too large.

**Advantages**
- Largest and most durable fixed-cost reduction
- Simplifies plant and product allocation
- Can restore utilization at the remaining sites

**Disadvantages**
- High transition disruption
- Strong labor and political resistance
- Large restructuring charges
- Risk of strikes, implementation delay, and governance veto
- Potential loss of skills, supplier relationships, and future flexibility

**Assessment**
Economically powerful, but in this governance setup it is only feasible if the economic case is overwhelming and the board coalition is secured. Otherwise, it is likely to stall or be heavily watered down.

### 2) Closure-free restructuring
This relies on:
- working-time flexibility
- remuneration moderation
- temporary or permanent labor-cost concessions
- internal redeployment
- qualification and voluntary exits
- overhead reduction
- capacity reduction without full plant shutdowns

**Advantages**
- Lower immediate social and political disruption
- Better chance of labor agreement
- Preserves sites and industrial continuity
- Less risk of catastrophic labor conflict

**Disadvantages**
- Usually delivers only **partial** savings versus closures
- Savings are often less durable unless locked in tightly
- Can leave excess capacity in place
- May require repeated renegotiation later
- Risk of “delay without reset” if concessions are too shallow

**Assessment**
Closure-free restructuring can work **only if the gap is moderate** and the concessions are large, durable, and enforceable. If the structural cost gap is wide, it tends to underperform closure-based restructuring.

### 3) Hybrid restructuring
This is often the most realistic outcome in a German co-determination setting.

Typical form:
- one or more targeted footprint reductions
- combined with labor-cost concessions
- voluntary workforce reduction
- internal reallocation
- investment commitments for the remaining sites

**Advantages**
- More feasible politically than pure closures
- More economically credible than pure closure-free adjustment
- Can spread burden across stakeholders
- Often the best compromise between durability and implementability

**Disadvantages**
- Hard to design
- May satisfy nobody fully
- Still requires difficult concessions from labor and capital
- If poorly calibrated, can end up too small to fix the economics

**Assessment**
If a deal exists, this is the most likely shape. It is the most common route when neither side can impose its preferred outcome.

## Main analytic point
The most important question is not “closures or no closures?”  
It is:

**What is the minimum savings package that restores competitiveness, and can it be achieved without full plant closures?**

That requires:
- exact savings target
- plant-level economics
- labor-cost contribution
- overhead and productivity measures
- investment commitments
- durability through at least 2028–2030

## Practical conclusion
Based on restructuring logic alone:

- A **pure closure-free settlement** is only credible if the required savings gap is relatively modest and the labor contribution is unusually deep and durable.
- A **pure closure-based settlement** may be financially cleaner, but is institutionally hard to execute in this governance structure.
- A **hybrid package** is the most plausible economically and politically, provided it includes real footprint reduction plus measurable labor and management contributions.
