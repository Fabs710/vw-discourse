I’ll respond plainly and directly.

## Where I agree

I agree with the works council, the state government, and the expert on the basic point: **this is a structural problem and a temporary patch will not solve it**. I also agree that a deal must be **durable, enforceable, and quantified**.

I further agree that **prolonged conflict would damage the company** and that burden-sharing is unavoidable. Employees cannot be the only group contributing.

## Where I disagree

### 1) I do **not** accept closures as the default answer
Management, the family, and investors keep speaking as if plant closures are the obvious “rational” path. They are not — at least not yet.

What has been established is:
- there is a cost gap,
- savings are needed,
- and the current setup is too expensive.

What has **not** been established is that German closures are the only way to close that gap.

If closure-free or low-closure measures can deliver a meaningful and durable share of the savings, then closures are not a necessity. They are a preference.

### 2) I do **not** accept compulsory redundancies
This remains a hard line.

If the company wants labor to contribute, then the contribution must come through:
- working-time flexibility,
- temporary remuneration adjustments,
- bonus restraint,
- internal mobility,
- qualification and redeployment,
- and where needed, capacity reduction through negotiated transitions.

That is burden-sharing.

Forced layoffs are not burden-sharing. They are a one-sided transfer of crisis costs onto workers.

### 3) I do **not** accept dismantling the collective wage framework
I am willing to discuss **temporary, negotiated labor-cost relief**. I am not willing to let the collective agreement be hollowed out permanently under the pressure of closure threats.

If the company wants a durable settlement, then the collective framework must survive. Otherwise the “solution” merely sets up the next conflict.

## What I think the current debate is missing
Everyone keeps repeating “durable” and “structural,” but we still do **not** have the numbers.

I want a **board-ready, site-by-site comparison** showing:
- the minimum annual savings target,
- the cost gap by plant and function,
- what working-time changes deliver,
- what remuneration measures deliver,
- what internal reallocation delivers,
- what management and ownership contribute,
- and the **marginal savings from closures versus non-closure measures**.

Until that is on the table, no one can honestly claim closures are unavoidable.

## To management
You say a closure-free package may be too weak. Fine — prove it.

Put the full economics on the table:
- exact savings required,
- which savings are structural,
- which are temporary,
- which are site-specific,
- and how much only closures would add.

If your case is strong, then show it. But stop treating closure as a foregone conclusion before the evidence is complete.

And let me be clear: if the real intention is to force acceptance by threatening plants, that will trigger resistance in the workforce and on the supervisory board. Co-determination is not symbolic.

## To the works council
We are aligned on the core red lines:
- no compulsory redundancies,
- no plant closures,
- no destruction of the wage framework.

Your insistence on binding site and job guarantees through 2030 is understandable, and I support strong security **if** it is paired with a real savings package that preserves viability.

But let’s be honest: guarantees without a serious contribution from all sides will not hold. We need measurable labor measures, management restraint, and owner contribution as well.

## To the state government
Your rejection of closures is politically and industrially important, but it must become concrete. Use your leverage to demand:
- transparent site-by-site numbers,
- a closure-free or at least closure-light path as the starting point,
- binding investment commitments,
- and protection of German industrial capacity.

Do not let this drift into “closure by stealth” through attrition, outsourcing, or silent capacity erosion.

## To the controlling family and investors
You say you want durable value. Then support a package that actually protects the company’s industrial base.

Yes, ownership should not endorse a cosmetic compromise. But neither should ownership demand that workers bear the pain while management and capital remain largely insulated.

If you want a credible turnaround, then ownership must also contribute:
- lower payout expectations,
- executive restraint,
- willingness to fund the transition,
- and acceptance that a slightly lower gross savings package may still be the better risk-adjusted outcome if it avoids conflict and preserves execution.

## My position, clearly stated
The union is prepared to negotiate a **real, quantified, durable burden-sharing settlement**.

We are **not** prepared to accept:
- compulsory redundancies,
- German plant closures,
- or the dismantling of the collective wage framework.

A package that truly moves us would need to include:
1. strong employee contribution,
2. comparable management and ownership contribution,
3. binding investment commitments,
4. protection of jobs and sites,
5. and enforceable rules through 2030.

If management cannot bring that, then it should not expect labor to sign off on restructuring built on layoffs and closures.

## What I need next
I want the model the facilitator already pointed to:
- closure-free,
- hybrid,
- and closure-based scenarios,
with:
- annual savings,
- restructuring charges,
- durability through 2030,
- implementation risk,
- and the impact on jobs and sites.

Until that is produced, I do not accept the claim that closures are necessary. They may be one option — but they are not yet proven to be the only viable one.
