## 1) Executive conclusion

Based on the governance constraints and the typical economics of German auto restructuring:

- **Closure-based packages** usually deliver the **largest durable fixed-cost reduction**, but also the **highest one-time charges and implementation risk**.
- **Closure-free packages** are often **faster to negotiate and lower conflict**, but they usually **do not remove enough fixed cost** unless the labor contribution is unusually large and durable.
- A **hybrid** is often the most plausible outcome: limited footprint reduction plus wage/flexibility/overhead measures.

My preliminary view is:

- If the company’s annual structural gap is **modest** relative to domestic fixed cost, a closure-free deal may work.
- If the gap is **material** but not extreme, a hybrid is most likely to clear it.
- If the gap is **large**, closures become economically hard to avoid unless the company accepts a very large labor contribution and/or much lower capacity utilization.

## 2) Model structure

I would compare the scenarios on five dimensions:

1. **Annual recurring savings**
2. **One-time restructuring charges**
3. **Durability through 2030**
4. **Implementation risk / probability of delivery**
5. **Risk-adjusted net present value**

A useful board test is:

> **Risk-adjusted value = PV of recurring savings × probability of delivery − restructuring charges − lost margin during transition**

## 3) Illustrative scenario ranges

Because site data is missing, the ranges below are **illustrative for a large German OEM domestic base**, not company-specific facts.

### A. Closure-free restructuring
Typical tools:
- working-time moderation
- wage restraint / temporary labor-cost relief
- bonus restraint
- overhead cuts
- internal redeployment
- voluntary exits
- productivity measures

**Illustrative annual savings:**  
- **€0.3bn to €1.0bn per year**

**One-time charges:**  
- **€0.2bn to €0.8bn**  
  mostly severance, qualification, redeployment, systems costs

**Durability:**  
- Often **medium**, because labor concessions can expire or need renewal
- Strongest if embedded in a long agreement through 2030

**Implementation risk:**  
- **Low to medium** operationally  
- **Medium** financially if savings depend on recurring concessions

**Main limitation:**  
This path often struggles to remove enough **fixed cost** if the company has genuine structural overcapacity.

---

### B. Hybrid restructuring
Typical tools:
- limited footprint rationalization
- voluntary exits / attrition / redeployment
- labor cost concessions
- partial capacity reduction
- some function consolidation
- investment commitments to remaining sites

**Illustrative annual savings:**  
- **€0.8bn to €1.8bn per year**

**One-time charges:**  
- **€0.7bn to €2.5bn**  
  depending on number of sites/lines adjusted and workforce transition costs

**Durability:**  
- **Medium to high** if the footprint reduction is real and the labor package is contractually durable

**Implementation risk:**  
- **Medium**  
  because it depends on board approval, labor acceptance, and sequencing

**Main advantage:**  
This is often the best trade-off between **structural savings** and **governance feasibility**.

---

### C. Closure-based restructuring
Typical tools:
- plant closure or major line shutdown
- workforce reduction
- transfer of production elsewhere
- permanent capacity removal

**Illustrative annual savings:**  
- **€1.5bn to €3.0bn+ per year**

**One-time charges:**  
- **€2.0bn to €6.0bn+**  
  severance, asset write-downs, transfer costs, lease exit, cleanup, ramp-up elsewhere, supply-chain disruption

**Durability:**  
- **High** on paper, because fixed cost is permanently removed

**Implementation risk:**  
- **High** in a co-determined German setting  
- savings may arrive later than planned if conflict delays execution

**Main weakness:**  
The risk-adjusted value can be worse than the gross savings suggest if strikes, vetoes, or political delay are severe.

## 4) What “minimum savings target” likely means

Without company numbers, the target is usually set by combining:

- current domestic fixed cost
- excess capacity
- target return on sales
- required funding for EV/product transition

For a large European carmaker, the implied annual gap that restructuring must close is often in the range of:

- **€1bn to €3bn per year** for a material turnaround

That does **not** mean the full gap must come from labor. Usually it is shared across:

- labor cost
- plant footprint
- overhead
- procurement
- product mix
- capital allocation

But if domestic labor and plant structure are central, then the restructuring package must remove enough recurring fixed cost to matter.

## 5) Site-by-site logic

A board-ready model should be built at **plant level**, not just company level.

For each site, I would calculate:

### Inputs
- annual units
- capacity utilization
- labor cost per unit
- fixed overhead
- logistics cost

### Output by site

  - work-time changes
  - wage moderation
  - voluntary exits
  - function consolidation
  - productivity gains

- **hybrid incremental savings**
  - reduced lines
  - partial site rationalization
  - internal relocation

- **closure savings**
  - full fixed-cost removal
  - less relocation cost
  - minus transfer/ramp-up costs elsewhere

### Decision rule
A site is a candidate for closure or major rationalization if:

> **annual avoidable fixed cost > annual savings from non-closure measures + transition penalty**

Where the transition penalty includes:
- lower output during changeover
- logistics costs
- duplicate overhead
- governance delay risk

## 6) Illustrative break-even test

A simple management test:

If closure-free measures can deliver:

- **at least 70% to 80% of the savings of closure**
- with materially lower conflict risk
- and without sacrificing future capacity

then a closure-free or low-closure deal may be economically preferable.

If closure-free measures deliver only:

- **30% to 50%** of the closure savings

then they are usually insufficient unless the company’s required margin improvement is small.

## 7) Margin impact illustration

Assume annual vehicle volume of a domestic business large enough that:

- **€1bn savings** = roughly **tens of basis points to low single-digit points** of ROS improvement depending on revenue base

Very roughly:

- **€1bn savings** could move margin by **~0.5 to 1.0 percentage points** at a large OEM revenue base
- **€2bn savings** could move margin by **~1.0 to 2.0 points**

That is why the exact savings target matters so much.

## 8) Probability-adjusted view

If I probability-weight the scenarios in a co-determined German setting:

- **Closure-free**: higher probability of signing, lower probability of full economic sufficiency
- **Hybrid**: moderate probability of signing, highest probability of acceptable economic sufficiency
- **Closure-based**: lower probability of timely execution, but highest gross savings if implemented

So the risk-adjusted winner is often the **hybrid**, even if the gross savings are not the largest.
