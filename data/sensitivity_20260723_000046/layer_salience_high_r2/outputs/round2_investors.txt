I appreciate the independent assessment, and it confirms the central point: **the company needs durable cost and capacity reduction, not cosmetic compromise**.

## Where we agree
I agree with management, the family, and the expert on one key issue: **the current structure is not sustainable**. If German operations remain structurally above competitor cost levels, shareholder value will keep eroding. We are not interested in a settlement that simply postpones the margin problem.

I also agree with the works council, union, and state government on one important point: **a prolonged strike or governance deadlock would destroy value**. Lost production, reputational damage, and broken trust are not acceptable outcomes for capital markets either.

## Where I disagree
I do **not** accept the employee side’s current position as sufficient:
- **No closures**
- **No compulsory redundancies**
- **Binding job guarantees through 2030**
- while still expecting the company to achieve a meaningful turnaround

That is not yet a credible restructuring. It may be politically understandable, but from an investor perspective it risks leaving the cost base uncompetitive and margins structurally impaired.

The expert’s point matters here: closure-free measures can work, but usually only if the cost gap is modest to medium and the concessions are large, durable, and enforceable. Management has argued the gap is structural and significant. If that is true, then a closure-free plan must be **proven**, not presumed.

## What would move us
We are open to a non-closure solution **only if it is economically equivalent in substance**, meaning it delivers:

1. **Quantified fixed-cost reduction**
2. **Durable capacity reduction**
3. **Real labour-cost relief, not symbolic concessions**
4. **Enforceable timing and compliance**
5. **A credible return to target return-on-sales**
6. **Clear investment capacity for future products**

If the employee side can produce that, we would support it. But “protect every plant and every job” cannot be the benchmark if the economics do not work.

## On plant closures
We are **not ideologically attached** to closures. The expert is right that they are the cleanest way to remove fixed costs when overcapacity is persistent. If closures are required to restore competitiveness, we will not oppose them on principle.

But we would prefer a hybrid or closure-light solution **only if it genuinely closes the gap**. A compromise that preserves more sites but leaves the company with structurally weak margins would be a bad deal for shareholders and, eventually, for the workforce too.

## On burden-sharing
Burden-sharing must be real. That means:
- labor contributes,
- management contributes,
- and capital accepts whatever restructuring cost is necessary.

But burden-sharing is not a substitute for economics. It is only credible if the final package actually restores competitiveness.

## To management
You need to be more precise on the minimum economics:
- What savings do you need?
- What share must come from labour?
- What share must come from footprint reduction?
- What is the shortest path back to acceptable margins?

Right now, the risk is that management uses structural crisis language to justify maximum disruption without proving that all of it is necessary. That would not be acceptable to capital markets.

## To the works council and union
Your closure-free alternative must now be translated into numbers. We need to know:
- how much capacity reduction you will accept,
- what wage or time sacrifices are on offer,
- how durable those concessions are,
- and how the package will be enforced through 2030.

If the answer is still “jobs first, numbers later,” then that is not investable.

## To the state government
Your insistence on preserving the regional industrial base is understandable. But preserving the base requires a viable company, not a permanently protected cost structure. If the company cannot get to competitive economics, then no level of political protection will save the value of the enterprise.

## Bottom line
Our position is unchanged but now sharper:

- **We support deep, durable restructuring**
- **We do not support cosmetic compromise**
- **We will support a closure-free deal only if it closes the economics**
- **We will support closures if they are genuinely required for competitiveness**
- **We will not support a settlement that leaves margins structurally impaired**

So the burden is now on management and the employee side to demonstrate, with numbers, that a no-closure or low-closure solution can actually deliver equivalent economics. If it cannot, then capital markets will not reward delay disguised as compromise.
