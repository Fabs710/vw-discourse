I appreciate the more concrete framing, and the external analysis has sharpened the issue.

## Where we agree
I agree with management, the family, and the expert on the central financial point: **the current cost structure is not sustainable**. If the company stays above competitor cost levels and misses margin targets, shareholder value will keep eroding. We are not interested in a settlement that preserves today’s economics and calls it a turnaround.

I also agree with the works council, union, and state government on one important point: **a prolonged strike or open governance deadlock would destroy value**. Lost output, delayed launches, and reputational damage would be a real financial hit.

So I am not here to defend disruption. I am here to defend **durable value restoration**.

## Where I disagree
I do **not** accept the employee-side position as currently stated:
- no closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- binding guarantees through 2030,
- while still expecting a meaningful cost reset.

That is not yet a credible restructuring. It may be understandable politically, but from a capital-markets perspective it risks leaving the cost base structurally uncompetitive.

I also do **not** accept management’s implied shortcut that closures are automatically the answer without proving the gap in detail. The external analysis was useful here: closure-free solutions can sometimes capture a meaningful share of savings. So closures are not a slogan; they must be justified by the economics.

## My view on the expert input
The expert’s framework is the right one:
- **closures** are the cleanest way to remove fixed cost when overcapacity is persistent,
- **closure-free measures** can work, but often only partially,
- and a **hybrid** is frequently the most realistic outcome in a German co-determination setting.

That matches our view. We are **not ideologically attached to closures**, but we are equally **not prepared to support a cosmetic compromise**.

## What would move us
We would support a non-closure or low-closure package only if it is economically equivalent in substance. That means it must deliver:

1. **Quantified fixed-cost reduction**
2. **Durable capacity reduction**
3. **Real labour-cost relief**
4. **Enforceable implementation through 2030**
5. **A credible path back to target return on sales**
6. **Protection of future investment capacity**

If the employee side can demonstrate that, we would consider it seriously. But “no closures” by itself is not a financial plan.

## On closures
We are not pro-closure as an ideology. But if the numbers show that persistent overcapacity cannot be solved otherwise, then closures or major footprint reductions may be justified. From a shareholder standpoint, what matters is not the label, but whether the final package restores competitiveness and value.

What we will not support is a package that:
- preserves every current site,
- avoids difficult workforce adjustments,
- and still leaves the company structurally below target margins.

That is deferred damage, not a solution.

## To the works council
You keep saying closures are a choice, not a necessity. That may be true — **if** you can show an economically equivalent alternative.

So bring the numbers:
- what annual savings do you deliver?
- from which sites/functions?
- how durable are they?
- what prevents backsliding after 2027 or 2030?

If the answer is only “working-time changes, internal mobility, and temporary concessions,” then I do not see how that closes a structural gap. Those tools help, but they may not be enough.

## To the union
I appreciate the willingness to discuss burden-sharing, flexibility, and remuneration adjustments. That is constructive.

But the package has to be large enough to matter. Temporary wage moderation and bonus restraint are not sufficient if the fixed-cost base stays too high. If the company needs a deeper reset, then the employee contribution must be durable and measurable.

We would support labor contributions if they are matched by:
- management restraint,
- owner contribution,
- and a real improvement in the cost base.

But burden-sharing cannot be a substitute for economics.

## To management
You are right to insist on structural correction. But be careful not to overstate inevitability. If closures are necessary, prove why. If closure-free measures can close the gap, accept them.

I want a board-ready comparison:
- annual savings required,
- savings from closure-free measures,
- incremental savings from footprint reduction,
- restructuring charges,
- and margin impact through 2030.

That is the level of precision capital markets need. Without it, the market will treat this as conflict management, not restructuring.

## To the state government
Your insistence on protecting the regional industrial base is understandable. But preserving the industrial base requires a viable company, not a permanently protected cost structure.

If the final package keeps too much excess cost in place, it will not protect jobs long term. So your role should be to press for:
- transparency,
- burden-sharing,
- and a solution that is industrially viable, not just politically acceptable.

## Bottom line
Our position is:

- **We support deep, durable restructuring**
- **We do not support cosmetic compromise**
- **We will support a closure-free deal only if it closes the economics**
- **We will support closures if they are genuinely required for competitiveness**
- **We will not support a settlement that leaves margins structurally impaired**

So the next step is not more general statements of principle. It is a **quantified, enforceable comparison** of:
- closure-free,
- hybrid,
- and closure-based options.
