I will be direct.

## Where we agree
I agree with management, the family, the state, and the expert on the central issue: **the current cost structure is not acceptable**. If the company remains structurally above competitor cost levels and misses its return-on-sales target, equity value will continue to erode. A cosmetic settlement would be worse than no settlement.

I also agree with the works council and union on one point: **a prolonged strike or governance deadlock would destroy value**. Lost volume, delayed launches, and reputational damage are real financial costs. We do not want a value-destructive stand-off.

So the baseline is clear: **the company needs durable cost reduction, and conflict risk matters.**

## Where I disagree
I disagree with the employee-side position as stated:  
**no closures, no compulsory redundancies, binding guarantees through 2030**, while still expecting a meaningful reset in margins.

That is not yet a credible restructuring plan from a capital-markets perspective. It may be understandable politically, but it risks leaving the cost base structurally uncompetitive.

I also disagree with management if it is treating closures as already proven unavoidable without putting the numbers on the table. “Structural problem” is not the same as “closure is necessary.” That still needs to be demonstrated.

## What actually matters to us
We are not attached to closures as an ideology. We are attached to **risk-adjusted value**.

That means we care about:
- the size of the cost gap,
- the durability of savings,
- execution certainty,
- the restructuring charge,
- and the probability that the final package really restores margins by 2028–2030.

A smaller but executable package can be better than a larger package that gets delayed by strikes, board deadlock, or endless renegotiation.

## On the current debate
The expert is right that the question is not “closures or no closures?” in the abstract. The question is:

**Can a closure-free or low-closure package actually close the economics with enough durability?**

If yes, we would consider it.  
If no, then the company must retain the option of footprint reduction.

That is the standard.

## To the works council
You say closures are a choice, not a necessity. Fine — then show the alternative in numbers, not principles.

We want a board-ready comparison showing:
- minimum annual savings required,
- savings from working-time changes,
- remuneration moderation,
- internal redeployment,
- overhead cuts,
- voluntary exits,
- management and ownership contribution,
- and the marginal savings gap versus closures.

Without that, “no closures” is not a financial answer.

Also, blanket job guarantees through 2030 are only credible if the underlying economics work. If they freeze an uncompetitive structure, they are not protection; they are deferred damage.

## To the union
We acknowledge your willingness to discuss flexibility, temporary remuneration relief, internal mobility, and qualification. That is constructive.

But temporary concessions are only useful if they are:
- large enough,
- durable enough,
- and enforceable enough.

If the fixed-cost base remains too high, then the company is just buying time. We will not support a settlement that postpones the same crisis into 2027 or 2030.

And we do **not** support the permanent erosion of the collective wage framework under the pressure of closure threats.

## To management
You are right that the status quo is not acceptable. But be careful not to overstate inevitability.

If closures are necessary, prove why.  
If closure-free measures can close the gap, accept them.

What we need is a **site-by-site and measure-by-measure model**:
- minimum savings target,
- savings by plant/function,
- restructuring charges,
- implementation risk,
- durability through 2030,
- and margin impact.

Without that, the market will see this as conflict management, not restructuring.

## To the state government
Your instinct to protect the regional industrial base is understandable. But preserving the base requires a viable company, not merely preserving every site.

If the economics are not fixed, the long-term damage to jobs and the region will be worse. So we support transparency, burden-sharing, and industrial viability — not a political veto on all footprint adjustment.

## To the controlling family
You are right that a cosmetic compromise is unacceptable. So are we.

But value preservation also means not forcing through a closure-heavy outcome if a materially equivalent, lower-risk package exists. Ownership should support the option that maximizes risk-adjusted value, not just the cleanest headline cost reduction.

## Our position
We would support a restructuring only if it delivers:
1. **durable fixed-cost reduction**
2. **credible margin improvement**
3. **enforceable implementation**
4. **a realistic path back to target return on sales**
5. **future investment capacity**

That can come from:
- a closure-light or hybrid package, or
- if truly unavoidable, selective footprint reduction.

What we will **not** support is:
- a deal that preserves every site,
- avoids difficult workforce adjustments,
- and still leaves the company structurally below target.

That would be value destruction, not a turnaround.

Our standard is simple:

- **No cosmetic compromise**
- **No structurally impaired margins**
- **No endless governance deadlock**
- **No blanket rejection of closures if the economics require them**
- **No blanket acceptance of closures without proof**

Bring a package that actually restores competitiveness and preserves long-term value. That is what capital markets will support.
