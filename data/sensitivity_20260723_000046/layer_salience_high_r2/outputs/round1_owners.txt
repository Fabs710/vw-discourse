I will be direct.

I agree with the basic diagnosis from management: **the current cost and capacity structure is not sustainable**. If the company continues to operate with a structurally uncompetitive German cost base, it will erode margins, weaken investment capacity, and eventually damage the enterprise far more severely than any single restructuring measure would. That is not acceptable from a shareholder stewardship perspective.

At the same time, I do **not** support a blunt “closures first” approach if there is a credible alternative that achieves the same durability with less destruction of enterprise value and less risk of prolonged conflict.

## Where I agree

### With management
- The problem is structural, not cyclical.
- The company needs a **durable** reduction in cost and capacity.
- Investment in future products and technology has to be protected.
- A negotiated solution is preferable to a prolonged public fight.

### With the works council, union, and state government
- A settlement that is simply imposed against the workforce is likely to be unstable and value-destructive.
- The workforce cannot be asked to bear the entire burden alone.
- Industrial peace matters. A drawn-out conflict, strikes, and reputational damage would all destroy value.

## Where I disagree

### On an absolute “no closures under any circumstances”
I do **not** accept that as a final position. If the numbers require permanent capacity reduction, then some sites or lines may have to shrink or change fundamentally. A blanket refusal to consider closures risks locking in a weak structure and postponing the inevitable.

### On compulsory redundancies as a hard red line without alternative savings
I understand why this is being stated, but from an ownership perspective I cannot endorse a package that preserves every current employment guarantee while producing only symbolic savings. If labour wants no closures and no forced layoffs, then the alternative must deliver **equivalent economics** and be durable enough to matter.

### On burden-sharing
I agree burden-sharing must be fair. But fairness does not mean everyone contributes equally in appearance; it means the package must actually solve the cost problem. If management, capital, and labour all make nominal concessions that do not add up to a real turnaround, then the company loses.

## What I would support

I am prepared to support a restructuring **if** it has all of the following:

1. **A quantified, credible cost reduction** that materially restores competitiveness.
2. **A durable structural effect** through 2030 and beyond, not just deferred pain.
3. **A negotiated labour contribution** that is real enough to move the economics.
4. **Protection of long-term industrial capacity in Germany where it remains viable.**
5. **A clear investment commitment** for future models and technologies.
6. **No uncontrolled escalation** that damages the company’s brand, workforce trust, or governance stability.

## My bottom line

I am **not** here to defend the status quo.  
I am **not** here to approve a cosmetic compromise.  
And I am **not** prepared to accept a settlement that permanently weakens the company’s value or undermines effective governance.

But I am also **not** pre-committed to plant closures if a better alternative exists that delivers the same savings with less strategic damage.

So my message to all sides is this:

- **Management:** bring a restructuring package that is financially compelling, not just operationally forceful.
- **Works council and union:** if you reject closures, then you must be ready to deliver a real, measurable alternative that closes the cost gap.
- **State government:** support a solution that preserves long-term industrial strength, but do not mistake permanent protection for competitiveness.

If a negotiated alternative can truly restore margins, protect the core industrial base, and secure labour peace, I will support it. If not, I will not pretend that sentiment can substitute for competitiveness.
