Below is a finance-and-cost-model view of the decision, not a strategic or labor-relations opinion.

## Short answer

If management’s assessment is that German operations have **persistent structural overcapacity**, then a **closure-based package will usually generate materially higher and more durable savings** than a closure-free package. A closure-free package can still be viable, but only if:

- the required savings gap is **modest to medium**,
- employee concessions are **large, durable, and enforceable**,
- the company can reduce capacity without keeping too much fixed cost in place,
- and management accepts a **slower, less certain** path to margin recovery.

In most industrial cases, a closure-free alternative captures roughly **40%–80%** of the savings of a closure-based plan, depending on how much fixed overhead can be taken out without closing sites. If the true gap is at the high end, a no-closure plan probably **won’t fully close the economics**.

---

## 1) Analytical framework

For this decision, the relevant comparison is:

### A. Closure-based restructuring
Savings come from removing:
- direct labor
- site overhead
- maintenance
- utilities
- logistics complexity
- indirect support functions
- some working capital and transport inefficiency

Costs include:
- restructuring charges
- severance
- asset write-downs / impairment
- remediation
- transition / dual-running costs
- possible volume disruption

### B. Closure-free restructuring
Savings come from:
- working-time reduction or flexibility
- wage moderation or temporary remuneration cuts
- reduced bonuses / special payments
- internal mobility and redeployment
- voluntary exits / natural attrition
- capacity reallocation across existing plants
- overhead reduction

Costs / limits:
- savings are typically slower
- fixed-cost base remains partly intact
- concessions may expire
- underutilized plants can erode savings over time

---

## 2) What “equivalent economics” means

For a closure-free plan to be a true substitute, it must be comparable on four tests:

1. **Magnitude** — does it get near the cost gap?
2. **Durability** — does it last through the cycle?
3. **Speed** — does it hit the P&L fast enough?
4. **Enforceability** — can management rely on it?

A package can be politically attractive but still fail these tests.

---

## 3) A practical savings range

Without company-specific plant data, I can only give bounded estimates.

### Closure-based package
For a large auto manufacturer, closing one major German plant or equivalent footprint reduction often produces annual run-rate savings in the broad range of:

- **€200 million to €600 million per major site**, depending on:
  - site size
  - product mix
  - amount of shared overhead eliminated
  - whether production is fully reallocated
  - what portion of indirect costs are removed

For a multi-site restructuring, total annual savings can be much larger, but the range is very wide.

### Closure-free package
A serious no-closure package can still generate substantial savings, but usually less:

- **€80 million to €300 million per major site equivalent** in annual run-rate savings, if the concessions are deep and durable
- perhaps more if there are large wage, time, and overhead changes across the entire German footprint

But many closure-free savings are:
- partly temporary
- dependent on future bargaining
- vulnerable to volume slippage
- limited by the fact that the infrastructure remains in place

### Rule-of-thumb comparison
A closure-free plan often captures about:

- **40%–60%** of the savings of a full closure-based footprint reduction in a true overcapacity case
- sometimes **60%–80%** if the company is able to combine strong labor concessions, major capacity reductions without closure, and overhead elimination
- less if utilization is poor and too much fixed cost stays embedded

---

## 4) Margin impact

The margin effect depends on whether the savings are fixed or variable.

### If the package saves €300 million annually
Illustratively:
- on €50 billion revenue, that is **0.6 percentage points of revenue**
- on €100 billion revenue, **0.3 points**

But what matters more is **operating margin / return on sales**:
- if the brand is missing its target by, say, 2–4 points, then a €300 million saving may help but not fully solve the gap
- a closure-based package is more likely to move the needle decisively

### Fixed-cost absorption effect
If volume is reduced but plants remain open, the company can suffer:
- lower utilization
- worse absorption of depreciation and overhead
- weaker unit economics even after labor concessions

That is why closure-free savings can overstate the real margin benefit if underutilization persists.

---

## 5) Restructuring charges and cash flow

### Closure-based
Typically higher up-front cash and accounting charges:
- severance
- site shutdown costs
- write-downs
- contract exit costs

This means:
- short-term cash outflow is larger
- reported earnings may be hit more severely in the near term
- but future cash savings are stronger and more certain

### Closure-free
Lower up-front charges, usually:
- less severance
- fewer impairment charges
- lower disruption costs

This means:
- easier short-term cash profile
- but lower and less certain future savings
- higher risk that the company pays twice: once for concessions, again later for deeper restructuring

---

## 6) Sensitivity analysis

Here is the key sensitivity:

### If the structural cost gap is modest
Example: annual gap of **€100m–€250m**
- Closure-free solution may be sufficient
- especially if labor concessions are durable
- likely lower execution risk

### If the gap is medium
Example: **€250m–€500m**
- closure-free can work only with aggressive concessions
- likely needs capacity reductions plus wage/time changes plus overhead cuts
- hybrid solution becomes more likely than pure no-closure

### If the gap is large
Example: **€500m+ annually**
- closure-free solution is usually unlikely to fully solve the problem
- a hybrid or closure-based plan becomes much more probable
- otherwise the company risks permanent sub-target margins

These are not company facts; they are scenario bands used in restructuring analysis.

---

## 7) Employment impact in financial terms

From a cost-model perspective:

### Closure-based
- strongest reduction in payroll and site overhead
- biggest one-time restructuring charge
- greatest certainty of run-rate savings
- greatest risk of production disruption during transition

### Closure-free
- preserves more headcount in the near term
- lowers immediate charge
- but can leave excess labor and indirect cost in the system
- risk of “hidden overcapacity” persists

If management needs a clean reduction in break-even volume, closures work better.

---

## 8) What I would say to the decision-makers

### To management
If you need a durable improvement in operating margin, you should quantify:
- annual cost gap
- savings per plant
- fixed vs variable cost by site
- cost to close versus cost to keep open and concede

Without that, the closure debate is not fully financeable.

### To the employee side
A closure-free package is financially credible only if it delivers:
- permanent labor-cost relief
- real capacity reduction
- enforceable duration
- and overhead reduction, not just wage restraint

### To shareholders
The key question is not “closures or no closures,” but:
- which package gets you to target margin fastest,
- with the lowest risk-adjusted present value of costs,
- and the least probability of needing a second restructuring later

---

## 9) My neutral judgment

If the company truly has **structural overcapacity**, then:

- **closure-based restructuring** is the stronger tool for deep, durable cost correction
- **closure-free restructuring** is only likely to work if the required savings are limited enough and the negotiated concessions are unusually strong
- a **hybrid package** is often the most realistic outcome in a German co-determination setting

In plain terms:  
**No-closure can be a compromise; it is not automatically an equivalent substitute.**
