I will be direct.

## Where I agree
I agree with the works council, the union, the state government, management, and the experts on the **basic diagnosis**:
- the company has a **structural competitiveness problem**,
- the current cost base is too high,
- and a **temporary patch** would only postpone the crisis.

I also agree that a **prolonged strike or open governance breakdown would destroy value**. That is not in the interest of the company, the workforce, or the owners.

## Where I disagree
I do **not** accept the employee-side claim that **“no closures, no compulsory redundancies, binding guarantees through 2030”** is automatically a viable solution.

That may be a valid demand from a labor perspective. It is **not yet a restructuring plan**.

And I do **not** accept the opposite claim from management that closures are already proven unavoidable. That has also **not** been demonstrated with enough precision.

The current debate is still too abstract. We need the economics, not just positions.

## What I take from the expert and finance input
The external analysis was useful because it clarified something important:
- **closures are the cleanest and most durable way** to remove fixed cost where overcapacity is real;
- **closure-free measures can work**, but usually only if the savings gap is modest enough and the concessions are deep, durable, and enforceable;
- a **hybrid** is often the most realistic outcome.

That is the correct framework.

So my position is not ideological:
- I am **not pro-closure as a principle**.
- I am **pro durable value restoration**.
- If closures are needed to achieve that, I will not rule them out.
- If a closure-free package truly closes the gap, I will support that instead.

But I will not support a package that preserves every site and every guarantee while leaving the company structurally uncompetitive.

## To the works council
You said closures are a choice, not a necessity. That may be true — **if** a closure-free package can close the economics.

So bring the numbers:
- how much annual savings do you deliver,
- from which sites/functions,
- how durable are those savings,
- and how do you prevent backsliding after 2027 or 2030?

A general refusal of closures is not enough.  
If you want blanket site and job guarantees, they must be backed by an equally concrete and durable contribution to competitiveness.

I also need to be candid: if you insist that every German plant must remain open **regardless of economics**, then you are asking ownership to accept permanent value erosion. That is not stewardship-friendly and I cannot support it.

## To the union
I appreciate that you are willing to discuss burden-sharing, working-time flexibility, remuneration adjustments, and qualification measures. That is constructive.

But I am not convinced that the measures you describe automatically close the gap:
- temporary wage moderation,
- bonus restraint,
- internal mobility,
- and working-time changes

may help substantially, but they may still leave too much fixed cost in place.

If you want us to support a no-closure settlement, it has to be shown that the package is:
1. **economically equivalent**,  
2. **durable**, and  
3. **enforceable**.

Otherwise it is just deferred conflict.

## To management
You are right that the cost problem is real and that the status quo is not acceptable.

But I want to push you as well:
- Do not use closure talk as a blunt instrument if you have not exhausted credible alternatives.
- Do not assume that conflict pressure automatically strengthens your hand.
- Be precise about the **minimum savings target**, the **site-by-site gap**, and the difference between **temporary relief** and **structural correction**.

If the company needs plant rationalization, say exactly why. If non-closure measures can suffice, you should be ready to accept them.

Right now, I do **not** see a fully quantified, board-ready comparison that proves which mix is necessary. That is a problem.

## To the state government
You are right to insist on regional industrial strength and co-determination. I support that.

But I also need you to recognize the ownership reality: preserving German capacity in the abstract is not enough if the company remains unable to compete. If the economics are not fixed, the region will not be protected in the long run.

So your role should be to push for:
- transparent numbers,
- real burden-sharing,
- and a solution that is industrially viable, not just politically convenient.

## My current ownership view
I am prepared to support a restructuring only if it delivers:
- **durable margin improvement**,
- **real cost reduction**,
- **credible future investment**,
- and **industrial peace**.

That can come from:
- a closure-light or hybrid package,
- or, if unavoidable, selective footprint reduction.

What I will **not** support is:
- an all-closure-free settlement that fails the economics,
- or a closure-first rush without proving there is no equivalent alternative.

## What I need next
At this point, I want a **site-by-site and measure-by-measure comparison** that shows:
1. the minimum annual savings required,
2. what closure-free measures can actually deliver,
3. what management and ownership contribute,
4. what the marginal savings from closures really are,
5. and what package is durable enough to hold through 2030.

Until I see that, I will not endorse either extreme.

## Bottom line
My position is simple:
- **No cosmetic compromise**
- **No permanent impairment of enterprise value**
- **No governance deadlock**
- **No blanket acceptance of closures without proof**
- **No blanket refusal of closures if the economics require them**

Bring a package that actually restores competitiveness and preserves long-term value.  
That is the standard I will judge this by.
