## 1) What can be said with confidence
From an automotive restructuring finance perspective, three points are clear:

1. **The company has a structural cost problem**, not just a cyclical one.
2. **A durable fix must reduce fixed cost and/or raise utilization**, not merely defer pain.
3. In a German co-determination setting, **execution risk matters almost as much as headline savings**.

That means the right comparison is not just “which option saves the most on paper,” but:
- how much savings is **actually realizable**,
- how durable it is through 2030,
- what restructuring charges it requires,
- and what the risk-adjusted net present value looks like after delay, strikes, and implementation slippage.

## 2) Working assumptions for a usable model
Because we do not have the company’s actual site economics, any estimate must be scenario-based.

I would use the following structure:

### Definitions
- **Closure-free package**: no plant closures; savings come from working-time changes, wage moderation, voluntary exits, redeployment, overhead cuts, and partial capacity reductions.
- **Hybrid package**: one or more targeted site/line closures or major consolidations, plus labor-cost concessions and redeployment.
- **Closure-based package**: broader footprint reduction, with major site closures or exits from excess capacity.

### Key financial variables
- **Annual gross savings**
- **Restructuring charge**: severance, asset impairments, contract termination, relocation, idling costs
- **Implementation lag**: time until savings fully realized
- **Durability**: whether savings recur through 2030 or require renegotiation
- **Conflict risk**: strike/protest delay cost and launch disruption
- **Utilization effect**: whether remaining plants gain absorption benefit

## 3) Scenario ranges: what is economically plausible
Without site data, bounded estimates are the right tool.

### A. Closure-free restructuring
Typical savings capture versus closure-based:
- **Lower bound:** ~40% of the savings that closures would generate
- **Base case:** ~55%–65%
- **Upper bound:** ~75%–80%

This is consistent with the idea that labor-cost measures and internal reallocation can reduce burden, but they usually do **not** remove all fixed overhead and capacity cost.

#### What drives the savings
- Working-time flexibility: modest to moderate
- Wage moderation / bonus restraint: moderate
- Voluntary exits and attrition: moderate
- Internal redeployment: moderate
- Overhead reduction: moderate
- Plant footprint reduction without full closure: limited

#### Main financial weakness
The biggest issue is **residual fixed cost**. If excess capacity remains, you still carry:
- plant overhead,
- maintenance,
- indirect labor,
- and under-absorption cost.

So closure-free solutions are often strongest when the gap is **modest**, weaker when the gap is **structural and wide**.

---

### B. Hybrid restructuring
This is often the best risk-adjusted solution.

Typical savings capture versus closure-based:
- **Lower bound:** ~70%
- **Base case:** ~80%–90%
- **Upper bound:** ~95%

A hybrid can combine:
- one plant closure or line exit,
- production consolidation,
- labor concessions,
- voluntary exits,
- and investment commitments for the remaining sites.

#### Why hybrids often dominate
They remove some fixed cost structurally while avoiding the full social and execution cost of a broad closure program. In practice, that often gives the best balance of:
- savings durability,
- board feasibility,
- and operational continuity.

---

### C. Closure-based restructuring
This gives the largest gross cost reduction, but also the highest one-time cost and implementation risk.

#### Typical characteristics
- Highest long-run fixed-cost relief
- Strongest utilization improvement at remaining plants
- Largest restructuring charge
- Highest strike and delay risk
- Highest governance execution risk

In a German automotive context, this can be financially optimal **only if**:
- the cost gap is large,
- the board coalition can be held together,
- and the company can survive the transition without major volume loss.

### Base annual savings potential by package

| Package | Annual gross savings realized | Typical restructuring charge | Execution risk | Durability |
|---|---:|---:|---|---|

| Hybrid | 70–95 | Medium to high | Medium | High |
| Closure-based | 90–100 | High to very high | High | High if executed |

### Net present value logic
The economically relevant comparison is:

**NPV = PV of annual savings – restructuring charges – expected delay cost – expected conflict cost**

In many real cases:
- a closure-based plan has the highest gross savings,
- but the **risk-adjusted NPV** may be only modestly ahead of a hybrid if implementation risk is high,
- and a closure-free package may still be attractive if the cost gap is moderate and the labor settlement is durable.

## 5) What “durable” means in financial terms
For this kind of restructuring, durability matters a great deal.

### Durable savings
Savings are durable if they:
- are built into contracts or permanently reduce structural headcount/capacity,
- do not require annual renegotiation,
- are not easily reversed by the workforce or new management,
- and improve the fixed-cost base through 2030.

### Less durable savings
Savings are less durable if they depend on:
- temporary wage restraint,
- short-term working-time reductions,
- one-off bonus waivers,
- or annual “peace” agreements.

Those can help, but they should be valued more conservatively because they can unwind.

## 7) Key trade-offs by scenario

### Closure-free
Best if:
- the gap is moderate,
- the brand can recover without major footprint reduction,
- labor concessions are deep and durable,
- and management can credibly enforce productivity gains.

Weak if:
- the gap is wide,
- plant utilization remains poor,
- or concessions expire too soon.

### Hybrid
Best if:
- the company needs visible structural cost reduction,
- but full closure strategy is politically or institutionally infeasible,
- and both labor and capital need a face-saving but real compromise.

This is usually the most practical equilibrium.

### Closure-based
Best if:
- the excess capacity is severe,
- utilization is chronically low,
- and the company can absorb the transition pain.

Weak if:
- it triggers prolonged disruption,
- impairs product launches,
- or fails to clear governance hurdles.

## 8) What I would tell decision-makers financially
If I were advising strictly on cost-model grounds, I would say:

1. **Do not assume closures are automatically necessary.**
   They may be, but the burden of proof is economic, not rhetorical.

2. **Do not assume closure-free measures can fully substitute for closures.**
   They often cannot, especially if excess capacity is material.

3. **The most likely economically viable outcome is a hybrid.**
   That is especially true where governance blocks a full closure program.

4. **Focus on risk-adjusted NPV, not gross savings.**
   A smaller but executable package may beat a larger package that gets delayed for 18–24 months.
