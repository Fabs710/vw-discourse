I’ll respond directly.

## Where I agree
I agree with everyone on the basic diagnosis:
- the company has a **structural competitiveness problem**,
- the cost base is too high,
- temporary patches are not enough,
- and a prolonged strike would damage the company.

I also agree that any settlement must be **durable** and not just a short-lived ceasefire.

## Where I disagree — and strongly

### 1) “No closures” is not a solution
The works council and union keep treating “no plant closures” as if it were a viable restructuring plan on its own. It isn’t.

If the German footprint is structurally too large for the market, then the company must reduce capacity and fixed cost. That can happen through closures, line consolidations, or other permanent footprint reductions. But **it must happen**.

A closure-free package is only acceptable if it delivers **equivalent economics**:
- comparable fixed-cost reduction,
- comparable capacity reduction,
- comparable durability,
- and a credible path back to target margins.

So far, I have not seen that. I have heard demands, not a financially equivalent alternative.

### 2) “No compulsory redundancies” cannot be absolute
I prefer negotiated transitions. I prefer voluntary exits. But I will not accept a rule that every current job is protected regardless of economics.

If the numbers do not close otherwise, then workforce reductions may be required. We can and should discuss how they happen. We cannot pretend they are off the table in all circumstances.

### 3) Blanket job and site guarantees through 2030 are not credible if they freeze an uncompetitive structure
Guarantees are only meaningful if the underlying economics work.

If the package still leaves:
- too much excess capacity,
- too much fixed cost,
- and too little flexibility,

then a guarantee is just a delay mechanism. That is not a turnaround.

## To the works council
You keep asking for proof that closures are necessary. Fair enough — then let’s be precise.

What I need from you is not a refusal, but a **board-ready alternative**:
- exact annual savings from working-time changes,
- remuneration moderation,
- internal reallocation,
- overhead cuts,
- voluntary exits,
- and management/owner contributions,
- plus the marginal savings gap versus closure.

If your package really closes the gap, show it. If it does not, then you are not offering a solution — you are asking the company to live with its cost problem.

And let me be clear: co-determination is real, but it is not a veto over competitiveness.

## To the union
I acknowledge your willingness to discuss flexibility, temporary remuneration relief, and redeployment. That is constructive.

But I need to be blunt: **temporary concessions do not solve a structural problem** unless they are large enough and durable enough.

If the company still carries the same structural overcapacity, then short-term labor-cost relief just postpones the next crisis. I am not interested in that.

You say the collective framework must remain intact. Fine — but the framework must also be fit for the future. If parts of it block competitiveness, then they have to change.

## To the works council and union together
You are aligned on rejecting closures and layoffs. I respect that. But your position still does not answer the central question:

**How do you structurally close the cost gap?**

Not politically. Not rhetorically. Financially.

That means:
- how much fixed cost comes out,
- where it comes out,
- how durable it is,
- and why it will still hold in 2028, 2029, and 2030.

If you cannot answer that, then your position is not a restructuring plan.

## To the state government
I appreciate your concern for the regional industrial base. But preserving industrial strength requires a viable company, not just preserving every site on paper.

If we avoid the necessary footprint adjustment now, we risk weakening the company long-term, which is worse for the region than a managed restructuring today.

So I support transparency and political responsibility — but not a stance that rules out the tools needed to restore competitiveness.

## To the controlling family and investors
I agree with you on the key point: a cosmetic compromise is unacceptable.

But I also want to be clear that I do **not** accept endless ambiguity. If you want durable value restoration, then support a package that actually reduces cost and capacity enough to matter.

If the employee side can produce an equivalent, durable, enforceable alternative, I am open to it. If not, then the company must retain the option of stronger structural action.

A serious solution must include all of the following:
1. **structural reduction in cost and capacity**
2. **meaningful labor contribution**
3. **management and ownership contribution**

That means I am **not** accepting:
- a blanket no-closure commitment,
- an absolute ban on employment reductions,
