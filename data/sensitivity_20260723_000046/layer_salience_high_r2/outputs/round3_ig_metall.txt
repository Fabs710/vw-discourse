I will respond plainly.

## Where I agree

I agree with the works council, the state government, and even parts of management and the expert on one core point: **this company needs a durable restructuring, not a cosmetic fix**.

I also agree that:
- a one-off patch will not work,
- prolonged industrial conflict would hurt everyone,
- and some form of burden-sharing is unavoidable.

So no, I am not defending the status quo. I am defending a **fair and workable** adjustment.

## Where I disagree

### 1) I do **not** accept closures as the default answer
Management keeps speaking as if plant closures are simply the “clean” or “rational” solution. That is not a conclusion, it is a preference unless and until the numbers prove otherwise.

The finance view was actually useful here: closure-free solutions can capture a significant share of savings, sometimes a lot, depending on the true gap. That means the burden is on management to prove that:
- the gap is so large,
- and the closure-free options so insufficient,
that shutting German plants is genuinely unavoidable.

You have not proven that yet. Repeating “structural overcapacity” does not magically justify closures.

### 2) I do **not** accept compulsory redundancies
This remains a hard line.

If the company wants labor to contribute, then the contribution must come through:
- working-time flexibility,
- temporary remuneration adjustments,
- reduced bonuses or special payments,
- internal mobility,
- qualification and redeployment,
- and perhaps a negotiated capacity reduction.

That is burden-sharing.

Forced layoffs are not burden-sharing. They are one-sided damage transferred onto workers.

### 3) I do **not** accept dismantling the collective wage framework as the price of “competitiveness”
I am willing to discuss **temporary, negotiated labor-cost relief**. I am not willing to accept a permanent erosion of the collective agreement under the pressure of closure threats.

If the company wants a durable settlement, then the collective framework must remain intact as a framework — even if certain components are adjusted for a period in a controlled, negotiated way.

## What I think is actually needed

A serious package has to be built around **shared sacrifice with enforceable guarantees**.

That means, in practical terms:

1. **No compulsory redundancies**
2. **No German plant closures**
3. **Binding site security**
4. **A meaningful employee contribution**
5. **Comparable contributions from management and capital**
6. **Investment commitments for future products and technology**
7. **A durable collective agreement, not a one-off ceasefire**

## To management
If you want us to engage seriously, put numbers on the table.

Not general claims. Not pressure tactics.

I want a concrete comparison:
- what savings come from working-time changes,
- what savings come from remuneration adjustments,
- what savings come from internal reallocation and overhead cuts,
- what management and owner contributions are on the table,
- and exactly how much the company claims only closures would add.

If you cannot show that the non-closure package fails economically, then closure is not a necessity. It is a choice.

And if it is a choice, then we will oppose it.

## To the works council
We are aligned on the essentials:
- no forced layoffs,
- no closures,
- no destruction of the wage framework.

Your insistence on binding site guarantees through 2030 is understandable. I support strong job and site security, but I want to be precise: the guarantees must be tied to a **real package of measures** that preserves viability. We cannot just promise everything and expect the economics to heal themselves.

So I support your position **if** it is paired with serious, measurable contributions from all sides. That is the only path to a durable settlement.

## To the state government
Your rejection of closures is important, but it must not become a symbolic stance. Use your influence to push for:
- transparent cost calculations,
- a closure-free restructuring path,
- binding investment,
- and a fair burden-sharing package.

Also: do not let management hide behind “market logic” when the real issue is how the burden is distributed. If workers are being asked to contribute, then executives, owners, and capital must contribute too.

## To the controlling family and investors
You are right about one thing: a fake compromise is worthless.

But a package that pushes the entire adjustment onto labor is also not a real solution. It may look cleaner on paper, but it creates conflict, destroys trust, and invites future escalation.

If you want enterprise value protected, then support a package that:
- avoids closures,
- avoids forced layoffs,
- reduces costs durably,
- and preserves industrial peace.

That is not softness. That is realism.

## My bottom line

The union is prepared to negotiate a **real, quantified, durable** burden-sharing settlement.

We are **not** prepared to accept:
- compulsory redundancies,
- German plant closures,
- or the dismantling of the collective wage framework.

If management wants a deal, it needs to come back with a closure-free package that is economically credible and fair. If it cannot do that, then it should not expect labor to sign off on restructuring built on layoffs and closures.
