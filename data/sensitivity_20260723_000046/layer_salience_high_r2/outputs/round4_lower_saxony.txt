I will be equally direct.

## Where I agree
I agree with management, the owners, the union, the works council, and the expert on the basic diagnosis:

- this is a **structural** competitiveness problem,
- a **temporary patch** would be irresponsible,
- a **prolonged conflict** would damage the company,
- and a durable solution must involve **burden-sharing** and **future investment**.

I also agree that we need **numbers**, not slogans. The repeated references to “equivalent economics” are only useful if they are actually quantified.

## Where I disagree
I do **not** accept the current drift toward treating plant closures as the default or inevitable answer.

That is not just a technical disagreement. For me, it crosses the line into:
- unacceptable damage to the regional economy,
- destruction of jobs and skills that cannot simply be rebuilt,
- and a weakening of the company’s long-term role as an industrial anchor.

### On management
You keep saying closures may be necessary if the economics require it. That may be true in theory. But you have not yet shown, in board-ready form, that closure-free or low-closure options are insufficient.

If the company wants to move toward a footprint reduction, then I need:
- the **minimum annual savings target**,
- the **site-by-site gap**,
- the **marginal savings from closures** versus non-closure measures,
- and the contribution expected from **management, owners, and capital**.

Until then, closures remain a proposed solution, not a proven necessity.

### On the works council and union
I support your core red lines:
- **no compulsory redundancies**
- **strong protection of jobs**
- **real burden-sharing from management and ownership**
- **meaningful investment commitments**

I also understand your insistence that guarantees must be binding, not merely declaratory.

Where I part company with you is only this: you are still speaking as if “no closures” can be defended purely as a principle. It cannot. It has to be defended as a **credible industrial plan**.

That means I need to see:
- what savings can be achieved through working-time flexibility,
- remuneration moderation,
- internal redeployment,
- qualification,
- overhead cuts,
- and voluntary departures,
- and whether those measures are durable enough through 2030.

If that package is genuinely sufficient, I will support it. If it is not, then I will not pretend otherwise.

### On the controlling family and investors
You are right about one thing: cosmetic compromise is worthless.

But I do **not** accept the implication that the burden should be loaded onto labor and the state’s industrial base simply because that is the cleanest route on paper. A solution that destroys trust, triggers conflict, and weakens the German footprint may look efficient in the spreadsheet and still be strategically foolish.

If you want long-term value, then value also includes:
- social peace,
- industrial capability,
- training and skills,
- supplier stability,
- and political legitimacy.

## My position, clearly
I can support a restructuring only if it:
1. **protects jobs and sites in the state as far as possible**
2. **preserves the company as a regional economic anchor**
3. **maintains meaningful co-determination**
4. **delivers durable competitiveness**
5. **includes real contributions from management, owners, and labor**
6. **avoids compulsory redundancies**
7. **does not start from plant closures as the baseline**

That does **not** mean I am defending the status quo. It means I am insisting on a solution that does not hollow out the regional industrial base.

## What would move me
I would be open to a pragmatic package if it were clearly shown that:

- a **closure-free or near-closure-free** plan can close most of the savings gap,
- any remaining footprint adjustment is **strictly limited** and used only if absolutely unavoidable,
- workforce adjustment happens through **voluntary, negotiated, and socially responsible mechanisms**,
- and the company commits to **investment and product allocation for the German sites**.

That is the only kind of compromise I can support.

## What I will not support
I will not support:
- a package that starts with plant closures,
- compulsory redundancies as a first resort,
- or any deal that strips the state’s practical veto role and co-determination influence over major site decisions.

That is not a bargaining position. It is a line I will hold.

## [SUMMON: independent site-by-site comparative model]
To move this forward, I want a **board-ready, site-by-site comparative model** showing:

- minimum savings target
- savings from working-time changes
- savings from remuneration measures
- savings from internal reallocation and overhead cuts
- management and ownership contribution
- marginal savings from closures
- implementation risk
- durability through 2030
- and impact on jobs, sites, and regional suppliers

Without that, nobody can honestly claim that closures are necessary or that a closure-free package is sufficient.

## Bottom line
My stance remains:

- **No plant closures as the starting point**
- **No compulsory redundancies**
- **No weakening of co-determination**
- **Yes to a durable restructuring**
- **Yes to burden-sharing**
- **Yes to investment and competitiveness**
- **Only a carefully bounded hybrid, if and only if the numbers truly justify it**

Bring the numbers. Then we can judge whether any footprint reduction is genuinely unavoidable. Until then, I will continue to oppose any attempt to make the region pay for a restructuring that has not yet been proven necessary.
