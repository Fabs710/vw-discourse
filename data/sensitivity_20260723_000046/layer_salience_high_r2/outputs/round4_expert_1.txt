The clearest issue now is that all parties are converging on the same analytical requirement: **a board-ready, site-by-site comparison of the restructuring options**. Until that exists, claims that closures are necessary or unnecessary remain unproven.

## Independent restructuring assessment

### 1) What can be inferred already
From the positions stated:

- The company has a **structural cost and capacity problem**, not just a cyclical one.
- A **durable** solution will need some combination of:
  - fixed-cost reduction,
  - capacity reduction,
  - labor-cost contribution,
  - management/owner contribution,
  - and investment commitments.
- A purely symbolic compromise is unlikely to restore competitiveness.
- A package that triggers prolonged industrial conflict likely destroys some of the savings it is meant to create.

### 2) What cannot yet be inferred
No one has provided the key data needed to determine whether a **closure-free package** is economically sufficient. Specifically missing are:

- the **minimum annual savings target** required to restore margin,
- the **cost gap by plant/function**,
- the **restructuring charge** associated with each option,
- the **durability** of savings through 2030,
- and the **probability of successful implementation** under co-determination and labor conflict risk.

Without those, it is not possible to conclude whether:
- closures are indispensable,
- closure-free measures can close most of the gap,
- or a hybrid is optimal.

## 3) How the options typically compare industrially

### A. Closure-based restructuring
**Strengths**
- Highest probability of durable fixed-cost removal.
- Clearest way to eliminate overcapacity.
- Usually strongest on long-run margin improvement.

**Weaknesses**
- Highest near-term disruption and political/labor resistance.
- Large one-time restructuring charges.
- High risk of implementation delay if governance vetoes or strikes occur.
- Can damage supplier networks, know-how, and launch stability.

**Typical industrial conclusion**
Most effective financially if overcapacity is severe and persistent, but only if governance and labor conflict can be managed. In a co-determined German setting, implementation risk can be very high.

### B. Closure-free restructuring
**Strengths**
- Lower conflict risk.
- Better labor relations and lower governance resistance.
- Less risk of losing plant-specific skills and local supply-chain efficiency.
- Faster to negotiate if trust exists.

**Weaknesses**
- Usually delivers less fixed-cost reduction.
- Savings may depend on recurring labor concessions, which can erode after 2030.
- If capacity is genuinely excessive, the company may retain too much structural cost.
- Risk of becoming a “delay package” rather than a reset.

**Typical industrial conclusion**
Viable only if the cost gap is moderate and if labor-side measures, plus management/owner contributions, can generate a large enough durable effect. It is often insufficient when the competitive gap is large.

### C. Hybrid restructuring
**Strengths**
- Most realistic in a co-determined industrial setting.
- Allows some structural footprint reduction while limiting workforce shock.
- Can combine voluntary exits, redeployment, working-time changes, and limited rationalization.
- Better chance of political and supervisory-board acceptance.

**Weaknesses**
- Can become internally inconsistent: not enough closures to fix costs, but enough disruption to trigger conflict.
- Requires careful sequencing and enforceable metrics.
- Savings may be lower than a pure closure case, but execution risk lower than a pure closure case.

**Typical industrial conclusion**
Often the best compromise if the company’s cost gap is substantial but not so large that only closures can solve it. The success of a hybrid depends heavily on whether the savings are **structural and durable**, not temporary.

## 4) What would make a closure-free package credible
For a closure-free package to be credible, it would usually need to show all of the following:

1. **Quantified annual savings** close to the minimum target.
2. Savings that are **durable through 2030** without repeated renegotiation.
3. A meaningful **management and owner contribution**.
4. A credible path to reduce cost via:
   - working-time changes,
   - remuneration moderation,
   - bonus restraint,
   - overhead cuts,
   - internal redeployment,
   - qualification,
   - voluntary departures.
5. Evidence that these measures are sufficient even after allowing for:
   - wage drift,
   - inflation,
   - and future product-cycle fluctuations.

If those savings are too dependent on temporary concessions, then the package is not structurally durable.

## 5) Main analytical risk in the current debate
The biggest risk is that each side is optimizing for a different objective:

- Management and owners are optimizing for **durable cost reduction**.
- Works council and union are optimizing for **job and site preservation**.
- State government is optimizing for **regional industrial continuity**.
- Investors are optimizing for **risk-adjusted value**.

A package that ignores any one of those tends to fail in implementation.

## 6) My likely assessment, absent hard numbers
Based on the governance structure described, the most likely viable outcome is **a hybrid package**, not a pure closure-free or pure closure-based solution.

Why?
- Pure closure-based restructuring faces major veto and conflict risk.
- Pure closure-free restructuring may not remove enough structural cost.
- A hybrid can sometimes satisfy:
  - enough savings for management and capital,
  - enough employment protection for labor,
  - and enough political defensibility for the state.

But whether that hybrid works depends entirely on the numbers.

## 7) What data should be requested next
A useful board model should include, for each plant and major function:

- output and capacity utilization
- fixed cost base
- variable cost position
- labor cost per unit
- implied savings from:
  - working-time changes
  - wage moderation
  - overhead reductions
  - voluntary exits
  - internal transfers
  - line rationalization
  - full closure
- one-time restructuring charges
- timing of savings
- implementation risk
- probability-adjusted net savings through 2030

## Bottom line
At this stage, the dispute is not about whether restructuring is needed. It is about **how much structural cost must come out, how durable the savings are, and whether the company can achieve that without closures**.

From an industrial-restructuring perspective:
- **closures** are the strongest cost-reset tool,
- **closure-free measures** are the least disruptive but often insufficient,
- **hybrid restructuring** is the most plausible compromise.
