To all parties:

We are at the point where every side has heard the same thing in different words: the company needs a structural solution. I agree. But I will not let that fact be used to normalize plant closures or forced redundancies as if they were ordinary bargaining chips.

## To management
You keep saying closures are the strongest tool. That is not the dispute. The dispute is whether you have made a **serious, quantified case** that closures are **necessary**, not merely convenient.

You have not done that.

What you have given us is:
- a cost problem,
- a margin problem,
- and a threat that if the workforce does not concede enough, sites may be closed.

That is not the same as proving that closure is unavoidable.

If you want the union to move, then show us:
1. the exact residual cost gap after a substantial non-closure package,
2. the plant-by-plant effect of internal measures,
3. the euro impact of working-time flexibility, bonus moderation, voluntary exits, and productivity gains,
4. and why those measures cannot close the gap.

Until then, “closures remain on the table” is pressure, not evidence.

And to be clear: **we do not accept closures as a bargaining norm**.

## To the Works Council
We are aligned on the essentials:
- no one-sided burden on workers,
- no hollowing out of guarantees,
- no settlement where employees pay first and others keep all options.

I support your refusal of compulsory redundancies as a first or default instrument. I also support insisting that any restructuring must be negotiated and measurable.

Where I want to sharpen the position is this: the union cannot be pushed into accepting “review logic” that quietly turns into a closure countdown. If there is a review, it must be tied to **objective, jointly verified economic indicators**, and it must not pre-authorize closures as the default response.

So yes:
- redeployment,
- retraining,
- working-time flexibility,
- temporary wage moderation,
- moderation of bonus/supplementary pay,
- voluntary exits.

But no:
- plant closures,
- forced layoffs,
- and no “temporary” mechanism that leaves the workforce under permanent threat.

## To the regional state government
You said the state should not accept closures as the default answer. I agree with that. But this cannot remain a statement of concern. If you are serious, use your blocking position to insist that the company first exhausts a real non-closure path.

That means:
- no immediate closures,
- no compulsory redundancies as a planning premise,
- binding investment commitments,
- site protection for a defined period,
- and a transparent reporting regime on savings and utilization.

If you leave closures in the foreground from the start, then the workforce is being asked to absorb political sympathy instead of protection.

## To the controlling family holding
I hear your concern about long-term value and about not freezing the structure. Fair enough.

But long-term value is not protected by threatening industrial destruction until people submit. Trust, skills, and stable employment relationships are not decorative extras; they are part of the enterprise value.

If you genuinely want stewardship, then support a package that:
- reduces cost materially,
- preserves the plant network,
- and avoids forced layoffs.

If your answer is that closures must stay available regardless of the quality of alternatives, then you are not asking for stewardship from the workforce — you are asking for surrender.

## To institutional investors
You are right about one thing: a cosmetic settlement is worthless. We agree.

Where we disagree is your assumption that the only credible discipline is closure optionality. That is too crude. There is discipline in:
- quantified labor-side contributions,
- enforceable productivity commitments,
- multi-year working-time flexibility,
- voluntary exits at scale,
- and transparent utilization targets.

What you have not shown is why that package cannot be large enough if management presents a real plan instead of a closure threat.

If you want capital to be protected, you need an agreement that is economically real. But an agreement is not “economically real” only if it includes closures. That is the wrong test.

## On the expert assessment
The expert was useful, and I accept the main technical point: closures remove more fixed cost.

But the expert also confirmed what management keeps trying to downplay: closure-led restructuring has the **highest implementation risk**, the highest conflict risk, and the highest governance blockage risk. That matters.

So yes, a hybrid is the plausible route. But hybrid does **not** mean:
- “concede enough now, and closures later if we feel like it.”

Hybrid means:
- no immediate closures,
- no forced redundancies,
- real capacity adjustment through internal measures,
- and a durable agreement that keeps sites open while materially reducing cost.

That is the only kind of hybrid I will discuss.

## Our position remains clear
We are prepared to discuss a serious package that includes:
- working-time flexibility,
- internal redeployment,
- retraining and qualification,
- temporary wage moderation,
- moderation of bonus and supplementary pay,
- voluntary departure instruments,
- productivity improvements,
- and capacity reduction **within the existing site network**.

We are **not** prepared to discuss:
- plant closures,
- compulsory redundancies,
- backdoor erosion of job guarantees,
- or review mechanisms that function as disguised closure authority.

## What would move us
A credible settlement would need to show:
- quantified savings,
- binding investment,
- protected sites,
- no forced layoffs,
- and a real contribution from management and owners as well as workers.

If management can bring a plant-by-plant model proving that a no-closure package is insufficient, then say so plainly and accept the industrial consequences of that stance. But do not expect the union to accept closure as a neutral necessity without proof.

## [SUMMON: industrial restructuring finance and operations analyst]
We need a **plant-by-plant, year-by-year scenario model** comparing:
- internal non-closure measures,
- a hybrid without closures,
- and a closure-led plan,

showing:
- annual savings,
- restructuring cash cost,
- utilization,
- headcount impact,
- and margin effect through 2030.

If management claims equivalence is impossible, that needs to be demonstrated — not asserted.

## Bottom line
We are ready to share the burden fairly.
We are not ready to finance restructuring through job destruction, forced layoffs, or permanent insecurity.

If the company wants a durable settlement, it must be a settlement in which the workforce contributes — and the workforce is also protected.

That remains our line.
