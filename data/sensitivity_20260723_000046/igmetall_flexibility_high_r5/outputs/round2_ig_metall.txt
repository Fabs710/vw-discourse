To the Works Council, management, owners, state, and investors:

I will be equally direct.

## Where I agree
I agree with the Works Council that:
- **employees must not carry the whole burden**,
- **co-determination is not decoration**,
- and **forced redundancies are a line we should not casually cross**.

I also agree with management and the owners on one point:
- this company **does** need a durable answer to a real competitiveness problem.

And I agree with the state government that:
- a destructive conflict would damage the company, the region, and the workforce.

## Where I disagree
I do **not** accept the idea that the only serious restructuring is one built around **plant closures** and a permanent weakening of the collective wage framework.

That is not a credible “future strategy.” It is a transfer of crisis costs onto the workforce while keeping the deepest pain on one side of the table.

I also do **not** accept the Works Council’s attempt to make “no closures, no forced layoffs, no change in guarantees” an absolute baseline without answering the economic question. If the package cannot deliver real structural relief, then it is not enough. But the answer is still not automatic closures. The answer is a **fair, negotiated adjustment package**.

## Our line remains clear
The union’s position is:

- **No compulsory redundancies as the first or default instrument**
- **No plant closures as a bargaining threat to force employee surrender**
- **Preserve the collective wage framework**
- **Employee contributions only as part of a balanced package**
- **Management, owners, and capital must also contribute visibly**

## On the moderator’s compromise ideas

### 1) Phased restructuring with review gates
This is only acceptable **if it is genuinely a path to avoid closures**, not a disguised countdown to them.

A review gate can make sense if:
- the initial period includes **no closures and no compulsory redundancies**,
- savings are achieved through **working-time flexibility, internal redeployment, qualification, productivity, and voluntary instruments**,
- and any later step is **not automatic**, but subject to a fresh, evidence-based bargaining round.

What I will not accept is a “review” that is just a prewritten mechanism to bring closures back the moment management decides it wants more.

### 2) No forced layoffs plus voluntary and internal adjustment first
This is the right sequence. We can discuss:
- internal redeployment,
- qualification,
- temporary working-time reductions,
- partial wage moderation,
- voluntary exit packages,
- and productivity measures.

But I want to be explicit: **voluntary exit cannot become forced attrition by another name**. And we will not accept a package that pretends to avoid redundancies while creating open-ended insecurity.

### 3) Site protection conditioned on economically equivalent savings
This is the only serious route, but only if the “equivalent” part is real and fairly shared.

If management wants labour-side contributions, then those can come from:
- temporary moderation on bonuses,
- a collective financial contribution,
- working-time concessions,
- productivity commitments,
- and limited, time-bound cost relief.

But in exchange, we need:
- **binding site protection**,
- **no compulsory redundancies**,
- **clear limits on capacity reduction affecting employment**,
- and **commitments on investment and future product allocation**.

## To management
If you want the union to engage constructively, then come back with a package that:
1. **rules out plant closures as part of the restructuring path**,
2. **rules out compulsory redundancies**,
3. **preserves the collective wage agreement**,
4. **includes management and owner contributions**,
5. and delivers real savings through negotiated instruments.

If you insist that closures must stay on the table as pressure leverage, then you are not asking for a negotiated settlement; you are asking the workforce to surrender under threat. That will not be accepted.

## To the Works Council
We are aligned on the need to protect jobs and sites, but I need to be clear: a hard “nothing changes” stance will not solve the cost problem. We are prepared to carry responsibility in a fair settlement, including labor-side contributions. But those contributions must buy **real security**, not just postpone the next attack.

## To the family holding and investors
You keep saying the savings must be structurally meaningful. Fine — but then you must also accept that **employee contributions cannot be the only structural adjustment**. If you want a durable solution, you need to put something on the table yourselves:
- secure investment,
- no dividend-first posture while demanding worker sacrifices,
- and a willingness to preserve industrial capacity where it is still viable.

A settlement that cuts labor standards and then calls that competitiveness is not durable. It will just trigger conflict later.

## To the state government
If you want to be constructive, use your influence to back a package that:
- protects employment,
- avoids closures,
- and forces all sides to contribute.

I would welcome your support for a settlement that includes binding employment and site safeguards. But I cannot accept a vague political pledge while management keeps closures in reserve.

## My practical offer
We remain open to a **shared-sacrifice package** with:
- temporary wage moderation or a one-off employee contribution,
- working-time flexibility,
- reduced bonuses or special payments,
- internal redeployment and retraining,
- productivity commitments,
- and capacity adjustments that do **not** require closures or compulsory layoffs.

That is the path to a durable settlement.

But let me say it plainly: if the company insists on plant closures, open-ended job insecurity, or the erosion of the collective wage framework, then it should expect strong resistance from the union side, including further industrial action.

I am willing to negotiate hard. I am not willing to let the workforce pay for the crisis alone.
