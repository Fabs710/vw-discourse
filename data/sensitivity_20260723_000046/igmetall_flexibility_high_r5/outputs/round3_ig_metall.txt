To all parties:

I will not repeat slogans. The question is whether the company is willing to reach a **real, balanced restructuring** or whether it expects the workforce to absorb the crisis while management and owners preserve their options.

## To the Works Council
We are aligned on the essentials:
- no one-sided burden on employees,
- no hollowing out of co-determination,
- no settlement that makes workers pay for management’s strategic mistakes.

I also agree that **plant closures and forced redundancies cannot be the starting point**. They should not be treated as normal instruments.

Where I would push back slightly is this: if we want a durable settlement, we cannot limit ourselves to defending the status quo in principle. We need a package that produces **real, measurable relief**. That means:
- internal redeployment,
- qualification,
- working-time flexibility,
- voluntary departures,
- productivity gains,
- and real employee contributions.

But I am clear on the limit: **no compulsory redundancies, and no plant closures as part of the agreed path**. If management wants to keep them as a threat in reserve, that is not a good-faith negotiation.

## To management
Your argument is still incomplete.

Yes, the company faces a competitiveness problem.
Yes, fixed costs matter.
Yes, capacity and utilization matter.

But you have not shown that closures are **necessary**, only that they are **powerful**. That is not enough to justify putting German sites on the chopping block.

The expert assessment was useful, but it did **not** prove that closure is unavoidable. It showed that:
- closure-led restructuring has the strongest fixed-cost effect,
- a no-closure package can be too weak if it is merely symbolic,
- and a **hybrid** is usually the best risk-adjusted path.

That matters. Because a risk-adjusted hybrid is not the same thing as a closure agenda.

If you want the union to accept meaningful labor-side contributions, then you need to come with:
1. a quantified gap,
2. a quantified non-closure package,
3. and proof that the package is actually sufficient.

Until then, we will not accept “closures remain on the table” as a bargaining premise. That is pressure, not problem-solving.

## To the state government
Your role matters, and I acknowledge that you are not simply echoing management.

I agree with you that a cosmetic settlement is not enough. I also agree that the company needs competitiveness and that the region cannot thrive if the business weakens structurally.

Where I disagree is on the idea that the state should accept “capacity adjustment” language while leaving closures and forced layoffs available in practice. That is too vague.

If you are serious about social responsibility, then use your blocking position to insist on:
- no plant closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- binding job and site security,
- and a proper share of burden from management and owners.

That is what real public stewardship looks like.

## To the controlling family holding
You keep saying you want a durable solution and not a frozen status quo. Fine — but durability cannot mean making workers live under permanent closure threats.

Your “stepping back from closure-first” language is welcome only if it is real. What we need from you is not abstract stewardship talk, but a commitment to a **serious no-closure settlement** if the economics can be met through other means.

And yes: the workforce will make contributions. But these contributions must be tied to **security**, not to a future where the company can reopen the closure question as soon as it wishes.

If you want trust, then stop treating trust as something workers must finance alone.

## To institutional investors
You are right that a symbolic deal is unacceptable. I agree with that.

Where I disagree is your assumption that the only credible discipline comes from closure capability. That is too narrow.

There are serious, measurable tools available:
- multi-year working-time flexibility,
- moderation of supplementary pay and bonuses,
- voluntary exits,
- retraining and redeployment,
- productivity and process improvements,
- capacity rationalization **within** the existing plant network.

If management actually comes with a real plan, those tools can generate meaningful savings. The burden is on management to show the numbers, not on workers to accept closures because they are easier to model.

## On the expert analysis
The expert is right that closures remove fixed cost. Nobody disputes that.

But the expert also said something crucial: a **hybrid package** is often the most credible and best risk-adjusted outcome. That is exactly where the discussion should go — not toward closure threats, but toward a genuinely negotiated industrial solution.

So let me be blunt:
- A **pure closure plan** is socially destructive and highly risky.
- A **pure labor-concession plan** is probably too weak if it is only symbolic.
- A **hybrid settlement without closures and without forced layoffs** is the only path I consider credible.

That hybrid must be large enough to matter. I am not offering a token gesture. But I am also not accepting coercive restructuring.

## Our position remains firm
The union will support a settlement only if it includes:
- **no compulsory redundancies**,
- **no plant closures**,
- **preservation of the collective wage framework**,
- **fair labor-side contributions**,
- **management and owner contributions**,
- and a **durable employment and investment perspective** for the German sites.

What can move us:
- temporary wage moderation,
- reduced bonuses or special payments,
- working-time flexibility,
- voluntary departure instruments,
- redeployment,
- retraining,
- productivity commitments,
- capacity adjustment that does not destroy sites.

What will not move us:
- closures,
- forced layoffs,
- open-ended insecurity,
- or any deal where employees carry the crisis alone.

## [SUMMON: Industrial restructuring finance and operations analyst]
We need a concrete comparative model for a **no-closure hybrid** versus a **closure-led plan**, with:
- annual savings by measure,
- restructuring cash cost,
- impact on utilization,
- effect on 2030 operating margin,
- and implementation risk.

Without that, “closures are necessary” remains an assertion, not a conclusion.

## Final word
We are ready to take responsibility.
We are ready to contribute fairly.
We are **not** ready to finance competitiveness through job destruction and permanent insecurity.

If management wants a settlement, it must be a **shared-sacrifice package with binding site and job protection**. Anything else will meet resistance.
