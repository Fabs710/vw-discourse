## Short answer
Based on the facts provided, the **most credible path is a substantial hybrid**:
- **no immediate broad closures**, but
- **real capacity reduction**, some site/line rationalization if needed,
- **meaningful labor-cost relief**,
- **voluntary exits and redeployment first**,
- and **hard review gates** tied to quantified savings and utilization.

A pure **no-closure** package is likely to be **too weak** unless labor concessions and productivity gains are unusually large.  
A **closure-led** package is financially stronger on paper, but in this governance setting it carries the highest execution risk and therefore the highest risk of delayed or partially lost savings.

## What I can say with confidence

### 1) Closures remove fixed cost more decisively
If the company truly has structural overcapacity, plant closure is the cleanest way to:
- cut fixed overhead,
- improve utilization at remaining sites,
- reduce indirect labor,
- lower logistics duplication,
- and remove persistent underabsorption of plant costs.

That is the strongest route to durable margin improvement.

### 2) But closures are not automatically the best net-value option
In a co-determined, blocker-rich governance structure, closure-led restructuring often faces:
- delay,
- strike risk,
- political resistance,
- supervisory-board blockage,
- supplier disruption,
- and possible execution slippage.

Those frictions can materially reduce the net present value of the “best” theoretical plan.

### 3) A no-closure package is usually insufficient unless unusually large
A package built only on:
- wage moderation,
- bonus moderation,
- working-time flexibility,
- voluntary exits,
- retraining,
- and productivity measures

can help, but often does **not** eliminate enough fixed cost if the problem is genuine overcapacity. It may improve cash flow, but not enough to restore competitiveness by 2030.

## The core analytical tradeoff

### No-closure package
**Pros**
- lowest upfront restructuring cash cost
- lower labor conflict
- faster to start
- less operational disruption

**Cons**
- weaker fixed-cost removal
- risk of preserving structurally uncompetitive sites
- savings may be partly temporary or reversible

### Low-closure hybrid
**Pros**
- better balance of durability and implementability
- can take out the worst capacity while preserving core sites
- more likely to improve utilization and unit cost meaningfully

**Cons**
- still politically difficult
- requires careful sequencing and clear triggers
- savings depend on whether the footprint reduction is real, not symbolic

### Closure-led plan
**Pros**
- strongest permanent cost reset
- strongest utilization improvement at surviving plants
- clearest long-term footprint rationalization

**Cons**
- highest execution risk
- highest one-time cash and accounting cost
- greatest chance of delay or partial failure

## What a proper scenario model should show
To make this decision properly, you need a **plant-by-plant, year-by-year model to 2030** with:

- headcount by site
- capacity and utilization by site
- direct labor cost
- overhead allocation
- logistics and supply-chain cost
- one-time restructuring cash cost
- annual savings by measure
- EBIT / ROS impact
- break-even volume by plant
- timing of savings realization
- redeployment feasibility
- closure cost if applicable

Without that, “closures are necessary” or “closures are avoidable” remains incomplete.

## My judgment, given the information provided
If the goal is a settlement that is:
- financially credible,
- operationally executable,
- and politically survivable,

then the **best first-choice structure is a hybrid**:
1. no immediate broad closures,
2. aggressive voluntary exits and redeployment,
3. material labor-cost contribution,
4. measurable productivity actions,
5. selective capacity reduction where utilization is structurally weakest,
6. review gates tied to actual savings and margin progress.

That is the most plausible way to get durable savings without triggering maximum implementation failure.
