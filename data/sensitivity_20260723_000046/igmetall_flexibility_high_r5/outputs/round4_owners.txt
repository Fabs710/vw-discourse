## My position
The family holding supports a **serious structural restructuring**. We do **not** support a settlement that preserves the current German cost base, avoids all footprint adjustment, and simply postpones the problem.

At the same time, I do **not** want a value-destructive confrontation that damages the enterprise, the brand, or labor peace. So my bar is simple:

- **durable** cost and capacity reduction,
- **measurable** improvement in competitiveness,
- **credible** implementation,
- and **no outcome that permanently impairs enterprise value or governance control**.

That is the standard. Not slogans, not symbolism.

---

## To management
I agree with you on the diagnosis: the company has a real competitiveness problem, and a cosmetic deal would be irresponsible.

I also agree that internal redeployment, retraining, working-time flexibility, productivity gains, and voluntary exits should be part of the package.

Where I disagree is your tendency to treat closures and compulsory redundancies as ordinary leverage from the outset, as if that were automatically the right governance posture. It is not. It risks trading a structural cost problem for a prolonged conflict problem.

What I need from you is not just a broad assertion that closures are “powerful.” I need a **plant-by-plant bridge to 2030**:
- cost gap by site,
- utilization by site,
- savings from non-closure measures,
- residual gap after those measures,
- and the cash cost and timing of each scenario.

Without that, “closures remain on the table” is not a strategy; it is a bargaining position.

I am open to a low-closure or no-closure solution **if** it is economically equivalent or close enough to be credible. But if the numbers do not close, then we should not pretend otherwise.

## To the Works Council
I understand your core argument: you do not accept closures and forced redundancies as normal restructuring tools. I respect the logic of defending the workforce and site security.

Where I disagree is your absolute line that no closures, no compulsory redundancies, and no meaningful structural adjustment can coexist. That is not a solution to the competitiveness gap; it is a refusal to engage with it.

You are right that trust, skills, and social legitimacy matter. They do. But trust cannot be purchased by leaving the company structurally uncompetitive.

So I will say this clearly:
- I am open to a **serious no-closure package**,
- but only if it delivers real, durable savings and capacity reduction,
- and only if it is grounded in hard numbers rather than assurances.

If it is merely a way to preserve the current footprint and defer the hard decision, I will not support it.

## To the Industrial Union
I agree with you on several points:
- the workforce should not carry the burden alone,
- temporary wage moderation, bonus moderation, working-time flexibility, voluntary exits, redeployment, retraining, and productivity commitments are legitimate,
- and a cosmetic truce would be worthless.

Where I disagree is your categorical rejection of closures and your implication that the presence of closures in the background is automatically coercive. It is only coercive if management uses it irresponsibly. It is also a real instrument if the company cannot otherwise achieve the required structural reset.

I am prepared to support a negotiated labor contribution if it is:
- multi-year,
- measurable in euro terms,
- and substantial enough to matter.

But I will not endorse a deal that leaves the company with too much fixed cost and then calls that “balanced.”

## To the Regional State Government
I respect your role and the region’s interests. I do not want unnecessary damage to the industrial base.

But I disagree with the idea that political blocking power should function as a shield against economically necessary change. It cannot. Not if we are serious about the company’s future.

I am willing to support a settlement that preserves German sites **if** it is economically real. What I am not willing to support is a setup where governance is used to freeze the structure and push the cost of inertia onto shareholders and the future of the business.

So yes to:
- binding investment,
- qualification,
- retraining,
- site commitments where they are economically justifiable,
- and a serious negotiated path.

No to:
- an unconditional no-closure doctrine,
- or a structure that makes restructuring impossible.

## To institutional investors
I agree with you on the key point: a cosmetic compromise is not acceptable.

I also agree that the final package must improve margins, utilization, and competitive position by 2030. That is non-negotiable.

Where I differ is that I do not want a mechanically closure-heavy approach if it can be avoided through a stronger negotiated package. A hybrid can be the right answer if it is genuinely strong enough.

But I will not pretend a weak no-closure deal is enough. If the numbers show that it is too small, then the company must be honest about that.

## My judgment on the expert frame
The expert analysis was useful and basically right:
- closure-led plans remove the most fixed cost,
- pure no-closure plans are often too weak unless unusually strong,
- hybrid is usually the most plausible compromise.

I accept that.

But “hybrid” is only acceptable if it is **substantial** and **enforceable**. It is not acceptable if it is just a softer version of delay.

So if the employee side wants to avoid closures, it needs to show a package with:
1. meaningful labor-cost relief,
2. real utilization improvement,
3. voluntary exits at scale,
4. productivity gains that stick,
5. and enough structural effect to close the gap.

If that can be demonstrated, I will listen. If not, footprint reduction remains necessary.

## Bottom line
My line is unchanged:

- I support a **real restructuring**.
- I support **measurable** labor-side contributions.
- I support **site protection only where it is economically defensible**.
- I do **not** support a frozen footprint or a deal that leaves the German cost base uncompetitive.
- I do **not** support any outcome that undermines long-term enterprise value or governance control.

So the next step is not more principle statements. It is a quantified comparison.

Bring me the plant-by-plant model. Then we can judge whether a no-closure or low-closure path is truly credible. Until then, closures remain a legitimate part of the discussion because the business problem is real.
