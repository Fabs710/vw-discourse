Below is the restructuring-finance and operations assessment of the three package types. I’ll keep this within industrial economics, not labor politics.

## Executive view

**Most risk-adjusted value for equity holders is usually a hybrid package with targeted footprint reduction**, not an extreme “all closures” or “no closures” outcome.

Why:
- A pure **closure-led restructuring** typically produces the largest structural savings, but also the highest execution risk, highest upfront cash cost, and highest probability of industrial disruption.
- A pure **no-closure labor-concession package** has the lowest near-term disruption, but is often **insufficient** if the company truly has excess capacity and elevated fixed costs.
- A **hybrid** can capture much of the fixed-cost benefit while reducing implementation risk and preserving some operational continuity.

That said, if the underlying overcapacity is large, a no-closure package can easily become a **temporary truce** rather than a real solution.

---

## 1) Comparative economics

### A. Closure-led restructuring
**Economic upside**
- Removes fixed manufacturing overhead at scale.
- Reduces capacity and improves utilization at surviving plants.
- Cuts direct and indirect labor, management layers, maintenance, and site costs.
- Offers the strongest path to improving unit cost and return on sales.

**Economic downside**
- Large cash restructuring charges:
  - severance
  - decommissioning
  - write-downs/impairments
  - transfer and logistics costs
  - requalification of suppliers
- Savings often arrive with a lag.
- Production ramp risk at remaining sites can delay realization.

**Assessment**
- Best on **steady-state cost reduction**.
- Worst on **near-term cash and execution risk**.

---

### B. No-closure labor-concession package
**Economic upside**
- Lower immediate cash cost.
- Less disruption to output, suppliers, and plant operations.
- Can produce meaningful savings if concessions are multi-year and broad.

**Economic downside**
- Usually does **not** remove enough fixed cost if plants remain underutilized.
- Labor concessions can be partially offset by residual overhead and complexity.
- Savings may be reversible or time-limited unless contractual and durable.
- If the capacity problem is structural, this may not restore target margins.

**Assessment**
- Best on **stability**.
- Weakest on **structural de-risking** if overcapacity is real.

---

### C. Hybrid with targeted footprint reduction
**Economic upside**
- Removes some fixed cost and excess capacity.
- Retains operational flexibility.
- Reduces the amount of severance/disruption versus a full closure program.
- Often has the highest chance of actually being implemented.

**Economic downside**
- Savings may be less than a full closure plan.
- Requires careful design to avoid “half measures.”
- If too diluted, it can underdeliver: some capacity goes away, but not enough to solve the margin gap.

**Assessment**
- Often the **best risk-adjusted** outcome.
- Most credible when the goal is to improve 2030 margin without triggering full-scale operational disruption.

---

## 2) Year-by-year operating margin impact: directional view

Because no plant-level data are provided, I can only give a **relative** path, not exact numbers.

### Closure-led
- **Year 1–2:** margin may look worse or flat due to restructuring charges and disruption.
- **Year 2–4:** margin improves as fixed cost is removed and utilization rises at remaining sites.
- **By 2030:** strongest chance of a materially higher operating margin, assuming execution succeeds.

### No-closure labor-concession
- **Year 1:** faster visible P&L relief if wage moderation/bonus cuts are immediate.
- **Year 2–3:** improvement can plateau if underlying underutilization remains.
- **By 2030:** could still fall short if site overhead and capacity surplus are not addressed.

### Hybrid
- **Year 1:** some restructuring cost, but less than full closure.
- **Year 2–4:** more durable margin lift than labor-only, with less operational shock than full closure.
- **By 2030:** likely the best balance of margin recovery and execution probability.

---

## 3) Restructuring cash costs

### Closure-led
Highest cash burden:
- severance and social-plan costs
- asset write-downs
- site shutdown costs
- transitional logistics and capex at receiving plants

This can stress liquidity unless the balance sheet is strong.

### No-closure labor-concession
Lowest cash burden:
- mostly legal/administrative cost and some voluntary exit or retraining expense
- but savings may be slower and smaller

This is easiest on liquidity in the short run.

### Hybrid
Moderate cash burden:
- targeted closures or consolidations create some upfront cost
- but less than a full closure program
- usually more manageable from a financing standpoint

**Liquidity implication:**  
If the company is not highly liquid, a full closure plan can be dangerous unless funded and phased carefully. A hybrid usually gives the best balance between savings and cash consumption.

---

## 4) Utilization effects

This is the central operational issue.

### Closure-led
- raises utilization at the remaining plants
- reduces duplicate overhead
- improves per-unit economics if the volume transfer is well executed

### No-closure labor-concession
- utilization remains the same, or only slightly better if output is rebalanced
- fixed-cost dilution remains weak if demand is soft
- the system may still carry too many plants for the volume base

### Hybrid
- can meaningfully improve utilization without requiring the whole network to be restructured at once
- better chance of getting some plants over the economic threshold while avoiding total network shock

**Important:**  
If several sites are below economic utilization, labor concessions alone cannot fully compensate. A plant that is underfilled remains expensive even if wages are reduced.

---

## 5) Probability of implementation failure

This is where the packages differ sharply.

### Closure-led: highest failure risk
Failure modes:
- prolonged industrial action
- board/governance deadlock
- supplier disruption
- delayed site transfer
- legal or procedural blockage
- talent loss and morale damage

### No-closure labor-concession: moderate failure risk
Failure modes:
- short-term peace, then cost re-emerges
- insufficient savings
- internal resistance to productivity measures
- savings eroded by weak enforcement or expiry dates

### Hybrid: usually lowest failure risk
Why:
- each side gets something tangible
- the package can be structured in phases
- the company avoids the most destabilizing elements of a full closure program
- more likely to survive governance constraints and maintain operating continuity

---

## 6) Risk-adjusted outcome for equity holders

If I were ranking by **expected value adjusted for implementation risk**, I would usually rank them as:

1. **Hybrid with targeted footprint reduction**
2. **Closure-led restructuring**
3. **No-closure labor-concession package**

### Why not closure-led first?
Because the gross savings can be high, but the probability-weighted outcome may be inferior if disruption, delays, or blockage are substantial.

### Why not no-closure first?
Because the odds of underdelivering on structural savings are high if the company truly has excess capacity and a high fixed-cost base.

### Why hybrid first?
Because it maximizes the chance of:
- real savings,
- acceptable liquidity impact,
- manageable industrial disruption,
- and governance feasibility.

---

## 7) What would make no-closure viable?

A no-closure package could still work if it is unusually strong and durable. For that, I would want to see:

- multi-year wage moderation
- removal or reduction of variable compensation
- longer working-time flexibility
- permanent productivity improvements per employee-hour
- voluntary exits at meaningful scale
- internal redeployment that actually reduces labor burden
- enough capacity adjustment through output consolidation or line rationalization to materially lift utilization

If those elements are small or temporary, the package is unlikely to restore competitiveness.

---

## 8) What data are needed to decide properly

To quantify this properly, management needs plant-level and program-level modeling of:

- fixed cost by plant
- variable cost per vehicle
- utilization by line and by model
- direct and indirect labor per vehicle
- logistics and supplier transfer costs
- restructuring cash charges
- savings by year through 2030
- effect on EBIT and operating margin
- probability-weighted disruption cost

Without that, any side claiming certainty is overstating the case.

---

## Bottom line

**Best risk-adjusted outcome for equity holders: a hybrid package with targeted footprint reduction.**

- It is usually better than a labor-only deal because it addresses real overcapacity.
- It is usually better than an all-closure plan because it reduces execution and liquidity risk.
- It offers the most plausible bridge to improved utilization, lower fixed cost, and a stronger operating margin by 2030.
