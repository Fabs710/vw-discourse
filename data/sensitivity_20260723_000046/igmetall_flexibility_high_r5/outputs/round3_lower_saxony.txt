I will be direct.

The state sees the **economic problem** and does not deny it. But I will not accept the conclusion that the only serious answer is to keep **closures and forced redundancies permanently on the table** and then expect the regional public to bless that as “responsible restructuring.”

That is not my mandate, and it is not acceptable for this company.

## To management
I agree with you on one point: this company needs a **durable** solution, not a cosmetic pause. I also agree that wage flexibility, productivity, redeployment, qualification, and voluntary instruments all have to be part of the package.

Where I disagree is your insistence that closures must remain an ordinary option if the numbers are uncomfortable. That may be technically true in a narrow sense, but it is not a sufficient basis for governing this company in the public interest.

You have not yet shown that the restructuring cannot be done **without** plant closures. You have shown only that closures are the most forceful tool. Those are not the same thing.

If management wants my support, it must bring forward a package that:
- preserves German sites,
- avoids compulsory redundancies,
- delivers real and measurable savings,
- and keeps the company competitive long term.

If you cannot do that, then you are not asking for a settlement; you are asking the state to pre-approve industrial damage.

## To the Works Council
I agree with you that the workforce must not be the sole correction mechanism and that closures are not the default answer. I also agree that trust, skills, and social partnership are part of this company’s value.

But I will also be frank with you: simply saying “no closures, no redundancies, no changes” is not enough if it does not answer the cost problem. The company cannot be protected by slogans.

So yes, I support your core line:
- no plant closures as the starting point,
- no compulsory redundancies as the first instrument,
- no erosion of co-determination,
- no one-sided burden on workers.

But that position must be paired with a credible, quantified contribution from the workforce side that is actually large enough to matter.

## To the Industrial Union
I agree that the workforce should not pay alone, and I agree that a conflict would be destructive. Your emphasis on negotiated burden-sharing is sensible.

What I need from you is not a symbolic concession, but a package that is **durable, measurable, and meaningful**. That means labor-side measures that do more than delay the problem:
- multi-year working-time flexibility,
- moderation of supplementary payments,
- voluntary exits at real scale,
- retraining and redeployment that reduce overhead,
- and productivity commitments that stick.

If those measures are substantial enough, then they can justify keeping sites open. If they are not, then we are not discussing an equivalent alternative to restructuring.

## To the controlling family holding
I agree that a purely closure-led reflex would be irresponsible. You are right to warn against value destruction through conflict.

But you must also accept that long-term value is not protected by threatening German sites until everyone surrenders. Stewardship includes the social legitimacy of the company, not just the balance sheet.

So I am aligned with you on one condition: if there is a credible **no-closure or low-closure** settlement, I will support it. But it has to be real, not rhetorical.

## To the institutional investors
You are right that cosmetic deals are not enough. I do not dispute that. But I will not endorse a framework that treats closures as the benchmark of seriousness before the alternatives have been properly tested.

A deal is credible if it:
- reduces cost structurally,
- improves utilization,
- protects the industrial base in the region,
- and can be implemented without triggering a destructive governance or labor conflict.

That is the standard I will use.

## On the expert assessment
The expert has made the important point that closures are powerful because they remove fixed cost, while non-closure packages are only credible if they are unusually strong. I accept that.

But that analysis also supports a **hybrid** approach as the most plausible compromise. For me, hybrid does **not** mean “closures later if the workforce fails.” It means:
- no immediate closures,
- no compulsory redundancies as the default path,
- real capacity adjustment,
- real labor-cost measures,
- and a binding review of whether the cost gap is actually being closed.

That is the only form of hybrid I can support.

## My line, plainly
The state government will support a restructuring only if it protects:
1. the company’s long-term competitiveness,
2. German jobs and sites,
3. co-determination and our blocking rights,
4. and the regional economy’s role as an industrial anchor.

We will not support:
- a closure-led demolition of the German footprint,
- a backdoor erosion of job guarantees,
- or any attempt to sideline the state as merely decorative capital.
