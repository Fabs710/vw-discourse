## To management
I acknowledge the competitiveness problem. I do **not** deny that the company needs structural improvement, and I am not interested in preserving inefficiency for its own sake.

But I reject the way you keep framing the issue: as if closures and compulsory redundancies are the natural baseline and everyone else must justify why they are not acceptable.

That is not how I will approach this as a shareholder with a blocking stake and a responsibility to the region.

You have still **not** demonstrated that a no-closure or low-closure package cannot close the gap **if it is serious, durable, and measurable**. What you have shown is that closures are the most forceful instrument on paper. That is not the same as proving they are necessary or proportionate.

I will not support a restructuring that:
- guts jobs in the state,
- weakens the industrial base,
- or treats site closures as the first real answer.

If management wants my support, it needs to present a **quantified bridge** showing:
- what savings remain after labor flexibility, wage moderation, voluntary exits, redeployment, and productivity measures,
- what capacity reduction can be achieved **without closures**,
- and why that package is insufficient if it is claimed to be insufficient.

Until then, I do **not** accept your closure logic as settled fact.

## To the Works Council
I agree with your core line: the workforce cannot be asked to absorb the crisis alone, and German sites are not disposable bargaining chips.

I also agree that closures and forced redundancies must **not** become normal restructuring tools. That is especially true in a company that is a major regional anchor and where co-determination is part of the governance settlement.

Where I would press you is this: your position is strongest when it is **defensive and constructive**, not merely absolutist.

You are right to insist on:
- no plant closures as a default,
- no compulsory redundancies as a first resort,
- retraining, redeployment, working-time flexibility, voluntary exits, and productivity measures.

But if you want the state and the broader shareholder base to stand behind a no-closure path, it has to be more than a principled refusal. It has to be a **credible industrial plan** with hard numbers and enforceable commitments.

## To the Industrial Union

You are right that a cosmetic deal is worthless. You are right that the workforce should not carry the burden alone. And you are right that a negotiated package with temporary wage moderation, bonus moderation, working-time flexibility, voluntary exits, redeployment, and productivity commitments is the proper first path.

But I need to be frank: I cannot endorse language that leaves me believing closures are just “temporary pressure” rather than a real threat. That would undermine trust and make the whole process politically and industrially explosive.

So my position is:

- **no** to closures as a starting assumption,
- **no** to forced redundancies as a bargaining weapon.

- binding investment and qualification commitments,
- and clear protection against compulsory redundancies where alternatives exist.

What I will **not** support is a scheme where the state blocks restructuring but offers no viable economic replacement. That would leave us with drift, not stewardship.

## To Institutional Investors

- measurable,
