To the Works Council, the Union, the State Government, the Family, and Investors:

I’ll be blunt, because we are past the point where polite formulations solve a structural cost crisis.

## First: what I agree with
I agree on several points:

- This company needs a **durable** solution, not a cosmetic pause.
- The workforce should **not** be the only party contributing.
- Internal redeployment, qualification, working-time flexibility, productivity improvements, and voluntary exits are all legitimate tools.
- A prolonged strike would damage everyone, including management credibility and shareholder value.
- A settlement that destroys trust without improving the business is worthless.

So yes — I accept the principle of shared sacrifice.

## But here is the hard line
I do **not** accept the idea that:
- every German plant must stay open regardless of economics,
- compulsory redundancies can never be part of the toolset,
- job guarantees can remain untouched even if they block restructuring,
- or that “social responsibility” means preserving an uncompetitive footprint.

That is not a restructuring strategy. That is a request to freeze the problem in place.

---

## To the Works Council
You keep saying closures are coercion and that we have not proved they are unavoidable.

I disagree.

We have shown that:
- German costs are materially too high,
- the European market is shrinking,
- competitive pressure is intensifying,
- and the brand is missing its return-on-sales target.

Against that backdrop, preserving every site and every guarantee is not “stability”; it is **cost rigidity**. If you want to rule out closures, then you must offer a package that is **economically equivalent**, not just politically more comfortable.

You have not done that yet.

### Where I agree with you
- No one wants industrial destruction for its own sake.
- Any deal should avoid unnecessary disruption.
- Voluntary and internal measures should come first.

### Where I disagree
- “No closures” as a fixed principle is not credible if the numbers don’t work.
- “No compulsory redundancies” cannot become a permanent veto on structural change.
- “Job guarantees through 2030” cannot be treated as sacred if they prevent the company from restoring competitiveness.

If your answer is simply “keep all sites open and find savings elsewhere,” then show me the scale. Show me the hard euro impact. Show me how it closes the cost gap. So far, I have not seen that.

---

## To the Industrial Union
I appreciate that you acknowledge the need for real savings and not just symbolism. But you are still trying to box management into a settlement that preserves too much and transfers too little burden.

You say closures should not be the starting point. Fine. They are not my first choice either.

But you also want:
- no plant closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- preserved collective wage structures,
- and binding site security.

That combination may be politically attractive, but it only works if the savings are large enough to match the scale of the problem. You have not shown that.

### What I would consider serious
- multi-year wage moderation,
- bonus and supplementary-pay reductions,
- longer working-time flexibility,
- meaningful voluntary exits,
- real productivity commitments,
- and measurable capacity reduction.

### What is not enough
- a one-off contribution,
- temporary language about solidarity,
- or vague promises that future efficiency will somehow cover the gap.

If you want to avoid footprint reduction, then the burden on labour costs must be substantial and durable. Otherwise, it is not a restructuring.

And to be clear: I will not sign up to a deal where management gives up the right to act if the package later proves insufficient.

---

## To the Regional State Government
I respect your role, and I respect the fact that this company matters to the region.

But I need to say this plainly: your blocking power does not change the economics.

You can insist on social responsibility. You can insist on negotiated solutions. You can insist on a serious industrial future.

What you cannot do is guarantee that the current footprint stays untouched if that footprint is structurally too expensive.

That would not be stewardship. It would be political deferral.

### Where I agree
- The state should not bless a reckless demolition of the industrial base.
- Employee contributions should not be one-sided.
- Industrial peace matters.

### Where I disagree
- A no-closure posture as an unconditional rule is not acceptable.
- “No forced redundancies” cannot be treated as a total veto on future adjustment.
- Political protection cannot substitute for economic viability.

If the state wants to support this company, it should support a package that is **economically real**. Not one that simply avoids the hard decision.

---

## To the Controlling Family Holding
You are right about one thing: a purely closure-driven reaction is not the only possible answer.

I agree. I do **not** want closures if a credible alternative can deliver the same structural effect.

But your position has to be more than a general preference for “durability.” Durability requires actual cost correction.

You say you are open to a no-closure or low-closure path if it is economically equivalent. Good. Then let’s be precise:
- How much labour-cost relief?
- How much capacity reduction?
- How much productivity gain?
- By when?
- And what if the targets are missed?

If the answer is “we’ll see later,” then that is not a strategy.

I am prepared to support a negotiated solution that preserves sites **if** it genuinely fixes the cost structure. But I will not support a package that hides overcapacity behind positive language.

---

## To the Institutional Investors
You are right to focus on economics, and I agree with your core point: **the final package must restore competitiveness and return on sales**.

Where I disagree is your tendency to treat closure threat as the benchmark for seriousness. That is too narrow.

What matters is whether the company gets:
- lower unit cost,
- better utilization,
- lower overhead,
- and a believable path to 2030 margins.

If a hybrid package can deliver that, then I am open to it. But if the package is just a softer version of the current structure, then it is not acceptable.

And I will say this clearly: investors also need to understand the cost of implementation failure. A closure-heavy path that triggers long strikes or board blockage can destroy value faster than it creates it. So the question is not simply “what cuts most on paper,” but “what actually can be executed.”

That said, I will not let “execution risk” become an excuse for doing too little.

---

## My position, stated plainly
Management’s position is not a bluff.

We need:
1. a **durable structural reduction in cost**,
2. a **meaningful reduction in capacity**,
3. a **genuine labour-cost contribution**,
4. a **credible path to improved return on sales by 2030**,
5. and a package that frees funds for future products and technology.

If the employee side can produce a package that achieves that **without closures**, I will take it seriously.

If it cannot, then closures remain on the table because they are one of the few instruments that can remove fixed cost at the necessary scale.

That is not threat language. That is financial reality.

---

## On the expert analysis
The expert was useful, and I accept the core conclusion:
- closure-led restructuring removes more fixed cost,
- no-closure packages are only credible if unusually strong,
- a hybrid is often the best risk-adjusted outcome.

That is exactly why I am not demanding immediate closures as the only route.

But “hybrid” is not a euphemism for preserving the status quo.

A hybrid must still produce:
- real capacity adjustment,
- real cost reduction,
- and real implementation certainty.

If the hybrid does not move the economics enough, then it is just delay with better branding.

---

## What I need next
We need to stop talking in abstractions.

I want a concrete package with:
- plant-level savings,
- capacity changes,
- labor-cost measures,
- timing to 2030,
- and a clear answer on whether the savings are structurally sufficient.

If the employee side can demonstrate equivalence, we can discuss how to deliver it without closures.

If not, then the discussion must include footprint reduction — because I will not accept an agreement that leaves the German cost base uncompetitive.

## Bottom line
I am open to alternatives to closures.  
I am **not** open to a deal that fails to fix the structure.

That is the line.
