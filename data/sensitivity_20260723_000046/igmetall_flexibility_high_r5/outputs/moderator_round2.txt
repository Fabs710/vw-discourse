### Moderator Summary — Round 2

#### 1) What has been agreed
- All sides agree the company faces a **real competitiveness problem** and needs a **durable solution**, not a cosmetic delay.
- There is broad agreement that **workers should not carry the full burden**.
- Most participants accept that **internal redeployment, qualification/retraining, working-time flexibility, productivity improvements, and voluntary exit instruments** should be part of any package.
- There is general agreement that a **destructive conflict, prolonged strike, or stalemate would damage the company, workforce, region, and value**.
- The expert assessment was accepted as a useful frame: **closure-based restructuring removes more fixed cost**, while **non-closure packages may be insufficient unless unusually strong**; a **hybrid** was presented as the most plausible compromise structure.

#### 2) Where the core disagreements remain
- **Plant closures in Germany**
  - Works Council, union, and state government reject closures as a default or early instrument.
  - Management, investors, and the family holding say closures must remain available if needed to achieve the required structural adjustment.
- **Compulsory redundancies**
  - Employee-side actors want them excluded as a first/default step, ideally avoided altogether.
  - Management and capital-side actors will not rule them out if alternatives fail to deliver equivalent savings.
- **Job guarantees and site protection**
  - Employee-side actors insist that existing guarantees and site commitments remain intact.
  - Management and capital-side actors say such guarantees cannot block necessary restructuring.
- **Whether alternatives can be economically equivalent**
  - The key unresolved issue is whether a no-closure or low-closure package can produce savings, capacity reduction, and utilization improvements comparable to footprint reduction.
- **Role of state governance**
  - The state government supports protection of sites and jobs, but management, investors, and the family question whether political blocking rights should effectively prevent economically necessary change.

#### 3) What key trade-offs are still unaddressed
- **Savings versus social stability**: how much structural cost reduction is required, and how much disruption is acceptable to obtain it?
- **Speed versus durability**: immediate relief through labor measures versus slower but deeper restructuring through footprint change.
- **Cash cost versus long-term margin**: closure-led plans promise stronger long-term savings but higher upfront restructuring charges.
- **Capacity reduction versus site preservation**: if all sites remain open, can utilization and unit costs improve enough to restore competitiveness?
- **Voluntary adjustment versus mandatory measures**: whether voluntary exits and internal redeployment can be scaled sufficiently without eventually requiring forced redundancies.
- **Governance legitimacy versus financial autonomy**: how far state co-determination and blocking rights can shape the outcome without overriding financial necessity.

#### 4) Brief refocus for the next round
The next round should move from principle statements to **quantified, comparable options**:
- Define the **minimum required cost reduction** and **capacity adjustment**.
- Compare a **closure-led**, **no-closure**, and **hybrid** package using the same metrics:
  - annual savings,
  - restructuring cash cost,
  - headcount impact,
  - utilization improvement,
  - and expected margin effect through 2030.
- Identify which measures are **binding and measurable** versus merely temporary or symbolic.
- Clarify what conditions would make a **no-closure settlement credible**, and what conditions would make **footprint reduction unavoidable**.
