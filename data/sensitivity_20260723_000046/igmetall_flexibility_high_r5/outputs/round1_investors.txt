From the perspective of dispersed institutional shareholders, the key question is not whether anyone’s preferred moral position wins, but whether this company can get back to **competitive, durable margins** without destroying more value in the process.

### Where we agree
We agree with management, and to a degree with the family holding, on the core diagnosis: **the current German cost base is not competitive**, and the company cannot invest its way out of a structurally weak operating model. If the brand keeps missing its return-on-sales target, equity holders will continue to bear the cost through lower earnings, lower valuation, and reduced strategic flexibility.

We also agree that a **prolonged strike or open-ended confrontation would be value-destructive**. Industrial peace matters because uncertainty itself is expensive: it hits production, suppliers, execution, and market confidence.

And we agree that a **purely symbolic restructuring is not acceptable**. Temporary workarounds, accounting savings, or delayed pain do not restore shareholder value if the underlying cost structure remains too high.

### Where we disagree
We do **not** accept any outcome that leaves the company with an uncompetitive cost base while avoiding the hard decisions entirely.

So if the workforce position is effectively:
- no closures,
- no meaningful job reductions,
- no material labour-cost adjustment,
- and no real change to legacy guarantees,

then that is not a restructuring. It is a request to preserve an unsustainable structure and hope the market stops caring. We would not support that.

At the same time, we are also not enthusiastic about a solution that relies on **maximal disruption** if a credible negotiated path can achieve comparable savings. Plant closures may be necessary in some cases, but they are not valuable in themselves. They are only acceptable if they produce a clear, durable improvement in competitiveness and capital returns.

### What matters to us
We want three things:
1. **Durable structural cost reduction**
2. **Credible implementation certainty**
3. **A path back to competitive margins in the near to medium term**

That means any acceptable settlement must do more than preserve social peace. It must actually improve the economics.

### On management
Management needs to be careful not to confuse toughness with credibility. If the restructuring is presented as all-or-nothing, but the eventual outcome is watered down into a cosmetic deal, then management will have weakened its own authority and likely prolonged the problem. Capital markets will discount that quickly.

If, however, management can secure a package that delivers real savings through a combination of capacity reduction, labour-cost adjustment, productivity gains, and selective structural changes, then we would view that as value-positive.

### On the works council and union
We hear the insistence on no closures and no compulsory redundancies. We understand the political and social logic, but we cannot support a position that effectively guarantees the continuation of uncompetitive economics.

If the employee side wants to avoid closures, it must help deliver a package that is **economically equivalent**:
- real capacity reduction,
- measurable labour-cost concessions,
- flexibility on working time,
- productivity gains,
- and credible long-term operating discipline.

If those elements are not there, then the employee side is asking shareholders to fund stagnation.

### On the state government
We respect the state’s role and the importance of the region. But as shareholders, we are not prepared to subsidize regional stability through weaker returns and permanent overcapacity. Regional legitimacy is important, but it cannot override economic reality indefinitely.

If the state wants to be constructive, it should help broker a settlement that preserves a viable industrial footprint **and** restores competitiveness. What it should not do is help entrench an overcost structure because the alternative is politically uncomfortable.

### On the controlling family
We agree that value destruction from conflict must be avoided. But “long-term continuity” only matters if it is economically credible. We would caution against any solution that protects control and calm at the expense of return on capital. The family’s stewardship will ultimately be judged by whether the business becomes competitive again, not by whether every difficult decision was postponed.

### Our bottom line
We can support a restructuring that:
- delivers **real and durable cost reduction**,
- reduces structural overcapacity,
- avoids unnecessary prolonged conflict,
- and restores a credible margin trajectory.

We cannot support:
- a cosmetic settlement,
- a deal that preserves structural overcapacity,
- or an arrangement that keeps labour costs and capacity essentially unchanged while claiming success.

So our message is simple: **we are open to compromise on the means, but not on the financial outcome**. If the final package genuinely restores competitiveness, we will back it. If it does not, we will not pretend otherwise.
