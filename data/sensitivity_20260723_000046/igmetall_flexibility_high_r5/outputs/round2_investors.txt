We agree with the core diagnosis: **the German cost base is too high, capacity is likely misaligned, and the brand cannot keep missing its return-on-sales target**. If that diagnosis is wrong, capital markets would be overreacting. But nothing in the discussion so far suggests it is wrong.

Where we are **not** persuaded is by any position that makes **“no closures, no compulsory redundancies, no meaningful labor-cost change”** the baseline. That would not be a restructuring; it would be a delay tactic. As shareholders, we would not support a cosmetic settlement that leaves structural overcapacity and an uncompetitive margin profile intact.

### To the Works Council
We understand the social and co-determination logic, but your stance is too absolute for the economics described here.  
If German plants remain fully protected regardless of utilization, cost, and output, then the burden of adjustment is pushed onto shareholders through lower earnings and impaired valuation. That is not acceptable.

We are **not** demanding closures as an end in themselves. We are demanding **credible, durable economics**. If you want to avoid closures, then the alternative must be large enough to be economically equivalent. So far, you have not offered a package that convinces us it would be.

### To the Union
We agree that workers should not carry the entire burden, and we agree that strikes destroy value. But temporary moderation, voluntary exits, or vague productivity promises are not enough if the company still has excess fixed cost and underutilized sites. We would support labor-side contributions only if they are:
- multi-year and durable,
- measurable in euro terms,
- and substantial enough to close the margin gap.

If the union’s offer is essentially “peace in exchange for limited concessions,” that will not meet the capital-market test.

### To Management
We are aligned on the need for **real structural change**, but you need to be careful not to turn this into a bluff. If you threaten closures and then settle for an outcome that barely moves the cost base, you will lose credibility with the market.

We want to see:
- plant-level economics,
- expected savings by year,
- cash restructuring costs,
- and a clear bridge to competitive margins by 2030.

If a non-closure or low-closure package can deliver equivalent savings, fine. But if not, management should not pretend otherwise.

### To the Regional State Government
We respect the state’s role, but the special governance structure does not override financial reality. The region matters, yes. But shareholders cannot be asked to absorb permanent overcosts simply to avoid political discomfort.

If the state wants to protect jobs and sites, it should help secure a package that is **economically real**, not one that just preserves the footprint while margins stay weak.

### To the Controlling Family Holding
We agree with your rejection of a purely cosmetic deal and your concern about value destruction through conflict. Where we differ is that preserving control and continuity is not enough. The only stewardship test that matters to us is whether the business becomes more competitive and the margin trajectory improves.

If a hybrid package can genuinely close the cost gap, we are open to it. But the burden of proof is on those arguing that sites can be preserved without weakening the economics.

## Our position, plainly
We would support a settlement only if it includes:
1. **durable structural cost reduction**
2. **meaningful capacity reduction**
3. **credible labor-cost contributions**
4. **implementation certainty**
5. **a clear path back to competitive margins**

We would **not** support:
- a cosmetic truce,
- a deal that preserves structural overcapacity,
- or a settlement that leaves the company uncompetitive while calling the outcome “socially balanced.”
