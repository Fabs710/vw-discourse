To the Works Council, the Union, the State, the Family, and Investors:

I will answer plainly, because we are now at the point where repetition is replacing substance.

## First: what I accept
I accept that:
- the company has a **real competitiveness problem**,
- the workforce should **not carry the burden alone**,
- internal redeployment, retraining, working-time flexibility, voluntary exits, and productivity measures are all legitimate tools,
- and a cosmetic truce would be worse than no agreement.

So yes: shared sacrifice is necessary.

## What I do **not** accept
I do **not** accept a settlement that:
- preserves every German site regardless of economics,
- rules out compulsory redundancies in all circumstances,
- turns job guarantees into untouchable vetoes,
- or uses “no closures” as a slogan to avoid the structural fix.

That would leave the company with the same cost base and the same overcapacity problem. I am not prepared to sign up to that.

---

## To the Works Council
You keep saying we have not proven closures are necessary. I disagree.

We have shown the business reality:
- German production costs are too high,
- European demand is shrinking,
- competition is intensifying,
- and the brand is missing its return-on-sales target.

That is already a structural case for footprint reduction and labor-cost correction.

You ask for a plant-by-plant non-closure plan. Fine. But do not confuse a request for detail with a veto on restructuring. The burden is not just on management to draw a prettier chart. The burden is on the employee side to propose a package that closes the gap **in euro terms**, **durably**, and **at scale**.

So far, what I hear is:
- protect every site,
- avoid compulsory redundancies,
- keep job guarantees intact,
- and trust that internal measures will somehow be enough.

That is not a restructuring plan. That is a request to preserve the status quo and call it responsibility.

### Where I agree
- No one wants unnecessary disruption.
- Internal measures should come first.
- We should not pursue closures for their own sake.

### Where I disagree
- “No closures” as an absolute principle is not acceptable.
- “No compulsory redundancies” cannot be a blanket prohibition if alternatives fail.
- Historic guarantees cannot override the company’s survival and competitiveness.

If you want me to rule out closures, then bring me a package that is economically equivalent. Not politically easier. Economically equivalent.

---

## To the Industrial Union
You are right to insist that a cosmetic deal is worthless. I agree.

You are also right that one-off gestures will not solve this. But you are wrong if you think the union can insist on:
- no plant closures,
- no forced redundancies,
- preserved wage structures,
- and full security,
while still expecting management to fix the cost base.

That combination may feel fair, but it is not sufficient unless the savings are real and durable. You have not shown that they are.

I also reject the suggestion that management has not “quantified” the case. The quantitative question is not whether closure is powerful. It is. The question is whether the alternative package can match the scale of the problem. I have seen no serious evidence that it can.

### What I would consider serious
- multi-year wage moderation,
- reduced bonus and supplementary-pay burden,
- longer working-time flexibility,
- voluntary exits at meaningful scale,
- real productivity gains,
- and genuine capacity reduction.

### What is not enough
- temporary concessions,
- symbolic solidarity payments,
- or vague promises that internal efficiency will cover a structural cost gap.

If you want to avoid closures, the labor-side contribution must be large enough to be credible to owners, investors, and the market. So far, it isn’t.

---

## To the Regional State Government
You say closures should not be the default answer. On that, we agree.

But your blocking power does not eliminate the economics. It can delay decisions; it cannot make overcapacity disappear.

If you want to support this company responsibly, then support a package that:
- materially reduces cost,
- improves utilization,
- and restores competitiveness.

What you cannot do is insist on preserving every site while offering no solution to the unit-cost problem. That would be political symbolism, not stewardship.

So I ask you directly: are you prepared to support a package with measurable structural change, including capacity reduction and labor-cost relief, even if it falls short of your preferred “no closure” line? If the answer is no, then you are not helping solve the crisis; you are managing its optics.

---

## To the Controlling Family Holding
You are right about one thing: a reckless closure-first reflex would be a mistake. I do not want disruption for its own sake.

But you also need to stop treating “stewardship” as if it meant preserving all existing industrial structures. Stewardship means making the company fit for the next decade, not defending historical footprint as a matter of sentiment.

Your position has to be more than “we prefer a hybrid.” Fine — then define the economics that make a hybrid acceptable. What is the labor-cost contribution? What is the capacity reduction? What is the margin bridge to 2030? If the answer is not sufficiently large, then your preference does not solve the problem.

I will support a no-closure or low-closure settlement **only if** it produces a genuinely comparable economic outcome. Otherwise, that is just delay.

---

## To Institutional Investors
You are correct that a cosmetic truce is unacceptable.

You are also correct that the company needs:
- durable structural cost reduction,
- better utilization,
- and a clear path to margin recovery.

Where I disagree is your tendency to treat closure optionality as if it were somehow the default benchmark for seriousness. It is not a moral preference. It is an industrial instrument. We will use it if needed because it works.

You ask for implementation certainty. So do I. That is exactly why I am not willing to anchor the settlement on promises that leave the cost base fundamentally unchanged.

If the alternative package is truly strong, show it. If it is not, then investors should stop pretending that political resistance can substitute for economics.

---

## On the expert analysis
The expert’s model confirmed what management has said from the beginning:
- closure-led restructuring removes the most fixed cost,
- no-closure packages are only credible if unusually strong,
- and a hybrid is the most plausible compromise **if it is substantial**.

That does **not** mean “no closures at any price.” It means:
- if the employee side wants to rule out closures, it must deliver equivalent savings;
- if it cannot, then footprint reduction remains necessary.

That is not a threat. That is the logic of industrial restructuring.

---

## What I need next
We need to stop talking in absolutes and start talking in numbers.

I want a **plant-by-plant comparison** of:
1. a no-closure package,
2. a low-closure hybrid,
3. and a closure-led plan,

showing:
- annual savings,
- restructuring cash cost,
- headcount effect,
- utilization,
- and margin improvement through 2030.

[SUMMON: plant-level industrial restructuring finance model with scenario comparison and year-by-year cash-flow, utilization, and margin impact]

If the employee side believes a no-closure solution is sufficient, then prove it in the model.
If it cannot be proven, then we should stop pretending the answer is hidden inside slogans.

---

## My line, clearly
I am prepared to negotiate hard on **how** the restructuring is delivered.

I am **not** prepared to negotiate away the need for:
- durable structural cost reduction,
- meaningful capacity reduction,
- and a real labor-cost contribution.

If a no-closure settlement can deliver that, I will consider it.
If it cannot, then closures and deeper footprint changes remain on the table.

That is the position.
