I will respond directly.

## Where I agree

I agree with management, the expert, and the institutional investors on the core diagnosis: **this company has a real structural competitiveness problem**. If German operations remain overcosted and underutilized, then long-term value erodes for everyone — employees, the region, and the family owners.

I also agree with the union, works council, and state government on one crucial point: **a prolonged industrial conflict would be value-destructive**. A strike, reputational damage, execution risk, and supplier disruption would not restore competitiveness. They would weaken the business further.

And I agree that **one-sided burden shifting onto employees is not a durable solution**. If the company wants meaningful labor contributions, they must be part of a broader package that also includes management discipline and owner responsibility.

## Where I disagree

I do **not** accept the idea that German plants are automatically off-limits, or that the current footprint can be preserved regardless of economics.

The works council’s position — no closures, no compulsory redundancies, no meaningful change to guarantees — is understandable politically, but it is not sufficient to solve the problem. If the result is merely to freeze the current structure, then we are not restructuring; we are postponing decline.

I also do **not** accept a closure-first reflex. Closing plants is not a value in itself. It is only justified if it is necessary to restore a viable cost base and cannot be matched by other measures.

So I reject both extremes:
- **not** a frozen status quo,
- **not** a reckless closure agenda that destroys trust and industrial peace.

## My position

The family holding wants a settlement that does three things at once:

1. **Restores competitiveness and margins**
2. **Preserves as much industrial peace as possible**
3. **Protects the long-term value and control of the core asset**

That means I will support a package only if it delivers **real, durable structural improvement**. Temporary deferrals, symbolic concessions, or political declarations will not be enough.

## On the expert’s analysis

The expert is right that plant closures are a powerful tool for removing fixed cost and reducing overcapacity. That matters.

But the analysis also shows that a **hybrid solution** can work if it is large and durable enough. That is the route I prefer if it genuinely closes the cost gap without triggering a destructive conflict.

So my question is not “closures or no closures” in the abstract. My question is:

**Can the employee side and management together produce a package that is economically equivalent to meaningful footprint rationalization?**

If yes, I am open to that.
If no, then closures cannot be ruled out.

## To the Works Council

You say no closures, no compulsory redundancies, no changes to guarantees. I respect the intent, but I must be blunt: **that is not a complete answer**.

If you want the family holding to back a no-closure settlement, then it must still produce:
- substantial cost relief,
- actual capacity reduction,
- measurable productivity gains,
- and durable flexibility in labor cost and working time.

If it cannot do that, then it does not solve the underlying problem. And I will not support a deal that leaves the company structurally weak just to avoid hard decisions.

## To the Industrial Union

I agree that the burden cannot sit only with workers. But employee contributions must be **material**, not symbolic.

I would view as serious:
- multi-year working-time flexibility,
- moderation of bonus and supplementary payments,
- voluntary departure instruments with meaningful take-up,
- productivity commitments,
- and binding internal redeployment rules.

What would not be enough:
- a one-off contribution,
- temporary language about shared sacrifice,
- or vague promises of future efficiency.

If you want closures off the table, the savings must be real enough to convince any reasonable owner that the economics have changed.

## To Management

I support the principle that the company needs a durable structural reset. But I do **not** want management to use threat language carelessly and then settle for a cosmetic deal.

If management cannot quantify:
- plant-level fixed cost,
- capacity utilization,
- labor-cost impact,
- and margin improvement through 2030,

then it is not ready to ask the workforce or owners for serious concessions.

You need to show:
1. what the gap is,
2. what each measure delivers,
3. and how the final package restores the brand’s earnings power.

Without that, you will not get durable support from governance.

## To the State Government

I respect the state’s role and its concern for the region. But I cannot accept a position that effectively gives an unconditional veto to any industrial footprint change.

The company’s future cannot be governed by political comfort alone. If the economics require meaningful adjustment, then the state must help shape a viable compromise — not freeze the structure indefinitely.

That said, I am open to a settlement that protects sites **if** the economic equivalent is real and enforceable.

## What would move me

A credible no-closure or low-closure package would need to demonstrate:

- **durable** cost reduction, not temporary deferral,
- **measurable** capacity adjustment,
- **material** labor-cost relief,
- **clear** productivity commitments,
- **credible** implementation milestones,
- and a path to competitive margins by 2030.

If those conditions are met, then I would prefer that to a closure-heavy solution.

If they are not met, then I will not pretend otherwise: the company cannot afford to keep everything as it is.

## My bottom line

The family holding’s position is:

- **Yes** to a serious restructuring.
- **Yes** to negotiated alternatives if they are economically equivalent.
- **Yes** to burden-sharing across all sides.
- **No** to cosmetic deals.
- **No** to prolonged conflict.
- **No** to preserving an uncompetitive structure simply because it is politically easier.

I am prepared to support a durable compromise — but only if it actually restores competitiveness and protects long-term value. If the final package does not do that, then it fails the stewardship test.
