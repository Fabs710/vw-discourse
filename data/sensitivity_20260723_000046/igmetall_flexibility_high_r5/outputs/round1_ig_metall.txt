I agree with the core line of the Works Council: **Germany’s plants cannot be treated as disposable, and compulsory redundancies are not an acceptable starting point**.

Management’s framing is too one-sided. It is asking the workforce to absorb the entire adjustment burden while ownership, capital, and management decision-making are largely protected. That is not a fair restructuring; it is a transfer of crisis costs onto employees.

Our position is clear:

- **No plant closures**
- **No compulsory redundancies**
- **No dismantling of the collective wage framework**
- **No forced breaking of long-standing job-security commitments**

That said, we are not denying the economic reality. We have already signaled willingness to discuss **shared-sacrifice measures** if they genuinely protect jobs and sites. That can include:
- temporary working-time reductions,
- capacity adjustments,
- internal redeployment,
- qualification and retraining,
- moderation on bonuses or other employee-side financial contributions,
- productivity measures agreed in collective bargaining.

But this only makes sense if the burden is **shared fairly**. If management wants contribution from employees, then management, the owners, and the capital side must also contribute visibly. That means no narrative of “workforce pays, everyone else waits.”

So I want to be explicit: **we are open to a durable settlement, but not to a restructuring that relies on closures, mass layoffs, or the erosion of wage standards.** If that remains the direction, then you should expect resistance from the union side, including industrial action.

If management is serious about a future for this company, it needs to come back with:
1. a credible alternative to closures,
2. binding job and site security,
3. real owner and management contributions,
4. and a negotiated package that preserves the collective-bargaining framework.

Without that, there is no basis for consent.
