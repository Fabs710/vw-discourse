## To management
I agree with the core diagnosis: the company has a real competitiveness problem, and we cannot solve it with a cosmetic pause. If German operations remain overcosted and structurally underutilized, long-term value erodes.

Where I disagree is your implied conclusion that **closures and compulsory redundancies must stay as ordinary tools from the outset**. That is too blunt, and it risks destroying value through conflict, disruption, and reputational damage. As a controlling owner, I will not back a path that weakens the enterprise in order to prove toughness.

What I need from management is not rhetoric about “hard decisions,” but a **credible quantified bridge to 2030**:
- plant-by-plant economics,
- utilization by site,
- fixed-cost reduction by measure,
- labor-cost impact by year,
- and the cash cost of each scenario.

Without that, “closures are available” is just a threat posture, not a strategy.

## To the Works Council
I understand your refusal of plant closures and forced redundancies. I also agree that trust, skills, co-determination, and industrial legitimacy matter.

Where I disagree is your absolute framing: **“no closures, no compulsory redundancies, no changes.”** That does not answer the cost problem. If the result is merely to preserve the current footprint and keep the cost base structurally too high, then you are asking shareholders and management to finance drift.

So I will not support a frozen structure. But I also will not support a closure-led reflex. Your side needs to show a package that is not symbolic: real savings, real flexibility, real capacity adjustment, and durable effect.

## To the Industrial Union
I agree that employees should not carry the burden alone, and I agree that a destructive conflict would be value-destructive.

But I do **not** accept the union’s insistence that closures must be ruled out categorically while asking management to give up its only real leverage. If the workforce wants site protection, then the alternative must be economically equivalent, not just politically preferable.

That means:
- multi-year wage moderation,
- reduced bonuses/supplementary pay,
- working-time flexibility,
- voluntary exits at meaningful scale,
- productivity gains that stick,
- and genuine capacity reduction.

Temporary concessions are not enough. The package must materially close the cost gap.

## To the Regional State Government
I respect your role and your concern for the region. I also agree that a reckless demolition of the industrial base would be unacceptable.

Where I disagree is your apparent position that the state can insist on preservation without accepting the economic consequences. It cannot. Political blocking power does not eliminate industrial reality.

You are right that closures are not the default answer. But you are not entitled to freeze the current structure if it is uncompetitive. If you want my support, you need to support a package that is economically real, not just socially comforting.

## To the Institutional Investors
I agree with your economics-first logic. The brand cannot miss its return-on-sales target indefinitely, and a cosmetic settlement would be unacceptable.

Where I disagree is any assumption that closure-led restructuring is automatically the superior equity outcome. It may remove more fixed cost on paper, but if it triggers governance blockage, industrial action, and execution failure, the risk-adjusted outcome can be worse than a disciplined hybrid.

So I am aligned with you on the goal:
- durable structural cost reduction,
- meaningful capacity adjustment,
- and credible margin recovery.

But I am not aligned with treating closure as the benchmark by which seriousness is measured.

## On the expert assessment
The expert’s analysis was useful and, frankly, confirms the key point: **a pure no-closure labor-concession package is likely too weak if the overcapacity is real**, while a pure closure-led plan carries the highest disruption and implementation risk.

That points to a hybrid — but only if it is **large enough** to matter. I do not mean a soft compromise. I mean a package with measurable economic effect.

What I cannot accept is a “hybrid” that simply postpones the hard choice and keeps every side dissatisfied.

## My position, clearly
I will support a restructuring only if it delivers all of the following:

1. **Durable structural cost reduction**
2. **Meaningful capacity adjustment**
3. **Real labor-cost contribution**
4. **No value-destructive instability**
5. **A credible path to improved margins and competitiveness by 2030**

If the employee side can achieve that without closures, that is preferable.

If it cannot, then footprint reduction remains on the table — not because I want disruption, but because I will not endorse permanent underperformance.

## What would move me
A serious no-closure or low-closure package would need to show:
- quantified savings by year,
- actual reduction in excess capacity,
- productivity gains that are enforceable,
- labor-cost relief that is substantial and durable,
- and a clear bridge to competitive returns.

If those numbers do not exist, then the discussion is not about stewardship; it is about delaying the inevitable at the expense of the company.
