Here is the restructuring answer in industrial finance and operations terms.

## Bottom line

The key question is not whether the company can make *some* savings without closures. It is whether a non-closure package can produce **durable, quantified, and credible structural improvement** of roughly the same magnitude as a closure-based plan.

In a large automotive manufacturing system, that is usually difficult because:
- **plant closures remove fixed cost at scale**, especially depreciation overhead, maintenance, logistics, indirect labor, and management complexity;
- **labor-cost measures alone are often not enough** if the company has both **capacity excess** and a structurally high German cost base;
- **capacity utilization matters as much as headline headcount**: if plants run materially below economic utilization, the margin drag persists even if wages are moderated.

So the finance question is:  
**Can the company close its cost gap and lift return on sales by 2030 without shutting plants?**  
That depends on the size of the gap.

---

## 1) What a closure scenario usually changes

A plant closure typically does four things:

1. **Reduces fixed manufacturing overhead**
   - plant maintenance
   - utilities and site services
   - local indirect labor
   - depreciation on underused assets
   - production planning and logistics complexity

2. **Cuts capacity**
   - this may improve utilization at the remaining plants
   - higher utilization usually improves unit cost, assuming demand is sufficient

3. **Forces workforce reduction**
   - direct labor, indirect labor, and management layers fall
   - some cost is delayed by severance, redeployment, and social plan expense

4. **Can improve medium-term margin**
   - if volumes are consolidated on fewer sites, the cost per vehicle can fall meaningfully

### But closures are not “free savings”
Near-term costs can be large:
- restructuring charges
- severance
- decommissioning
- write-downs or impairments
- transfer costs and supplier requalification
- temporary disruption to output

So the cash benefit is usually **later than the accounting benefit**.

---

## 2) What a non-closure package can realistically do

A non-closure deal can still produce real savings through:

- working-time flexibility
- lower bonus or supplementary payments
- wage moderation
- voluntary exits
- retirement bridge schemes
- redeployment and attrition
- process productivity improvements
- reduced overhead and management layers
- capacity throttling without closure

### The limitation
These measures often reduce **labor cost**, but they do **less** to eliminate:
- site fixed costs
- duplicated infrastructure
- logistics inefficiency
- underutilized production lines
- complexity in scheduling and industrial footprint

So non-closure packages can be substantial, but they generally need to be **very large and very disciplined** to match the economics of a closure program.

---

## 3) The central metric: utilization

For automotive manufacturing, the decisive variable is often **capacity utilization**.

If a plant or network is running below an economic threshold, the company pays for:
- buildings
- equipment
- utilities
- planning
- quality assurance
- maintenance
- supervisory layers

even when output is too low to absorb those costs.

### In practice
- A network can be operationally viable at moderate underutilization for a period.
- But if several German plants are structurally underfilled, then the per-unit cost remains too high.
- That makes return on sales hard to defend, especially in a weak European market and under pricing pressure from lower-cost competitors.

So:
- **closure scenarios** usually improve utilization at remaining sites;
- **non-closure scenarios** must compensate through labor flexibility and productivity gains, or the cost gap remains.

---

## 4) Labor-cost impact: what matters and what does not

The employee-side measures that matter financially are the ones that are:
- large enough in euros,
- durable through 2030,
- and not easily reversed.

### More meaningful
- wage moderation over several years
- reduction in supplementary allowances
- lower bonus formulas
- longer working time without full pay compensation
- structural changes to shift patterns
- permanent productivity gains per hour worked

### Less meaningful on their own
- one-off employee contributions
- temporary freezes
- symbolic concessions
- short-term deferrals that just move cost into later years

### Important point
Even a significant labor concession may still be insufficient if:
- the company is carrying too much capacity,
- fixed manufacturing overhead is high,
- and demand remains weak.

In that case, you can improve the cost line but not enough to fully restore competitiveness.

---

## 5) Return on sales through 2030

To lift return on sales, the company needs one or more of the following:
- higher volumes
- better pricing
- lower material cost
- lower manufacturing cost
- lower overhead
- better mix
- less underutilized capacity

In the scenario described, the first two are uncertain:
- European demand is shrinking
- competition is intense
- pricing power is limited

That means the company is likely dependent on **cost reduction** rather than revenue expansion alone.

### Therefore
A return-on-sales recovery by 2030 probably requires:
- durable lower unit labor cost
- fewer fixed-cost sites or meaningfully lower site overhead
- a cleaner capacity structure
- and no major implementation disruption

If a non-closure deal does not materially change the cost base, then the margin improvement may be too small.

---

## 6) Comparing the scenarios

### A. Closure-based restructuring
**Pros**
- largest fixed-cost reduction potential
- clearer capacity alignment
- strongest chance to reset unit cost
- more credible for investors if executed well

**Cons**
- high upfront cash cost
- industrial conflict risk
- legal and co-determination hurdles
- disruption to production and supplier base
- execution risk can erode savings

### B. No-closure restructuring
**Pros**
- lower immediate disruption
- better labor relations if agreed
- preserves site continuity and local supplier ecosystems
- avoids some severance and shutdown cost

**Cons**
- savings may be smaller and slower
- may not remove enough structural overhead
- can preserve excess capacity
- risk of creating a “temporary peace” without solving the economics

### C. Hybrid deal
This is often the most realistic option:
- no immediate closure
- targeted capacity reduction
- site specialization
- workforce reductions via voluntary means
- labor-cost contribution
- productivity commitments
- review gates with pre-agreed triggers

This can work **if** the numbers are large enough and enforceable.

---

## 7) Key implementation risks

Even if the economics look good on paper, execution matters.

### Main risks
1. **Delayed realization**
   - savings arrive later than promised

2. **Leakage**
   - voluntary exits do not reduce the right skills
   - internal redeployment just moves cost around

3. **Production disruption**
   - strikes, work-to-rule, or transition problems reduce output

4. **Supplier effects**
   - supplier volumes fall unevenly
   - tooling and logistics costs rise during transition

5. **Residual overhead**
   - sites remain open but underfilled, so fixed cost persists

6. **Governance deadlock**
   - blocking positions prevent full execution
   - compromise becomes too diluted to matter financially

---

## 8) My assessment of the likely economics

Based on the facts given, I would assess as follows:

- Management’s claim of **structural overcapacity and above-target German costs** is financially plausible.
- A **non-closure package** can contribute meaningful savings, but it usually needs to be very large to offset the absence of plant rationalization.
- If the company truly has multiple underutilized German plants, then **keeping all sites open while preserving near-full guarantees is unlikely to deliver full competitiveness restoration**.
- A **hybrid restructuring** is the most operationally credible path if closures are politically blocked:
  - no immediate closure,
  - but real capacity reduction,
  - labor-cost moderation,
  - voluntary workforce reduction,
  - and hard productivity commitments.

### In plain terms
If the workforce side can offer enough cost relief and capacity adjustment to materially narrow the unit-cost gap, then closures may not be necessary.  
If it cannot, then closures or equivalent footprint reduction are usually the only way to remove enough fixed cost.

---

## 9) What would need to be quantified to decide properly

To compare closure vs. non-closure rigorously, management should model:

- plant-level fixed and variable cost
- current and projected utilization
- direct labor per vehicle
- indirect labor and overhead by site
- severance and restructuring charges
- capex required to keep sites competitive
- logistics and supplier transfer costs
- expected savings by year through 2030
- margin impact by vehicle program
- cash flow timing, not just accounting savings

Without that, the dispute remains political.

---

## Conclusion

From a restructuring finance and operations perspective:

- **Closure scenarios** are generally the strongest tool for removing structural overcapacity and fixed cost.
- **Non-closure scenarios** can work only if they deliver large, durable savings through labor flexibility, productivity, and actual capacity reduction.
- If the cost gap is as large as management suggests, then **a purely no-closure settlement is unlikely to be sufficient unless it includes unusually strong employee contributions and genuine footprint rationalization by other means**.
- The decisive issue is not principle; it is whether the package closes the cost gap and lifts utilization enough to support a sustainable return on sales by 2030.
