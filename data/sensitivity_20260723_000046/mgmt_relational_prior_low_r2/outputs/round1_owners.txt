I appreciate the clarity from all sides. Let me be equally direct from the ownership perspective.

We are not here to preserve sentiment; we are here to preserve the long-term value and viability of a core industrial asset. That means I do **not** take a posture of automatic defense of the status quo. If the company’s German footprint is structurally uncompetitive, then pretending otherwise would be irresponsible.

Where I agree with management:
- The company needs a **durable** competitiveness reset.
- Temporary fixes alone are not enough.
- Cost and capacity problems cannot be ignored.
- A serious labor-cost contribution and productivity improvement are part of the answer.

Where I agree with labour and the state:
- A reckless confrontation would destroy value.
- Labour peace matters.
- The company cannot be restructured in a way that permanently damages its industrial base or its reputation.
- Any solution has to be negotiated and credible, not imposed blindly.

Where I disagree with the workforce-side red lines:
- **“Every German plant must remain open” is not a decision principle I can endorse.**
- **A blanket “no compulsory redundancies” position is not workable if the economics require structural adjustment.**
- **Binding guarantees through 2030 regardless of market and technology conditions are too rigid if they prevent necessary adaptation.**

At the same time, where I disagree with management:
- A restructuring that treats closures as the default answer is not acceptable as a first move.
- If alternative measures can deliver equivalent and durable savings, they should be exhausted before crossing the plant-closure line.
- A package that simply shifts the burden onto employees while preserving too much legacy cost would be value-destructive and unsustainable.

So my position is this:

1. **I support a restructuring that materially restores margins and competitiveness.**
2. **I am open to alternatives to plant closures only if they deliver equivalent structural savings with real certainty.**
3. **I will not support a “half-measure” settlement that preserves capacity, preserves cost, and merely buys time.**
4. **I will not support a solution that permanently impairs governance control or the company’s ability to act in the future.**

To the works council and union: if you want ownership support for avoiding closures, then you need to come forward with a package that is not just defensive, but economically serious. That means measurable labor-cost relief, flexibility on working time and deployment, and productivity commitments that actually close the gap.

To management: if you want support for deep structural change, you need to prove that you have examined all credible alternatives before reaching for closures. You also need to show that the burden is being shared in a way that is socially sustainable and does not trigger a prolonged conflict that would erode value faster than any restructuring gain.

To the state: I respect the anchor-shareholder and regional responsibility role, but that does not mean the company can be held in a permanent bind. Regional interests matter, but so does the long-term competitiveness of the enterprise as a whole.

In short:  
**I support decisive restructuring, but not value-destructive brinkmanship.**  
**I support negotiated solutions, but not cosmetic ones.**  
**I support flexibility on the method, but not on the requirement that the result be real.**

If there is a path that delivers durable savings, restores margins, and preserves stable labour relations without weakening the company’s future, I am prepared to back it. If not, then we should not pretend that a weak compromise is better than a hard decision.
