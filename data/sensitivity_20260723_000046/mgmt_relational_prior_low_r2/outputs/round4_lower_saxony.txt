I will be very direct.

## To management
I agree with you on the diagnosis: this company has a serious structural competitiveness problem, and temporary fixes alone will not solve it.

But I do **not** accept the way you keep turning that diagnosis into a default case for **German plant closures** and a broad erosion of job security. That is too blunt an instrument for a company that is not just a balance-sheet asset but a regional industrial anchor.

You still have not shown that you have exhausted the other levers in a credible, quantified way:
- management restraint,
- overhead reduction,
- procurement savings,
- product and investment discipline,
- working-time flexibility,
- voluntary exits,
- redeployment,
- and productivity gains.

Until I see that full package, I am not prepared to treat closures as necessary.

## To the works council
I agree with you on the core political and social point: a plant closure is not just another restructuring measure. It has irreversible consequences for jobs, apprenticeships, suppliers, and the regional economy.

I also agree that no one should force the workforce into a false choice between “accept closures” or “be blamed for refusing reality.”

Where I need to be honest, though, is this: your line that **all German plants remain open through 2030** is only defensible if there is a **real**, not symbolic, alternative package behind it. Simply rejecting closures is not enough.

So I support your direction, but only if you are prepared to engage seriously on:
- quantified employee contributions,
- voluntary exits,
- reallocation of work inside the group,
- and productivity improvements that actually move the numbers.

What I will **not** support is a disguised closure process, whether through hollowing-out, staged attrition, or functionally identical capacity destruction.

## To the union
I appreciate that you are not denying the structural problem. That matters.

But I also need to say: if the employee contribution is to be meaningful, it cannot be purely cosmetic and it cannot be one-sided. A limited, time-bound contribution can be part of a fair deal — but only if management and owners also contribute visibly and the state’s governance role is respected.

I agree with you that a permanent wage reset or a hidden dismantling of the collective framework is not acceptable.

I do **not** agree that labour can be asked only for marginal sacrifices while everyone else keeps all strategic options open. That would not be social partnership; that would be burden shifting.

## To the controlling family
I agree with you on one point: a weak compromise would destroy value.

But I do **not** accept your conclusion that the only serious route is to keep closures as a live pressure point until labour yields enough. That is a high-risk strategy in a company of this importance. It may look decisive on paper; in practice it can trigger strikes, political deadlock, and long-term damage to the company’s legitimacy.

You say you are open to non-closure solutions only if they are near-equivalent in effect. Fine. Then the question is not whether closures are “cleaner,” but whether a negotiated alternative can preserve the company’s industrial base **and** restore competitiveness.

That is the standard I want applied — not a reflexive drift toward shutting sites.

## To institutional investors
You are right that the company cannot afford a cosmetic settlement.

But I would caution you against over-interpreting “near-equivalent economics” as meaning closures are automatically the benchmark and everything else must imitate them. That is too narrow.

A durable solution can also come from:
- keeping sites open,
- reducing capacity in a smarter way,
- using time-limited labour measures,
- and binding investment/product commitments that support future margins.

The issue is not whether the package looks elegant to capital markets. The issue is whether it preserves value over the long term without detonating the regional industrial base.

## My position
Here is the state’s position plainly:

- **No plant closures in the state as a starting point**
- **No compulsory redundancies as the default route**
- **No weakening of co-determination or the special governance role of the state**
- **Yes to a serious negotiated restructuring**
- **Yes to real, measurable contributions from all sides**
- **Yes to a package that restores competitiveness without hollowing out the region**

That is not a refusal of reality. It is a refusal to accept that the only realistic path is to sacrifice jobs and sites first.

## What would move me
I remain open to a negotiated package if it includes:
1. quantified savings by source,
2. meaningful management restraint,
3. real overhead and procurement cuts,
4. a limited and time-bound labour contribution,
5. voluntary exits and redeployment,
6. and a binding investment and product roadmap for German sites.

If that package genuinely closes the gap without closures, I will support it.

If it does not, then I will not pretend that closures are politically or economically the only option. I will continue to resist that framing.

## Bottom line
I am not here to defend every legacy structure.

I am here to protect the company as a long-term industrial anchor in this region, and that means:
- protecting jobs where possible,
- protecting sites where possible,
- preserving co-determination,
- and only accepting the hardest measures if there is no credible alternative.

So far, I do **not** see a compelling reason to move to closures.  
I do see a strong need for a real, quantified, negotiated alternative.
