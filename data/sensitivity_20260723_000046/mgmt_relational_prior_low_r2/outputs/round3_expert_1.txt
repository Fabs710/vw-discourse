From a plant-network economics perspective, the key question is whether a **no-closure package can realistically match closure-level savings**. In most large auto restructurings, the answer is:

**Partly yes on cost, rarely yes on full economics, unless the “non-closure” package includes deep and durable capacity removal.**

## Core economics

### What closures usually save
A plant closure removes, more or less permanently:
- direct labor cost tied to that site,
- local fixed overhead,
- maintenance and utilities,
- plant-level logistics,
- depreciation/underutilization drag,
- and often some complexity cost in the network.

It does **not** automatically remove:
- product-development cost,
- group HQ cost,
- all procurement cost,
- all pension/legacy obligations,
- or all transition costs.

### What non-closure measures can save
Alternatives like:
- wage moderation,
- working-time cuts,
- voluntary exits,
- redeployment,
- productivity measures,
- and temporary capacity cuts

can reduce:
- direct labor cost,
- some overtime and shift premiums,
- some variable conversion cost,
- and some near-term cash outflow.

But they usually **do not fully eliminate structural fixed costs** unless the footprint is actually reduced in practice.

## Order-of-magnitude comparison

For a large German auto plant, the annual recurring savings from closure-level action are often dominated by:
- labor and overhead at the site,
- plus complexity reductions across the network.

A serious no-closure package can typically recover:

- **20–40%** of closure-level savings from labor-cost and working-time measures alone,
- **40–70%** if it also includes substantial voluntary exits, redeployment, and productivity redesign,
- **70–90%** only if it effectively removes enough capacity that the remaining network no longer carries the same fixed-cost burden.

That last case is where “no closure” starts to become economically similar to closure in all but name.

## Time horizon

### Fast savings
Working-time cuts, wage moderation, and freeze measures can start within:
- **0–12 months**

### Medium-term savings
Voluntary exits, redeployment, and process redesign usually take:
- **12–36 months**

### Structural savings
True fixed-cost reduction from footprint change is often:
- **24–60 months**
- and sometimes longer if labor protections, product reallocation, or political constraints slow implementation.

So if the decision horizon is **through 2030**, a no-closure package can still be credible **only if savings start immediately and are locked in contractually**. If it relies mainly on future productivity promises, it is usually too weak.

## Transition costs matter

Closure-level savings are not “free.” They require:
- severance,
- relocation,
- decommissioning,
- transfer of production,
- tooling duplication,
- disruption risk,
- and possible strike losses.

A no-closure package usually has **lower one-time transition cost**, but often **higher residual fixed cost**. So the comparison is not:
- “closure saves more, therefore better”  
but:
- “does the alternative reduce recurring cost enough to justify keeping the network intact?”

## Practical conclusion

### A no-closure package is credible if it includes:
- a **large** labor-cost contribution,
- **permanent** capacity reduction somewhere in the network,
- meaningful overhead simplification,
- enforceable implementation,
- and a clear margin-improvement path by 2027–2030.

### It is not credible if it relies on:
- voluntary exits only,
- temporary wage restraint,
- soft productivity promises,
- and unchanged plant structure.

That kind of package can buy time, but it usually does **not** match closure economics.

## My estimate
If the dispute is over whether non-closure measures can **fully replace** closures, my estimate is:

- **Yes, but only under aggressive assumptions**:
  - deep labor concessions,
  - real capacity reduction,
  - and strict enforcement.
- Otherwise, **no**:
  - the package will likely fall short by a material margin, often **30–60% of closure-equivalent recurring savings**.
