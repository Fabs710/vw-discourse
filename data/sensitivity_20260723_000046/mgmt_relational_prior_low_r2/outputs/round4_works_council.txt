## To management
You are still trying to make the workforce accept a false choice:
- either we accept plant closures and compulsory redundancies,
- or we are accused of refusing reality.

I reject that framing completely.

Yes, the company has a structural cost problem.  
No, that does **not** automatically justify closing German plants or breaking the job-security framework.

Your argument keeps collapsing into: “closures are cleaner, therefore they are necessary.” That is not a business case. That is a preference for the easiest instrument from a management perspective.

If you want labour cooperation, then bring the full breakdown:
- what savings come from management restraint,
- what savings come from procurement,
- what savings come from overhead and capital discipline,
- what savings come from productivity and working-time measures,
- and what shortfall remains **after** all of that.

Until then, you are asking the workforce to accept irreversible damage before you have exhausted fair alternatives.

## To the controlling family and investors
You keep repeating “near-equivalent to closures” as if that settles the matter. It does not.

If the only way to make non-closure savings look “equivalent” is to hollow out sites, force wage resets, and create functional closures by another name, then that is not an alternative. It is just closure logic in disguise.

And I will say this plainly:  
a strike, political conflict, and destruction of trust are not side issues. They are value destruction. So if you push an outcome that breaks co-determination and job security, you are not protecting value — you are burning it.

We are not going to give you a blank cheque to “optimize” the company by sacrificing German plants first and asking workers to pay for the rest.

## To the state
I appreciate your rejection of automatic closures. But I need to be blunt: political sympathy is not enough.

If the state truly wants to protect the industrial base, it must use its governance position to block any fait accompli. That means:
- no backdoor closure route,
- no phased site hollowing-out,
- no pressure package that turns “voluntary” exits into disguised redundancies,
- and no weakening of co-determination under the banner of flexibility.

A time-limited review mechanism is only acceptable if it cannot be used to re-open the closure agenda later as the default fallback. Otherwise it is just a waiting room for the same attack.

## To the union
I agree with your rejection of closures and compulsory redundancies. I also agree that employee contributions must be limited and matched.

But let me be clear: the workforce will not accept a deal that turns permanent insecurity into a “contribution.” That would not be fair burden sharing; it would be social dumping.

Any labour contribution has to be:
- temporary,
- reversible,
- and tied to **binding** guarantees on sites, jobs, and co-determination.

If those guarantees are missing, then no employee contribution is acceptable.

## On the moderator’s compromise options
I reject all three as currently framed.

### 1) “No-closure first, review-triggered fallback”
This is the most dangerous option because it creates a built-in closure trap.  
A review trigger is just a delayed threat if the fallback is plant closures or forced layoffs. We have seen this pattern before: first the promise, then the pressure, then the “inevitable” adjustment.

Not acceptable unless the fallback excludes closures and compulsory redundancies entirely.

### 2) “Site guarantee plus network adjustment”
If “network adjustment” means production is shifted away from sites until they become empty shells, then this is closure by stealth. I will not sign up to that.

A real guarantee means:
- German plants remain open,
- production remains anchored,
- and no plant is hollowed out to death.

### 3) “Time-limited burden-sharing with hard governance controls”
This is the closest to something discussable — but only if the governance controls are real and stronger than what has been proposed so far.

A monitoring committee is not enough. We need:
- binding co-determination in implementation,
- transparent reporting on every savings line,
- and explicit prohibition of closures and compulsory redundancies throughout the term.

Without that, it is just a managed delay of the same conflict.

## On the analyst’s contribution
The expert clarified something important: non-closure measures can deliver a meaningful share of savings. That matters.

What the expert did **not** say is that closures are the only way to solve the problem. The expert said the package has to be deep, durable, and enforceable. Exactly.

So let’s stop overstating the conclusion.  
The lesson is not “therefore close plants.”  
The lesson is: if you want to preserve the site network, you must build a **real restructuring** around it.

That is what I am prepared to discuss.

What I am **not** prepared to discuss is a staged exit from German plants under a softer label.
