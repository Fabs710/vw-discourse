I will respond plainly and without pretending the core conflict has disappeared.

## First: what I agree with
Yes, the company has a serious structural competitiveness problem.  
Yes, temporary fixes alone are not enough.  
Yes, any viable answer must be quantified, enforceable, and durable.  
And yes, a prolonged strike would damage everyone.

So I am not disputing the need for a real restructuring.

## But I reject the central framing from management, ownership, and investors
You are still treating **German plant closures and compulsory redundancies** as the default benchmark, and everything else as acceptable only if it comes close enough to that outcome.

I do **not** accept that logic.

From the workforce’s perspective, this is not a spreadsheet exercise where sites and livelihoods are interchangeable inputs. These are plants, families, and regional industrial ecosystems. Closing a German plant is not a “measure” like any other. It is a decision with irreversible consequences, and I will not endorse it as a starting point.

## To management
You keep saying closures remain on the table unless a near-equivalent alternative is proven. Fine — then prove it.

But do not pretend that because an analyst says closure savings are cleaner, closure is therefore justified. “Cleaner” is not the same as “necessary.”

If management wants labour cooperation, then management must first show:
1. the exact cost gap,
2. the exact savings from management restraint,
3. the exact savings from procurement, overhead, and capital discipline,
4. and the exact shortfall that remains after genuine non-closure measures.

So far, what I hear is a desire to force the workforce into a bargaining position where it must either accept permanent damage or be blamed for resisting. That is not honest restructuring; it is pressure politics.

## To ownership and investors
You both speak as if the only credible route is the one that maximizes short-term structural relief. But if your route triggers strikes, destroys trust, weakens co-determination, and tears up the job-security framework, then you are not preserving value — you are destroying industrial legitimacy.

And let me be clear: we will not give you a blank cheque to “optimize” the company by hollowing out German sites and using temporary employee sacrifices as a bridge to future cuts.

A deal that leaves workers with binding sacrifices while ownership keeps all options open is not fair burden-sharing. It is one-sided risk transfer.

## To the state
I appreciate your rejection of closures as a default, but I will also say this directly: a conditional stance is not enough if management uses it as a stepping stone to demand “functionally equivalent” closures later. We need more than political sympathy. We need governance action that blocks a fait accompli.

If the state wants to protect the industrial base, it should help lock in:
- site security,
- job security,
- no forced redundancies,
- and meaningful co-determination over implementation.

Anything less risks turning “protection” into a temporary pause before the same attack returns.

## On the analyst’s argument
The expert said something important: non-closure measures can reduce cost meaningfully, but often not fully match closure-level savings unless they effectively become a disguised closure.

That is exactly why I reject the management frame.

If “matching closure economics” requires a functional closure, then the comparison is not between a closure and a real alternative — it is between a closure and a disguised closure. We are not going down that road.

A legitimate alternative must be a **different restructuring logic**, not simply a euphemism for shutting down sites through the back door.

## My minimum conditions remain unchanged
1. **All German plants remain open.**
2. **No compulsory redundancies.**
3. **Binding site and job guarantees through 2030.**
4. **No erosion of co-determination or the historic job-security framework.**
5. **A fair burden-sharing package with real contributions from management and owners.**

These are not decorative demands. They are the floor for any agreement we can defend to the workforce.

## What I am prepared to discuss
I am still prepared to discuss:
- working-time reductions,
- voluntary exits only,
- redeployment across the group,
- qualification and reskilling,
- productivity gains,
- targeted labor-cost contribution,
- and investment commitments for German sites.

But I will not discuss those as a substitute for closures if the real intent is to dismantle the site network in stages.

## What I want next
If management, ownership, and investors insist that closures are avoidable only if an equivalent package exists, then put the package on the table in hard numbers:

- savings by source,
- timeline,
- job impact,
- site impact,
- reversibility,
- and governance safeguards.

And I want one additional point spelled out clearly: **how this package preserves every German plant without hidden capacity destruction.**

If you cannot do that, then your position is not “there is a better alternative.”  
Your position is simply that you prefer the closure route.

We will resist that.

## Bottom line
The workforce will not accept:
- closures,
- forced layoffs,
- or the dismantling of co-determination and job security under the label of “competitiveness.”

If management wants a serious deal, it must stop treating those as negotiable starting points and instead build a restructuring that protects jobs, sites, and governance first.

Until then, our position stands.
