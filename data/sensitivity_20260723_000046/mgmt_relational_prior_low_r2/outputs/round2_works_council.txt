I will respond plainly.

We agree on one point: the company has a real competitiveness problem and a purely temporary fix is not enough. But that does **not** justify treating German plants and jobs as the first variable to cut. I do **not** accept the logic that because management created an overcapacity problem, the workforce must now pay for it through closures or compulsory redundancies.

### What I accept
I am prepared to discuss a serious package that includes:
- working-time reductions,
- voluntary exits only,
- redeployment within the group,
- qualification and reskilling,
- productivity and process improvements,
- targeted, negotiated labour contributions,
- and investment commitments for German sites.

I am also prepared to discuss **site-by-site capacity adjustments** if and only if they **do not** mean plant closure, outsourcing, or forced layoffs.

### What I reject
Let me be crystal clear:

- **No German plant closures.**
- **No compulsory redundancies.**
- **No “flexibility” that is really just management freedom to cut jobs at will.**
- **No blank cheque to reduce capacity while stripping away the historic job-security framework.**
- **No settlement that leaves workers with binding obligations while management and owners keep all strategic options open.**

Those are not bargaining positions to me; they are red lines.

### On management’s argument
Management says it needs “durable savings.” Fine. Then bring durable savings **without destroying sites and jobs**. Do not tell me the only credible route is closure. That is not a technical necessity; it is a choice. If you want labour cooperation, you need to prove that you have exhausted alternatives first and that the burden is being shared fairly.

So I ask management directly:

1. What exact cost gap must be closed?
2. What portion comes from management overhead, capital allocation, procurement, and executive restraint?
3. What portion comes from voluntary labour measures?
4. Why is a plant closure supposedly unavoidable if those alternatives are fully used?

Until those numbers are on the table, “closure” is an assertion, not a conclusion.

### On the union’s position
I support the union’s insistence that there will be no forced layoffs and no destruction of the collective bargaining framework. But I want to be equally direct: “moderated wage development” cannot become a permanent wage sacrifice while management preserves its decision-making freedom. Any employee contribution must be **limited, reversible, and matched** by contributions from the top and by binding guarantees on sites and jobs.

### On the state and the family
I welcome your rejection of closures as a starting point. But your support will be meaningful only if you use your governance power to rule out a fait accompli. The workforce will not accept being told that German plants are “open” in words while capacity is quietly hollowed out and jobs disappear through backdoor measures.

### My minimum conditions for any agreement
1. **All German plants remain open.**
2. **No compulsory redundancies.**
3. **Binding site and job guarantees to 2030.**
4. **A negotiated package with real co-determination rights over implementation.**
5. **A fair burden-sharing arrangement involving management and owners, not only workers.**

### My challenge to management and investors
If you want this council to accept a restructuring at all, show a **numerically credible alternative to closures**. Not slogans. Not vague “shared sacrifice.” A concrete package with quantified savings, timing, and enforceable safeguards.

If that cannot be delivered, then we are not discussing a restructuring we can endorse; we are discussing the forced dismantling of a German industrial base. That is not acceptable to us, and we will resist it with all the tools available to us.
