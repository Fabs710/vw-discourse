I will respond plainly.

## To the Works Council
I agree with your core rejection of closures and compulsory redundancies. That is not just a tactical line; it is the only basis on which a durable settlement can be defended to the workforce.

I also agree that “closure economics” must not become a disguised excuse for hollowing out sites or turning voluntary exits into forced layoffs by another name.

Where I would sharpen the position is this:  
we must not let management reduce the debate to a closure-versus-denial binary. There is a serious restructuring path that preserves sites **if** it is real, quantified, and shared fairly.

But I will also be direct: if the deal is to protect all German plants and avoid forced layoffs, then the employee contribution cannot be unlimited, and it cannot be one-sided. It must be matched by:
- management restraint,
- owner contributions,
- and binding investment commitments.

That is the price of labour cooperation.

## To management
You keep saying “closures are cleaner, therefore they are necessary.” I reject that logic.

Cleaner for whom? For management’s restructuring spreadsheet. Not necessarily for the company, and certainly not for the workforce or the industrial base.

If you want labour to accept sacrifice, then show the full arithmetic:
1. cost gap,
2. savings from management restraint,
3. savings from procurement and overhead,
4. savings from productivity and working-time measures,
5. and the residual gap after **genuine** non-closure measures.

Do not ask us to sign up to plant closures before you have exhausted fair alternatives.

And I need to be very clear:  
a package that combines wage restraint, working-time flexibility, and voluntary exits while keeping the threat of closures and forced redundancies in reserve is **not** fair restructuring. It is pressure bargaining.

We will not sign that.

## To the controlling family and investors
You are right about one thing: a weak deal would be bad.  
But you are wrong if you think the only strong deal is one that preserves closures as a live threat.

The analyst’s point should not be abused. The expert said non-closure measures can deliver meaningful savings, and only under aggressive assumptions do they approach closure economics. That does **not** mean closure is the default answer. It means a non-closure package has to be serious and enforceable.

If you want our cooperation, then accept the logic of burden sharing:
- workers contribute, but not alone;
- management contributes, materially;
- owners contribute, visibly;
- and the company commits to German sites and industrial capacity.

If you refuse that, then you are not asking for a settlement. You are asking the workforce to absorb the pain while everyone else keeps strategic freedom.

That is unacceptable.

## To the state
I appreciate your rejection of automatic closures. But political sympathy is not enough, and neither is a vague commitment to “monitoring.”

If you want to protect the industrial base, then use your governance role to make site security real:
- no backdoor closure route,
- no staged hollowing-out,
- no disguised redundancies through forced attrition,
- and no weakening of co-determination.

A review mechanism is only acceptable if it cannot be used to reopen the closure agenda as the default fallback. If the fallback is closures or forced layoffs, then the review clause is not a safeguard — it is a delayed threat.

## On the moderator’s compromise options
I’ll be direct about those:

### 1) “No-closure first, review-triggered fallback”
This is only acceptable if the fallback does **not** include plant closures or compulsory redundancies.  
If it does, then it is not a compromise; it is a ticking clock on job destruction.

### 2) “Site guarantee plus network adjustment”
This can only work if “network adjustment” means real efficiency measures, not production draining away from sites until they become empty shells.

A guarantee that the plants remain technically open while their work is gradually removed is not a guarantee. It is closure by stealth.

### 3) “Time-limited burden-sharing with hard governance controls”
This is the closest thing to a basis for agreement, but only if the governance controls are strong:
- transparent reporting,
- joint monitoring,
- binding implementation rules,
- and explicit protection against closures and forced redundancies during the term.

Without that, it is just a managed delay.

## What the union can accept
We remain open to a package that includes:
- temporary working-time reductions,
- limited and time-bound wage moderation,
- voluntary exits only,
- redeployment across the group,
- qualification and reskilling,
- productivity gains,
- and management/top-level restraint.

We are also willing to discuss a **quantified employee contribution** if — and only if — it is matched by:
- management contribution,
- owner contribution,
- and hard guarantees on jobs and sites.

That is the integrative solution. Not closures. Not coercion. Shared sacrifice.

## What we cannot accept
Let me remove any ambiguity:

- no plant closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- no hidden closures through “network rationalization,”
- no permanent wage reset,
- no dismantling of the collective wage framework,
- no one-sided burden shift onto workers.

If those are the terms, then there is no deal.

## What I am challenging management to do now
Bring us a concrete, quantified package with two things side by side:

### Scenario A: closure-based
- savings,
- transition costs,
- job impact,
- strike risk,
- and long-term value consequences.

### Scenario B: no-closure, shared-sacrifice package
- savings by source,
- duration,
- enforcement,
- governance safeguards,
- and the exact employee, management, and owner contributions.

If Scenario B does not close the gap, say so honestly.  
If it does, then stop using closures as the default threat.

## Bottom line
We are prepared to negotiate seriously.  
We are prepared to contribute fairly.  
We are prepared to accept temporary, limited sacrifices.

But we are **not** prepared to finance competitiveness by sacrificing jobs, sites, and collective bargaining security while management and owners keep all strategic options open.

If the others want an agreement, it must be:
- job-protective,
- site-protective,
- shared in burden,
- and durable.

If they refuse that, then they should expect resistance, not consent.
