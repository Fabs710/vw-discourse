Yes. From an industrial restructuring standpoint, the key question is whether the **no-closure package removes enough permanent fixed cost and installed capacity** to be economically close to closures.

## Practical interpretation

### Scenario A: closure-based
This is the **stronger instrument** for eliminating structural overcapacity. It is the most reliable way to:
- remove a site’s fixed overhead,
- reduce complexity,
- align installed capacity with demand,
- and force a hard reset of the cost base.

But it is also the most expensive and conflict-heavy path in the short run.

### Scenario B: no-closure
This can be credible **only if it is not just a soft savings package**. To come close to closure economics, it must include:
- real and durable capacity reduction,
- shift-line reductions or line resizing,
- major work-rule and working-time changes,
- meaningful labor-cost relief,
- overhead and management cuts,
- and procurement/capital discipline.

If it is mainly:
- voluntary exits,
- temporary wage moderation,
- and generic productivity promises,

then it is **not** economically equivalent to closures; it mostly delays the problem.

## What “credible” means in this case

A no-closure package becomes economically credible only if it delivers:
1. **Permanent fixed-cost reduction**, not just variable-cost relief  
2. **Installed-capacity reduction**, not merely labor appeasement  
3. **Implementation certainty**, with measurable milestones  
4. **Durable labor contribution**, not a one-off concession  
5. **Visible management/owner contribution**, or it will fail politically  

## Timing judgment

For a German automotive group under current conditions, a realistic sequence is:

- **0–6 months:** negotiation, governance alignment, strike risk, package design
- **6–18 months:** first savings from exits, shift changes, overhead cuts
- **18–36 months:** full impact from capacity reduction, product reallocation, and organizational simplification

So if the company needs a **hard margin fix quickly**, closures are usually faster to *lock in* savings, even if not faster to *announce* them.

## Bottom line

- **Closures**: higher savings certainty, higher one-time cost, much higher conflict risk.
- **No-closure alternative**: socially and politically easier, but only matches closure economics if it includes **deep capacity reduction and durable fixed-cost removal**.

If the non-closure plan does **not** remove at least something like a **full site-equivalent of fixed cost** or an equivalent amount of production complexity, I would not treat it as closure-equivalent.
