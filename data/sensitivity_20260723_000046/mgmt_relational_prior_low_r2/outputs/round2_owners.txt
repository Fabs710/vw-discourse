I appreciate the analyst’s clarification, because it confirms what I have been saying all along: **a “no closures” position is not automatically a solution unless it is backed by very deep, durable, and enforceable capacity and cost reductions.**

So let me respond directly to each side.

## To the Works Council
I agree with you on one point: **this must not become a one-sided burden shift onto the workforce**. A prolonged conflict would destroy value, and the company does need a negotiated solution.

But I disagree with your core red lines as stated.

- **“All German plants remain open”** is not a principle I can support.
- **“No compulsory redundancies”** cannot be absolute if the economics require permanent structural adjustment.
- **2030 guarantees regardless of market conditions** are too rigid if they prevent the company from adapting.

You are asking for binding protection, but the analyst’s assessment shows that if closures are off the table, then the alternative has to come very close to closure economics. That means: permanent capacity removal, major cost reduction, and enforceable labor-cost relief. If you reject closures while also rejecting anything that looks and feels like hard restructuring, then you are not offering a solution — you are offering a delay.

What I could support is:
- voluntary exits,
- redeployment,
- working-time flexibility,
- productivity gains,
- and time-limited employee contributions,

**but only if these are large enough to close most of the structural gap and are implemented with real certainty.**

## To the Union
I hear your insistence on fair burden sharing, and I agree in principle. The workforce should not be the only party making sacrifices.

But I do not accept the idea that labor can contribute only in a narrow, reversible way while the company carries all the structural risk. If the company is missing its margin target and facing structural overcapacity, then labor’s contribution must be meaningful enough to matter economically.

So yes:
- no one-sided wage suppression,
- no permanent insecurity for workers,

but also no illusion that a modest, temporary concession will solve a structural competitiveness problem.

## To Management
I am aligned with you on the central business diagnosis: **the company needs a durable reset in cost and capacity**. The analyst has confirmed that temporary measures alone will not do the job.

However, I also want to be clear: if you come to the board with closures as the default answer, you will have to demonstrate that all credible alternatives have been quantified and found insufficient. You do **not** have a blank cheque to dismantle the industrial base simply because it is easier to model on paper.

If you want ownership support, then you need to present:
1. the exact structural cost gap,
2. the savings from non-closure measures,
3. the shortfall that remains,
4. and why that shortfall cannot be closed without a plant closure or a functionally equivalent measure.

If you cannot show that, then plant closure is not yet justified.

## To the State
I respect the state’s role and the governance realities, but I will not support a position that treats the company as if it could be insulated from market reality indefinitely. Regional stability matters; so does competitiveness.

If the state wants to block closures, then it also has to support a package that is economically credible. A political veto without an economic substitute is not stewardship.

## To Investors
I agree with your insistence on hard numbers. The analyst confirms that there is no free lunch here. A package that preserves everything politically and saves too little economically would be value-destructive. We will not back that.

But I also caution against assuming that the fastest-looking cut is always the best answer. A rushed closure that triggers strike escalation, political deadlock, and reputational damage can destroy more value than it saves. We need **durable** improvement, not just fast headline savings.

## My position
Here is the ownership view, plainly stated:

1. **I support a restructuring that materially restores margins and competitiveness.**
2. **I will not support a cosmetic compromise that preserves the current cost base.**
3. **I am open to avoiding plant closures only if the alternative is economically near-equivalent, durable, and enforceable.**
4. **I will not support an outcome that permanently weakens the enterprise or our governance ability to act in the future.**

## What would move me
A credible alternative to closures would need to include:
- permanent capacity removal,
- meaningful and measurable labor-cost reduction,
- serious productivity and complexity cuts,
- a time-limited but significant employee contribution,
- and a binding product/investment roadmap.

If those elements are present, I am willing to consider avoiding closures. If they are not, then closure remains on the table.

## My bottom line
I am not here to defend every site or every legacy arrangement. I am here to protect the long-term value of the company. If a no-closure package can truly deliver the necessary structural savings, I will support it. If it cannot, then we should not pretend otherwise.

So my message to all sides is simple:

- **Do not ask me to bless a weak compromise.**
- **Do not ask me to support a strategy that preserves cost and calls it peace.**
- **Bring me a package that closes the structural gap, or accept that harder measures remain necessary.**

If helpful, I can next state the **ownership conditions for support** in a more concrete form: what level of labor contribution, capacity reduction, and governance safeguard would be sufficient for us to back a no-closure deal.
