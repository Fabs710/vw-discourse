I will respond directly.

## Where I agree
I agree with everyone on the basic diagnosis:
- the company has a real structural competitiveness problem,
- temporary fixes alone will not solve it,
- and a prolonged strike would be damaging for the company, the workforce, and the region.

I also agree that any viable solution must be:
- quantified,
- durable,
- and enforceable.

So I am not defending a cosmetic compromise. We need a real restructuring.

## Where I disagree
I do **not** accept the current framing from management, ownership, and investors that plant closures and compulsory redundancies are the default benchmark and that everything else must merely approximate that outcome.

That is too narrow, and for a company with this regional importance it is also too destructive.

A plant closure in this state is not just an efficiency measure. It has consequences for:
- thousands of jobs,
- supply chains,
- apprenticeships and qualification,
- the regional economy,
- and the company’s social legitimacy.

I will not treat that as a normal line item.

## To management
You say closures remain on the table unless a near-equivalent alternative is proven. Fine. But then prove the closure case, not just assert it.

So far, what I have heard is:
- high costs,
- overcapacity,
- and a need for durable savings.

That may be true. But it does **not** automatically justify closures. You still need to show:
1. the exact structural gap,
2. the savings from management restraint,
3. the savings from procurement, overhead, and capital discipline,
4. the savings from productivity and working-time measures,
5. and the residual shortfall.

If that residual shortfall is truly unavoidable, then we can discuss further. But until then, closure is a threat, not a demonstrated necessity.

## To the works council and union
I support your insistence that workers should not pay for management’s mistakes alone. That is legitimate.

But I also have to be honest: if the alternative package contains only temporary working-time changes, voluntary exits, and soft promises, then it will not be enough.

The analyst’s contribution made that clear: non-closure measures can save a lot, but to really close the gap they have to be deep, durable, and in practice very close to structural network change.

That means if closures are to stay off the table, the alternative must be serious. Not symbolic. Serious.

What I can support is:
- no forced redundancies as a first resort,
- voluntary exits,
- redeployment,
- qualification and reskilling,
- targeted productivity measures,
- and a time-limited employee contribution.

What I cannot support is a settlement that leaves the company uncompetitive and then asks the state to defend that as social policy.

## To the union specifically
I agree that employee contributions must be limited, time-bound, and matched. That is fair.

I do **not** accept a permanent wage sacrifice or a hidden reset of the collective framework.

But I also do **not** accept the idea that workers can contribute only minimally while management and owners are asked to accept all the strategic risk. If the company needs a structural reset, then the contribution from labour must be economically meaningful.

## To the controlling family and investors
You are right that a weak compromise would destroy value. I do not want that either.

But I caution you against assuming that the fastest-looking cut is necessarily the best answer. A rushed closure could trigger:
- strikes,
- political deadlock,
- long-term reputational damage,
- and lasting harm to the company’s regional standing.

That is value destruction too.

If you want my support, then the package must preserve the company’s role as a regional anchor and protect the state’s legitimate co-determination role. I will not back any solution that weakens those foundations.

## My position
Here is the state’s position plainly:

1. **No automatic plant closures**
2. **No forced dismantling of the industrial base in this state**
3. **Yes to a serious negotiated restructuring**
4. **Yes to shared burden**
5. **Yes to strong site and employment protections**
6. **No weakening of co-determination or the special governance role of the state**

I am open to a package that protects jobs and sites while restoring competitiveness. That is the only path I see as politically and economically sustainable.

## What would move me
If management wants support for structural change without closures, I need to see a package with:
- a quantified cost gap,
- a quantified employee contribution,
- meaningful management restraint,
- productivity and process savings,
- and a binding investment and product roadmap for German sites.

If that package truly closes the gap, I will examine it seriously.

If it does not, then I will not help legitimize closures as the default answer either — because I do not see that as a necessary conclusion at this stage.

## Bottom line
I am not here to defend every legacy structure. I am here to protect the company as a long-term industrial anchor in this region.

So my message is:
- no rushed closures,
- no one-sided burden shifting onto workers,
- no erosion of the state’s co-determination role,
- and no “solution” that preserves financial neatness while hollowing out the region.

Bring forward a real shared-savings package that protects jobs and sites, and we can talk. Until then, closures remain politically and substantively unacceptable to me.
