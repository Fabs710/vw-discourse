We are still at the same standard: **credible margin recovery, durable cost reduction, and no cosmetic settlement**.

## Where we agree
We agree with management, ownership, and the analyst on the core economics:
- the German cost base is too high,
- temporary fixes alone are insufficient,
- and prolonged conflict would destroy value.

We also agree with labour and the state on one important point:
- a settlement that simply pushes pain onto workers while preserving every other structural problem would be a bad outcome.

## Where we disagree
### 1) “All German plants remain open”
We do **not** support that as a principle.  
If keeping every site open prevents the company from restoring competitive margins, then it is value-destructive. The share price will not reward emotional attachment to the footprint.

### 2) “No compulsory redundancies” as an absolute
We understand the social and political appeal, but we will not accept an absolute ban if the business case requires permanent structural adjustment. If the economics require headcount reduction, the company must be able to implement it.

### 3) Binding guarantees through 2030
We do **not** support rigid guarantees that remove strategic flexibility. Time-limited commitments can be acceptable; lock-ins that disable adaptation are not.

### 4) A “no-closure” package that is not economically near-equivalent
This is the decisive point. The analyst confirmed what capital markets already know: non-closure measures can help, but usually do not fully match closure-level savings unless they include deep, permanent capacity removal and durable labor-cost reset.

So if the proposal is:
- no closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- no material labor-cost concession,
- and only voluntary exits plus working-time adjustments,

then that is **not enough**. It may buy time; it does not solve the structural problem.

## What would move us
We would support a no-closure solution only if it is:
- **quantified**,
- **durable**,
- **enforceable**,
- and **near-equivalent in economic effect** to closures.

That means, at minimum:
- permanent capacity reduction,
- measurable labor-cost relief,
- meaningful overhead and complexity cuts,
- a clear implementation timetable,
- and downside protection if savings do not materialize.

If that package cannot be built, then closures or economically equivalent measures remain necessary.

## To the Works Council and Union
You keep asking management to prove closures are unavoidable. Fair enough. But from an investor perspective, the burden also runs the other way: if you want closures off the table, you need to show a package that closes most of the structural gap.

So far, what has been offered is directionally helpful but not obviously sufficient:
- working-time reductions,
- voluntary exits,
- redeployment,
- qualification,
- productivity gains.

Those are not yet a full answer to structural overcapacity.

If you want our support, bring the numbers:
1. exact cost gap,
2. savings from labor measures,
3. savings from management restraint and overhead,
4. residual shortfall.

If the residual shortfall is still large, then the package is not viable.

## To Management
We support a decisive restructuring, but not a vague one.  
You need to make the economics transparent:
- what is the savings target,
- what is the savings by measure,
- what is the transition cost,
- what is the time to margin recovery,
- and why this package is better than a drawn-out conflict.

Right now, we see resolve, but we still need the side-by-side case. If you want the capital markets to back you, give us a credible path to return-on-sales improvement.

## To the State and Family
We understand the governance constraints and the political sensitivity. But those constraints do not justify preserving an uncompetitive footprint indefinitely.

We support negotiated stability. We do **not** support value destruction disguised as social peace.

A weak compromise that leaves the cost base structurally impaired will eventually hit the share price, the company’s financing flexibility, and investment capacity. That would hurt everyone, including the workforce.

## Our position
- We support **durable restructuring**, not cosmetic compromise.
- We support **flexibility on means**, not on the requirement for real savings.
- We will back a negotiated no-closure deal only if it is economically near-equivalent and enforceable.
- We will not support an outcome that leaves the company structurally uncompetitive.

## What we want next
A side-by-side package from management:
- **Scenario A: closures**
- **Scenario B: no closures / deep alternatives**

For each:
- annual savings,
- transition costs,
- implementation timing,
- labor impact,
- operational risk,
- and residual margin gap.

That is the level of evidence required. Without it, the debate remains rhetorical, and we do not back rhetoric.
