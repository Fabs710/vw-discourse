We agree on the diagnosis, not yet on the remedy.

## Where we agree
- The company has a **real structural cost problem**.
- **Temporary fixes are not enough**.
- A **prolonged strike would destroy value**.
- Any settlement must be **quantified, durable, and enforceable**.
- We do not want a cosmetic compromise that preserves an uncompetitive cost base.

That said, we are not here to protect every legacy site regardless of economics. We are here to protect **risk-adjusted shareholder value**. If margins remain structurally impaired, share price and financing flexibility will suffer. That is our hard limit.

## Where we disagree
### 1) “All German plants remain open”
We do **not** accept that as a principle.

If a no-closure package cannot deliver **near-equivalent economics**, then refusing closures simply shifts the burden onto future margins. That is not value-preserving. Emotional attachment to the footprint does not change the economics.

### 2) “No compulsory redundancies” as an absolute
We understand the social logic, but we will not back an absolute ban if the economics require permanent capacity reduction. If structural headcount has to come out, the company must retain the ability to do it.

### 3) Binding guarantees through 2030
Rigid guarantees are only acceptable if they do not freeze adaptation. Time-limited, conditional protections can work. A blanket lock-up cannot.

### 4) A “no-closure” package that is not economically near-equivalent
This is the decisive point. The specialist’s assessment aligns with what capital markets already know: non-closure measures can help materially, but unless they include **deep and durable capacity reduction plus meaningful labor-cost relief**, they usually fall short of closure-level savings.

That means:
- voluntary exits only,
- working-time flexibility,
- and soft productivity promises

are **not enough** if they leave the cost base structurally high.

## To the works council and union
You are right that closures are not a slogan to be waved around casually. They are a serious step with real human costs. But you have not yet shown a package that closes the structural gap without them.

If you want closures off the table, bring a **credible economic substitute**:
1. quantified recurring savings,
2. durable labor-cost relief,
3. real capacity reduction,
4. implementation certainty,
5. and downside protection if targets are missed.

Until then, closures remain a legitimate option, not an ideological one, but an economic one.

## To management
We support a hard restructuring goal, but we will not support a vague or confrontational one. If you want capital-markets backing, show the full case:
- baseline cost gap,
- savings by measure,
- one-time transition costs,
- timing to margin recovery,
- and why the proposed package is better than a prolonged conflict.

We also want clear evidence that management overhead, procurement, and capital discipline are being cut before labor is asked to carry the entire burden.

## To the state and family
We respect the governance constraints and the political sensitivity. But preserving a high-cost footprint by declaration is not stewardship. If non-closure measures can truly get close to closure economics, fine. If they cannot, then blocking structural adjustment would be value destructive.

## Our position
We will support a negotiated solution **only if** it credibly restores competitive margins. That means:
- durable cost reduction,
- measurable capacity alignment,
- enforceable implementation,
- and a clear path back to return-on-sales target.

We will **not** support:
- a cosmetic settlement,
- an arrangement that leaves the cost base uncompetitive,
- or a deal that merely delays the reckoning.
