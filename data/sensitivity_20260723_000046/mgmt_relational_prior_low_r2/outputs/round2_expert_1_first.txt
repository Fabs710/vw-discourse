From a plant-network economics perspective: **non-closure measures can sometimes approach closure-level savings, but only under fairly aggressive assumptions and usually only over a longer time horizon.** In many automotive cases, they **do not fully match the fixed-cost relief of a closure** unless the alternative package includes deep, durable capacity removal and permanent labor-cost reset.

## Bottom line
A plant closure typically delivers the largest and cleanest savings because it removes:
- direct labor,
- plant overhead,
- maintenance,
- utilities and site services,
- logistics complexity,
- depreciation/capital support for the site,
- and often some working capital and management overhead.

By contrast, non-closure measures usually save only a subset of those items:
- wage moderation,
- working-time reductions,
- voluntary exits,
- redeployment,
- process productivity,
- procurement,
- and possibly capacity consolidation within the remaining network.

Those measures can be substantial, but they **rarely eliminate the full fixed-cost burden** of an underutilized German plant network unless they are paired with:
1. **permanent headcount reduction**,  
2. **permanent line/capacity shutdown**, and  
3. **durable labor-cost concessions**.

## Quantitatively, what is plausible?
For a large automotive plant, a closure often yields savings on the order of **hundreds of millions of euros per year** once fully implemented, depending on scale and load factor. The exact figure varies widely, but the relevant principle is:

- **Closure savings are mostly structural and recurring.**
- **Alternative measures often start smaller and degrade over time** unless converted into permanent changes.

A non-closure package might realistically achieve:
- **20–40% of closure-equivalent savings** through working-time measures, voluntary exits, and overhead cuts alone;
- **40–70%** if it also includes major productivity gains, deep labor concessions, and sustained capacity consolidation;
- **near-closure-level savings only if it effectively replicates closure economics** by taking out a full production line, eliminating a large amount of fixed labor, and changing the cost base permanently.

That last case is not really “no closure” in economic substance; it is a **functional closure without the legal label**.

## Time horizon
The time profile is critical:

### 1) Immediate term: 0–12 months
Non-closure measures can produce relatively fast cash savings:
- short-time work,
- hiring freezes,
- overtime reduction,
- voluntary exits,
- temporary wage moderation.

But these are usually **not enough** to match closure-level annual savings.

### 2) Medium term: 12–36 months
This is where a negotiated package can become more meaningful if it includes:
- attrition plus severance,
- internal transfers,
- line rationalization,
- process automation,
- procurement savings,
- reduced complexity in the network.

Still, the savings usually remain **below a full plant closure**, unless capacity is genuinely removed.

### 3) Long term: 3–5 years
Only with durable labor agreements and a redesigned network can non-closure measures approach closure economics. But by then:
- implementation risk is high,
- savings may be diluted by inflation and catch-up wage rounds,
- and the company may have financed the transition at the expense of current margin.

## What non-closure measures cannot replicate well
They usually do **not** fully eliminate:
- site-level overhead,
- duplicate logistics,
- fixed maintenance/security costs,
- underutilized utilities and real estate,
- management complexity,
- and the strategic drag of excess installed capacity.

So if the problem is truly **structural overcapacity**, non-closure measures often reduce cost but do not fully solve the capacity problem.

## What would have to be true for non-closure to match closure savings?
A credible closure-equivalent alternative would need all of the following:
- a **large and permanent workforce reduction**,
- **binding wage or labor-cost concessions** lasting several years,
- **permanent removal of capacity**,
- **no meaningful rebound in staffing via later rehiring or reclassification**,
- and **real governance enforceability** so the measures do not unwind after 1–2 bargaining cycles.

If those conditions are not met, the package is usually a **delay-and-buy-time solution**, not a full substitute for closure.
