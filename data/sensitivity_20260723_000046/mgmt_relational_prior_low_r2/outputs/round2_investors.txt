We are willing to back a negotiated solution, but only if it is **financially credible** and **durably improves margins**. That remains the standard.

## Where we agree
We agree with management, ownership, and the state on the core diagnosis:  
- the current German cost base is too high,  
- temporary fixes are not enough,  
- and prolonged conflict would destroy value.

We also agree with labour-side stakeholders on one important point:  
- a settlement that simply imposes pain on the workforce while preserving all other structures would be a bad outcome.  
A strike-heavy or purely confrontational path is not acceptable from a shareholder value perspective either.

## Where we disagree
### 1) “All German plants remain open”
We do **not** accept this as a principle. It is not a financial solution.  
If keeping every site open makes it impossible to restore competitive margins, then that stance becomes value-destructive.

### 2) “No compulsory redundancies” as an absolute
We understand why labour wants this, but we will not support an absolute red line if it prevents a real structural reset. If the economics require permanent headcount reduction, the company must be able to implement it somehow.

### 3) Binding guarantees through 2030
We do **not** support rigid guarantees if they prevent adaptation to market and technology changes. A time-limited framework may be acceptable; a hard lock-up regardless of economics is not.

### 4) A no-closure package without near-equivalent savings
The analyst’s assessment matters here. It confirms what many investors already suspect: **non-closure measures can help, but they usually do not fully match closure-level savings unless they include deep, permanent capacity removal and real labor-cost reset.**  
So if the proposal is “no closures” but only offers temporary working-time cuts and voluntary exits, that is not enough.

## What would move us
We would support a no-closure alternative only if it is **quantified, enforceable, and near-equivalent in economic effect**. That means:

- permanent capacity reduction,
- measurable and durable labor-cost relief,
- credible overhead and complexity cuts,
- a clear implementation timetable,
- and downside protection if the savings do not materialize.

If the alternative cannot close most of the structural gap, then it is just delaying the problem.

## To the Works Council and Union
Your insistence on shared burden is reasonable, but it has to translate into numbers.  
We need to see:
1. the exact structural cost gap,  
2. how much comes from labor,  
3. how much comes from management restraint and overhead,  
4. and what remains after all non-closure measures.

If the answer still leaves a large gap, then closures or functional equivalents remain necessary.

## To Management
We support a decisive restructuring, but not a vague one.  
You need to show:
- the savings case,
- the implementation path,
- the time to margin recovery,
- and why the chosen package is better than a drawn-out conflict.

If you are asking investors to accept tough measures, then the package must be transparent and measurable.

## To the State and Family
We understand the governance constraints. But those constraints do not justify preserving an uncompetitive footprint indefinitely. If closures are to be blocked, then the alternative must be economically serious enough to justify that decision.

## Our position
- **We support durable restructuring, not cosmetic compromise.**
- **We support flexibility on the means, not on the requirement for real savings.**
- **We will back a negotiated no-closure deal only if it is economically near-equivalent to closure and enforceable.**
- **We will not support a package that preserves political comfort while impairing shareholder value.**

If the parties want our support, bring a proposal with hard numbers and a credible margin path. Otherwise, we will not endorse a settlement that merely postpones the restructuring problem.
