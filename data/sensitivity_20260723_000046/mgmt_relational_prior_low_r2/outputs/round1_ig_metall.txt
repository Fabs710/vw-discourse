I largely agree with the Works Council on the red lines: **no plant closures, no compulsory redundancies, and no restructuring that shifts the pain one-sidedly onto the workforce**.

Let me be equally clear from the trade-union side:

- **We will not accept a settlement that destroys the collective wage framework.**
- **We will not accept forced layoffs as the price of management’s strategic mistakes.**
- **We will not accept open-ended insecurity for workers while management keeps full flexibility.**

That said, we are not ignoring the business reality. We know the company is under pressure, and we are prepared to discuss a **fair shared-sacrifice package** if it actually protects jobs and sites. That could include, for example:

- temporary working-time reductions,
- moderated wage developments,
- voluntary rather than compulsory exits,
- a contribution from employees to support the restructuring,
- productivity and process improvements,
- and capacity adjustments that do **not** mean plant closures.

But our willingness to contribute has conditions. A labour contribution is only defensible if it is matched by contributions from **management, owners, and the company itself**. That means:

1. **Binding guarantees for all German sites**
2. **No compulsory redundancies**
3. **No closure decisions disguised as “capacity rationalisation”**
4. **A real investment and product commitment for the German plants**
5. **A fair burden-sharing model, including sacrifices at the top**

If management insists that competitiveness can only be restored by closing plants and cutting jobs, then we are in fundamental disagreement. We have already shown that the workforce is prepared to act. The peace obligation has ended, and the strike tool is now on the table.

So our message is simple:  
**We are ready for a negotiated restructuring, but only one that protects the workforce and distributes the burden fairly.** Anything else will face determined resistance.
