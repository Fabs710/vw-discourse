## Expert assessment: can a closure-free package credibly replace plant closures and forced layoffs?

**Short answer:**  
On the record provided, **a closure-free restructuring is not yet proven impossible, but it is also not yet proven sufficient**. The key issue is **quantified equivalence**: a no-closure package must deliver **roughly comparable, durable** savings and capacity reduction to a closure/redundancy package. The scenario as given does **not** contain the plant-by-plant evidence needed to establish that it can.

### What can be verified from the scenario
The following points are operationally credible:

- The company faces **structural overcapacity in Germany**.
- Its German cost base is judged **above target and above competitors**.
- The core passenger-car brand is **below return-on-sales target**.
- Therefore, **material capacity and cost reduction is required**.
- A viable alternative to closures would need to combine:
  - **capacity reduction**
  - **headcount reduction**
  - **labour-cost relief**
  - **productivity gains**
  - **and ideally higher utilization via product/volume allocation**

### What is not yet verified
The record does **not** provide:

- plant-level fixed and variable cost data,
- current and projected utilization by site,
- model allocation and volume outlook by plant,
- headcount by function and site,
- pension/termination/redeployment costs,
- energy, logistics, and overhead differences across sites,
- or the savings generated by each alternative measure.

Without that, no one can rigorously say whether:
- **all German plants can stay open**, or
- **closures are economically necessary**.

### Industrial logic
In automotive manufacturing, if a footprint is structurally oversized, savings from a closure usually come from:
- elimination of **fixed overhead**
- removal of **duplicated support functions**
- better **fixed-cost absorption**
- lower **maintenance and capex burden**
- and, often, an associated **headcount reduction**

A closure-free package can only match that if it does one or more of the following at meaningful scale:
1. **reduces capacity** enough that fixed costs fall materially,
2. **cuts labour costs** durably,
3. **raises utilization** sufficiently,
4. **reassigns volume** without creating new hidden costs,
5. **or brings in offsetting financial support** from owners/shareholders.

If these elements are modest, temporary, or vague, the package is unlikely to close the gap.

### Assessment of the works council / union position
Their position is **not unsupported in principle**; it is **unsupported in quantified form**.

What they are asserting is effectively:
- no closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- and a closure-free savings path.

That is a legitimate bargaining position, but from an industrial verification standpoint it is **a claim that requires proof**. At present, the record does not show that the claim is operationally closed.

### Assessment of management’s position
Management’s view that closures may be needed is **plausible**, but the scenario does **not** prove necessity.

To justify closures as necessary, management would need to show:
- the savings gap after all non-closure options,
- the utilization constraints at each site,
- why redeployment/working-time/productivity measures are insufficient,
- and why closure-free measures cannot deliver comparable durability.

That evidence is not in the record.

## Practical conclusion
From an industrial and financial verification standpoint:

- **Supported:** The company needs substantial restructuring in Germany.
- **Not yet supported:** That closures are the only feasible way.
- **Not yet supported:** That a no-closure, no-forced-layoff package can fully replace closures without detailed plant-by-plant proof.

## What would make a closure-free deal credible
A credible closure-free alternative would need a spreadsheet-level case showing, by plant:

- baseline and projected utilization,
- unit and fixed costs,
- headcount impacts,
- specific savings from working-time changes,
- savings from wage moderation/bonuses,
- savings from productivity actions,
- redeployment costs,
- capex required to keep plants competitive,
- and the resulting net present value versus a closure scenario.

If that model closes the gap, a closure-free settlement is feasible. If it does not, then the claim is not credible on industrial grounds.

## Bottom line
The evidence in the scenario supports **testing** a closure-free alternative, but **not accepting it as proven**. Likewise, it supports considering closures as a serious option, but **not calling them proven necessary** without plant-by-plant verification.
