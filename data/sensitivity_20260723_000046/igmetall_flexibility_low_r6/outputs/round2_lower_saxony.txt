I’ll respond plainly.

I agree with management on one point: the company cannot simply freeze the current structure and hope the market turns around. We do need a durable restructuring, and we do need competitiveness, investment, and a credible path back to stronger margins.

But I **do not** agree that this requires plant closures in Germany as the default solution, nor do I accept compulsory redundancies as an acceptable bargaining outcome. From the perspective of the state, as a long-standing anchor shareholder and political actor, that would be economically and socially damaging, and it would undermine the company’s role as a regional industrial anchor.

## Where I agree
- The current cost base is too high.
- Capacity adjustment is necessary.
- Productivity improvements, qualification, and investment are necessary.
- A negotiated solution is better than an open conflict.
- Any employee contribution should be balanced by real contributions from management, owners, and shareholders.

## Where I disagree
### 1) Plant closures are not a starting point
Management keeps presenting closures as if they were the obvious consequence of the numbers. I have not seen a convincing case that they are the only way to achieve the required adjustment. “Hard numbers” are being demanded from labour; that is fair. But the same standard applies to management. If closures are being kept on the table, then prove they are truly unavoidable, not simply the fastest way to impose pain.

### 2) Compulsory redundancies are not acceptable as a routine fallback
If the company wants social peace and long-term stability, then forced job cuts are the wrong instrument unless every serious alternative has been exhausted and fully tested. I am not prepared to support a plan that treats workers as the first and easiest variable.

### 3) A closure-free solution must be real, not symbolic
I agree with labour that temporary concessions alone are not enough. But I also agree with capital that cosmetic measures are not enough. If we are going to avoid closures, then the package must deliver:
- durable capacity reduction,
- meaningful cost reduction,
- operational flexibility,
- and a credible investment path.

That means a serious restructuring, not just a pause.

## What I expect from management
If you want support from the state, you need to come forward with a package that protects the company **and** its regional footprint. That means:
- no plant closures as the first resort,
- no mass compulsory redundancies,
- a quantified closure-free alternative,
- a clear investment commitment for German sites,
- and a credible role for co-determination in monitoring implementation.

If management cannot produce a package that avoids closures, then I will not treat closures as automatically legitimate. They would still face strong resistance, including from me in my shareholder role and on the supervisory board.

## To the Works Council and union
Your insistence on site protection and against forced layoffs is legitimate, and I support that core position. But I also need to be honest: a simple “no” is not enough if the company’s economics remain broken. If we want to defend jobs and sites, we need to engage seriously on:
- working-time flexibility,
- internal redeployment,
- qualification,
- productivity,
- and a fair temporary employee contribution.

However, that contribution must be matched by real sacrifices elsewhere — from management, from owners, and from capital. I will not back a one-sided deal.

## To the family owners and investors
If you care about long-term value, then you should not support a restructuring strategy that destroys trust, provokes strikes, and weakens the company’s standing in its home region. Long-term stewardship means preserving the industrial base while restoring competitiveness. A plan that guts jobs and sites may look efficient in the short term, but it risks lasting damage.

## My bottom line
I am open to a serious restructuring. I am **not** open to plant closures being treated as inevitable, and I am **not** open to compulsory redundancies as the default answer.

If there is a viable closure-free package, show it in numbers:
- how much capacity is reduced,
- how much cost is saved,
- how jobs are protected,
- and how investment is secured.

If that cannot be shown, then the burden is not on labour or the state to accept closures; the burden is on management to explain why it failed to find a more socially and economically responsible solution.

So my position is clear:
**competitiveness yes, social demolition no.**
