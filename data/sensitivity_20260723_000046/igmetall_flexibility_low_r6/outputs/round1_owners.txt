I appreciate the clarity from all sides. From the perspective of the controlling family, I want to be equally straightforward.

We agree on the diagnosis that the status quo is not sustainable. The company’s cost base, capacity footprint, and margin performance are not where they need to be. If we do not restore competitiveness, we will eventually damage not only jobs and sites, but the long-term value of the core enterprise itself. That would be a failure of stewardship.

Where I agree with labour and the state:
- We need a durable solution, not a cosmetic one.
- We need investment in future products, technology, and qualification.
- We need social peace if we are to execute any restructuring successfully.
- We should exhaust serious alternatives before forcing a confrontation that burns value.

Where I disagree:
- A blanket refusal of plant closures is not a credible governing position if the economics do not work.
- A blanket refusal of compulsory redundancies is only defensible if a negotiated package truly delivers equivalent savings and flexibility.
- Site guarantees to 2030 are not automatically meaningful if they simply freeze an uncompetitive structure and defer the problem.

I am not interested in value-destroying theatre on either side:
- not management using closures as a negotiating hammer without proving necessity,
- and not labour treating every structural adjustment as illegitimate regardless of the numbers.

My position is therefore conditional and firm:

1. The company needs a binding restructuring that restores margins and competitiveness on a durable basis.
2. Labour peace matters, but it cannot be purchased at the price of preserving structural overcapacity forever.
3. Any alternative to closures must show, in hard numbers, that it replaces the same savings and capacity reduction over time.
4. A negotiated employee contribution can be part of the answer, but only if it is meaningful and sustained, not symbolic.
5. Investment commitments and qualification must accompany restructuring, because the family’s interest is long-term enterprise value, not short-term extraction.

So I will not endorse an outcome that simply rules out closures in principle. Nor will I support a restructuring that destroys the company’s social legitimacy and triggers a prolonged conflict. The right answer must be economically credible and governable.

To management: bring forward a plan that proves the required savings and capacity adjustment. If closures are truly unavoidable, they must be justified as a last resort, not as a first reflex.

To labour and the state: if you want closure-free restructuring, then you must be prepared to accept deep, durable changes elsewhere — in labour cost, working time, capacity, and operational flexibility — sufficient to match the economics. If that cannot be done, then your red lines are not protecting the company; they are postponing a harder reckoning.

That is where I stand. I support competitiveness. I support long-term value. I support negotiated stability. But I will not support a settlement that leaves the enterprise structurally weak.
