## Where I agree
I agree with everyone on one fundamental point: this company needs a real restructuring. The status quo is not sustainable. We need:
- investment in future products and technology,
- qualification and redeployment,
- productivity gains,
- and a cost base that is genuinely competitive.

I also agree with the experts and the moderator on one procedural point: **we need plant-by-plant verification**, not slogans.

## Where I disagree
I do **not** accept the continued attempt to make **plant closures and compulsory redundancies** the default benchmark for what counts as “serious” restructuring.

That is not neutral analysis. It is a choice to put the burden of adjustment first on workers and on the regions that depend on these sites.

As the state shareholder, I have a clear responsibility to protect:
- jobs in the state,
- sites in the state,
- and the company’s role as a regional economic anchor.

That is not sentimentality. It is long-term industrial stewardship.

## To management
You have still not shown that plant closures are necessary. You have shown that they are convenient from a cost-cutting perspective. That is not enough.

If you want deeper structural measures, then you must demonstrate — plant by plant — why a closure-free restructuring cannot achieve the required savings and capacity reduction.

Until then, closures remain a **proposal**, not a proven necessity.

What I will not support:
- closures as a bargaining lever,
- compulsory redundancies as a routine fallback,
- or a restructuring that offloads nearly all the risk onto workers and the region.

If the company’s answer to every competitiveness issue is to shrink its domestic base, then it will damage the very industrial strength it depends on.

## To the works council and union
I agree with you that:
- no worker should be forced out as the first answer,
- the burden cannot be one-sided,
- and temporary employee contributions must be matched by management, owners, and shareholders.

But I will also be frank: a political line of “all sites stay open” is only defensible if it can survive the industrial test. I am not prepared to defend a closure-free position forever **without** seeing a serious operational plan.

So yes, your red lines are legitimate. But they have to be matched by a plan that is more than a statement of refusal.

## To the controlling family holding
You speak of long-term value, and I take that seriously. But long-term value is not created by forcing through the harshest possible restructuring at the first opportunity.

It is created by:
- preserving trust,
- avoiding industrial conflict,
- securing the company’s domestic production base,
- and making adjustment in a socially sustainable way.

If you back a plan that destroys trust in the workforce and in the regions where this company is anchored, you will weaken the company beyond the immediate savings you extract.

## To investors
You are right to insist on durability and accountability. But durability is not only a question of cost. It is also a question of governance stability, labour peace, and regional legitimacy.

A solution that triggers conflict, strikes, and long-term damage to the company’s reputation in Germany is not a durable solution.

## What I want next
I support the expert’s call for a **plant-by-plant verification**. But the mandate must be balanced.

I want the review to examine:
1. the savings from closure and redundancy scenarios,
2. the savings from closure-free alternatives,
3. the regional and employment effects,
4. the investment requirements to keep sites competitive,
5. and the long-term risks of industrial conflict.

## My position, clearly stated
