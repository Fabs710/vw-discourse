From an industrial restructuring and capacity-assessment standpoint, the works council’s position is understandable but only partially supported by operational economics.

## What the data and the scenario support
- The company has **structural overcapacity in Germany** and a **cost base above target and above competitors**.
- The core passenger-car brand is **missing its return-on-sales target**, so the issue is not cyclical weakness alone.
- In that setting, **capacity reduction is not optional** if the objective is durable competitiveness.
- A closure-free package can work **only if** it produces savings and footprint reduction **roughly comparable in scale and durability** to a closure/redundancy package.

## What is not established
- There is **no plant-by-plant evidence in the record** showing that:
  - working-time moderation,
  - temporary pay restraint,
  - redeployment,
  - productivity gains,
  - and shift/capacity adjustments
  can fully replace the savings from one or more plant closures.
- There is also **no evidence that closures are the only feasible route**. That remains unproven on the information provided.

## Assessment of the works council’s core claim
The claim “all German plants can remain open with no compulsory redundancies and still achieve the required restructuring” is **not yet substantiated** on the evidence given.

That does **not** mean it is false. It means:
- it would require a **quantified operating plan**,
- with **site-level volume forecasts**,
- **capacity utilization assumptions**,
- **fixed-cost absorption effects**,
- and **specific labour-cost savings**.

Without that, it is a policy position, not an industrial plan.

## Industrial judgment
If German plants are already operating below efficient scale, then:
- keeping all sites open will usually require either
  - **very large and durable internal concessions**, or
  - **substantial volume increases**, which are not guaranteed,
  - or **cross-subsidy from the group**, which is a strategic choice rather than an operational solution.

In most automotive footprints, if demand has fallen structurally, simply spreading the same work across all sites tends to leave **fixed-cost dilution** too weak. That is why management is pushing closures.

## What would make a closure-free plan credible
A closure-free restructuring would need, at minimum:
1. **site-by-site capacity reduction**
2. **verified headcount reduction through attrition/redeployment**
3. **measurable labour-cost relief**
4. **binding productivity improvements**
5. **product and volume commitments sufficient to raise utilization**
6. **a clear financial bridge** showing the savings gap is closed

If those elements are missing, then the plan is unlikely to restore competitiveness.

## Bottom line
- **Supported by the evidence:** the company needs material capacity and cost reduction in Germany.
- **Not supported as yet:** that closures are definitively necessary.
- **Also not supported:** that a no-closure, no-forced-layoff plan can achieve the required savings without detailed proof.

My assessment is that the works council is right to demand a quantified alternative, but wrong to treat “all plants stay open” as operationally credible **without** showing how the cost and capacity gap will be closed.
