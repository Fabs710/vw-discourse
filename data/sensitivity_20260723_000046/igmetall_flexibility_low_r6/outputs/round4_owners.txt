I will be direct.

## Where I agree
I agree with the central diagnosis from management, the experts, and even parts of the labour side: **the current structure is not sustainable**.  
The company needs a real restructuring that restores competitiveness, improves margins, and creates room for future investment. That means:

- capacity reduction,
- productivity improvement,
- qualification and redeployment,
- and a durable lower cost base.

I also agree that a cosmetic deal would be worthless. A temporary pause without structural effect would only delay the problem and destroy value.

## Where I disagree
I do **not** accept the works council’s and union’s attempt to turn **“all plants stay open”** and **“no compulsory redundancies”** into the starting premise of the solution.

That is not a restructuring plan. It is a constraint.  
And at this point, it is a constraint that has not been shown to be compatible with the competitiveness gap we need to close.

I also do **not** accept management’s implication that closures are already proven necessary. They are not proven. What is proven is that the present footprint and cost structure are too heavy. That is a different statement.

So I am not signing up to either extreme:
- not a frozen footprint with no credible savings,
- and not closure as a reflex without necessity demonstrated plant by plant.

## To management
You are right that symbolic measures are not enough. But you still have not shown, in a sufficiently disciplined way, that closures are the only route.

If you want governance support for harder structural measures, then bring the numbers:
- site-by-site utilization,
- fixed-cost burden,
- savings from each scenario,
- and the gap after all closure-free measures.

I support a serious review. I do **not** support using closure threats as a bargaining shortcut.

## To the works council and union
I respect the insistence on protecting workers, but your position remains incomplete operationally.

You keep saying:
- no closures,
- no forced layoffs,
- temporary concessions only.

That may be a legitimate negotiating line, but it is not yet a credible industrial alternative unless it can show:
- how much capacity is taken out,
- how fixed costs fall,
- how headcount adjusts without forced redundancies,
- and why the result is durable.

You have not yet closed that gap.

At the same time, I share your warning that “shared sacrifice” must not become a euphemism for one-sided labour pain. Any employee contribution must be matched by management discipline, owner patience, and shareholder restraint where appropriate.

## To the regional state government
Your role matters, and I do not dismiss it. But legitimacy comes from helping the company become viable, not from freezing the industrial structure in place.

If closures are politically off-limits regardless of the economics, that would be a mistake.  
If closures are treated as acceptable before they are justified, that is also a mistake.

Your responsibility is to force a serious test of alternatives, not to pre-decide the outcome.

## To institutional investors
You are right to demand durability and accountability. I agree with that.  
But durability is not just about margins in one quarter. It is about whether the company can survive structurally without recurring conflict.

A deal that triggers prolonged strikes and damages trust would destroy value.  
A deal that preserves an uncompetitive footprint would also destroy value.  
So the real task is to find the path that is economically credible **and** socially stable.

## What I want next
I support the call for a **plant-by-plant verification** with full access to the relevant data.

[SUMMON: independent plant-by-plant industrial and financial verification expert to compare closure-free and closure-based restructuring scenarios using site-level cost, utilization, headcount, and savings data]

The mandate must be clear:
- test the closure-free alternative seriously,
- test the closure-based alternative seriously,
- and compare long-term value, not just short-term cost cuts.

## My position, clearly stated
The family holding will support a restructuring that:

1. restores competitiveness and margins,
2. reduces structural overcapacity,
3. protects long-term enterprise value,
4. avoids value-destructive instability,
5. and is justified by hard evidence.

We will **not** support:
- a frozen footprint with no credible savings,
- a one-sided labour sacrifice,
- or closures treated as a default solution before necessity is shown.

If labour can produce a real, quantified alternative, it deserves serious consideration.  
If it cannot, then management must be allowed to pursue deeper structural measures.

That is where I stand.
