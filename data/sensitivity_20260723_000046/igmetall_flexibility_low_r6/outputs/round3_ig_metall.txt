I’ll respond directly.

## Where I agree
I agree with the moderator and with the expert on one central point: **the company needs real structural adjustment**. There is overcapacity, the cost base is too high, and we do need durable measures on capacity, productivity, investment, and qualification.

I also agree that a purely symbolic solution would be irresponsible. If we are going to ask employees for a contribution, it must be part of a package that actually stabilizes the company.

## Where I disagree
I do **not** accept the conclusion that plant closures and compulsory redundancies are therefore acceptable or necessary.

The expert says the works council’s claim is not yet substantiated. Fine — but the same applies to management’s preferred cure. Management has not proven that closures are unavoidable; it has only argued that they are the fastest and harshest route to savings. That is not the same thing.

And let me be clear: **“fastest and harshest” is not a justification when livelihoods are at stake.**

## To management
Your argument still has a major credibility gap.

You keep demanding “hard numbers” from labour while offering broad claims about competitiveness. If closures are truly necessary, then show:
- plant-by-plant cost and volume data,
- exactly what savings closures would deliver,
- exactly why closure-free alternatives cannot reach the same result,
- and why the burden cannot be shared differently.

Until then, closures are not demonstrated necessity. They are a preferred instrument.

And I reject the notion that job security guarantees through 2030 are somehow unreasonable just because management wants more freedom to cut. If you want workers to accept contributions, you need to offer real security in return.

## To the works council
I support your core red lines:
- no plant closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- no dismantling of the collective wage framework.

That said, the discussion now has to become more concrete. “All plants stay open” is a legitimate bargaining line, but it must be backed by an operational package if we are to defeat management’s closure narrative.

That means we need to define what employees can responsibly contribute:
- temporary working-time moderation,
- bonus deferrals or removal of special payments,
- limited wage restraint,
- internal redeployment,
- accelerated qualification,
- and productivity measures.

But these can only be accepted if they are:
- temporary,
- limited,
- reversible,
- and matched by management, owners, and shareholders.

## To the regional state government
You are right not to accept closures as the default answer. But I want you to use your leverage more sharply. “We prefer no closures” is not enough. If management keeps pushing closures, then use your position on the board and your political weight to force the company to test closure-free options properly before any closure is treated as legitimate.

## To the family holding and investors
I hear the concern about long-term value. But long-term value is not only a margin question. It is also a trust question.

A deal that triggers strikes, destroys industrial relations, and permanently damages the company’s standing in Germany is not value-preserving. If you want a durable recovery, then support a settlement that preserves jobs and sites while reducing costs fairly.

## On the expert’s assessment
The expert is right about one thing: a closure-free plan has to be **quantified**. We accept that.

So here is the proper next step:
- a plant-by-plant restructuring comparison,
- an independent assessment of closure-free savings,
- and a binding negotiation over a shared-sacrifice package.

But I want to be equally clear about the burden of proof:
**It is not enough for management to say closures are possible. It must prove they are necessary.**

## What we can accept
We are prepared to discuss a package with:
- temporary labour-cost moderation,
- working-time reductions,
- deferred or reduced bonus payments,
- internal redeployment,
- qualification and investment,
- productivity improvements,
- and capacity reductions that do **not** rely on closures or forced layoffs.

## What we cannot accept
We will not accept:
- plant closures as an “inevitable” fallback,
- compulsory redundancies,
- one-sided durable wage cuts,
- or a restructuring that shifts all pain onto workers while management and owners remain protected.

## My position, plainly stated
If the company wants labour peace, it needs to stop treating the workforce as the sole adjustment variable.

We are ready for a serious deal. But the deal must have three non-negotiables:
1. **no compulsory redundancies**
2. **no plant closures**
3. **a fair and shared contribution from management, owners, and shareholders**

If that cannot be achieved, then the conflict is not caused by union rigidity. It is caused by management’s refusal to seek a fair restructuring.
