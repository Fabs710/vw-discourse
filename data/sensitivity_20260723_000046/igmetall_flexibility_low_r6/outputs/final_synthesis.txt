## 1. Decision context

A large German carmaker is confronting structural overcapacity, weak European demand, and an uncompetitive domestic cost base. Management seeks a durable restructuring that may include plant closures, workforce reductions, and labour-cost cuts; labour-side stakeholders reject closures and compulsory redundancies, insisting on site and job security. Governance is unusually constrained: co-determination gives employees an effective veto, the controlling family and the state shareholder both hold significant blocking power, and major capital-market investors want credible, durable savings.

## 2. Stakeholder assembly

- **Group & brand management**: operational decision-makers driving restructuring.
- **Works Council**: plant-level employee representation with supervisory-board power.
- **Industrial union**: collective-bargaining actor with strike capacity.
- **Regional state government**: anchor shareholder and political protector of the industrial base.
- **Controlling family holding**: controlling owner through governance.
- **Institutional investors**: dispersed capital providers seeking return and credibility.
- **Independent expert**: verification role; not a principal stakeholder, but crucial to the decision process.

## 3. Final positions

### Group & brand management
Management maintains that the current German footprint is structurally too costly and that durable competitiveness requires meaningful capacity reduction, productivity gains, and possibly closures or deeper headcount adjustment. It is open to an expert review, but only if it can test closure-free options against closure-based alternatives on plant-by-plant economics.

### Works Council
The works council’s position hardened into a categorical rejection of plant closures, compulsory redundancies, and any “fallback” closure logic. It will discuss temporary employee contributions only if all German plants remain open, co-determination is preserved, and job security is binding through 2030.

### Industrial union
The union broadly aligns with the works council: it accepts the need for restructuring and some employee contribution, but only if it is temporary, limited, and matched by management/owner/shareholder sacrifice. It rejects closures and forced layoffs as the benchmark of seriousness, and insists on an independent verification process that tests closure-free restructuring first.

### Regional state government
The state wants to protect jobs, sites, and the regional industrial base, while also preserving the company’s long-term viability. It rejects making closures the default answer and supports a balanced, independent plant-level comparison, but stops short of endorsing an unconditional no-closure doctrine without operational proof.

### Controlling family holding
The family is willing to support serious restructuring, including capacity reduction and a lower cost base, but does not want closure threats used as a bargaining shortcut. It supports a rigorous comparison of scenarios and will back deeper measures if the closure-free option cannot close the gap.

### Institutional investors
Investors want a durable, quantified solution that restores margins and reduces structural overcapacity. They do not accept a cosmetic no-closure deal, but also do not support closures without proof of necessity; their position is conditional on credible plant-level evidence and NPV/margin comparison.

### Independent expert
The expert concludes that restructuring is necessary but neither side has yet met the burden of proof. The expert’s consistent recommendation is a common-model, plant-by-plant verification comparing closure-free, mixed, and closure-based scenarios using site-level data and a binding timetable.

## 4. Genuine conflicts (structurally incompatible positions)

1. **No-closure red line vs. closure as possible necessity**  
   Labour-side stakeholders now treat all German plants remaining open as non-negotiable, while management, family, and investors insist closures must remain available if justified by the numbers.

2. **No compulsory redundancies vs. structural headcount reduction**  
   Labour rejects forced layoffs categorically; management says some structural headcount adjustment may be unavoidable if equivalently effective alternatives fail.

3. **Proof standard conflict**  
   Labour wants necessity for closures proven first; management wants equivalence of closure-free savings proven first. Neither side will move without the other producing evidence.

4. **Temporal protection vs. permanent restructuring**  
   Labour accepts only temporary concessions with binding guarantees, while capital-side actors want durable cost reduction and flexibility that extends beyond a temporary pause.

## 5. Areas of convergence

- The current situation is unsustainable.
- Restructuring is necessary.
- Investment in future products, technology, and qualification is required.
- Redeployment, working-time flexibility, productivity improvement, and negotiated solutions are preferable to unilateral action.
- Any employee contribution should be matched by contributions from management, owners, and/or shareholders.
- Prolonged industrial conflict would destroy value.
- Plant-by-plant verification is the right procedural next step.
- A purely symbolic compromise will fail.

## 6. Key trade-offs for the decision-maker

1. **Job and site security vs. durable competitiveness**  
   Stronger guarantees improve trust but may limit flexibility needed to reduce costs.

2. **Speed vs. legitimacy**  
   Closures can produce faster reported savings, but a negotiated, verified alternative may be more legitimate and less conflict-prone.

3. **Breadth of restructuring vs. social stability**  
   Deeper cuts may improve financial metrics, but increase strike risk, political backlash, and governance strain.

4. **Temporary concessions vs. structural change**  
   Temporary labour sacrifices may bridge the gap, but may not solve the underlying cost problem without matching capital-side restraint.

5. **Fallback closure authority vs. bargaining trust**  
   Keeping closures on the table may preserve strategic leverage, but it is also the main source of mistrust and negotiation breakdown.

## 7. Integrative pathways

### A. Sequenced verification-first deal
A binding two-stage process: first, an independent plant-by-plant verification of closure-free measures; second, only if the gap remains, comparison with limited closure options under the same assumptions. This is the cleanest bridge between labour’s demand that closures not be the default and capital’s demand for proof.

### B. Time-limited no-closure moratorium
A temporary moratorium on closures and compulsory redundancies, paired with immediate employee contributions, investment commitments, and milestone-based review. This gives labour immediate security while preserving a structured reopening of the issue if the savings do not materialize.

### C. Mixed restructuring with site differentiation
Rather than one blanket rule, classify sites into core, adjustment, and review categories. Core sites get protection and investment; adjustment sites get capacity and product-mix changes; review sites are revisited only if objective metrics are not met.

### D. Shared-sacrifice package
Employee contributions are matched by management restraint, shareholder patience, reduced distributions, and state facilitation/training support. This could reduce the perception that workers are bearing the adjustment alone.

## 8. Open questions

- What is the exact plant-by-plant cost and utilization picture?
- Which sites are structurally uncompetitive versus temporarily underutilized?
- How much savings can be generated by non-closure measures alone?
- What are the one-time costs and conflict risks of closures?
- What level of labour contribution is acceptable if matched by management and capital-side concessions?
- What milestones would trigger reconsideration of deeper structural measures?
- Can the supervisory-board coalition sustain a decision that is economically credible but politically costly?

## 9. Recommended next step

Proceed immediately to an **independent, plant-by-plant verification** with a binding timetable, common assumptions, and access to site-level cost, utilization, headcount, and investment data. The mandate should compare:
1. closure-free restructuring,
2. mixed restructuring,
3. closure-based restructuring,

and include NPV, margin impact, employment effects, one-time closure costs, and industrial-conflict risk. No final decision on closures should be taken before that comparison is complete.

## 10. Emergent insights that only appeared through multi-turn interaction

- The real deadlock is not “whether restructuring is needed,” but **who must prove what first**.
- Labour’s position evolved from conditional openness to a **hard no-closure doctrine**; that shift became clear only after repeated bargaining pressure and clarification of fallback options.
- Management’s stance also sharpened: it is not merely asking for sacrifice, but for **quantified durability**; symbolic concessions are now explicitly insufficient.
- The state and family are not neutral bystanders: both want to protect the industrial base, but neither will endorse a deal that cannot survive the numbers.
- The expert contributions clarified that **neither side has yet met the burden of proof**; this is the strongest factual finding in the interaction.
- The most consequential procedural issue is not the level of sacrifice, but whether the verification process is designed as a genuine test or as a pretext for pre-committed outcomes.
- A closure moratorium or phased approach is attractive only if it is not just a delay tactic; this became evident only once labour rejected “conditional flexibility” as staged insecurity.
- The discussion revealed a deeper governance tension: the company’s formal consensus structure makes “force” less available than in ordinary industrial disputes, so legitimacy and evidence matter more than positional power alone.
