I’ll be direct.

I agree with everyone on one point: **the status quo is not sustainable**. The company needs a real restructuring, not a symbolic one. If margins do not improve and capacity stays misaligned, long-term value erodes for everyone — including the family holding.

But I do **not** accept the current labour-side position as a complete answer, because it still does not show how the required savings and capacity adjustment will be achieved at scale without closures or deeper structural change.

## Where I agree
I agree with:
- the need for a negotiated solution rather than a unilateral clash,
- the need for investment in future products, technology, and qualification,
- the importance of internal redeployment where possible,
- and the need to avoid a prolonged industrial war that destroys value.

I also agree that any employee contribution should not be one-sided. Management, owners, and the company itself must all carry responsibility.

## Where I disagree
I disagree with the idea that:
- all German plants must remain open as a premise,
- compulsory redundancies can simply be ruled out in advance,
- and site guarantees to 2030 can be demanded before the economics are fixed.

Those are not governing solutions. They are constraints that may feel desirable, but they do not yet answer the cost problem.

I also disagree with management if it thinks that merely invoking “market reality” is enough. If closures are being pursued, they must be justified as a last resort and backed by hard numbers. A threat without proof is not stewardship.

## My position
From the family holding’s perspective, the standard is simple:

1. **We need durable competitiveness, not temporary relief.**
2. **We need measurable capacity reduction and cost reduction.**
3. **We need labour peace, but not at any price.**
4. **We need to preserve the enterprise’s long-term value and governance stability.**

So I am prepared to support a closure-free solution **only if** it genuinely closes the gap:
- in labour cost,
- in capacity,
- and in operational flexibility.

If that cannot be shown, then plant closures remain on the table as a last resort — not because we prefer them, but because we cannot support a structure that remains permanently uncompetitive.

## What I need to see next
The next step should be a **quantified alternative package**, not more declarations.

I want to see:
- how much capacity is removed,
- what durable savings result,
- what employee contribution looks like,
- what management is contributing,
- and how the company’s margins recover on a sustainable basis.

If labour wants to rule out closures completely, then the alternative must be just as real as closures would be. If not, then the discussion is not about restructuring anymore — it is about postponing the reckoning.

### To the Works Council and union
Your insistence on protecting workers is understandable. But if your line means no closures, no compulsory redundancies, and no meaningful structural change, then you are not protecting the company — you are protecting an uncompetitive status quo. That is not acceptable to me.

### To management
Do not use closures casually. If you want support from governance, prove necessity and proportionality. A hard restructuring must still be a credible one.

### To the state government
I understand your concern for the region, but long-term regional stability depends on a competitive company. If the business remains structurally weak, the jobs you protect today may be the ones lost tomorrow.

### To investors
I share the concern about value erosion, but value is also destroyed by industrial conflict and governance deadlock. We need a settlement that is durable, not just harsh.

## Bottom line
I am open to a negotiated deal. I am **not** open to a settlement that freezes structural overcapacity and calls it compromise.

Bring me a package that restores competitiveness in hard numbers. If you cannot, then you should not expect governance support for a closure-free outcome that leaves the company weak.
