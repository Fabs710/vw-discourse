We agree on the diagnosis far more than on the remedy.

## Where we agree
- The status quo is not acceptable.
- The company needs a **durable** restructuring, not a symbolic one.
- Capacity has to come down.
- Labour costs have to become competitive.
- Investment in future products, technology, and qualification is necessary.
- Prolonged industrial conflict would destroy value.

That is the right starting point. From a shareholder perspective, the priority is not ideological: it is restoring margins, earnings quality, and capital discipline.

## Where we disagree
### 1) A blanket “no closures” line is not credible
To the Works Council, union, and state: I understand the political and social reasons for rejecting closures. But from an investor standpoint, a categorical ban is not a solution unless you can show a package of equal scale and durability.

So far, what I hear is mostly:
- job guarantees,
- site guarantees,
- temporary contributions,
- working-time measures,
- redeployment.

Those may help, but they do **not yet prove** that the cost base will actually become competitive on a sustained basis.

If the company still carries excess capacity and structurally high labour costs, then shareholders will be left with lower margins, lower valuation, and recurring conflict. I will not support that.

### 2) Management still has not proven that closures are necessary
To management: I do **not** accept closures as a reflex. If you want capital-market support for a harsh restructuring, then you need to show hard evidence that:
- closure-free measures cannot close the gap,
- the proposed savings are real and durable,
- and the restructuring will restore returns on sales, not just shift the pain around.

Right now, your case sounds directionally right but not yet sufficiently evidenced. “Market reality” is not a substitute for a quantified plan.

### 3) Compulsory redundancies are not acceptable unless truly unavoidable
I am open to tough measures. I am not open to a plan that jumps immediately to forced layoffs if a serious alternative has not been fully tested.

But the converse is also true: if labour-side actors cannot deliver a credible substitute with comparable savings, then refusing redundancies forever just preserves an uncompetitive structure. That is also unacceptable.

## What would move us
We would support a deal if it credibly delivers all of the following:
- **durable cost reduction**
- **meaningful capacity reduction**
- **a clear path back to competitive margins**
- **no prolonged strike damage**
- **transparent milestones and accountability**
- **investment linked to the restructuring**

That can happen with closures or without closures. The instrument matters less than the outcome.

## On “shared sacrifice”
We support shared sacrifice, but only if it is real.

That means:
- employee contribution should be substantive, not symbolic;
- management should commit to operational discipline and execution credibility;
- owners should accept that value preservation may require patience and governance restraint;
- and the state should help facilitate a solution rather than simply freeze the problem.

But “shared” does **not** mean shareholders fund an expensive compromise that leaves the company structurally weak. That would be a bad deal dressed up as social balance.

## My bottom line
- I am **not** aligned with a closure-at-all-costs stance.
- I am **not** aligned with a closure-free stance that cannot demonstrate equivalent savings.
- I am **not** aligned with cosmetic concessions or prolonged conflict.

What I want is simple: a **quantified, durable restructuring package** that restores competitiveness and margins.

If labour can produce a closure-free plan that genuinely matches the economics, I will support it. If not, then management must be able to justify tougher measures. But no one should expect shareholders to back an arrangement that leaves the business uncompetitive and calls that compromise.
