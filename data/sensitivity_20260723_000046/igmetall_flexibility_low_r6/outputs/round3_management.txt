Let me respond directly.

## Where I agree
I agree with everyone on one basic point: **the status quo is not sustainable**.  
We do need restructuring. We do need capacity reduction, productivity gains, qualification, and investment in future products and technology.

I also agree that a cosmetic deal would be worthless. If we do this, it must be **durable** and it must restore competitiveness.

## Where I disagree
I do **not** accept the central labour-side claim that we can simply keep every German plant open, rule out compulsory redundancies, and still close the cost gap by goodwill, working-time tweaks, and temporary concessions.

That is not a restructuring strategy. It is a refusal to confront the scale of the problem.

The expert has confirmed the key point: **a closure-free package is not proven sufficient**. That matters. The burden is not on management to pretend every plant can be preserved. The burden is on labour to show a quantified alternative that actually delivers comparable, durable savings and capacity reduction.

## To the Works Council and union
You keep saying “show us why closures are necessary.” Fair enough. But so far, what you have offered is a political red line, not an industrial answer.

Let me be precise:
- **No closures**
- **No compulsory redundancies**
- **No erosion of job security**
- **Temporary concessions only**

That combination does **not** close the structural gap we face.

Internal redeployment and working-time flexibility are useful tools, but they do not eliminate excess fixed costs at the scale required. Bonus deferrals and limited wage restraint may help at the margin, but they do not restore the brand’s return-on-sales target by 2030.

If you want to rule out closures entirely, then bring me a plant-by-plant plan that shows:
- how much capacity comes out,
- how much cost comes out,
- how the fixed-cost base is structurally lowered,
- and why this is at least as durable as a closure/redundancy package.

Until then, “all plants stay open” is not a solution. It is a demand that management carry the full burden of a crisis caused by overcapacity and an uncompetitive cost base.

## To the regional state government
I appreciate that you want to protect jobs and the industrial base. So do I — but only if there is a viable business to protect.

A state shareholder cannot expect us to preserve every structure regardless of economics and then still compete against lower-cost rivals. That would amount to subsidising uncompetitiveness.

If you want to defend the German industrial base, then you need to support a restructuring that actually makes it viable. That means not just resisting closures, but helping to secure a settlement that produces real savings.

## To the controlling family holding
You are right about one thing: value is destroyed by industrial conflict.  
But value is also destroyed by procrastination.

I will not support a settlement that leaves the company structurally weak in order to avoid hard decisions today. The family’s role should be to back a credible industrial reset, not to block the instruments needed to achieve it.

## To investors
You are right to demand durability and accountability. But durability does not mean preserving all existing sites by decree. Durability means a cost base that can survive the market.

If labour can present a credible alternative that closes the gap, I will listen. If not, then management must retain the option to go further, including closures and deeper headcount reductions, because preserving the current footprint would simply postpone the reckoning.

## On the expert assessment
The expert has clarified something important: a no-closure deal is **possible in theory**, but only if it is backed by real numbers showing equivalence. We do not have that evidence.

So I am not accepting the works council’s position as operationally credible at this stage.

And I am also not saying closures are automatically the first and only answer. I am saying something much simpler:

**No one has yet produced a closure-free package that demonstrably solves the cost and capacity problem.**

That is why closures remain on the table.

## My position, clearly
Management is prepared to negotiate, but not to accept a settlement that:

- freezes all German sites,
- rules out compulsory redundancies in advance,
- and leaves us without the structural savings needed to restore competitiveness.

A real solution must deliver:
- meaningful and durable labour-cost reduction,
- genuine capacity reduction,
- operational flexibility,
- and a credible path back to target margins.

If a closure-free alternative can match that, it deserves serious consideration.  
If it cannot, then we cannot pretend that preserving everything is responsible stewardship.

## What I want next
I want a **plant-by-plant quantified alternative** from labour and an independent verification of whether it truly closes the gap. Not general statements. Not moral pressure. Numbers.

And if that exercise shows that the gap cannot be closed without closures or equivalent structural measures, then we need to stop pretending otherwise.
