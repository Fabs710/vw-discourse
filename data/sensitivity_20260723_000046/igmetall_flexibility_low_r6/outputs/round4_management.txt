I’ll be equally direct.

## Where I agree
I agree with the central diagnosis from almost everyone: **the current structure is not sustainable**.  
We need a real restructuring, not a cosmetic one. That means:
- capacity reduction,
- productivity improvement,
- investment in future products and technology,
- and a cost base that can compete in Europe and globally.

I also agree that a purely symbolic deal would fail. If we are going to negotiate, it must produce **durable** savings and a credible path back to target margins.

## Where I disagree
I do **not** accept the repeated attempt to make “all plants stay open” and “no compulsory redundancies” the starting premise of a serious industrial solution.

That is not a restructuring plan. It is a constraint on reality.

The company has:
- structural overcapacity in Germany,
- a cost base above target and above competitors,
- and a brand that is missing its return-on-sales objective.

That combination cannot be repaired by slogans about trust or by temporary concessions that leave the fixed-cost base intact.

## To the Works Council
You say management has not proven that closures are necessary. Fair enough — but you have also not proven that a closure-free model can deliver equivalent savings at scale.

You keep asserting:
- no plant closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- no erosion of job security,
- temporary concessions only.

That may be your bargaining line, but it is **not yet an industrial answer**.

The expert assessments made one thing clear: a closure-free package is only credible if it is backed by **plant-by-plant numbers** showing:
- capacity reduction,
- fixed-cost reduction,
- headcount impact,
- productivity gains,
- and durability through 2030.

So far, that evidence has not been delivered.

And let me be frank:  
if you want to rule out closures, you must show how the cost and capacity gap is actually closed. “Keep every site open” is not a financial plan.

## To the trade union
I hear your point on fairness and shared sacrifice. I accept that employee contributions must be matched by management, owners, and shareholders. That is not the issue.

The issue is that you are still trying to protect the workforce from the very structural adjustment the business needs.

Temporary working-time moderation, bonus deferrals, and limited wage restraint can help. But they do **not** solve structural overcapacity. They do not restore competitiveness by themselves.

And I reject the idea that compulsory redundancies can simply be ruled out in advance regardless of the economics. If no alternative closes the gap, then refusing all structural headcount adjustment is not responsible.

## To the regional state government
I respect your responsibility for jobs, sites, and regional stability. But the state cannot use its position to freeze the industrial structure in place.

If the company’s cost base remains uncompetitive, then preserving every site at any price is not industrial policy — it is delay.

You are right to demand a proper comparison between closure-based and closure-free options. But that comparison must be honest. It must not be designed to preclude hard decisions before the numbers are even known.

## To the controlling family holding
You are right that value destruction through industrial conflict would be serious. I agree.

But value is also destroyed by refusing to confront overcapacity. A company that preserves all structures out of caution will simply lose competitiveness faster.

I am not asking for closures for their own sake. I am saying they must remain available if the numbers show they are required. Governance support is needed for a restructuring that actually restores the business, not one that delays the adjustment until the problem is worse.

## To institutional investors
You are right to demand durability and accountability. I agree with that.

But durability does not mean preserving the current footprint. It means a structure that can generate returns, fund investment, and survive competition. If the closure-free route can truly do that, prove it. If not, then the business has to be allowed to take deeper structural measures.

## On the experts and the verification debate
I support the expert review — but I want to be very clear about the mandate.

[SUMMON: independent plant-by-plant industrial and financial verification expert to compare closure-free and closure-based restructuring scenarios using site-level cost, utilization, headcount, and savings data, with a binding timetable]

The point of the review is **not** to create a permanent shield against closures. The point is to establish whether a closure-free plan genuinely closes the gap.

If it does, fine — then we have a basis for a settlement.  
If it does not, then we stop pretending that “no closures” is a credible solution.

## My position, plainly
Management will not sign up to:
- a frozen footprint,
- a permanent no-closure doctrine,
- or a deal that leaves us structurally uncompetitive.

What we need is:
1. **material and durable cost reduction**
2. **meaningful capacity reduction**
3. **a genuine labour-cost contribution**
4. **a credible path to restoring margins and funding future investment**

If labour can produce an alternative that achieves that without closures, we will consider it seriously.  
If not, then closures and deeper structural measures remain on the table, because protecting every site regardless of economics is not stewardship — it is denial.

I’ll be blunt: we are not here to negotiate away the restructuring. We are here to shape it into something that actually restores competitiveness.
