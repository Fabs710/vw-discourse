I will be direct.

## Where I agree
I agree with everyone on the fundamental diagnosis: **the status quo is not sustainable**.  
The company needs a restructuring that restores competitiveness, improves margins, and creates room for future investment.

I also agree that:
- this cannot be a cosmetic exercise,
- capacity reduction is necessary,
- productivity improvements matter,
- qualification and redeployment matter,
- and prolonged industrial conflict would destroy value.

So I am not disputing the need for change. I am disputing whether the current labour-side position is a credible industrial solution.

## Where I disagree
I do **not** accept the premise that:
- all German plants must remain open,
- compulsory redundancies can be ruled out in advance,
- and binding guarantees through 2030 should be the starting point.

Those are not solutions to the cost problem. They are constraints.  
If they can be reconciled with a genuinely competitive structure, fine. But no one has shown that yet.

I also do **not** accept management’s implication that closures are already proven necessary. They are not. What has been shown is that the current structure is too expensive and too bulky. That is not the same as proving the only answer is plant closure.

## To the Works Council and union
I respect the insistence on protecting workers, but I do not accept the conclusion that “no closures, no forced layoffs” is enough on its own.

The expert has made the point clearly: a closure-free package is **not yet proven sufficient**. That matters.  
If you want to rule out closures, then you need to bring a **quantified operating plan** showing how the cost and capacity gap is closed in a durable way.

What I have heard so far is a set of legitimate red lines, but not yet a complete industrial alternative.  
Temporary concessions, working-time changes, and redeployment may help. But unless they materially and durably lower the fixed-cost base and capacity footprint, they do not solve the problem.

So I disagree with the claim that management is simply asking workers to pay for a strategic failure. There is a real economic challenge here, and it cannot be wished away by moral argument.

## To management
I agree with you on one thing: we cannot pretend that symbolic measures will fix structural overcapacity.

But I do **not** give you a free pass to use closures as leverage without proving necessity.  
If you want governance support for harder measures, you must present:
- plant-by-plant economics,
- the savings from closure scenarios,
- the savings gap after closure-free options,
- and the case for why less disruptive measures cannot close that gap.

Right now, the burden of proof remains incomplete on your side too.

## To the regional state government
Your concern for sites, jobs, and regional stability is legitimate. I understand it. But we cannot confuse preserving the footprint with preserving enterprise value.

A state-backed insistence that all sites remain untouched, regardless of economics, would risk freezing inefficiency into the structure. That would damage the company and ultimately the workforce.

So I agree with your demand for a balanced review. I disagree if that means closures are politically off-limits regardless of the numbers. They cannot be ruled in or out by sentiment alone.

## To investors
You are right to insist on durability, accountability, and margins.  
But durability is not achieved by forcing a conflict to its harshest conclusion. A settlement that destroys trust and triggers prolonged strikes would also destroy value.

I do not share a simplistic “cut harder, cut faster” view. If closures are pursued, they must be justified, proportionate, and clearly superior to alternatives — not just the quickest route.

## My position
The family holding’s standard is straightforward:

1. **We need durable competitiveness.**
2. **We need a structural reduction in capacity and cost.**
3. **We need labour peace if at all possible, but not at any price.**
4. **We must not permanently impair the enterprise’s value or governance stability.**

That means:
- I am open to a closure-free solution **only if** it closes the gap in hard numbers.
- I am open to closures **only if** they are proven necessary and proportionate.
- I am not open to a settlement that preserves the current structure and calls it compromise.

## What is still missing
What is missing is not more statements of principle. It is a **plant-by-plant comparison** of:
- current costs,
- utilization,
- volume outlook,
- savings from closure-free measures,
- savings from closures,
- and the long-term value impact of each path.

That is the only way to move this beyond rhetoric.

## Bottom line
I will support a restructuring that restores competitiveness and protects long-term value.  
I will not support:
- a frozen footprint with no credible savings,
- a one-sided labour sacrifice,
- or a politically comfortable solution that leaves the company structurally weak.

If labour can produce a real, quantified alternative, it deserves serious consideration.  
If it cannot, then management must be able to justify deeper structural measures.

But nobody should expect the family holding to endorse a deal that trades away the company’s future for temporary peace.
