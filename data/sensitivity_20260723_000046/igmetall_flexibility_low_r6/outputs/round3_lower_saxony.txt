I will be equally direct.

## Where I agree
I agree with management, the family, and the investors on one point: **the status quo is not sustainable**.  
This company needs a real restructuring, not a symbolic one. We do need:
- investment in future products and technology,
- qualification and redeployment,
- productivity improvements,
- and a meaningful reduction in the cost base.

I also agree with the expert that a credible solution has to be **quantified plant by plant**. General declarations are not enough.

## Where I disagree
I **do not** accept the way management is trying to turn “hard numbers” into a one-way demand on labour while keeping closures and forced layoffs as an implicit threat.

And I **do not** accept the idea that the only serious path is one that risks gutting the company’s German footprint.

As the state shareholder, I have a legitimate responsibility to protect:
- jobs in the state,
- sites in the state,
- and the company’s role as a regional economic anchor.

That is not sentimentality. It is long-term industrial policy and stewardship.

## To management
You have still not shown that plant closures are necessary, only that they are convenient from a cost-cutting perspective.

That is not enough.

If you want us to consider deeper structural measures, then you need to demonstrate — plant by plant — why a closure-free restructuring cannot achieve the required savings and capacity reduction. Until then, closures remain a **proposal**, not a proven necessity.

What I will not support:
- closures as a bargaining lever,
- compulsory redundancies as a routine fallback,
- or a restructuring that offloads nearly all the risk onto workers and the region.

If the company’s answer to every competitiveness issue is to shrink its domestic base, then the company will damage the very industrial strength it depends on.

## To the Works Council and union
I agree with you that:
- no worker should be forced out as the first answer,
- the burden cannot be one-sided,
- and temporary employee contributions must be matched by contributions from management, owners, and shareholders.

That said, I will be frank with you too:  
a political line of “all sites stay open” is only defensible if it can survive the industrial test. I am not prepared to defend a closure-free position forever **without** seeing a serious operational plan.

So yes, your red lines are legitimate. But they have to be matched by a plan that is more than a statement of refusal.

## To the controlling family holding
You speak of long-term value, and I take that seriously. Long-term value is not created by forcing through the harshest possible restructuring at the first opportunity.

It is created by:
- preserving trust,
- avoiding industrial conflict,
- securing the company’s domestic production base,
- and making adjustment in a socially sustainable way.

If you back a plan that destroys trust in the workforce and in the regions where this company is anchored, you will weaken the company beyond the immediate savings you extract.

## To investors
You are right that durability matters. But durability is not only a question of cost. It is also a question of governance stability, labour peace, and regional legitimacy.

A solution that triggers conflict, strikes, and long-term damage to the company’s reputation in Germany is not a durable solution.

## What I want next
I support the expert’s call for a **plant-by-plant verification**. But the mandate must be balanced.

I want the review to examine:
1. the savings from closure and redundancy scenarios,
2. the savings from closure-free alternatives,
3. the regional and employment effects,
4. the investment requirements to keep sites competitive,
5. and the long-term risks of industrial conflict.

So I support the summoning of a specialist — but not as a fig leaf for closures. The task must be to test whether the company can be restructured **without sacrificing the German industrial footprint**.

## My position, clearly stated
I am open to a serious restructuring. I am **not** open to:
- plant closures as the default answer,
- compulsory redundancies as an easy fallback,
- or a deal that treats the state and the workforce as the shock absorber for management’s failure to solve the cost problem differently.

A settlement is only acceptable if it:
- protects core jobs and sites in Germany,
- preserves the state’s co-determination and veto role,
- delivers real competitiveness gains,
- and does so in a way that is socially and politically sustainable.

That is the standard I will apply.
