We are now at the point where the debate has to be judged on **substance**, not pressure.

## Where I agree
I agree with everyone on three things:
1. **The company needs real restructuring now.**
2. **The current cost base is too high.**
3. **We need a quantified, plant-by-plant comparison**, not slogans.

I also agree that a package must be **durable** and must show up in the **P&L**. We did not come to the table to defend inefficiency.

## Where I disagree — and this is the decisive point

### To management
You keep repeating that closures remain on the table unless a non-closure package is “economically equivalent.” That sounds neutral, but in practice it means: **the workforce must prove it can save the company while you keep every hard instrument available against it**.

That is not a fair bargaining basis.

We already put a real counter-proposal on the table:
- working-time flexibility,
- wage moderation,
- bonus sacrifice,
- voluntary exits,
- capacity reduction,
- productivity measures,
- and an employee financial contribution.

So no, it is not true that labour is just saying “no” to restructuring. We are offering a restructuring path. What we are **not** offering is a path where workers pay with wages, security, and jobs while closures and compulsory redundancies remain the backstop.

And let me be explicit:  
**No compulsory redundancies. No plant closures. No dismantling of the collective wage framework.**

Those are not starting preferences. They are hard limits.

### To investors
You are right that temporary gestures are useless. We agree. You are also right that savings must be durable. But you are wrong if you think **binding job and site security is automatically value-destructive**.

If you want durable savings, then you need labour peace, predictable production, and know-how retention. A conflict that runs for years will cost money too. So the question is not whether workers contribute — we already said we will. The question is whether management and owners are prepared to contribute **equally seriously**.

A non-closure package can be economically sound if it includes:
- real capacity reduction,
- real labour-cost contribution,
- management overhead cuts,
- owner patience,
- and binding investment commitments.

What I will not accept is a package where only the workforce gives up certainty while capital keeps maximum flexibility.

### To the controlling family
You keep talking about stewardship and long-term value. Then act like long-term stewards.

Long-term stewardship does **not** mean preserving the right to close plants whenever the next quarter gets difficult. It means building a settlement that preserves the company’s productive base, the workforce’s trust, and the ability to invest in the future.

If you push for a deal that freezes worker concessions in place while keeping closures as an ever-present threat, you will not get a durable settlement. You will get anger, conflict, and eventually lower performance.

### To the regional state government
I appreciate that you reject closure as a default reflex. But “not default” is not enough.

You have a blocking role, and you should use it to force a **serious non-closure restructuring**, not just to delay the closure debate. That means:
- capacity reduction,
- redeployment,
- retraining,
- working-time adjustments,
- and investment commitments.

But I have to be honest: if the state supports a settlement that still leaves closures as the implied threat, then it is not protecting the regional industrial base — it is just postponing the fight.

## On the trigger idea
A trigger mechanism is only acceptable if it does **not** function as a backdoor to plant closures.

I will not sign up to a structure where:
- workers make concessions now,
- job and site guarantees are weakened now,
- and later management can simply reopen closures if targets are missed.

That is not a compromise. That is a delayed ultimatum.

If there is a trigger, then it must trigger:
- additional management contribution,
- additional owner contribution,
- further non-closure measures,
- and a fresh negotiation on the economics.

It must **not** be a hidden closure clause.

## What workers are prepared to contribute
We have been clear from the start:
- working-time flexibility is possible,
- wage moderation is possible,
- bonus sacrifice is possible,
- voluntary departures are possible,
- capacity reduction is possible,
- and productivity improvement is possible.

But those contributions are conditional on a fair package:
- **no forced layoffs**
- **no plant closures**
- **site security**
- **collective wage framework preserved**
- and **real contributions from management and owners**

That is the only kind of burden-sharing that is sustainable.

## What is still missing
Management keeps asking us to accept “economic equivalence” as if that were obvious. It is not obvious. It has to be proven. So bring the numbers — but not in a way that assumes closures are the benchmark by default.

I want the **plant-by-plant bridge** with:
1. current cost gap,
2. savings from non-closure measures,
3. savings from management overhead cuts,
4. savings from owner-supported transition measures,
5. remaining gap after a no-closure / no-forced-redundancy package,
6. and the comparison to closure-based restructuring.

If that model shows a gap, then the question is not “why won’t labour accept closures?”  
The question is: **what additional contribution are management and owners prepared to make before asking workers to surrender jobs and sites?**

## My bottom line
We are ready for a durable settlement. But it must be a **shared-sacrifice package**, not a workforce-only correction.

So I will repeat our line clearly:
- **No compulsory redundancies.**
- **No plant closures.**
- **No dismantling of the collective wage framework.**
- **Yes to wage restraint, flexibility, bonus sacrifice, voluntary exits, and capacity reduction** if management, owners, and shareholders contribute fairly and the package protects jobs and sites.

If the others insist that closures remain the price of cooperation, then they are not negotiating a fair restructuring. They are demanding that workers pay for strategic failure. That is a line we will not cross.
