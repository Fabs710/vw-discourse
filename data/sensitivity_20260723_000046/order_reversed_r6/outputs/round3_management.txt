We are now at the point where slogans must give way to enforceable outcomes.

## First: what I agree with
I agree with everyone on one fundamental point: **this company needs a real structural reset now**.  
No one in this room seriously believes the current cost base is sustainable. No one should pretend that delay, cosmetic savings, or another round of vague promises will restore competitiveness.

I also agree that:
- the decision must be **durable**, not temporary;
- it must be **quantified**;
- and it must be judged against **return on sales, cash impact, and execution certainty**.

That is exactly the standard management is applying.

## Where I disagree sharply

### To the union and works council
Your position still amounts to:  
**no closures, no compulsory redundancies, full site guarantees through 2030, and meaningful savings anyway.**

That is not a restructuring proposition. That is a constraint package that may make restructuring impossible.

I need to be blunt: if you preclude the tools that create structural cost reduction, then you are asking the company to remain above target cost and hope the market forgives us. It will not.

You keep saying the workforce should not bear the whole burden. I agree. But that does **not** mean the workforce can veto every instrument that actually lowers fixed costs. If the alternative package does not produce enough savings, then it fails — regardless of how acceptable it sounds politically.

### To the regional state government
I understand your responsibility for the industrial base. But you are still treating closures as if they must be ruled out in advance. They cannot be ruled out in advance.

If a site is structurally uncompetitive, then preserving it indefinitely is not regional stewardship — it is postponing decline while the rest of the company carries the cost. That is not acceptable to management, owners, or investors, and it should not be acceptable to the state either.

You say the burden is on management to prove closure is unavoidable. Fine. We accept that burden. But once the numbers show a gap that non-closure measures cannot close, then the state must not block the only remaining economically serious solution.

### To the institutional investors and the family holding
You are right to insist on numbers, and management will provide them. But I also want to be equally clear: if the non-closure package proves too weak, then we should not hide behind governance language to avoid the hard answer.

The company does not need polite ambiguity. It needs enough savings to restore competitiveness. If that means closures or deeper capacity cuts, then so be it.

## The economic reality
Here is the core issue:

We need a package that delivers:
- a **permanent reduction in labour cost**,
- a **permanent reduction in excess capacity**,
- and a **credible path back to target margins**.

Temporary wage restraint, voluntary-only exits, or soft protection clauses do not solve the problem unless they are large enough and durable enough to match the economics of a deeper restructuring.

If the non-closure route cannot close the gap, then it is not a solution. It is a delay mechanism.

## What management is prepared to do
We are prepared to table a **plant-by-plant bridge** that shows:
- current cost gap,
- annual savings by measure,
- one-off restructuring costs,
- timing of savings,
- ROS impact,
- and execution risk.

But let me be clear about what that exercise is for:  
it is **not** to justify preserving every site at any price. It is to identify whether a non-closure package can truly deliver an economically equivalent result. If it cannot, then closures remain on the table.

## On the proposed compromise paths

### 1) Conditional non-closure package with a trigger
This is only acceptable if the trigger is hard, objective, and near-term.  
I do **not** support a vague “review later” mechanism that simply defers the same conflict.

If we do this, the trigger must mean something real:
- if savings miss target,
- if utilization stays too low,
- if margins remain below threshold,
- then deeper structural measures are automatically reopened.

Anything less is just time-buying.

### 2) Site protection with partial flexibility
This can be part of a solution, but only if the flexibility is substantial enough to create real savings.  
If “partial flexibility” means only mild working-time adjustments and a few voluntary departures, then it will not be enough.

### 3) Tripartite burden-sharing
Yes, burden-sharing is necessary. But burden-sharing must show up in the P&L.

That means:
- workforce contribution through wages, working time, and flexibility,
- management contribution through overhead cuts and simplification,
- owner contribution through patience and support for the transition,
- and if needed, state support for implementation and transition.

But burden-sharing does **not** mean the company agrees to a structurally weak package just to keep peace in the room.

## My bottom line
Management will support a **non-closure path only if it is economically equivalent** to stronger structural measures.

That means:
- enough savings,
- enough permanence,
- enough enforceability,
- and enough impact on margins and cash.

If those conditions are not met, then we will not accept a cosmetic compromise that preserves every site but leaves the company uncompetitive.

So the next step is clear:  
**bring the plant-by-plant bridge, with hard numbers and clear failure thresholds.**

And once those numbers are on the table, I expect the other stakeholders to engage with the economics — not just with their preferred outcomes.
