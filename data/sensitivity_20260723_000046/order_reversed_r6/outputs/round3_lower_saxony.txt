We welcome the shift to a more concrete, quantified discussion. That is the right direction. But I need to be equally clear: **the state will not endorse a restructuring that achieves competitiveness by gutting regional employment or making plant closure the default instrument.** Our statutory role and our responsibility for the regional economy are not decorative.

## Where I agree
I agree with management, investors, and the family holding on one essential point:  
**the company needs real restructuring, not delay or cosmetics.**

I also agree that:
- the current cost base is too high,
- temporary gestures are insufficient,
- the final package must be durable and enforceable,
- and the numbers must be shown plant by plant.

If the company has structural overcapacity, then we should not pretend otherwise. But overcapacity is not the same as a license to sacrifice the regional industrial base.

## Where I disagree

### To management
You still have not proven that closures are unavoidable. Saying they are “on the table” is not a business case.

If you want the state to even contemplate painful measures, you must show:
- the **actual cost gap** by site,
- the savings from a **non-closure package**,
- the one-off costs of any closure,
- the timing of the savings,
- and the impact on labour peace, supply chains, and future investment.

Until then, I will not accept a framing in which closure is treated as the economically serious option and everything else as sentimentality. That is not evidence; it is a preference.

### To institutional investors
I hear your insistence on economic equivalence. That is fair in principle. But you are too quick to dismiss binding job and site protection as value-destructive.

In a company like this, legitimacy, labour peace, know-how retention, and regional stability are part of the value equation. A package that provokes years of conflict can destroy more value than it creates. So no, I do not accept a purely short-term flexibility test as the only standard.

### To the controlling family
I appreciate the long-term horizon, and I share the view that preserving every legacy arrangement is not a strategy. But I reject the implication that the only credible long-term decision is one that keeps closures available as a disciplinary threat.

This company is a regional anchor. If you permanently weaken the German footprint, you do not merely “optimize” the portfolio. You damage the base that has underpinned the company’s strength for decades.

### To the union and works council
I welcome your willingness to discuss real restructuring without cosmetic delay. But I also need clarity: if you insist on **no closures, no compulsory redundancies, and full site guarantees through 2030**, then you are setting a very high bar. I can support strong employment protection, but only if it is paired with genuine, measurable contributions and not just a defensive no.

The state will not back a settlement that leaves the company uncompetitive. We need a package that protects the bulk of jobs and the core sites **and** delivers real cost reduction.

## On the current proposals

### 1) Conditional non-closure package with a trigger
This is the most serious path so far. It is preferable to immediate closure plans because it preserves the possibility of restructuring **without** preemptively damaging the regional base.

But the trigger must be:
- objective,
- measurable,
- and not so loose that it becomes a hidden closure option after the fact.

If it is merely a device to postpone the same decision, I will not support it.

### 2) Site protection with partial flexibility
This can work only if “partial flexibility” is real and not symbolic. That means:
- capacity reduction,
- shift cuts,
- voluntary departures,
- working-time adjustments,
- and actual cost reduction.

A package of protections without genuine adjustments is not a compromise; it is a freeze.

### 3) Tripartite burden-sharing
Yes. This is the only politically and economically defensible path:
- **workers** contribute through flexibility and moderation,
- **management** contributes through overhead and operational simplification,
- **owners** accept a staged path and a longer payback horizon,
- **the state** supports transition measures and regional stabilization.

But burden-sharing must not mean that only the workforce carries the pain while the company reserves all strategic optionality. That would be unacceptable.

## My bottom line
The regional state government can support a restructuring only if it:
- **protects core sites and the bulk of jobs**
- preserves our **co-determination and veto role**
- includes **real, balanced employee contributions**
- secures **binding investment and product commitments**
- and proves that any job reduction is the minimum necessary

What I will not accept is:
- plant closures as a first resort,
- mass layoffs disguised as competitiveness,
- or a settlement that sidelines the state and regional interests.

## What I need next
If management wants a serious decision, bring forward a **plant-by-plant bridge** with:
1. current cost gap,
2. savings from non-closure measures,
3. savings from closure options,
4. one-off costs,
5. timing,
6. and the point at which the non-closure package would fail to meet the return-on-sales target.

If the non-closure package can truly achieve the needed savings without closures, we will engage constructively. If it cannot, then the burden remains on management to explain why it is asking the workforce and the region to pay for a strategy that is not yet proven.
