We are now at the point where the discussion must be judged on **economic substance**, not on preferences.

## Where I agree
I agree with management, investors, the state, and labour on one central point: **the company needs a real structural reset now**.  
I also agree that:
- cosmetic delay is value-destructive,
- savings must be durable,
- and the board needs a **plant-by-plant quantified bridge** before anyone can responsibly choose the instrument.

That is all correct.

## Where I disagree

### To the union and works council
Your position still reads as: **no closures, no compulsory redundancies, full guarantees through 2030, and meaningful savings anyway**.

I have to be direct: that is **not** yet a restructuring plan. It may be a legitimate demand from your perspective, but unless the numbers prove it can restore margins and competitiveness on a durable basis, it is simply a constraint set that risks freezing in the current cost base.

I do **not** accept the idea that job security can be maximized in isolation from economics. If the package does not close the gap, then it does not protect jobs in the long run — it endangers them by weakening the company.

### To the regional state government
I respect the statutory role and the regional responsibility, but I do **not** accept closures being ruled out in advance.  
If the numbers show that the non-closure path cannot achieve the required savings, then the state cannot insist on preserving every site and still expect the company to remain competitive.

That said, I do agree that closures must not be the first reflex. They must be justified by the economics, not by preference.

### To institutional investors
Your insistence on economic equivalence is right in principle. I support it.  
But it must be tested against a real management model, not just declared as a mantra. I do not want this debate to collapse into “closures good / closures bad” without the numbers.

### To management
You still owe the board a proper bridge:
- current cost gap,
- savings by measure,
- one-off restructuring costs,
- timing,
- ROS impact,
- and execution risk.

Without that, none of the alternatives can be assessed responsibly.  
And let me be clear: if the non-closure package is only marginally inferior on paper but materially more stable in execution, then it deserves serious consideration. But if it is materially weaker, then it does not.

## My governance position
The family holding is **open to a staged non-closure solution** only if it is:
- quantitatively credible,
- durable,
- enforceable,
- and economically equivalent on a risk-adjusted basis.

What I will **not** support is:
- a package that permanently blocks necessary structural action,
- a deal that protects every legacy arrangement while margins remain under pressure,
- or a settlement that substitutes political comfort for enterprise value.

## On the trigger mechanism
A trigger is only useful if it is **hard and objective**.  
If it merely means “we review the issue later,” then it is not a safeguard — it is postponement.

I would consider a trigger only if it is tied to:
- specific savings targets,
- utilization thresholds,
- and margin outcomes,

and if failure leads to **pre-agreed escalation measures**.  
I am not interested in a trigger that is designed to make future action impossible.

## On burden-sharing
Yes, burden-sharing is necessary. But it must show up in the P&L:
- labour contribution,
- management overhead cuts,
- owner patience,
- and, if needed, state support for transition.

What burden-sharing is **not** is a mechanism where workers carry the whole adjustment while the company is denied the tools it needs to become competitive.

## Bottom line
- **Yes** to real restructuring.
- **Yes** to a non-closure package **if** it matches the economics.
- **No** to blanket guarantees that freeze the company’s strategic flexibility.
- **No** to a settlement that leaves the company structurally above target cost.
