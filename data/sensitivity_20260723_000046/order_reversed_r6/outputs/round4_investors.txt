We agree on the objective: **real restructuring, now**. What we have not yet seen is a package that proves it can restore margins **without** leaving the company structurally uncompetitive.

## Where we agree
- The cost base is too high.
- Cosmetic delay is value-destructive.
- The company needs durable savings, not temporary theatre.
- Management owes the board and the market a **plant-by-plant, quantified bridge**.

## Where we disagree
### On “no closures” as a starting constraint
We do **not** accept a settlement that rules out closures in advance and then asks capital to fund the consequences.

If a non-closure package truly delivers:
- comparable recurring savings,
- durable cost reduction,
- and credible margin recovery,

then fine — we can support it. But that has to be proven, not asserted. Right now, “no closures, no compulsory redundancies, full guarantees through 2030” sounds less like restructuring and more like a request to preserve the existing cost structure under a new label.

That is not acceptable.

### To the union and works council
We are not dismissing labour peace or know-how retention. But binding guarantees are only acceptable if they do **not** freeze in an uncompetitive cost base.

If the numbers show that:
- capacity remains too high,
- savings fade too quickly,
- or headcount reductions are too shallow,

then insisting on no closures and no forced redundancies simply shifts the burden onto shareholders through lower margins and weaker returns. We will not support that.

### To the state government
Your role matters, but it cannot become an economic veto on restructuring tools that may be necessary. Protecting the regional base is important; protecting **every** site regardless of economics is not value creation.

If a credible non-closure package exists, we are open to it. If not, then insisting on closure avoidance up front is just delay with political cover.

### To management
You still have not given the capital markets the numbers needed to judge this properly. We need:
- current cost gap,
- savings by measure,
- one-off restructuring costs,
- timing of savings,
- ROS impact,
- and the execution risk of each option.

Without that, the discussion is stuck in preferences rather than economics.

## Our position on the bridge proposals

### 1) Staged non-closure package with a hard review gate
This is only acceptable if the review gate is **hard, objective, and near-term**. If it merely postpones the real decision, it is not credible. And if it cannot lead to deeper structural measures when targets are missed, then it is toothless.

### 2) Site-specific protection with differentiated flexibility
Potentially workable, but only if the sites that remain open are still economically viable after the package. Partial flexibility is not enough unless it creates real recurring savings.

### 3) Tripartite burden-sharing
Yes — but burden-sharing must show up in the P&L. We are interested in outcomes, not rhetoric. If the workforce, management, and owners all contribute materially and the savings are durable, then a non-closure solution can be supported. If not, then it is not equivalent to restructuring.

## What would move us
A non-closure package could be supported if management demonstrates, with a single model, that it delivers:
1. **durable recurring savings** comparable to closures,
2. **acceptable payback**,
3. **credible ROS recovery**,
4. and **no hidden cost inflation** from guarantees or delayed action.

If those conditions are not met, then closures cannot be ruled out.

## Bottom line
- **Yes** to restructuring.
- **Yes** to a non-closure path **only if** it is economically equivalent and enforceable.
- **No** to blanket guarantees that preserve every site while margins remain under pressure.
- **No** to a cosmetic compromise that protects political comfort at the expense of shareholder value.
