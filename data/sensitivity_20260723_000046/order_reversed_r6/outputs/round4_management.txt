We are past the point where anyone benefits from generalities.

I will respond directly to the arguments on the table.

## Where I agree

I agree with all of you on the basic diagnosis:
- the current cost base is too high,
- the company needs real restructuring now,
- cosmetic delay is not acceptable,
- and any package must be durable, measurable, and capable of restoring margins.

I also agree that we need a **plant-by-plant bridge**. Management will not ask the board or the workforce to sign up to blind faith. The numbers have to be shown.

## Where I disagree

### To the union and works council
Your position remains internally inconsistent.

You demand:
- no closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- full guarantees through 2030,
- and meaningful savings.

That may be understandable politically, but it is **not yet a credible restructuring architecture** unless the numbers prove it closes the gap. Right now, you are trying to lock in every protection first and ask management to solve the cost problem second. That does not work.

Let me be blunt: if the company keeps the same structural footprint, keeps too much capacity, and preserves legacy guarantees without deep enough labor-cost relief, then the cost base stays uncompetitive. That is exactly the outcome I cannot accept.

I am prepared to work with:
- working-time flexibility,
- wage restraint,
- bonus sacrifice,
- voluntary exits,
- capacity reduction,
- and productivity measures.

But I will **not** accept a settlement that leaves us with:
- too much fixed cost,
- too much overcapacity,
- and no credible path back to target ROS.

If the package does not produce structural savings, then it is not a solution.

### To the regional state government
I respect your statutory role and your responsibility for the regional industrial base. But your argument still starts too far from the economics.

You say closures should not be the default. I agree.  
You say the numbers must prove necessity. I agree.  
But you still speak as if closures can be ruled out as a matter of principle. They cannot.

If the plant-by-plant bridge shows that a non-closure package does not close the gap on a durable basis, then blocking closures in advance would be to force the company into continued underperformance. That would damage the regional base more, not less.

So yes, prove the numbers. But do not pre-empt the answer before the numbers are on the table.

### To institutional investors and the family holding
You are right to insist on economic equivalence, durability, and enforceability. Management supports that standard.

But let me also be clear: a “non-closure” package is only acceptable if it is **not merely politically softer, but financially equivalent**. If it is weaker on savings, weaker on permanence, or weaker on execution, then it is not equivalent.

That means we need the bridge in one model:
- current cost gap,
- savings from non-closure measures,
- savings from closures,
- one-off costs,
- timing,
- ROS impact,
- and the execution risk of each path.

Without that, nobody here can responsibly claim the non-closure route is enough.

## The central point

This is not a debate about whether restructuring is needed. It is.

This is a debate about whether the company can get to:
- a permanent reduction in labor cost,
- a permanent reduction in excess capacity,
- and a return to target margins,

without using plant closures.

My position is straightforward:
- **If yes, show it.**
- **If no, then closures remain on the table.**

I will not accept a compromise that is designed to avoid hard decisions while leaving the company structurally above target cost.

## On the trigger idea
A trigger mechanism is only useful if it is real.

I do **not** support a vague “review later” device. That just postpones the same conflict.

If we use a trigger, then it must be:
- objective,
- near-term,
- and tied to actual economic outcomes.

And if the package misses those outcomes, then deeper structural measures must be reopened. That is not a threat. That is governance.

What I will not accept is a trigger that becomes impossible to use because the package is protected from any meaningful escalation. That would make the entire exercise pointless.

## On burden-sharing
Yes, burden-sharing is necessary. But it must appear in the P&L.

That means:
- workers contribute through flexibility and moderation,
- management contributes through overhead reduction and operational simplification,
- owners accept a longer payback horizon,
- and the state supports transition where it can.

But burden-sharing does **not** mean management gives up the right to pursue a sustainable cost base. If the final package does not restore competitiveness, then everyone loses — including the workforce.

Management supports a non-closure path **only if it is economically equivalent** to stronger structural measures.

If it is not, then I will say so plainly: the package is not sufficient, and deeper measures remain necessary.
