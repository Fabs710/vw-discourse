Understood. In my restructuring finance/costing capacity, the key point is this:

## What can be said now
A no-closure package is economically acceptable only if, on a **risk-adjusted NPV basis**, it matches or exceeds the closure alternative after including:

- closure one-off costs
- saved fixed cash costs from closure
- extra logistics / transfer costs
- any replacement capex at retained sites
- timing delays to savings
- execution risk from labor conflict or underdelivery

So the real test is:

\[
NPV_{non-closure} \ge NPV_{closure}
\]

If that is not true, then “no closures” is financially inferior unless there is a compensating advantage such as much lower one-off cost or materially lower implementation risk.

## Practical threshold
Without plant data, I cannot give a credible single euro figure. The threshold depends on:
- severance cost
- number of sites
- utilization gap
- wage-concession duration
- required capex
- discount rate
- expected strike / delay risk

But as a rule of thumb:
- if a no-closure package delivers **less than ~70–80% of closure savings** on a durable cash basis, it usually needs a strong offsetting advantage to be competitive.
- if it delivers **90%+ of closure savings** and avoids large closure costs, it can often be superior.

## What the model must show
For each plant:
- current cash cost
- utilization
- closure one-off cost
- closure annual savings
- non-closure annual savings
- non-closure transition cost
- NPV of both options
- payback of both options
- ROS impact

## Main financial sensitivities
The biggest swing factors are:
1. severance and decommissioning costs
2. speed of labor-cost realization
3. durability of concessions
4. extra capex to keep sites open
5. demand forecast
6. strike / implementation delay risk
