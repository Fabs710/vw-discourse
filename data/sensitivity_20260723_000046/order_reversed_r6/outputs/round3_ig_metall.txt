We are now finally speaking about the real issue: **who pays, how much, and with what guarantees**. On that, I will be just as direct as management.

## Where I agree
I agree with management, owners, investors, and the state on one point: **the company needs a real restructuring now**.  
The current cost base is too high, and delay is not a plan.

I also agree that:
- temporary gestures are not enough,
- savings must be durable,
- and the decision must be based on plant-by-plant economics, not slogans.

That is exactly why we submitted a counter-proposal in the first place: not to block change, but to **make change possible without destroying jobs and sites**.

## Where I disagree sharply

### To management
You keep saying the workforce must accept “flexibility,” but what you actually mean is:
- plant closures still possible,
- compulsory redundancies still possible,
- and all the hard adjustment pushed onto employees.

That is not a fair restructuring. That is a transfer of crisis costs onto the workforce.

I reject the idea that closures are somehow the natural default if the model gets tight. The model is not neutral if it ignores:
- strike risk,
- loss of know-how,
- disruption from closing sites,
- and the value of labour peace when you ask workers to give up wages and protections.

If you want our contribution, then management must first prove that it has used the other levers seriously:
- overhead cuts,
- management cost reductions,
- investment discipline,
- capacity reductions without site destruction,
- and product allocation changes.

### To institutional investors
You say a non-closure deal must be economically equivalent. Fine. But you are too quick to treat **binding job and site guarantees** as value-destructive.

In German industrial reality, those guarantees are not a luxury. They are the condition for stable production and a workable settlement. A deal that strips workers of security while demanding concessions from them is not durable. It is a conflict machine.

If you want a package that survives the cycle, then labour peace, know-how retention, and predictable production are part of the economics. Not “soft” factors. Economics.

### To the controlling family
Long-term ownership should mean long-term responsibility. That includes not sacrificing the company’s industrial base for short-term flexibility.  
If you back a package that leaves the workforce with concessions and no security, then you are not preserving value — you are eroding trust and making the next conflict worse.

### To the regional state government
I agree that your veto role matters. But you need to do more than say you oppose closures in principle. Use your position to force a **serious non-closure restructuring**:
- capacity reduction,
- binding investment,
- retraining and redeployment,
- and a real job-security framework.

If you only reject closures without helping secure an alternative, then you are just moving the conflict into a later round.

## What is actually acceptable
We have already signaled flexibility. That remains true. A serious package can include:

- working-time flexibility,
- wage restraint,
- forgoing bonuses,
- voluntary departures,
- capacity reduction,
- productivity measures,
- and targeted labour-cost contributions.

But there are limits, and they are not negotiable:
- **no compulsory redundancies**
- **no plant closures**
- **no dismantling of the collective wage framework**
- **no open-ended job insecurity**

Those are not bargaining flourishes. They are the line beyond which there is no durable agreement.

## On the proposed “trigger” idea
A trigger mechanism is only acceptable if it does **not** function as a backdoor to closures. If the trigger is basically “concede now or we close later,” then that is not a compromise — it is a threat with a waiting period.

If the parties want a trigger, then it must trigger:
- additional management contribution,
- additional owner support,
- and further negotiated adjustments,

not automatic closure rights against the workforce.

We will not sign a settlement that makes worker concessions reversible only on paper while closure remains a loaded gun in the drawer.

## What workers are prepared to contribute
We are not refusing our share. Our position remains:
- labour-cost contribution is possible,
- capacity reduction is possible,
- working-time adjustments are possible,
- voluntary exits are possible,
- bonus sacrifices are possible,

**if and only if** the package also includes:
- no forced layoffs,
- no plant closures,
- site security,
- and real contributions from management and owners.

That is a fair settlement. Workers cannot be the only ones taking the pain while others keep all strategic options.

## What I want management to put on the table now
Not generalities. Numbers.

Bring a **plant-by-plant bridge** with:
1. current cost gap,
2. savings from capacity and working-time measures,
3. savings from management overhead cuts,
4. savings from worker contributions,
5. the remaining gap after non-closure measures,
6. and the impact of closures, if you still insist on treating them as necessary.

If the non-closure package leaves a gap, then the question is not “why won’t labour accept closures?” The question is: **what additional contribution are management and owners prepared to make before asking workers to pay with jobs?**

## Bottom line
We are ready for a durable settlement. We are not ready for a settlement where:
- workers give up wages,
- workers give up guarantees,
- and workers still face closures and forced redundancies.

That would be unfair, unstable, and entirely unnecessary if the company is willing to restructure seriously without sacrificing the people who built it.
