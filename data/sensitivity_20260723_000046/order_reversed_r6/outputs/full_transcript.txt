[Expert: restructuring finance/costing specialist — Round 2]
Below is the most defensible **finance-and-costing framework** for comparing a **closure-based restructuring** versus a **non-closure alternative**. Because no hard numbers were provided, I will give a **model structure**, the **required inputs**, and the **decision logic** that should be applied once management supplies the data.

## 1) What must be quantified

To compare the options properly, management needs a common baseline:

### A. Current run-rate cost base
Break the German operations into:
- **Fixed costs**: plant overhead, salaried support, depreciation, utilities minimum load, maintenance, property costs, logistics contracts, IT, etc.
- **Variable costs**: direct labour if flexed by output, materials, energy tied to volume, freight, warranty/quality tied to volume.
- **Semi-fixed / step costs**: shifts, line staffing, contractor pools, shared services.

### B. Target savings gap
You need to identify:
- current **EBIT / ROS shortfall**
- required **annual cost reduction**
- time horizon over which savings must be sustainable

A useful question is:
> What annual EBITDA or EBIT improvement is needed to restore the brand to target ROS after the restructuring?

### C. Restructuring cash costs
For each option, quantify:
- **severance and termination costs**
- **closure costs**: site decommissioning, remediation, contract breakage, transfer costs, asset write-downs
- **transition costs**: dual-running, move costs, hiring elsewhere, training, logistics reconfiguration
- **investment / capex**: automation, tooling, product transfer, new plant adaptation
- **labour-concession costs**: compensation for wage cuts, shorter hours, deferred pay, guarantees, voluntary severance premiums

### D. Timing
For each measure, specify:
- when savings start
- ramp-up profile
- whether savings are temporary or durable
- whether there is an upfront cash outflow before benefits begin

---

## 2) How to model the two alternatives

## Option 1: Closure-based restructuring
This typically has:

### Benefits
- larger **structural fixed-cost reduction**
- potential reduction in overcapacity and better plant loading
- stronger durability if the site is fully exited

### Costs
- high one-off restructuring charges
- severance and social-plan costs
- closure/remediation costs
- possible negative cash effects from asset impairments
- transition disruption and temporary inefficiency

### Financial logic
Closure makes sense if:

**PV of incremental savings from closure**
minus
**PV of closure and transition costs**
> **PV of savings from any non-closure alternative**

In other words, closure is justified only if the **net present value** is superior to the alternatives.

---

## Option 2: Non-closure alternative
This typically includes combinations of:
- capacity reduction without full site exit
- shift reduction / line rationalisation
- working-time changes
- wage moderation or temporary pay cuts
- removal of guarantees or allowances
- voluntary departures / natural attrition
- transfer of some production to lower-cost sites
- productivity improvements and automation

### Benefits
- lower immediate cash cost
- avoids some closure and remediation costs
- may reduce disruption and execution risk
- may preserve production continuity

### Costs / limits
- savings may be smaller or less durable
- may require ongoing concessions
- some costs can be “recurrent” rather than structural
- if job and site guarantees are too strong, future flexibility is reduced
- if overcapacity remains, the company may continue carrying excess fixed cost

### Financial logic
A non-closure package is acceptable only if it produces:
- **comparable annual savings**
- with **similar durability**
- and without hidden future costs that offset the headline savings

---

## 3) Decision test: the comparison that matters

The correct comparison is not “closure vs no closure” in the abstract. It is:

### A. Incremental annual savings
For each package:
- baseline annual cost
- post-restructuring annual cost
- annual savings by year

### B. One-time restructuring cost
Include all upfront and near-term cash costs.

### C. NPV over a planning horizon
Use a 5–10 year horizon, depending on product cycle and investment life.

A simplified formula:

**NPV = Σ [Savings_t / (1+r)^t] − Σ [RestructuringCashCost_t / (1+r)^t]**

Where:
- **Savings_t** = annual cost savings in year t
- **r** = discount rate / WACC
- **RestructuringCashCost_t** = closure, severance, transition, capex, and concession costs

---

## 4) What would make closure financially superior?

Closure-based restructuring is usually financially superior if:
1. the plant has **persistently low utilization**
2. it contains **high fixed cost per unit**
3. non-closure measures would require **large recurring concessions**
4. capacity reduction without closure leaves **too much stranded cost**
5. product allocation can be shifted elsewhere without excessive logistics cost

In that case, closure converts:
- recurring fixed losses
into
- one-time restructuring charges

That is often economically attractive if the payback period is acceptable.

---

## 5) What would make the non-closure alternative superior?

A non-closure package can be financially superior if:
1. the plant remains operationally efficient at lower volume
2. savings come from genuine productivity gains, not just temporary concessions
3. closure would require very high remediation or transfer costs
4. the site has specialized assets that would be expensive to duplicate
5. labour-cost measures are large, binding, and durable enough

But if the non-closure option depends mainly on:
- one-off wage concessions,
- temporary freezes,
- or deferred adjustment,

then the model should treat those as **time-limited**, not as permanent savings.

---

## 6) Key sensitivity variables

The final answer will likely turn on four variables:

### 1. Utilization after restructuring
If output remains low, fixed costs stay high.

### 2. Labour-cost savings durability
Temporary concessions have much lower NPV than permanent structural reductions.

### 3. Transition and logistics cost
Moving production elsewhere can be expensive and may erase headline savings.

### 4. Severance / closure magnitude
High redundancy payments and social-plan costs can make closure unattractive if the annual savings are modest.

---

## 7) Provisional recommendation on analysis method

Management should produce a **side-by-side bridge** for both options:

### For each option:
- current annual cost base
- headcount reduction
- wage bill reduction
- fixed overhead reduction
- capex required
- one-off restructuring cash costs
- annual savings by year
- NPV at the company’s WACC
- payback period
- sensitivity cases:
  - high / base / low savings
  - high / base / low implementation cost
  - delayed implementation scenario

---

## 8) What cannot be concluded yet

At present, it is **not possible** to say whether closure or non-closure is financially better, because the necessary inputs are missing:
- current cost gap
- target savings
- plant-level fixed cost and variable cost data
- severance and closure costs
- capex needs
- savings timing
- probability of execution and labour-concession durability

---

## 9) Bottom line

From a restructuring finance perspective:

- **Closure** is justified if it produces materially larger and more durable savings than non-closure measures, after all one-time costs are included.
- **Non-closure** is justified if it can deliver the same NPV savings with lower disruption and lower restructuring cash cost.
- The deciding metric is **not** the political acceptability of the package; it is the **net present value of durable cost reduction**.

- **Labor-cost reduction:** high if site is exited
- **Execution certainty:** medium to low, because of industrial-relations friction and legal/board constraints
- **One-off cash cost:** high

**Indicative annual savings per closed large plant**
- Direct and indirect labor: **€150m–€300m**
- Plant overhead / utilities / maintenance / logistics / admin: **€50m–€150m**
- Total annual run-rate saving: **€200m–€450m per large plant**

**Indicative one-off cost per large plant**
- Severance / social plan / transfer support: **€150m–€400m**
- Decommissioning / remediation / contract breakage / relocation: **€100m–€300m**
- Asset write-downs: accounting-heavy, cash-light, but economically relevant
- Total restructuring cash cost: **€250m–€700m per large plant**

**Timing**
- Savings often start **12–24 months** after announcement
- Full benefit usually only after **24–36 months**

### Option B: Non-closure alternative
Typical measures:
- Shift reduction
- Capacity rationalization without full exit
- Voluntary departures / natural attrition
- Working-time reduction
- Wage moderation or temporary pay cut
- Elimination of allowances/guarantees
- Productivity improvements / automation

**Indicative annual savings**
- If modest: **€50m–€150m per large plant equivalent**
- If strong and binding: **€150m–€300m+** per site cluster or business unit

**One-off cost**
- Usually lower than closure: **€50m–€250m**
- But can rise materially if it requires buyouts, wage compensation, or capital spending

**Timing**
- Faster to announce, but slower to convert into durable savings if measures are staged
- Savings may begin in **6–18 months**
- Durability depends entirely on whether concessions are permanent or time-limited

## 3) The real test: NPV, not headline savings

The correct comparison is:

**NPV = PV of annual savings − PV of one-off restructuring costs**

A closure-based plan wins if:
- it removes more fixed cost than the non-closure package, and
- the additional savings exceed the extra closure cost

A non-closure package wins if:
- it achieves similar savings without the closure premium, and
- the savings are durable enough to survive the next product cycle

## 4) Side-by-side comparison

| Dimension | Closure-based restructuring | Non-closure alternative |
|---|---:|---:|
| Annual savings potential | High | Medium to high, depending on depth |
| Durability | Very high | Medium unless legally/contractually locked in |
| One-off cash cost | High | Lower to moderate |
| Speed to savings | Slower | Faster initially |
| Execution risk | High | Medium |
| Industrial-relations conflict | Very high | High, but usually less extreme than closure |
| Flexibility for future change | Higher after exit | Lower if tied up in guarantees |
| Risk of stranded overcapacity | Low | Medium to high |

## 5) What makes a non-closure package economically credible

A non-closure package is only credible if it includes **all or most** of the following:

1. **Hard capacity reduction**
   - shift elimination
   - line consolidation
   - removal of excess production slots

2. **Permanent headcount reduction**
   - natural attrition alone is usually too slow
   - voluntary severance can help, but the scale must be sufficient

3. **Binding labor-cost reset**
   - lower wage progression
   - removal of allowances
   - working-time flexibility
   - lower bonus burden

4. **No pure time-buying**
   - temporary wage freezes are not enough
   - deferred measures need to be modeled as temporary, not structural

5. **Enforceability**
   - if the package depends on repeated renegotiation, the NPV should be discounted heavily

If those elements are absent, the “non-closure” option is usually just **delayed closure economics without the fixed-cost relief**.

## 6) What makes closure financially rational

Closure becomes rational when:
- the plant is **structurally underutilized**
- fixed cost per unit is too high
- product migration is possible without excessive logistics penalty
- remediation and move costs are not extreme
- the company needs a **hard reset** rather than incremental savings

In practical OEM terms, closure is often the superior economic choice when a site cannot be loaded to an efficient level after portfolio changes.

## 7) What makes closure dangerous economically

Closure can be a worse decision if:
- the plant has specialized capability that is expensive to replicate
- transfer of production creates major quality or ramp-up losses
- closure triggers very large severance/social-plan costs
- downstream supply chain disruption is severe
- the company loses capacity needed for future model launches

So closure is not automatically best. It is best only if the **incremental structural savings** justify those costs.

## 8) Execution risk: the hidden variable

This matters a lot in the German co-determination setting.

### Closure-based package: risks
- supervisory-board veto / two-thirds requirement
- works-council resistance
- union strike escalation
- delay in implementation
- legal and political friction
- possible value leakage while the dispute drags on

### Non-closure package: risks
- savings may be watered down in bargaining
- guarantees can freeze future flexibility
- measures may be reversible
- management may not get the full structural reset it needs

From a restructuring standpoint, a **delayed closure** can be the worst of both worlds: high conflict, high carrying cost, and no savings yet.

## 9) Practical decision framework

Management should compare the options on five metrics:

1. **Annual run-rate savings**
2. **One-off cash cost**
3. **Time to full benefit**
4. **Durability of savings**
5. **Execution probability**

A useful rule:
- If the non-closure package delivers **at least 80–90% of closure savings**, with materially lower conflict and similar durability, it may be preferable
- If it delivers only **50–70% of closure savings**, it is usually inadequate
- If it depends on temporary concessions, its NPV will usually be much lower than its headline savings suggest

## 10) Provisional conclusion

With the information available, I would not assume closures are inevitable, but I would say this:

- **Closure-based restructuring is more likely to produce the required structural cost reset**
- **Non-closure restructuring is only credible if it removes enough capacity and labor cost permanently**
- If the alternative is mainly promises, freezes, or time-limited concessions, it will probably **fail the economic test**

# 2) Core financial test: “economically equivalent” means what?

A non-closure package is financially equivalent to closure-based restructuring only if, **on a risk-adjusted basis**, it delivers:

1. **Equivalent recurring annual EBIT improvement**
2. **Equivalent NPV** over the restructuring horizon
3. **Acceptable payback period**
4. **Acceptable execution risk**
5. **No material hidden liabilities** from guarantees or deferred actions

A practical test is:

> **NPV(non-closure incremental savings after implementation costs) ≥ NPV(closure-based savings after implementation costs)**

or, more strictly,

> **NPV(non-closure package relative to “do nothing”) ≥ NPV(closure package relative to “do nothing”)**

---

# 3) Minimum model structure

## 3.1 Annual savings line items
For each scenario, separate into:

### Recurring savings
- payroll reduction
- overtime / shift premium reduction
- overhead reduction
- maintenance/utilities reduction from lower utilization
- logistics simplification
- procurement savings from volume and footprint consolidation
- lower depreciation if capex is deferred or assets are retired

### Recurring costs
- retained idle capacity cost
- extra logistics from keeping multiple sites open
- ongoing bonus or guarantee cost
- retraining / redeployment cost
- duplicate fixed cost during transition
- strike/disruption risk cost if you want a risk load

### One-time restructuring costs
- severance
- closure/wind-down costs
- demolition / remediation
- impairment/write-downs
- contract termination penalties
- IT / relocation / moving costs
- project management and advisory costs

# 4) Threshold formulae

## 4.1 Annual savings threshold for non-closure to match closure
Let:

- \( S_{NC} \) = recurring annual savings from non-closure package
- \( C_{NC,1} \) = one-time non-closure implementation costs
- \( S_{CL} \) = recurring annual savings from closure package
- \( C_{CL,1} \) = one-time closure costs
- \( r \) = discount rate
- \( T \) = analysis horizon in years

Then the non-closure package must satisfy:

\[
NPV(S_{NC}) - C_{NC,1} \ge NPV(S_{CL}) - C_{CL,1}
\]

If savings are level annuities:

\[
S_{NC} \cdot \frac{1-(1+r)^{-T}}{r} - C_{NC,1} \ge S_{CL} \cdot \frac{1-(1+r)^{-T}}{r} - C_{CL,1}
\]

Rearranged into a **minimum required annual savings threshold**:

\[
S_{NC} \ge S_{CL} + \frac{(C_{NC,1} - C_{CL,1}) \cdot r}{1-(1+r)^{-T}}
\]

So the non-closure package needs either:
- more recurring savings, or
- lower upfront costs, or
- both.

# 5) Payback logic

If management/investors want a payback test, calculate:

\[
Payback = \frac{\text{One-time restructuring cost}}{\text{Annual recurring savings}}
\]

Use this separately for each scenario.

Typical interpretation:
- **< 2 years**: very strong
- **2–4 years**: usually acceptable if durable
- **> 4 years**: usually harder to justify unless strategic risk reduction is large

For a carmaker in a cyclical sector, a **shorter payback** is usually preferred because market conditions can change before savings fully mature.

---

# 6) ROS impact

If the company’s core brand is below target ROS, measure impact as:

\[
\Delta ROS = \frac{\Delta EBIT}{Revenue}
\]

Where:
- \(\Delta EBIT\) = annual recurring improvement after restructuring
- Revenue should be the relevant steady-state revenue base

Example interpretation:
- If revenue is €100bn and restructuring improves EBIT by €1bn, ROS improves by **1.0 percentage point**.

This is often the cleanest “board-level” metric to compare scenarios.

---

# 7) Threshold savings by package type

## 7.1 Closure-based package
Usually characterized by:
- higher upfront cost
- larger recurring savings
- greater execution and industrial disruption risk
- potential write-downs/impairments

The closure package is preferable if:

\[
\text{Recurring savings gain} \times PVAF > \text{extra one-time closure cost}
\]

Where PVAF is the present value annuity factor.

- lower asset write-down risk

The non-closure package is preferable only if the **durable recurring savings** are large enough to compensate for the fact that fixed costs remain embedded.

# 2) The key economic test

A non-closure package is economically equivalent only if:

\[
NPV(\text{non-closure incremental savings}) - \text{one-time costs} \ge NPV(\text{closure-based incremental savings}) - \text{one-time costs}
\]

If you want the **minimum annual savings threshold** for the non-closure package:

\[
S_{NC} \ge S_{CL} + \frac{(C_{NC,1} - C_{CL,1}) \cdot r}{1-(1+r)^{-T}}
\]

Where:
- \(S_{NC}\) = annual recurring savings from the non-closure package
- \(S_{CL}\) = annual recurring savings from the closure package
- \(C_{NC,1}\) = one-off cost of the non-closure package
- \(C_{CL,1}\) = one-off cost of the closure package
- \(r\) = discount rate
- \(T\) = analysis horizon

Interpretation:
- If non-closure has **lower one-offs**, it can tolerate somewhat lower recurring savings.
- If non-closure savings are **temporary or reversible**, its effective NPV falls sharply.

---

# 3) Practical board-level decision matrix

| Criterion | Closure-based package | Non-closure package |
|---|---:|---:|
| One-off cost | High | Low to medium |
| Recurring annual savings | High | Medium to high only if concessions are deep |
| NPV | Often strong if savings are large | Must be benchmarked vs closure |
| Payback | Often longer due to one-offs | Often faster if savings start immediately |
| ROS impact | Usually stronger if structural | Can be sufficient if durable and large enough |
| Execution risk | Medium-high | Medium-high if voluntary / enforceability is weak |
| Industrial disruption | High | Lower |
| Risk of savings fade | Low | Higher unless binding and long-dated |
| Flexibility retained | Higher | Lower if guarantees are binding |

---

# 4) What makes a non-closure package fail financially

A non-closure package usually fails the economic test if one or more of these is true:

1. **Savings are too small**
   - wage restraint and voluntary exits do not offset the fixed-cost base

2. **Savings are not durable**
   - temporary concessions expire before the company is back on target

3. **Utilization remains too low**
   - keeping all sites open with too much slack destroys margin

4. **Execution underdelivers**
   - voluntary-only headcount cuts often miss target

5. **Hidden costs offset the savings**
   - duplicate overhead, logistics, retraining, or guarantee costs

6. **The package delays rather than solves**
   - a trigger mechanism without hard consequences becomes a time-buying device

---

# 5) Payback test

\[
Payback = \frac{\text{One-time restructuring cost}}{\text{Annual recurring savings}}
\]

Rule of thumb:
- **< 2 years**: very strong
- **2–4 years**: usually workable if savings are durable
- **> 4 years**: difficult unless the strategic risk reduction is substantial

For an OEM in a cyclical market, **early payback matters a lot** because savings delayed into later years are less reliable.

---

# 6) ROS impact

\[
\Delta ROS = \frac{\Delta EBIT}{Revenue}
\]

So if the package improves EBIT by:
- €500m on €100bn revenue = **+0.5 percentage points ROS**
- €1.0bn on €100bn revenue = **+1.0 percentage point ROS**

If the brand is materially below target, the restructuring needs to move ROS by enough to reach the hurdle **and** cover cyclical downside.

---

# 7) Trigger package: what “hard” means financially

A trigger mechanism only has value if it is:

- **objective**: based on EBIT, ROS, cash, utilization, or cost metrics
- **near-term**: not “review in three years”
- **automatic or highly credible**
- **linked to pre-agreed deeper measures**

If the trigger merely reopens talks later, its economic value is low.  
If it activates additional measures when targets are missed, it is much more credible.

---

# 8) Minimum non-closure threshold logic

To support a no-closure settlement, management should identify:

### Group-level thresholds
- minimum annual recurring savings
- minimum NPV
- maximum acceptable payback
- target ROS by year
- maximum allowed execution risk

### Plant-level thresholds
For each site:
- current fixed cost burden
- savings from shift cuts / line consolidation
- savings from labor concessions
- residual gap after non-closure measures
- incremental savings if the site were closed

Then the board can answer:
> Does keeping this site open, with the proposed concessions, still beat closing it on a risk-adjusted NPV basis?

That is the correct comparison.

- Payback period
- ROS at steady state
- Execution risk rating
- Sensitivity to volume, strike risk, and savings fade

- **Support a closure package** if non-closure savings are too small, too slow, or too reversible.

## How to define “economic equivalence”
A non-closure package is economically equivalent to closure only if, on a **risk-adjusted NPV basis**, it delivers at least the same value as the closure alternative after accounting for:
- one-off costs
- timing delays
- underdelivery risk
- any extra capex or operating subsidies required to keep sites open

A practical test:

\[
NPV_{nonclosure} \ge NPV_{closure}
\]

Where each scenario includes all incremental cash flows versus the current baseline.

If you want a stricter board test, use:
- **higher NPV**
- **shorter payback**
- **equal or better ROS**
- **lower execution risk**

## Provisional threshold logic
Because no hard data were provided, the most defensible way to frame the threshold is:

### Minimum recurring annual savings required for a no-closure package
\[
Annual\ Savings\ Threshold \approx \frac{Closure\ One\text{-}Time\ Costs - Avoided\ Transition\ Costs}{Present\ Value\ Factor} + Risk\ Premium
\]

In plain terms:
- if closure creates large one-time costs,
- a non-closure package can still win if it generates enough recurring savings fast enough,
- but it must also overcome:
  - slower realization,
  - weaker durability,
  - and any extra investment needed to keep plants viable.

A useful rule of thumb in restructuring cases:
- if the non-closure option delivers **less than about 70–80% of closure savings** on a durable cash basis, it usually needs compensating advantages such as much lower one-off costs, faster implementation, or materially lower execution risk.
- if it delivers **90%+ of closure savings** with lower one-offs and better execution, it is often superior financially.

That is only a heuristic, not a conclusion for this case.
