We are finally talking about the real issue, but let me be just as clear: **there will be no agreement from the works council that accepts plant closures or compulsory redundancies as a “necessary” starting point.** That line has not moved, and it will not move because others repeat “competitiveness” often enough.

## Where I agree
I agree that:
- the company needs **real restructuring**, not delay;
- the cost base is too high;
- temporary gestures are not enough;
- and we need a **plant-by-plant, quantified comparison**.

I also agree that any serious package must be:
- durable,
- enforceable,
- and capable of restoring competitiveness through 2030.

That is precisely why I insist on a solution that protects jobs and sites instead of blowing them up.

## Where I disagree

### To management
You keep presenting closures as if they are the natural consequence of arithmetic. They are not. They are a choice.

You have **not yet proven** that:
- every German site is structurally unviable,
- a non-closure package cannot close the gap,
- or that the damage from closures and conflict would not outweigh any theoretical savings.

You are asking workers to accept:
- wage restraint,
- flexibility,
- voluntary departures,
- and insecurity,

while still keeping closures and forced layoffs on the table. That is not a fair restructuring. It is a transfer of risk downward.

If you want the workforce to contribute, then management must first show its own contribution in hard numbers:
- overhead cuts,
- management cost reductions,
- investment discipline,
- and real operational simplification.

### To the institutional investors
You are right about one thing: **temporary concessions are not enough**. I agree.

But you are wrong if you think binding job and site guarantees through 2030 are automatically “value-destructive.” In this company, those guarantees are not decorative. They are the basis of labour peace, know-how retention, and stable production.

A package that offers capital maximum flexibility while giving workers maximum insecurity is not economically superior just because it is harsher. It is simply more one-sided.

### To the controlling family
You talk about long-term stewardship, and I take that seriously. Then act like it.

Long-term ownership should mean:
- preserving industrial capability,
- preserving trust,
- preserving co-determination,
- and preserving the job-security framework that has stabilized this company for decades.

Backing closures now would not be “disciplined realism.” It would be breaking the industrial compact and inviting a deeper conflict later.

### To the regional state government
I appreciate that you reject a closure-first reflex. But it is not enough to say closures should not be the default. You need to use your blocking role to ensure that they are **not used at all** if a real alternative exists.

And a real alternative does exist, but only if management and owners stop treating worker concessions as the only savings source.

## On the trigger idea
A “trigger” that reopens closure rights later is not a compromise. It is a delayed threat.

I will not accept a mechanism that says:
- concede now,
- accept insecurity now,
- and if management later decides the numbers are insufficient, closures become automatic.

That is not a jointly negotiated restructuring. That is a loaded gun kept on the table until the workforce gets tired.

If there is any trigger at all, it must trigger:
- further management contribution,
- further owner contribution,
- and further negotiated non-closure measures,

not a backdoor to plant exits.

## On the burden-sharing argument
Yes, burden-sharing is necessary. But burden-sharing means exactly that: **all sides contribute**.

So here is what is acceptable in principle:
- working-time flexibility,
- wage restraint,
- bonus sacrifice,
- voluntary departures,
- capacity reduction,
- productivity measures,
- management overhead reductions,
- and owner support for a longer payback horizon.

What is **not** acceptable:
- plant closures,
- compulsory redundancies,
- dismantling of the collective wage framework,
- or open-ended job insecurity.

## What needs to happen next
If management is serious, then bring the numbers in a way that respects co-determination and does not smuggle in closures as the assumed answer.

I want a **plant-by-plant bridge** with:
1. current cost gap,
2. worker contribution,
3. management contribution,
4. owner contribution,
5. remaining gap after a **no-closure / no-forced-redundancy** package,
6. and the exact comparison to any closure option.

If that model shows a gap, then the question is not whether workers should surrender sites and jobs. The question is what additional contribution management and ownership are prepared to make before demanding that from the workforce.

## My bottom line
The works council will support a serious restructuring only if it:
- keeps every German plant open,
- avoids compulsory redundancies,
- preserves co-determination and job security,
- and distributes the burden fairly across management, owners, and labour.

If the answer is still “closures may be necessary,” then we are not close to a solution. We are still at the point where management is asking the workforce to pay for strategic failure.
