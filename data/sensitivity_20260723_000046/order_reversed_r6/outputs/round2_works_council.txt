Let me be equally direct.

We welcome the move to a quantified bridge, because at least now everyone is forced to stop hiding behind abstractions. But I want to be crystal clear: **numbers do not make plant closures acceptable, and they do not make compulsory redundancies acceptable**. If the model is being used to “prove” that worker protections must be torn up, then we are starting from the wrong premise.

## Where I agree
I agree with the investors, owners, and management on one point:  
**the company needs a real restructuring, not a delaying tactic.**

I also agree that:
- the cost base is too high,
- cosmetic compromise will not solve the crisis,
- savings must be durable and enforceable,
- and the final package must be economically credible through 2030.

That is exactly why we insist on a solution that **protects every German plant and avoids forced layoffs**. A company with this scale and this governance structure does not need to destroy sites to become competitive. It needs to use the tools available under co-determination responsibly.

## Where I strongly disagree

### To management
You keep presenting closures as the logical endpoint if the numbers do not work. That is **your choice of instrument**, not an economic law.

You have not proven that:
- every German plant is structurally unviable,
- the required savings cannot be achieved otherwise,
- or that closures would not trigger long conflict, supply disruption, and loss of capability that damage the business further.

So no, I do not accept “closure-first” logic disguised as neutrality.

If you want labour to contribute, then management must first show real willingness to contribute itself:
- management cost reductions,
- overhead reductions,
- investment commitments,
- and a genuine willingness to preserve the workforce that produces value.

### To institutional investors
You say you want durable savings and no cosmetic compromise. Fine. But you are too quick to treat **binding job and site guarantees** as value-destructive.

That is not how industrial reality works in Germany. Labour peace, know-how retention, and stable production are not side issues. They are part of the value proposition. A deal that promises “flexibility” for capital but creates permanent insecurity for workers is not credible, and it is not sustainable.

You cannot demand all the adjustment from labour and then call it “economic logic.”

### To the controlling family
You speak of long-term ownership, but long-term ownership is not served by blowing up trust with the workforce and then expecting production continuity. The family’s horizon should tell you the opposite:
- preserve industrial capability,
- preserve social peace,
- preserve co-determination,
- and preserve the job-security framework that has made this company stable.

If you support closures, then do not pretend it is simply a technical adjustment. It is a direct attack on the industrial compact that has underpinned this company for decades.

### To the regional state government
I appreciate your rejection of closure-first thinking. But sympathy is not enough. If you are serious about your role as anchor shareholder, then use it to defend:
- core sites,
- jobs,
- and binding commitments to the regions that carry this company.

Do not hide behind “numbers” if the outcome is still a closure agenda. The political and social consequences are part of the economics.

## On the specialist model
The expert framework is useful only if it is not used selectively.

Yes, we should compare:
- annual savings,
- one-off costs,
- timing,
- and execution risk.

But the model must also reflect:
- strike and conflict risk from closures,
- loss of know-how,
- quality and ramp-up risk from site exits,
- and the value of labour peace when you do **not** provoke a major confrontation.

If those risks are ignored, then the closure case is being overstated.

## What a real non-closure restructuring must include
We are not saying “no change.” We are saying **no destruction of sites and jobs**.

A serious alternative can include:
- capacity reduction,
- working-time flexibility,
- temporary or partial wage restraint,
- forgoing bonuses,
- voluntary departures only,
- productivity measures,
- and product allocation changes.

But it must be within these limits:
- **no plant closures**
- **no compulsory redundancies**
- **binding job guarantees through 2030**
- **binding site guarantees through 2030**
- **preservation of co-determination and the job-security framework**
- **fair contributions from management and ownership**

If that is “too much” for management or investors, then say so openly. But do not pretend the only remaining option is closure.

## What is not acceptable
I want to be explicit:

- We will not accept a deal that uses the model to justify closures.
- We will not accept compulsory redundancies.
- We will not accept a settlement that breaks the historic job-security promise.
- We will not accept a package that leaves management free to reopen closure threats after workers have made concessions.

That would be workers paying for the crisis while ownership preserves all strategic options. That is not a fair restructuring; it is a transfer of risk downward.

## Bottom line
If the company wants our cooperation, it must come back with a **plant-by-plant non-closure plan** that:
1. preserves every German site,
2. avoids forced layoffs,
3. includes real capacity and cost reductions,
4. includes contributions from management and owners,
5. and locks in job and site security through 2030.

If that cannot be done, then the problem is not the workforce’s unwillingness to help. The problem is that management is trying to solve a structural issue by sacrificing the people and sites that built the company.

We will resist that. In the works council, in the supervisory board, and on the shop floor.
