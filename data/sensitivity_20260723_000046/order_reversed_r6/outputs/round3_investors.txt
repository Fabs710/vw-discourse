We are prepared to engage with a non-closure path, but only on one condition: it must be **economically equivalent** to the savings from closures, not merely more palatable politically.

## Where we agree
- The company needs **real restructuring now**.
- The cost base is too high.
- Temporary gestures and open-ended bargaining are not enough.
- A package can avoid closures **if** it still restores competitive margins and is durable through the cycle.

## Where we disagree

### To the union and works council
We do **not** accept “no closures, no compulsory redundancies, full guarantees through 2030” as a starting point. That is not a restructuring plan; it is a constraint set that may make restructuring impossible.

If the cost gap is large enough, then:
- some sites may need to shrink materially,
- some headcount reduction will be necessary,
- and flexibility must remain on the table.

We are **not** demanding layoffs for their own sake. We are saying that a deal which preserves every legacy protection but fails to fix margins will destroy value for everyone, including employees.

Binding job and site guarantees are acceptable only if they do **not** freeze in an uncompetitive cost base. If they do, they are value-destructive and we cannot support them.

### To the regional state government
We understand the political and social importance of the regional industrial base. But you cannot pre-empt the economics by ruling out closures in practice and then expect capital to fund the consequences.

If a non-closure package is genuinely strong enough, fine. But if it only shifts the problem into the future, then it is not a solution. It is delay with a nicer label.

Also, labour peace matters, but so does profitability. A prolonged conflict or a settlement that leaves the company structurally above target cost will do more damage to the region than a hard but credible restructuring.

### To management
You still owe the market the actual bridge:
- current cost gap,
- savings by measure,
- timing,
- one-off costs,
- margin impact,
- and execution risk.

Without that, no one can judge whether a non-closure package is truly equivalent. “Deep restructuring” is not yet a financial case.

### To the controlling family
We agree with the long-term horizon, but long-term ownership does not mean preserving every plant or every guarantee. It means making the hard call that restores the company’s competitiveness over the next product cycle, not just the next bargaining round.

## Our position
We will support:
- a closure-based package **if it is necessary** to restore competitiveness, or
- a non-closure package **if it delivers the same savings, durability, and enforceability**.

What we will **not** support is:
- a cosmetic compromise,
- a package that protects every site at the expense of margins,
- or a settlement that leaves the company unable to compete on cost.

## On the current proposals
### Conditional non-closure package with a trigger
This is only acceptable if the trigger is **hard, objective, and near-term**. If it simply postpones the closure debate for political convenience, it is not credible.

### Site protection with partial flexibility
This is closer to something we could support, but only if the reductions are deep enough. “Voluntary only” is usually too weak unless the numbers prove otherwise.

### Tripartite burden-sharing
Yes, burden-sharing is necessary. But it has to show up in the P&L, not just in rhetoric. We need quantifiable, recurring savings.

## What we need to see next
Before we move, management should provide a **single quantified model** showing:
1. the current cost gap,
2. the savings from each non-closure measure,
3. the savings from closures,
4. the resulting return-on-sales under each scenario,
5. the point at which the non-closure package fails,
6. and the timing of savings versus costs.

If the non-closure package closes the gap with durable savings, we can support it. If it does not, then closures cannot be ruled out in advance.
