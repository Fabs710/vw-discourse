## Provisional model structure
The cleanest way to compare the options is with a **scenario cash-flow model** at plant level and then consolidated:

### A. Closure scenario
For each plant:
- **Year 0–1 one-off closure cash costs**
  - severance
  - decommissioning
  - remediation
  - transfer costs
  - write-downs
- **Annual avoided cash costs**
  - fixed payroll
  - site overhead
  - utilities and maintenance
  - plant-level SG&A if directly attributable
- **Annual substitution costs**
  - extra cost at receiving plants
  - logistics
  - temporary inefficiency / re-ramp cost

### B. Non-closure scenario
For each plant:
- **Annual savings**
  - headcount reduction
  - wage concessions
  - shift reduction / capacity rationalization
  - productivity improvements
  - overhead simplification
- **Annual costs**
  - guarantee costs / retention costs
  - retraining
  - local capex
  - transition friction
- **Residual underperformance**
  - if utilization remains too low, fixed cost absorption stays weak

### C. Comparison metrics
- **Incremental EBIT**
- **Annual recurring savings**
- **Payback period**
- **NPV**
- **ROS impact**
- **Break-even savings threshold**
- **Execution risk haircut** on expected savings

## How to define “economic equivalence”
A non-closure package is economically equivalent to closure only if, on a **risk-adjusted NPV basis**, it delivers at least the same value as the closure alternative after accounting for:
- one-off costs
- timing delays
- underdelivery risk
- any extra capex or operating subsidies required to keep sites open

A practical test:

\[
NPV_{nonclosure} \ge NPV_{closure}
\]

Where each scenario includes all incremental cash flows versus the current baseline.

If you want a stricter board test, use:
- **higher NPV**
- **shorter payback**
- **equal or better ROS**
- **lower execution risk**

## Provisional threshold logic
Because no hard data were provided, the most defensible way to frame the threshold is:

### Minimum recurring annual savings required for a no-closure package
\[
Annual\ Savings\ Threshold \approx \frac{Closure\ One\text{-}Time\ Costs - Avoided\ Transition\ Costs}{Present\ Value\ Factor} + Risk\ Premium
\]

In plain terms:
- if closure creates large one-time costs,
- a non-closure package can still win if it generates enough recurring savings fast enough,
- but it must also overcome:
  - slower realization,
  - weaker durability,
  - and any extra investment needed to keep plants viable.

A useful rule of thumb in restructuring cases:
- if the non-closure option delivers **less than about 70–80% of closure savings** on a durable cash basis, it usually needs compensating advantages such as much lower one-off costs, faster implementation, or materially lower execution risk.
- if it delivers **90%+ of closure savings** with lower one-offs and better execution, it is often superior financially.

That is only a heuristic, not a conclusion for this case.

## Key financial uncertainty points
The biggest sensitivity drivers will likely be:
1. **Severance magnitude**
2. **Speed of labor-cost realization**
3. **Whether savings are permanent or temporary**
4. **Utilization after capacity reduction**
5. **Incremental capex needed to retain sites**
6. **Implementation delay from negotiation / strike risk**
7. **Demand forecast for the next 3–5 years**

In many restructuring cases, the decision flips depending on:
- discount rate
- how many years the savings are expected to last
- whether the non-closure package requires ongoing concessions that expire
- whether closure causes hidden network/logistics costs
