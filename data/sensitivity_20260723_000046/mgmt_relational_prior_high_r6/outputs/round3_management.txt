Let me respond directly, because the discussion is drifting toward a dangerous illusion: that a restructuring can be “serious” while leaving the cost base largely intact.

I agree with three things that have been said:

1. **This must be durable, not cosmetic.**  
   Everyone is right on that point. A temporary patch will fail.

2. **Industrial peace has value.**  
   A prolonged strike would damage output, reputation, and our ability to invest.

3. **Co-determination and implementation matter.**  
   A deal that cannot survive governance is worthless.

Where I disagree is on the central claim from the employee side and, to a degree, the state side: that we can preserve every German site, rule out compulsory redundancies entirely, maintain the existing job-security framework, and still close the structural gap.

That is not a restructuring. That is postponement.

### To the Works Council
You keep saying “no closures, no compulsory redundancies, no erosion of job security” as if repeating it makes the economics disappear.

It does not.

I am not denying the social cost of closures. I am saying something more basic: **if we do not materially reduce overcapacity and labour cost, the company will continue to underperform, and then we will all face even harsher measures later.**

You say closure-free restructuring is possible in principle. Fine. I accept that in principle. But principle is not enough. You still have not shown a package that is:

- quantified,
- durable,
- enforceable,
- and sufficient to restore competitiveness.

You keep listing instruments:
- working-time flexibility,
- voluntary exits,
- redeployment,
- productivity measures,
- temporary contributions.

Those are all legitimate components. But by themselves they do **not** automatically produce the fixed-cost relief and capacity reduction we need. If they are not deep enough, they become delay tactics.

So here is my position plainly:

- **I am open to avoiding closures if, and only if, the alternative closes the gap in hard numbers.**
- **I am not open to a package that preserves the current footprint and simply asks management to hope for better times.**

### To the Trade Union
I appreciate that you are now at least speaking in terms of burden-sharing rather than slogans. That is progress.

But you are still trying to position labour contribution as something that must be modest and conditional, while asking management and owners to carry the strategic burden. That will not close the gap.

If the workforce wants to avoid closures and compulsory redundancies, then the workforce contribution must be **material**. Not symbolic. Not temporary window dressing.

That means we need to see real numbers on:
- wage moderation,
- reduction or removal of special payments,
- working-time and shift reform,
- productivity increases,
- headcount reduction through voluntary means,
- and tighter staffing discipline.

If the labour contribution is too small, then the alternative is not equivalent. It is simply less painful for one side.

And let me be equally clear: management and ownership are also prepared to contribute through investment commitments and by carrying transition costs. But we cannot be asked to underwrite a structure that remains uncompetitive.

### To the State Government
I understand your concern about the regional industrial base. I do not dismiss that. But I cannot let “industrial continuity” become a code word for preserving too much capacity for too long.

You say closures are not free. Correct. They are not. But neither is keeping unneeded capacity alive indefinitely.

You want a credible alternative. So do I. Then the alternative must show:
- how much capacity comes out,
- how much cost comes out,
- how the remaining plants become competitive,
- and how the brand reaches its return-on-sales target.

If those numbers do not work, then the political preference for avoiding closures does not solve the industrial problem.

### To the Controlling Family and Investors
You are right about one thing: cosmetic compromise would be value-destructive. I agree.

Where I push back is on the idea that the only test of seriousness is whether closures remain on the table as a default. No. The test is whether the package delivers:

- a lower fixed-cost base,
- structurally lower capacity,
- better margins,
- and funds for future technology.

I am open to hybrid solutions. In fact, a hybrid is exactly the kind of path that can reconcile governance constraints with structural change. But only if it is real.

That means:
- capacity consolidation,
- shift reductions,
- voluntary workforce reduction,
- genuine labour-cost relief,
- and, where necessary, site-specific restructuring.

If the employee side cannot deliver equivalent savings without closures, then we must not pretend otherwise. The company cannot be held hostage to an outcome that fails the economics.

### On the expert input
The expert has already made the key point: a closure-free or hybrid solution is possible, but only if it is deep enough. I agree with that.

What I do **not** accept is the implication that because closure-free options are “governable,” they are automatically sufficient. Governance feasibility is not the same as competitiveness.

A deal can be politically easier and economically weaker. That is exactly the risk here.

### What I am prepared to discuss
I am prepared to discuss a serious hybrid package with:
- site-specific restructuring,
- capacity consolidation,
- working-time changes,
- voluntary exits,
- redeployment and qualification,
- productivity improvements,
- targeted compensation adjustments,
- and investment commitments tied to the agreement.

I am **not** prepared to endorse:
- a blanket no-closure rule,
- a blanket no-redundancies rule,
- or a settlement that leaves the company structurally uncompetitive.

### What I need next
We are past general statements. If the employee side wants to avoid closures, then bring us a package with:

1. **Annual run-rate savings in euros**
2. **Permanent capacity reduction**
3. **Headcount impact**
4. **Labour-cost contribution**
5. **Implementation timeline**
6. **Evidence of durability**
7. **Binding investment commitments**

If that package is strong enough, I will support it. If not, then closures remain a legitimate and necessary option.

### My bottom line
I will say this as clearly as possible:

- **No cosmetic compromise.**
- **No settlement that preserves the current cost base.**
- **Yes to a deep, quantified, durable restructuring.**
- **Yes to closure-free or hybrid solutions only if they are economically equivalent.**
- **Yes, if needed, to deeper structural actions including closures.**

The company needs competitiveness by 2030, not a comforting story today.
