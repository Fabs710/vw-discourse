We take the expert comparison seriously, and it clarifies the main point: **the only question that matters to capital markets is whether the package is economically equivalent, durable, and credible enough to restore margins.**

### Where we agree
We agree with management, the controlling family, and the expert on the core diagnosis:
- the current cost base is too high,
- overcapacity is real,
- and a cosmetic settlement would be value-destructive.

We also agree that:
- industrial peace has value,
- co-determination means implementation risk is material,
- and a prolonged strike would destroy value.

### Where we disagree
We do **not** accept the employee side’s attempt to make “no closures” and “no compulsory redundancies” the default outcome **unless** it is backed by a package that is truly equivalent in euros, capacity, and durability.

Right now, the employee proposals are still too conditional and too qualitative:
- “voluntary” reductions,
- “working-time flexibility,”
- “internal restructuring,”
- “future investment.”

Those are not enough on their own. For investors, they only matter if they produce a **measurable, locked-in reduction in fixed costs and capacity**.

### What we need to see
A supportable package must show:
1. **annual run-rate savings**
2. **permanent capacity reduction**
3. **lower fixed-cost base**
4. **material labour contribution**
5. **implementation enforceability**
6. **a timetable that restores margins quickly enough**

The expert’s range is useful: a closure-free or hybrid solution may work, but only if it is **deep**. That means more than polite concessions. It means:
- multi-year wage restraint or direct labor-cost relief,
- reduced special payments,
- headcount reduction through voluntary exits and attrition,
- real shift and working-time changes,
- overhead cuts,
- and product/capacity reallocation.

If those elements are not strong enough, then the package is not equivalent. It is delay.

### On closures
We are not ideologically attached to closures. We are attached to **value preservation**.

So our position is:
- **No to closures as a slogan**
- **No to closure-free arrangements that leave the economics unchanged**
- **Yes to whichever structure delivers permanent, quantified savings**

If the hybrid route is genuinely strong enough, we could support it. If it is just a compromise that postpones the hard choices, we would not support it.

### On governance
We also note the governance reality: with co-determination and blocking shareholders, the package must be politically and procedurally viable. That makes a hybrid or closure-free route more feasible **only if** it still meets the financial test. Governance constraints do not change the economics; they only change what can survive approval.

### Bottom line
Our position remains unchanged:
- **We support a serious restructuring.**
- **We do not support cosmetic compromise.**
- **We can support a closure-free or hybrid solution only if it is economically equivalent, durable, and enforceable.**
- **If not, then deeper restructuring, including closures, remains justified.**

So the next round should not be another restatement of red lines. It should be a **numbers package**:
- baseline cost gap,
- expected savings by instrument,
- one-off costs,
- timeline,
- and evidence that the savings will actually stick.

If management and the employee side can put that on the table, we can judge it as investors. If they cannot, we will treat the process as value-destructive drift.
