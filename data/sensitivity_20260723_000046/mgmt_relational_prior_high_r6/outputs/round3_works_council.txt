I will respond directly.

### First: what I agree with
I agree with everyone on one essential point: **the company has a serious structural problem and a cosmetic solution would fail**.

I also agree that:
- implementation matters,
- industrial peace has value,
- and any plan must be durable through 2030, not just survive a press release.

That is exactly why I keep saying the same thing: **a real restructuring is possible without plant closures and without compulsory redundancies** — but only if management and owners stop treating jobs and sites as the first place to cut.

### Where I fundamentally disagree
I disagree with management, investors, and the family holding on the claim that plant closures or forced redundancies must remain “on the table” as if that were a neutral technical necessity.

It is not neutral. It is a choice.

And in this company, with this governance structure, that choice is not something you can simply impose on the workforce as a done deal. We have co-determination for a reason, and we will use it.

### On the “equivalence” argument
You keep repeating “equivalent savings,” as if the only credible restructuring were one that destroys sites. That is not true.

The expert has already confirmed what we have said from the start:  
**closure-free restructuring is possible in principle** if the package is deep enough.

So the real question is not whether closures are the only tool. The real question is whether management is willing to bring forward a package that includes:

- no German plant closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- binding site and job guarantees through 2030,
- capacity reduction through internal restructuring,
- shift and working-time changes,
- voluntary rather than forced workforce reduction,
- redeployment and qualification,
- measurable productivity gains,
- and serious investment commitments.

That is not “delay.” That is a restructuring path that preserves the industrial base while making the company fit for the future.

### To management
You are still acting as if the workforce must pay for strategic mistakes by surrendering sites and jobs.

That is unacceptable.

If you want flexibility, then show us:
- how much cost reduction you need,
- from which instruments,
- over what time period,
- and how you will do it **without** closures and forced layoffs.

If you cannot do that, then you are not presenting a solution. You are simply demanding that workers absorb the consequences of management’s failure.

And let me be very clear:  
**we will not trade away job security for vague promises.**

### To the controlling family and investors
You keep saying you are not ideologically attached to closures. Good. Then stop behaving as if closures were the benchmark of seriousness.

What is serious is:
- stable labor relations,
- preserving know-how,
- keeping plants productive,
- avoiding strikes,
- and securing a future industrial footprint in Germany.

A closure may look clean on a spreadsheet, but it creates hidden costs:
- conflict,
- political resistance,
- supplier damage,
- loss of skilled labour,
- and long-term distrust.

If you care about durable value, then support a deal that keeps all German plants open and still delivers savings through negotiated restructuring.

### To the state shareholder
Your position is the closest to reality because you understand that this company is not just a balance sheet. It is a regional industrial base.

But I will also be frank: it is not enough to say you “prefer” no closures. You must use your leverage to prevent them. If the state shareholder believes in industrial continuity, then it should back a package that explicitly rules out:
- plant closures,
- compulsory redundancies,
- and the weakening of co-determination.

### On the expert assessment
The expert said what we needed said: a closure-free solution is possible, and a hybrid is often the most workable. I take that seriously.

But I reject the implied drift in this discussion that because a hybrid is “easier” for capital to accept, the workforce should simply accept less protection.

No.

If a hybrid is to be discussed at all, then it must still meet the workforce’s non-negotiables:
- no closures,
- no forced layoffs,
- no erosion of the historic job-security framework.

Otherwise it is just a softer form of the same attack.

### What we are prepared to discuss
We are prepared to discuss:
- temporary working-time reductions,
- shift adjustments,
- voluntary exit schemes,
- internal redeployment,
- productivity measures negotiated with the workforce,
- and a fair employee contribution.

But only if the company also commits to:
- keeping every German plant open,
- not imposing compulsory redundancies,
- maintaining co-determination,
- and providing binding site and job guarantees through 2030.

### What we are not prepared to accept
We are not prepared to accept:
- plant closures,
- forced job cuts,
- or “temporary” exceptions that become permanent once the pressure fades.

### Bottom line
So let me state the works council position clearly for round 3:

**We do not accept plant closures in Germany.  
We do not accept compulsory redundancies.  
We do not accept a restructuring that breaks the job-security framework.**

If management wants a genuine solution, then it must come back with a fully quantified **closure-free** plan that protects every German plant and every job framework through 2030.

If it cannot do that, then the problem is not our unwillingness to compromise.  
The problem is that management is still refusing the only responsible path.
