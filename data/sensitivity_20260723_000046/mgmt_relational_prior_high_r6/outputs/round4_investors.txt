We support the direction of the discussion more than the rhetoric from the employee side, but we are still **not** at a point where we can back a settlement on faith.

## Where we agree
We agree with management, the family holding, and the expert on the core issue:
- the company has a **structural cost and capacity problem**,
- a cosmetic deal would be value-destructive,
- and the solution must be **durable, quantified, and enforceable**.

We also agree with the union and state shareholder on one practical point:
- a prolonged strike or governance deadlock would destroy value.

## Where we disagree
We **do not** accept the works council and union position that “no closures” and “no compulsory redundancies” can be treated as a sufficient answer **unless** they are backed by hard numbers proving equivalence.

Right now, the employee side is still describing instruments, not a complete economic solution:
- working-time flexibility,
- voluntary exits,
- redeployment,
- productivity gains,
- temporary restraint.

Those are relevant, but by themselves they do **not** yet demonstrate that the cost base and capacity problem is fully closed.

We also do **not** accept the state shareholder’s implied suggestion that preserving every German site is the governing principle. It is not. The governing principle is whether the company can restore margins and remain investable over the medium term.

## Our position
We are **not ideologically attached** to closures. We are attached to **risk-adjusted value preservation**.

So our test remains simple:
- if a **closure-free or hybrid** package is **economically equivalent, durable, and enforceable**, we can support it;
- if not, then deeper restructuring, including closures, remains justified and necessary.

That is not a negotiating pose. It is the minimum threshold for capital support.

## What we need to see
The expert has already confirmed what matters: the discussion must be decided on a **side-by-side quantified comparison**. We need, at minimum:
1. baseline cost gap,
2. annual run-rate savings,
3. one-off restructuring costs,
4. permanent capacity reduction,
5. headcount impact,
6. implementation timeline,
7. durability through 2030,
8. governance feasibility.

Without that, there is no basis to say the alternative package is truly competitive.

## On burden-sharing
We are open to a hybrid route if it delivers real value. But burden-sharing must be **material**, not symbolic.

That means:
- labor contribution,
- management discipline,
- owner support where needed,
- and genuine industrial simplification.

If labor concessions are modest and temporary while the company keeps most of the structural overcapacity, then the package is not a solution. It is a delay.

## Our boundary
We will not support an outcome that:
- leaves the cost base structurally uncompetitive,
- leaves margins impaired,
- or substitutes politics for economics.

If the alternative package meets the numbers, we can back it. If it does not, we will not pretend otherwise.
