Below is a neutral, numbers-based comparison of the three restructuring archetypes from an industrial-finance and operations perspective. I will use industry benchmarks and the mechanics typical for large German automotive plants. Because I do not have the company’s internal data, these are **scenario ranges**, not audited facts.

## 1) Baseline framing: what has to be solved
For a large German OEM facing:
- excess domestic capacity,
- high fixed labor and energy overhead,
- weak European demand,
- EV transition investments,
- and a return-on-sales gap,

the restructuring problem is usually not just a wage issue. It is a **fixed-cost and utilization problem**.

A durable solution normally needs to remove:
- **capacity** that cannot be profitably loaded,
- **headcount** tied to that capacity,
- **labor cost per vehicle**,
- and some amount of **organizational complexity**.

In German automotive restructurings, the key distinction is:

- **Closure-based**: strongest at permanently removing fixed cost and capacity.
- **Hybrid**: can get close if it combines deep internal consolidation with strong labor concessions.
- **Closure-free**: feasible in principle, but only if the company can extract unusually large, durable concessions and productivity gains.

---

## 2) Side-by-side comparison

### A. Closure-based restructuring
**Typical instruments**
- Close one or more plants or major sections
- Shift production to remaining sites or external suppliers
- Reduce headcount via redundancy, transfer, retirement, and voluntary exits
- Renegotiate labor conditions as part of a broader reset

**Indicative annual run-rate savings**
- **€700m to €1.5bn+ annually** for a large German passenger-car footprint, depending on the number and size of sites closed and the amount of volume shifted out.
- The savings are strongest when the closed site has high fixed overhead and poor utilization.

**One-off restructuring costs**
- **€1.5bn to €4bn+** is plausible at group scale, driven by:
  - severance and social plan costs,
  - asset impairments,
  - site shutdown costs,
  - transfer/logistics changes,
  - supplier requalification,
  - and launch disruption.

**Permanent capacity reduction**
- Usually **material and unambiguous**, often **5–15%+ of domestic capacity** if one substantial site or major line is removed.

**Headcount impact**
- Often **several thousand to low tens of thousands** across direct and indirect roles, depending on scope and whether production is moved or eliminated.

**Implementation timeline**
- **2–5 years** to fully realize most benefits.
- Fastest on the accounting side, slower on operational transfer and product ramp-up.

**Durability**
- **High**. Once a plant is closed and the capacity is removed, the savings are hard to reverse.

**Implementation risk**
- **High industrial-relations and execution risk**, including:
  - stoppages,
  - governance resistance,
  - supplier disruption,
  - loss of know-how,
  - launch slippage.

**Assessment**
- Economically the strongest and most durable.
- Politically and industrially the hardest.
- Best when the capacity gap is large and there is no credible internal absorption route.

---

### B. Hybrid restructuring
By “hybrid” I mean:
- no full closure-first strategy,
- but substantial internal consolidation,
- line/cell reductions,
- shift reductions,
- voluntary exits,
- wage and time concessions,
- site specialization,
- selective outsourcing,
- and possibly one smaller closure or partial shutdown if needed.

**Typical instruments**
- Consolidate production into fewer shifts
- Reduce weekly working time or shift premiums
- Freeze or lower certain compensation elements temporarily or structurally
- Voluntary severance / retirement programs
- Redeploy staff across plants
- Raise productivity through process redesign and automation
- Eliminate low-margin variants or small-volume lines

**Indicative annual run-rate savings**
- **€400m to €1.0bn annually** is a realistic benchmark range if the package is deep and enforced.
- At the upper end, it can approach closure-based outcomes, but only with substantial labor and footprint changes.

**One-off restructuring costs**
- **€800m to €2.5bn** typically.
- Less than a closure-heavy plan, but still significant due to:
  - voluntary exit costs,
  - retraining,
  - internal relocation,
  - retooling,
  - process changes,
  - and possible partial write-downs.

**Permanent capacity reduction**
- **Moderate to material**.
- Often **3–10%** capacity reduction via shift cuts, line rationalization, and product concentration, even without plant shutdowns.

**Headcount impact**
- **Several thousand to perhaps low tens of thousands**, depending on how much is handled by attrition, voluntary programs, and productivity replacement.

**Implementation timeline**
- **1.5–4 years** for full run-rate impact.
- Can start faster than closures if the first steps are shift and wage measures.

**Durability**
- **Medium to high** if the concessions are:
  - multi-year,
  - tied to capacity changes,
  - and accompanied by hard productivity commitments.
- Durability is weaker if the deal is mostly temporary wage restraint without real capacity simplification.

**Implementation risk**
- **Moderate**.
- More governable than closures, but risk of underdelivery is real if the labor contribution is too small or time-limited.

**Assessment**
- Usually the most feasible in German co-determined governance.
- Can work if the company is willing to accept less than a full closure-based reset, but still needs real structural simplification.
- This is often the best compromise when closures are blocked but the economics still require deep action.

---

### C. Closure-free restructuring
By “closure-free” I mean:
- no German plant shutdowns,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- and reliance on internal measures only.

**Typical instruments**
- Working-time changes
- Shift reductions
- Temporary or multi-year wage restraint
- Reduction of special payments
- Voluntary exits only
- Redeployment and training
- Internal production consolidation
- Automation and productivity programs
- Product mix optimization

**Indicative annual run-rate savings**
- **€200m to €600m** is a realistic base-case range.
- **€700m+** is possible only if the package is unusually deep and includes:
  - significant labor cost concessions,
  - major shift/working-time changes,
  - meaningful volume consolidation,
  - and durable productivity gains.

**One-off restructuring costs**
- **€300m to €1.5bn**
- Lower than closure-based plans, but not trivial, because “no closure” does not mean “no cost.”

**Permanent capacity reduction**
- Usually **limited to moderate**, often **2–6%** unless the company truly consolidates volume and product lines across sites.

**Headcount impact**
- Usually absorbed through attrition, voluntary exits, and retirement.
- Direct cuts are lower, but the headcount reduction may still be **several hundred to several thousand** over time if the package is real.

**Implementation timeline**
- **1–3 years** to realize most benefits.
- Faster to announce, slower to prove, and benefits may plateau.

**Durability**
- **Low to medium** unless measures are locked in structurally.
- Temporary concessions alone are not durable enough for a long-cycle automotive transformation.

**Implementation risk**
- **Lower legal/industrial shock**, but **higher risk of insufficient savings**.
- The main risk is that the package looks acceptable but does not actually close the cost gap.

**Assessment**
- Feasible only if the underlying gap is not too large, or if non-labor measures are also very strong.
- In a situation with high German costs and structural overcapacity, this is often **not enough by itself** unless the company accepts a smaller reset and/or complements it with strong product and pricing improvements.

---

## 3) What is “equivalent” to a closure-based solution?
A closure-free or hybrid package is economically comparable to a closure-based one only if it closes the gap on four dimensions:

1. **Run-rate savings**
   - Not just year-one savings, but recurring annual savings.

2. **Fixed-cost removal**
   - Permanent rather than temporary cost reduction.

3. **Capacity reduction**
   - Enough to improve utilization and avoid reinflating overhead.

4. **Durability**
   - Savings must survive through 2030, not evaporate after a wage accord expires.

A common failure mode in automotive turnarounds is:
- temporary wage restraint,
- voluntary departures,
- some productivity gains,
- but no meaningful capacity cut.

That can stabilize cash for 12–24 months, but it often does **not** structurally reset competitiveness.

---

## 4) Practical benchmark conclusions

### If the cost gap is large
If management’s gap is truly in the **high hundreds of millions to low billions per year**, then:
- **closure-based** or **strong hybrid** becomes the economically credible zone.
- **closure-free** is unlikely to be sufficient unless the labor contribution is unusually large and durable.

### If the cost gap is moderate
If the gap is smaller and demand recovers somewhat, then:
- a **hybrid** package may be enough,
- especially if combined with product rationalization and better factory utilization.

### If closures are blocked
Then the company must replace the lost savings with some combination of:
- deeper working-time reductions,
- lower compensation,
- shift/plant specialization,
- more automation,
- more aggressive product concentration,
- and possibly supplier or footprint outsourcing.

Without that, the solution risks being politically acceptable but economically incomplete.

---

## 5) Durability through 2030: what really matters
For a deal to last to 2030, the following features matter more than slogans:

- **multi-year binding terms**, not one-off sacrifices
- **mechanical capacity reduction**, not just promises
- **clear staffing path** with attrition and voluntary exits
- **product allocation commitments** tied to volumes
- **investment triggers** linked to labor concessions
- **reset of overhead structure** through process redesign

Temporary wage cuts or short-lived freezes are weak unless paired with structural simplification.

---

## 6) Bottom line
My neutral assessment is:

- **Closure-based restructuring** offers the strongest and most durable cost reset, but carries the highest execution and conflict risk.
- **Hybrid restructuring** is usually the most feasible under German co-determination and can be economically sufficient if it is deep enough.
- **Closure-free restructuring** is possible in principle, but in a case with severe structural overcapacity and a high German cost base, it is the hardest to make truly equivalent. It tends to underdeliver unless the labor contribution and internal capacity reduction are unusually large.
