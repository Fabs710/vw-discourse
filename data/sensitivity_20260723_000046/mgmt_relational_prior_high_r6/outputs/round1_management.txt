I hear the red lines you are setting, but I need to be equally clear: **a package that preserves every site, rules out closures, rules out compulsory redundancies, and keeps all historic guarantees intact is not a structurally credible answer to the crisis**.

We are not dealing with a normal cyclical downturn. We have a **fundamental cost and capacity problem**:

- German factory costs are **materially above target**,
- European demand is shrinking,
- competition is intensifying,
- and the brand is not meeting its return-on-sales target.

Under those conditions, a purely defensive approach just delays the problem and burns cash that we need for future products, battery technology, software, and the 2030 portfolio. That is not acceptable from a management and fiduciary standpoint.

Where I agree with you:
- I agree that **this cannot be solved by unilateral diktat**.
- I agree that we need a **durable industrial plan**, not a cosmetic announcement.
- I agree that **worker participation matters** and that any settlement has to be negotiated.
- I also agree that **investment commitments are essential** if we are asking people to contribute.

Where I disagree, sharply:
- **No closures, no compulsory redundancies, and full guarantees through 2030 regardless of economics** is not a realistic basis for restoring competitiveness.
- A “shared burden” cannot mean that labour makes only symbolic concessions while management carries the full adjustment and the company remains uncompetitive.
- Capacity reductions that are merely reversible on paper, or internal shifts that do not remove structural excess cost, will not be enough.

So let me state the management position plainly:

We need a **real structural package** with:
1. **meaningful capacity reduction**,  
2. **a substantial labour-cost contribution**,  
3. **a lower fixed-cost base**, and  
4. **binding flexibility for the group to realign production**.

I am open on the **form** this takes. That includes:
- alternative measures to outright closure if they produce the same durable effect,
- working-time adjustments,
- site-specific restructuring,
- targeted redeployment,
- and negotiated concessions on pay and guarantees.

But I am **not** open to a deal that leaves the cost base fundamentally unchanged and simply postpones the hard choices.

To the union: I understand your concern about trust and industrial peace. But trust is already being damaged by the fact that the company is being asked to carry an uncompetitive structure into the next decade. If the workforce wants security, the company must first be viable. That viability cannot be built on slogans.

If you want to avoid closures, then bring forward a package that is **quantitatively equivalent** in cost and capacity terms — not just politically more comfortable. Otherwise, management will have to pursue the deeper restructuring path, including closures where necessary.

So my question back to you is simple:
**What concrete, durable savings are you prepared to put on the table that actually close the gap — in euros, in capacity, and over time?**
