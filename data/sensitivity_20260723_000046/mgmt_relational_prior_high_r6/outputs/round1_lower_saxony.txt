As the regional state shareholder, I need to be very clear: I **do** recognize the seriousness of the cost and capacity problem. No one here is pretending that the market has not changed or that the company can ignore competitiveness. A durable solution must improve profitability and secure future investment.

Where I **agree** with management:
- The company needs a credible restructuring, not cosmetic adjustments.
- The long-term viability of German operations matters.
- Any solution must be economically sound, not just politically convenient.

But I must draw a firm line where the discussion has gone too far.

I **do not accept** the idea that the only credible path is plant closures in Germany, mass compulsory redundancies, or breaking the co-determination and site-protection framework. That would be a destructive answer with severe consequences for:
- regional employment,
- supplier networks,
- social stability,
- and the company’s legitimacy as a regional industrial anchor.

That is not a path I can support, and given the special rights attached to our shareholding, it is not a path that will pass easily.

I also disagree with the implication that anything short of closures is merely “symbolic.” That is too narrow. There are **serious structural alternatives** that can create real savings while preserving the industrial base, for example:
- capacity consolidation without abandoning sites,
- shift adjustments and more flexible working-time models,
- voluntary rather than compulsory workforce reduction,
- internal redeployment and qualification,
- targeted efficiency measures across the group,
- and a negotiated labour contribution tied to binding investment and product commitments.

What I will not support is a one-sided restructuring that forces the workforce to absorb the pain while ownership and management preserve strategic freedom and then expect political backing for the fallout.

To the works council and union: your refusal of closures and compulsory redundancies is understandable and, in my view, legitimate. But if you want broad political and shareholder support, the workforce side will also need to show that it is prepared to contribute to a solution that is economically meaningful, not only defensive. I would expect openness to:
- temporary and reversible concessions,
- productivity agreements,
- flexibility in working time,
- and possibly moderated labour-cost adjustments,

**provided** there are binding guarantees on:
- no plant closures,
- no forced layoffs,
- continued investment in future products,
- and preservation of the company’s role in the region.

So my position is this:
1. **No plant closures as a starting point for negotiation.**
2. **No compulsory redundancies.**
3. **Yes to a real restructuring, but through negotiated, socially responsible measures.**
4. **Yes to labour contributions, but only if management and owners also contribute and the company commits to the German industrial base.**

If management wants our support, it needs to come back with a package that is not just a demand for sacrifice, but a balanced plan that protects the region, preserves co-determination, and restores competitiveness without gutting the company’s domestic footprint.

I am prepared to keep working toward that. I am **not** prepared to endorse closures as the default answer.
