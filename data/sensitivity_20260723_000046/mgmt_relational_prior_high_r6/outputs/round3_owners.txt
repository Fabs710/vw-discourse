We are now at the point where the decision has to be judged on **economic reality, not on wishful framing**.

## Where I agree
I agree with management, the expert, and the capital side on the core issue:  
- the company has a **structural cost and capacity problem**,  
- a cosmetic deal would be value-destructive,  
- and anything that is not **durable, quantified, and enforceable** is not serious.

I also agree with the works council, union, and state shareholder on one important point:  
- a prolonged conflict would destroy value, damage the brand, and weaken the company’s future investment capacity.

So there is common ground on the diagnosis and on the need for durability.

## Where I disagree
I do **not** accept the employee-side position that “no closures” and “no compulsory redundancies” can simply be declared as red lines and then treated as a complete restructuring answer.

That is not a solution. It is a preference.

If the existing footprint is structurally too large for the market, then refusing closures in principle does not make the economics go away. It only shifts the burden into:
- weaker margins,
- delayed investment,
- and, eventually, harsher measures later.

I also do **not** accept the state shareholder’s implication that preserving every German site is the governing principle. It is not.  
The governing principle is whether the enterprise remains competitive and investable over the long term.

## What I take from the expert
The expert has clarified the real point well:
- **closure-based restructuring** is the most direct way to remove fixed costs and overcapacity,
- **closure-free restructuring** is possible only if it is unusually deep,
- and a **hybrid** path is often the most feasible under German governance constraints.

That is broadly right.

But feasibility is not the same as sufficiency.  
A hybrid only works if it is **economically equivalent enough**, not merely politically more comfortable.

## My position
I will support a settlement only if it delivers all of the following:

- a **material and durable reduction in fixed costs**,
- **real capacity reduction**,
- a **measurable labor-cost contribution**,
- **enforceable implementation**,
- and a credible path to restoring margins and future investment capacity.

I am open on the instrument mix:
- closures,
- hybrid footprint reduction,
- working-time reform,
- voluntary exits,
- redeployment,
- productivity gains,
- and negotiated labor concessions.

What I am **not** open to is a package that preserves the current structure, delays the adjustment, and asks owners to finance the postponement.

## To the works council and union
If you want closures off the table, then bring a package that is genuinely equivalent in **euros, capacity, and permanence**.  
That means hard numbers:
- annual run-rate savings,
- headcount effect,
- capacity removed,
- and duration.

“Voluntary,” “flexible,” and “negotiated” are not enough by themselves. They are instruments, not outcomes.

And let me be equally direct: if the labor contribution is too small, then the alternative is not equivalent. It is simply less painful for labor and more painful for the enterprise.

## To the state shareholder
I respect your regional responsibility. But I cannot endorse a position that effectively immunizes German capacity from economic reality. That would be value-destructive over time.

If you want to rule out closures, then you must help make the alternative genuinely strong enough:
- lower fixed costs,
- less overcapacity,
- better utilization,
- and binding productivity improvement.

Otherwise, we are just deferring the problem.

## To management
You are right to insist on depth and durability. But do not confuse **maximum leverage** with **optimal outcome**.  
A package that triggers prolonged industrial conflict may be technically clean and economically contaminated.

So the right test is not “closures or nothing.”  
The right test is whether the final package closes the gap in a way that is:
- sustainable,
- governable,
- and value-accretive.

## What I want next
I support the request for the independent industrial finance comparison.  
But it must do more than restate generalities. It should give us a side-by-side view of:

- annual run-rate savings,
- one-off restructuring costs,
- headcount impact,
- capacity removed,
- implementation timeline,
- and governance feasibility.

## Bottom line
My position is unchanged:

- **No cosmetic compromise.**
- **No settlement that leaves the company structurally weak.**
- **Yes to a deep, quantified, durable restructuring.**
- **Yes to closure-free or hybrid solutions only if they truly match the economics.**
- **Yes to closures if that is the only way to protect long-term enterprise value.**

I will not support an outcome that permanently impairs the enterprise’s value or leaves governance control exposed. If the alternative package can truly solve the problem, I will back it through governance. If it cannot, then deeper restructuring must remain on the table.
