I will be direct.

## Where I agree
I agree with management, the expert, and the capital side on the central diagnosis:  
this company has a **structural cost and capacity problem**, and a cosmetic compromise would be value-destructive.

I also agree with the works council, union, and state shareholder on one important point:  
a prolonged conflict would destroy value, damage the brand, and weaken the company’s ability to invest.

So yes:
- the problem is real,
- the solution must be durable,
- and industrial peace matters.

## Where I disagree
I do **not** accept the employee-side position that **“no closures”** and **“no compulsory redundancies”** can simply be declared as red lines and then treated as a complete restructuring answer.

That is not a solution. It is a preference.

If the German footprint is structurally too large for the market, then refusing closures in principle does not make the economics disappear. It only shifts the burden into:
- weaker margins,
- delayed investment,
- and eventually harsher measures later.

I also do **not** accept the state shareholder’s implication that preserving every German site is the governing principle. It is not.  
The governing principle is whether the enterprise remains competitive and investable over the long term.

## On the expert’s assessment
The expert has clarified the core reality well:
- **closure-based restructuring** is the strongest instrument for removing fixed costs and overcapacity,
- **hybrid restructuring** is often the most feasible under German governance,
- **closure-free restructuring** is only credible if it is unusually deep and durable.

That is the right framework.

But I want to be equally clear:  
feasibility is not the same as sufficiency.

A hybrid may be the most practical route, but only if it is **economically close enough** to the needed reset. Otherwise it is just a politically easier way to underdeliver.

## To the works council
You keep saying “no closures, no compulsory redundancies, no erosion of job security” as if repeating it creates an industrial plan.

It does not.

You say closure-free restructuring is possible in principle. I accept that. But principle is not enough. You still have not shown a package that is:

- quantified,
- durable,
- enforceable,
- and sufficient to restore competitiveness.

You list instruments:
- working-time changes,
- voluntary exits,
- redeployment,
- productivity measures,
- temporary restraint.

All legitimate. But none of them automatically equals the fixed-cost relief of removing redundant capacity. If they do not add up to enough, they become delay tactics.

So my position remains:

- I am open to avoiding closures **if, and only if, the alternative closes the gap in hard numbers**.
- I am not open to a package that preserves the current structure and calls that restructuring.

## To the union
You are right to insist that workers should not carry the burden alone. I agree with that.

But I need to be equally blunt: if the workforce wants closures and compulsory redundancies off the table, then the workforce contribution must be **material**. Not symbolic. Not temporary window dressing.

That means real numbers on:
- wage moderation,
- reduction or removal of special payments,
- working-time and shift reform,
- productivity gains,
- voluntary departure,
- staffing discipline.

If the labour contribution is too small, then the alternative is not equivalent. It is just less painful for labor and more painful for the enterprise.

## To the state shareholder
I respect your regional responsibility. I do not dismiss it.

But “industrial continuity” cannot mean insulating the German footprint from economic reality. That would be irresponsible governance. If you want closures off the table, then the alternative must still close the gap in hard numbers:
- lower fixed cost,
- less overcapacity,
- better utilization,
- and a credible margin recovery path.

## To the management side
You are right to insist on depth and durability. I support that.

Where I would caution you is against treating **maximum leverage** as the same thing as **optimal outcome**. A package that triggers prolonged industrial conflict may be technically clean and economically contaminated.

So the right test is not “closures or nothing.”  
The right test is whether the final package is:
- sustainable,
- governable,
- and value-accretive.

## My position on the options
Let me be explicit.

### I am prepared to discuss
- a **hybrid restructuring**
- targeted capacity consolidation
- shift and working-time reform
- voluntary exits and redeployment
- productivity improvements
- negotiated labor-cost contribution
- binding investment commitments tied to the agreement

### I am not prepared to accept
- a blanket no-closure rule
- a blanket no-redundancies rule
- a settlement that leaves the company structurally uncompetitive
- a package that protects the current cost base and postpones the adjustment

## What I need next
We are past general debate. If the employee side wants to avoid closures, then bring a package with:

1. **annual run-rate savings in euros**
2. **permanent capacity reduction**
3. **headcount impact**
4. **labour-cost contribution**
5. **implementation timeline**
6. **durability through 2030**
7. **binding investment commitments**

If the package is strong enough, I will support it through governance. If not, then closures remain a legitimate and necessary option.

## Bottom line
I will not support a cosmetic compromise.

I will support a deal that:
- materially reduces overcapacity,
- lowers labour and fixed costs,
- restores competitiveness,
- and frees funds for future products and technology to 2030.

That is the test. Not whether the package sounds acceptable in the room. Whether it actually solves the problem.
