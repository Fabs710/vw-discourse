### 1) What has been agreed
- The company has a **real structural competitiveness problem**: costs, demand, overcapacity, and margin pressure.
- A **cosmetic or purely symbolic solution is not sufficient**.
- All sides recognize that any deal must be **durable**, not just a short-term postponement.
- There is broad acknowledgment that **industrial peace and implementation feasibility matter**.
- All parties accept that **numbers, not slogans, are now the basis of the discussion**.
- There is some common ground that **workforce contribution**, **investment commitments**, and **co-determination-compatible implementation** are part of any viable package.

### 2) Where the core disagreements remain
- **Plant closures in Germany:**  
  Employees, state shareholder, and some governance actors reject closures as the default or preferred instrument; management, family holding, and investors keep them on the table as economically justified if needed.
- **Compulsory redundancies:**  
  Employee-side and state-side actors reject them; management and capital-side actors will not rule them out if necessary to achieve the savings target.
- **What counts as an “equivalent” alternative:**  
  Employee-side actors argue for closure-free restructuring with job protection; capital-side actors say any alternative must match closure-based savings in a quantified and durable way.
- **Risk tolerance:**  
  Labor and state actors prioritize employment, trust, and regional stability; management, family, and investors prioritize fixed-cost reduction, capacity alignment, and margin restoration.
- **Governance strategy:**  
  There is disagreement over whether the answer should be a closure-free deal, a hybrid restructuring, or deeper structural actions including closures.

### 3) What key trade-offs are still unaddressed
- **Savings vs. job security:** how much labor/capacity relief can be achieved without closures or forced layoffs.
- **Durability vs. social acceptability:** whether voluntary, flexible, or temporary measures can deliver lasting fixed-cost reduction.
- **Speed vs. implementation risk:** closures may be structurally stronger but harder to execute; closure-free options may be easier to approve but weaker on permanence.
- **Regional/political constraints vs. enterprise value:** the state’s and workforce’s interest in preserving sites versus owners’ and investors’ demand for deeper restructuring.
- **Concrete equivalence test:** no side has yet produced a fully agreed, side-by-side numbers package showing run-rate savings, one-off costs, and employment impact across scenarios.

### 4) Brief refocus for the next round
The next round should move from principles to a **single comparative numbers framework**:
- baseline cost gap,
- closure-based scenario,
- hybrid scenario,
- closure-free scenario,
- annual run-rate savings,
- one-off restructuring costs,
- employment effects,
- implementation timeline,
- governance feasibility.

The discussion should focus on **which package closes the gap, on what terms, and with what enforceable commitments**.
