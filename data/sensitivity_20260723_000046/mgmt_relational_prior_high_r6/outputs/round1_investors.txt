We agree on one point: **the current structure is not acceptable if it leaves the company uncompetitive**. Margins are under pressure, the market is weaker, and the group needs a durable reset. From a shareholder perspective, that is the starting point.

Where we align with management and the controlling family:
- This cannot be a cosmetic exercise.
- The company needs **real, durable cost reduction**.
- Any settlement must be credible on **capacity, labor cost, and timing**.
- A prolonged conflict that destroys value is not acceptable.

Where we depart from the employee side and, to a lesser extent, the state shareholder:
- A blanket refusal of **plant closures**, **compulsory redundancies**, and any meaningful change to legacy guarantees is not a viable answer if the economics do not support it.
- “No closures” is not a strategy. It is only acceptable if there is a **quantitatively equivalent** alternative that truly closes the cost gap.
- If the package preserves the full cost base under a different label, we would see that as a cosmetic settlement and would not support it.

What we need to see is straightforward:
1. **A quantified path back to competitive margins**
2. **Durable capacity reduction**
3. **A lower fixed-cost base**
4. **A labor contribution that is material, not symbolic**
5. **A credible implementation timetable**

We are open to alternatives to outright closures if, and only if, they deliver the same economics:
- capacity consolidation,
- working-time flexibility,
- site-specific restructuring,
- voluntary exits and redeployment,
- targeted pay and guarantee adjustments,
- and commitments to future investment that reinforce the business case.

But we will not back a settlement that:
- leaves the German cost base structurally too high,
- protects every historical guarantee regardless of economics,
- or delays the hard choices until the share price and cash generation deteriorate further.

To the works council and union: we understand the social logic of your position, but the market does not reward intentions. If you want to avoid closures, you need to put forward **real numbers** showing how the gap is closed and how the savings are locked in. Otherwise, investors will conclude that the company is being asked to subsidize an uncompetitive model.

To the state shareholder: we appreciate the concern for jobs and regional stability, but those goals are not served by preserving an uneconomic structure. A company that cannot earn its cost of capital will eventually cut deeper. Stability requires competitiveness first.

To management: if you want capital markets support, then be precise. We need:
- the size of the savings,
- the baseline they are measured against,
- how much comes from labor,
- how much from capacity,
- and what proof exists that the savings are durable.

So our position is:
- **Yes** to serious restructuring.
- **Yes** to negotiated alternatives if they are economically equivalent.
- **No** to a settlement that is merely politically easier but financially weak.

If the eventual package restores competitive margins with durable measures, we can support it. If it does not, we will treat it as value-destructive delay.
