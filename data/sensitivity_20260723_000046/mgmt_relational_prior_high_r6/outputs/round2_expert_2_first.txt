Below is a neutral expert assessment of the requested comparison: **closure-based, hybrid, and closure-free restructuring scenarios** for a large German automotive manufacturer, with emphasis on **quantified savings ranges, employment impact, implementation risk, and governance feasibility** under **German co-determination** and **special-shareholder constraints**.

## 1) Executive conclusion

**A closure-free restructuring is possible in principle, but only if it is unusually deep and multi-year.**  
In most German automotive cases, a **hybrid solution** is the most feasible path because it can combine:

- real capacity reduction,
- labor-cost relief,
- voluntary headcount reduction,
- product and shift consolidation,
- and binding investment commitments,

while remaining more governable than outright plant closures.

**Plant closures remain the most direct and durable instrument** for eliminating overcapacity and fixed costs, but they also carry the highest:
- industrial conflict risk,
- political risk,
- litigation/implementation friction,
- and governance risk under co-determination and special-shareholder veto structures.

## 2) Decision framework: what must be solved

For a large German OEM in this situation, the restructuring must close four gaps:

1. **Annual run-rate cost gap**  
2. **Installed capacity gap**  
3. **Headcount gap**  
4. **Governance feasibility gap**  

A package is only credible if it is durable over the cycle, not just one bargaining round.

---

## 3) Scenario comparison

### A. Closure-based restructuring
**Definition:** One or more German plants closed or substantially mothballed; major capacity exits; significant workforce reduction; labor concessions may accompany but do not substitute for closures.

### Typical savings range
For a large automotive site, depending on size and utilization:

- **Annual run-rate savings:** roughly **€150m to €400m+ per major site** once fully implemented
- **One-off restructuring charges:** often **€300m to €1.5bn+** per site or larger network action, depending on severance, decommissioning, write-downs, logistics reconfiguration, and transfer support

These are broad industry ranges, not company-specific numbers.

### Employment impact
- High direct job loss
- Indirect supplier and regional effects
- Strong know-how loss risk if closures are poorly sequenced

### Advantages
- Highest certainty of permanent fixed-cost removal
- Clear capacity reduction
- Strongest long-term structural effect

### Risks
- Very high strike/escalation risk
- Potentially lengthy works-council/supervisory-board conflict
- Political intervention risk
- Reputational damage
- Execution delays can erode expected savings

### Governance feasibility
Under a **20-member, paritally codetermined supervisory board**, a major closure typically requires a supermajority and therefore effective agreement from employee-side representatives or a package strong enough to induce abstention/support.  
If the company also has a **special statutory state shareholder veto/blocking minority** and a controlling family acting through governance, then closure is **technically possible only if the major blocking actors converge**. In practical terms, that makes closure-based restructuring **governance-intensive and fragile**.

---

### B. Closure-free restructuring
**Definition:** No German plant closures and no compulsory redundancies; savings come from working-time, pay, shift, capacity, and voluntary measures.

### Typical savings range
A closure-free package can still be substantial if deep:

- **Annual run-rate savings:** roughly **€100m to €300m+** for a major group-scale package
- In unusually strong cases, higher figures are possible, but only with:
  - multi-year wage restraint,
  - meaningful reduction of special payments,
  - shift and working-time redesign,
  - strict hiring freezes,
  - voluntary exits,
  - and major productivity gains.

### Employment impact
- Better employment preservation
- Reductions mainly via attrition, voluntary departures, redeployment, and reduced hours
- Less regional shock

### Advantages
- Much lower immediate conflict risk than closure
- Easier to fit co-determination culture
- Better preserves skills and site continuity
- More politically acceptable

### Risks
- Savings often weaker than advertised
- Risk of temporary measures expiring while structural overcapacity remains
- Harder to prove “equivalent” savings to investors
- May fail if volume decline is persistent

### Governance feasibility
This is **most feasible** under German co-determination and special-shareholder constraints because it avoids the hardest veto points.  
However, it still requires:
- a majority on the supervisory board,
- likely support or at least non-opposition from employee representatives,
- and acceptance by the controlling shareholder and state shareholder that the package is economically sufficient.

A closure-free plan is **governable**; the question is whether it is **economically strong enough**.

---

### C. Hybrid restructuring
**Definition:** No blanket closure plan, but real footprint reduction through:
- line consolidation,
- shift elimination,
- product reallocation,
- partial site specialization,
- voluntary workforce reduction,
- targeted pay/bonus moderation,
- internal transfers,
- and possibly one limited site exit or partial closure.

### Typical savings range
This is often the most realistic compromise:

- **Annual run-rate savings:** roughly **€120m to €350m+**
- **One-off costs:** typically lower than full closure, but still meaningful, often **€150m to €800m+**

### Employment impact
- Material headcount reduction, but less abrupt than closure
- More scope for voluntary and attrition-based reduction
- Better retention of critical skills than a hard closure path

### Advantages
- Balances economics and governability
- Can deliver real capacity reduction without triggering maximum conflict
- More likely to pass co-determination and political scrutiny
- Can be tied to investment commitments and product allocation

### Risks
- Can become diluted if every side tries to protect its own interests
- Requires very precise implementation discipline
- If “hybrid” is only a euphemism for delay, markets will discount it

### Governance feasibility
**Highest of the three** in the current structure, because it gives each blocking actor something to support:
- management gets structural relief,
- employee side avoids broad forced closures,
- state government can defend employment and regional continuity,
- family owners/investors can claim durable savings.

---

## 4) What “equivalent savings” means in practice

A closure-free alternative is only credible if it closes the **run-rate cost gap**, not just the cash gap for one year.

In practical terms, a credible non-closure package usually must include some combination of:

- **2–5 years of wage moderation or direct labor-cost relief**
- **reduced special payments**
- **working-time reductions or annual hour flexibility**
- **higher utilization of remaining plants**
- **strict hiring freeze**
- **voluntary severance/retirement programs**
- **reduced temporary labor**
- **overhead and procurement cuts**
- **product allocation changes**

If the package only includes small or temporary concessions, it will not be equivalent to closure-based savings.

## 5) Typical implementation risk by scenario

### Closure-based
- **Industrial conflict risk:** very high
- **Legal/governance friction:** high
- **Execution certainty after decision:** high
- **Risk of underdelivery:** moderate, mostly due to delay and disruption

### Closure-free
- **Industrial conflict risk:** low to moderate
- **Governance friction:** moderate
- **Execution certainty:** moderate
- **Risk of underdelivery:** high if measures are temporary or shallow

### Hybrid
- **Industrial conflict risk:** moderate
- **Governance friction:** moderate
- **Execution certainty:** moderate to high if structured carefully
- **Risk of underdelivery:** moderate

## 6) Co-determination and shareholder constraints

### Co-determination
With a 50/50 supervisory board and a high approval threshold for plant closures, the employee side has an effective veto over hard closure decisions unless:
- there is a negotiated trade-off,
- abstentions are engineered,
- or the company uses a legally and politically sustainable restructuring path short of formal closure.

### Special-shareholder constraints
If the state shareholder has a veto/blocking-minority position under special statute, and the controlling family holds the voting majority, then major restructuring requires **alignment among multiple blocking actors**. That strongly favors:
- negotiated packages,
- phased implementation,
- and hybrid structures over abrupt closures.

### Practical governance implication
**A closure-free or hybrid plan is materially more feasible to approve and implement than a hard closure plan.**  
That does not make it economically superior, but it makes it more likely to survive the decision process.

---

## 7) Expert judgment on likely best path

### If the cost gap is modest to moderate
A **closure-free package** may be sufficient if it is deep, enforceable, and multi-year.

### If the cost gap is large and structural overcapacity is severe
A **hybrid package** is usually the best compromise between:
- economic sufficiency,
- labor peace,
- and approval feasibility.

### If the company truly needs maximum structural relief
A **closure-based path** is economically strongest, but only if the governance coalition can actually sustain it. In this case, governance may be the limiting factor, not the industrial logic.

---

## 8) Bottom line

My neutral assessment is:

1. **Closure-based restructuring**  
   - strongest structural savings  
   - highest disruption and lowest governance ease

2. **Closure-free restructuring**  
   - most socially and politically feasible  
   - but only credible if exceptionally deep and multi-year

3. **Hybrid restructuring**  
   - most likely to be approved and implemented  
   - usually the best balance of savings, employment impact, and durability

### My practical recommendation, as an expert witness
If the parties are trying to reach an actually implementable settlement under German governance constraints, they should test a **hybrid package** first, and measure it against three criteria:

- **Annual run-rate savings:** target a clearly quantified reduction, likely in the **low hundreds of millions of euros**
- **Permanent capacity reduction:** not merely temporary cuts
- **Enforceability:** co-determination-compatible and acceptable to the blocking shareholders

If the hybrid package cannot close the gap, then the discussion must return to deeper structural actions, including closures.  
But in the current governance environment, a pure closure strategy has the highest chance of becoming a conflict without a clean decision.
