I will be equally direct.

I agree with the broad diagnosis: this company cannot carry a structurally uncompetitive cost base indefinitely. If management is right that margins are under pressure, demand is weak, and investment needs are high, then inaction is not an option. A durable solution must restore competitiveness.

Where I **do** agree with the employee side and the state shareholder is this:
- a purely punitive approach would be self-defeating,
- industrial peace matters,
- and any restructuring has to be credible enough to last, not just create a brief accounting improvement.

Where I **do not** agree is the idea that the company can be made fit for the future while **ruling out closures altogether**, **ruling out compulsory redundancies altogether**, and **preserving all historic protections unchanged**. That is not a serious restructuring framework if the economics require structural capacity reduction. It may be politically attractive, but it is not automatically value-preserving.

From the perspective of a long-term owner, I am concerned about two equally damaging outcomes:
1. a destructive confrontation that drags on, harms reputation, and burns value, and
2. a “soft” settlement that avoids difficult adjustments but leaves the business structurally weak.

So let me be clear on our position:

- We support a real competitiveness reset.
- We support negotiated solutions rather than management theatrics.
- We support investment in future products and technology.
- We support stability where it is compatible with the enterprise’s long-term health.

But we will not support a settlement that simply freezes the current structure and calls it compromise.

To the works council and union: if you want to avoid closures, then the alternative must be **quantitatively convincing**. That means real, durable savings in labor cost and capacity, not just temporary postponements or accounting shifts. “No closures” cannot be the whole answer. If you are asking the owners and management to accept that, then you need to show:
- where the cost comes out,
- how much comes out,
- and why it stays out.

To management: I hear your point that the economics must work. But you should also be careful not to define “economics” so narrowly that you force a confrontation you cannot win. Given the governance structure here, any solution that ignores co-determination and the regional shareholder will not be durable. So if you want structural change, bring forward a plan that is genuinely negotiable and demonstrably sufficient.

My bottom line is simple:
- **No cosmetic compromise.**
- **No permanent impairment of the company’s value.**
- **No governance crisis that weakens control or destabilizes the enterprise.**
- **But also no illusion that the status quo can be preserved without meaningful burden-sharing.**

If management can show a package that restores margins and competitiveness with durable savings, and if the workforce side can contribute in a serious way, then I am open to backing it through governance. If not, I will not support a weak settlement that postpones the problem and erodes value later.
