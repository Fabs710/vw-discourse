We are not talking past each other on the diagnosis. We all agree the company has a structural competitiveness problem, and we all agree that cosmetic fixes are not enough. Where we part ways is on **who carries the burden** and **whether management is using “necessity” as a license to break the social contract**.

### To management
I hear your point that the cost base must come down in a durable way. That is legitimate. What is **not** legitimate is presenting **plant closures and compulsory redundancies as the default answer** before exhausting negotiated alternatives.

If you want a durable settlement, then bring a package that is **quantified and balanced**, not a threat dressed up as economic logic. Right now, your position still reads as: workers should absorb the main adjustment, while management retains maximum discretion. That will not fly.

We are prepared to discuss:
- temporary or multi-year working-time reductions,
- internal capacity consolidation,
- voluntary exit programs,
- redeployment and qualification,
- productivity agreements,
- and a negotiated labour contribution.

But we are **not** prepared to accept:
- German plant closures,
- compulsory redundancies,
- or a dismantling of the collective wage and job-security framework.

### To the works council
We are aligned on the core red lines: no closures, no forced layoffs, no break with job security. I support that. But we also need to be strategically honest: if we want to keep sites and jobs, then the workforce contribution must be **real and measurable**, not symbolic.

That means we should be ready to consider, for a limited and negotiated period:
- foregone bonuses or special payments,
- moderated wage increases,
- working-time flexibility,
- internal transfer rules,
- and productivity improvements,

**provided** these are tied to binding commitments from management and owners on:
- site security,
- employment security,
- investment,
- and future product allocation.

### To the state shareholder
Your rejection of closures and forced redundancies is fully understandable, and in my view justified. But political support will not be enough if the package doesn’t close the economic gap. The company needs a solution that is not only socially defensible, but also financially credible. That means you should back a deal that combines:
- investment commitments,
- industrial future products,
- and a serious employee contribution,
with **no plant closures and no compulsory redundancies**.

### To the controlling family and investors
You keep asking for “quantitative equivalence.” Fair enough. Then let’s be equally precise about what does **not** count as equivalence: destroying trust, provoking conflict, and creating long-term industrial instability.

A closure may look like a clean line item in a spreadsheet, but it also creates:
- strike risk,
- political resistance,
- loss of skills,
- damage to the supplier network,
- and a poisoned labor climate across the group.

So if you insist on judging only the cost line, you are missing the full cost.

What would count as a serious alternative?
- a real capacity reduction,
- lasting labour-cost relief,
- working-time and shift restructuring,
- voluntary workforce reduction,
- and binding productivity measures,
all without plant closures or compulsory redundancies.

That is the standard we should test against.

### Our position
We are not moving on the fundamentals:

- **No German plant closures.**
- **No compulsory redundancies.**
- **No erosion of the collective framework.**

But we are prepared to contribute to a deal that shares the burden fairly, if and only if management and owners also contribute materially and the company commits to a durable industrial future in Germany.

If management wants a strike-free path, then it must stop treating closures and forced layoffs as bargaining chips and start negotiating a genuine restructuring agreement. If it refuses, then it should not be surprised by escalation.
