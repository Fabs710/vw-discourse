We are moving from rhetoric to numbers, and I welcome that. But let me be equally clear: **numbers do not change our red lines**. If a “quantified” plan still relies on German plant closures or compulsory redundancies, then it is not a solution we can support. It is just a more polished way of attacking jobs and trust.

### Where I agree
I agree with the assessment that the company faces a structural challenge:
- costs are too high,
- the market is weaker,
- and the business needs a durable industrial plan.

I also agree that **cosmetic measures are not enough**. If the company wants to remain strong through 2030, there must be real restructuring, real investment, and real burden-sharing.

### Where I disagree
I do **not** accept the idea that the only “quantitatively credible” answer is closure and forced job cuts.

That is too narrow, and frankly too convenient for management and capital. You are treating workers as the easiest adjustable variable while leaving ownership structures, strategic misjudgments, and investment decisions largely untouched.

### What a real alternative must contain
If management wants a serious alternative to closures, then it must put forward a package built on all of the following:

1. **No plant closures in Germany**
2. **No compulsory redundancies**
3. **Binding site and job guarantees through 2030**
4. **Capacity reduction through internal restructuring, not abandonment**
5. **Working-time flexibility and shift model adjustments**
6. **Voluntary rather than forced workforce reduction**
7. **Redeployment, qualification, and internal transfers**
8. **A fair labor contribution, but only as part of a balanced package**
9. **Clear investment commitments for future products and technologies**
10. **A genuine co-determination role in implementation**

That is the only basis on which we can even discuss burden-sharing.

### On “durable savings”
To the investors and family owners: if you want durable savings, then stop pretending that only closures create durability. Durability also comes from:
- stable labor relations,
- secure sites with committed workforces,
- faster implementation through co-determination,
- and avoiding industrial conflict that destroys output and reputation.

A plant closure may cut one cost line, but it also creates:
- legal risk,
- political backlash,
- supplier damage,
- strikes,
- loss of know-how,
- and long-term distrust across the group.

That is not free. It is a very expensive way to “save” money.

### On the state shareholder
I appreciate the state shareholder’s position that no one wants to dismantle the industrial base. But support is not enough. If the state is serious, it should back a package that protects:
- employment,
- apprenticeships and skills,
- regional supply chains,
- and long-term production in Germany.

A solution that merely shifts the pain around while still forcing closures later is not acceptable.

### On management
Management keeps saying it needs “binding flexibility.” Fine. We say: then show us a binding industrial plan that does **not** begin with plant closures. If you want flexibility from the workforce, then the company must commit in return:
- no closures,
- no forced layoffs,
- no dismantling of job security,
- and no unilateral downgrade of co-determination.

You cannot demand trust after breaking trust.

### On what we can discuss
We are prepared to discuss:
- temporary working-time reductions,
- internal capacity adjustments,
- voluntary programs,
- redeployment across German sites,
- productivity improvements negotiated with the workforce,
- and a fair employee contribution **if** the company also contributes and the sites remain open.

We are **not** prepared to discuss:
- closing German plants,
- compulsory redundancies,
- or empty promises of “future investment” used to justify immediate destruction.

### Bottom line
If the package is built around preserving all German sites and jobs through 2030, then we can talk about how the burden is shared.

If it is built around closures, forced job losses, and the erosion of the historic protections that hold this company together, then there is nothing to negotiate from our side except resistance.

So I say again plainly:
**No closures. No compulsory redundancies. No break with job security.**
That is not a bargaining slogan. It is the boundary of what is acceptable.
