I will respond directly and plainly.

### To management
I agree with you on one point: this cannot be a cosmetic exercise. The company does need a credible restructuring and a lower cost base.

But I do **not** accept your framing that the only serious path is one that keeps plant closures and compulsory redundancies on the table as default instruments. Given the governance structure here, and given the company’s role in the region, that is not a neutral technical choice — it is a decision with major social and political consequences. You are underestimating those consequences.

What you are still not showing is a **quantified alternative pathway** that has been tested for:
- savings,
- durability,
- implementation risk,
- and regional impact.

If you want the state shareholder to support a difficult package, then bring something concrete:
- how much cost reduction,
- over what timeline,
- from which instruments,
- and how German sites remain viable.

I am open to hard restructuring. I am **not** open to treating German plants as disposable.

### To the works council and union
I agree with your red lines on two points:
- no compulsory redundancies,
- and no settlement that simply destroys trust.

I also agree that closures are not “free” and that the political, industrial, and social costs are real. That said, I would urge you not to make the discussion collapse into slogans. “No closures” can be a legitimate line only if you are prepared to show a real alternative that closes the economic gap.

So I need more than a principled refusal. I need a package that is credible to the capital side and durable over time:
- capacity reduction without abandonment,
- working-time flexibility,
- voluntary exit programs,
- redeployment and qualification,
- productivity improvements,
- and a fair employee contribution.

But I am very clear on this: any contribution from labour must be matched by commitments from management and owners. It cannot be one-sided sacrifice.

### To the controlling family and institutional investors
I understand the demand for durable savings and a lower fixed-cost base. That is legitimate. But I reject the idea that “durable” automatically means plant closures.

Durability can also come from:
- stable labor relations,
- an agreement that workers actually accept and implement,
- protection of critical skills,
- and avoiding years of industrial conflict, legal disputes, and regional damage.

You are right that cosmetic compromise would be a mistake. You are **not** right if you assume that closures are the only credible way to achieve structural change.

### What would move me
What could move the state shareholder is a package that:
1. **preserves German sites wherever possible,**
2. **avoids compulsory redundancies,**
3. **includes real and measurable labor contributions,**
4. **includes meaningful management/owner contributions,**
5. **binds future investment to the restructuring,**
6. **and keeps co-determination fully intact.**

That is the standard I would test against.

### My position
So let me be explicit:

- **No blanket acceptance of plant closures in Germany.**
- **No compulsory redundancies.**
- **No weakening of the state’s co-determination and veto role.**
- **Yes** to a serious restructuring if it protects employment, sites, and the regional industrial base.
- **Yes** to negotiated burden-sharing, but only if it is balanced and durable.

If management is serious, it should come back with a **fully quantified package of alternatives to closure** and show why that package is sufficient. If it cannot do that, then it has not yet made the case for the measures it wants.
