I’ll respond directly.

## Where I agree
I agree with the expert, the family holding, and the investors on the central point: **this must be a durable, quantified restructuring**. A cosmetic deal that merely postpones the problem would be unacceptable.

I also agree with the state shareholder and the employee side on one important practical point: **industrial peace and implementation feasibility matter**. A prolonged strike would damage output, reputation, and our ability to invest.

So yes:
- the solution must be durable,
- it must be enforceable,
- and it must actually work in practice.

## Where I disagree
I fundamentally disagree with the works council and union on the idea that we can start from:
- **no closures**,  
- **no compulsory redundancies**,  
- **no weakening of job guarantees**,  
and still expect the company to become competitive just because everyone wants it to.

That is not a restructuring plan. It is a boundary statement.

I also disagree with the state shareholder if “no closures” becomes a political principle rather than a conditional preference. If the economics require deeper changes, then governance cannot simply declare them impossible.

## The core issue
The company has a **structural cost and capacity problem**. That is not a slogan. It means:

- our German footprint is too expensive,
- our capacity is too high relative to demand,
- and our margin structure is not good enough to fund the transformation to 2030.

If we do not materially reduce fixed costs and overcapacity, then we will keep bleeding competitiveness.

## To the works council
You keep saying closure-free restructuring is possible in principle. I accept that.  
But “in principle” is not the same as **sufficient**.

You have not yet shown a package that produces:
- enough annual run-rate savings,
- enough permanent capacity reduction,
- enough labour-cost relief,
- and enough durability to restore returns.

You list tools:
- working-time changes,
- voluntary exits,
- redeployment,
- productivity gains,
- temporary restraint.

Those are all valid instruments. But by themselves they do **not automatically equal** the effect of shutting down redundant capacity. If they do not add up to real fixed-cost reduction, then they are just delay.

So my position remains:

- I am open to avoiding closures **if the alternative is economically equivalent**.
- I am not open to a deal that preserves the existing structure and calls that competitiveness.

## To the union
I appreciate that you are now speaking in the language of burden-sharing rather than slogans. That is progress.

But I must be equally clear: a meaningful employee contribution cannot just mean symbolic concessions or short-term restraint. If the workforce wants closures and compulsory redundancies off the table, then the contribution must be **material, measurable, and durable**.

That means real numbers on:
- wage moderation,
- special payments,
- shift and working-time reform,
- productivity,
- voluntary departures,
- staffing discipline.

If the labour contribution is too small, then the alternative is not equivalent. It is simply less painful for labour and more painful for the enterprise.

## To the state shareholder
I understand your regional responsibility. I do not dismiss it.

But “industrial continuity” cannot mean preserving every structure regardless of economics. That would be irresponsible. If you want closures off the table, then the alternative must still close the gap in hard numbers.

That means:
- less fixed cost,
- less overcapacity,
- better utilization,
- and a credible path to margin recovery.

## To the controlling family and investors
I agree with you that cosmetic compromise would destroy value.

Where I push back is the implication that only a closure-based benchmark proves seriousness. That is too narrow. The real benchmark is whether the package delivers:
- lower fixed costs,
- lower capacity,
- better margins,
- and funds for future investment.

I am open to hybrid solutions. In fact, that is likely where the feasible path lies. But it has to be a **real** hybrid:
- capacity consolidation,
- shift reductions,
- voluntary workforce reduction,
- labour-cost relief,
- and site-specific restructuring where necessary.

If the alternative package does not achieve equivalent effect, then we should not pretend it does.

## My position on the options
Let me be explicit.

### I am prepared to discuss
- **a hybrid restructuring**
- targeted capacity consolidation
- shift and working-time reform
- voluntary exits and redeployment
- productivity improvements
- negotiated labour-cost contribution
- binding investment commitments tied to the agreement

### I am not prepared to accept
- a blanket no-closure rule
- a blanket no-redundancies rule
- a settlement that leaves the company structurally uncompetitive
- a package that protects the current cost base and postpones the adjustment

## What I need next
We are past general debate. If the employee side wants to avoid closures, then bring a package with:

1. **annual run-rate savings in euros**
2. **permanent capacity reduction**
3. **headcount impact**
4. **labour-cost contribution**
5. **implementation timeline**
6. **durability through 2030**
7. **binding investment commitments**

If the package is strong enough, I will support it. If not, then closures remain a legitimate and necessary option.

## My bottom line
I will not support a cosmetic compromise.

I will support a deal that:
- materially reduces overcapacity,
- lowers labour and fixed costs,
- restores competitiveness,
- and frees funds for future products and technology to 2030.

That is the test. Not whether the package sounds acceptable in the room. Whether it actually solves the problem.
