### 1) What has been agreed
- The company has a **structural cost/capacity problem** and needs a **durable, non-cosmetic restructuring**.
- Any viable solution must be **quantified**, **enforceable**, and durable through the relevant planning horizon.
- **Industrial peace matters**, as do implementation feasibility and co-determination constraints.
- There is broad acceptance that a **purely symbolic package is insufficient**.
- The expert assessment broadly supports that **closure-free or hybrid options are possible in principle**, but only if they are deep enough.

### 2) Where the core disagreements remain
- **Closures and compulsory redundancies**
  - Employee-side representatives and the state shareholder reject them as default or acceptable instruments.
  - Management, controlling family, and investors insist they must remain available if needed to achieve sufficient savings.
- **Extent of labor burden-sharing**
  - The employee side accepts contributions in principle, but only with strong job/site protections.
  - Capital-side actors argue that the labor contribution so far is not yet shown to be large enough to close the gap.
- **What counts as “equivalent” restructuring**
  - One side argues that closure-free restructuring can be sufficient if designed deeply enough.
  - The other side argues that such a package may be politically easier but economically weaker unless proven otherwise.
- **Role of the state shareholder**
  - The state insists on protecting regional employment and co-determination.
  - Others question whether that can be reconciled with full competitiveness if capacity is not reduced enough.

### 3) What key trade-offs are still unaddressed
- **Savings vs. protection:** how much permanent cost relief is required, and how much employment/site protection can still be maintained.
- **Speed vs. durability:** closures may deliver faster fixed-cost reduction; hybrid/closure-free paths may be more governable but risk underdelivery.
- **One-off pain vs. long-term risk:** whether accepting sharper near-term disruption is preferable to a lower-disruption package that may not close the structural gap.
- **Governance feasibility vs. economic optimality:** what is economically strongest may not be politically or legally implementable.
- **Quantification gap:** the discussion still lacks a fully comparable side-by-side package showing savings, costs, capacity reduction, and job impact under each scenario.

### 4) Brief refocus for the next round
- Move from principles to **numbers**.
- Ask the independent expert to present a **side-by-side comparison** of closure-based, hybrid, and closure-free options:
  - annual run-rate savings,
  - one-off restructuring costs,
  - capacity reduction,
  - headcount impact,
  - implementation timeline,
  - durability,
  - governance feasibility.
- Require each side to state:
  - its **minimum non-negotiables**,
  - the **maximum burden-sharing it can accept**,
  - and the **specific conditions** under which it would support a deal.
- Keep the next round focused on whether a **closure-free or hybrid package can actually match the economic target**, rather than revisiting general positions.
