I will be direct.

I agree with everyone on one point: **this company needs a real restructuring, not a cosmetic one**. The cost problem is structural, and a durable answer has to improve competitiveness through 2030 and beyond. On that, the expert input is useful and management is right to insist that vague promises are not enough.

But I do **not** accept the idea that the only serious restructuring path is one that leaves **plant closures and compulsory redundancies** as the default instruments. For this state shareholder, that is not a technical detail — it is a line we cannot cross. We have a legitimate responsibility for regional employment, industrial stability, and the company’s role as an economic anchor. Those are not bargaining chips.

### Where I agree
- **Management:** Yes, the company must reduce costs and capacity in a durable way.
- **Investors and family holding:** Yes, the solution must be quantified and economically credible.
- **Works council and union:** Yes, job losses and closures have real social and industrial costs, and trust matters.
- **Expert:** Yes, a closure-free or hybrid solution is possible in principle, but only if it is deep enough.

### Where I disagree
I disagree with management, investors, and the family holding when you imply that closures must remain the benchmark of seriousness. That is too narrow.

I also disagree with the works council if “no closures” is treated as sufficient by itself. It is not enough to say no; there must be a **credible alternative** that closes the gap in hard numbers.

### My position
From the state shareholder’s perspective, the acceptable path is:

- **no German plant closures as the starting point or default**
- **no compulsory redundancies**
- **no weakening of co-determination or our veto role**
- **real capacity reduction through internal restructuring**
- **meaningful working-time and shift flexibility**
- **voluntary workforce reduction and redeployment**
- **binding investment and product commitments**
- **a fair labour contribution matched by management and owner contribution**

That is the only kind of package I can support.

### What would move me
What would move me is not rhetoric about “discipline” or “necessity,” but a **side-by-side quantified package** showing:

1. annual run-rate savings,
2. one-off restructuring costs,
3. headcount impact,
4. capacity reduction,
5. implementation timeline,
6. durability over the cycle,
7. and governance feasibility under co-determination.

If a closure-free or hybrid package truly matches the economics, I will support it. If it does not, then management has not made the case for the measures it wants.

### On the current debate
At this point, the discussion is stuck between two incomplete positions:
- management wants maximum flexibility, including closures;
- the employee side wants maximum protection, with too little quantified alternative so far.

Neither is enough.

What I am prepared to do is back a **hybrid restructuring test** if it is serious and enforceable. But I will not back a settlement that quietly sets the company on a path to plant closures later by underinvesting in the alternative now.

### My explicit boundary
I want to say this clearly:  
**I will not support any outcome that guts regional employment or removes the state’s co-determination and veto role.**

That is not a negotiation tactic. It is the basis on which the state shareholder participates in this company.
