From a restructuring-finance and industrial-capacity perspective, the positions in round 4 have now crystallized into a classic **governance-constrained overcapacity case**.

## What is clear
- Management believes the German footprint has **structural excess capacity** and a **cost base above competitive levels**.
- The employee side and state shareholder want a restructuring **without closures or compulsory redundancies**.
- Owners and investors insist that **closures and forced redundancies must remain available** if the numbers require them.
- Everyone accepts that **soft measures, burden-sharing, and an audit/model** are necessary.

## The real issue
The dispute is no longer mainly about whether restructuring is needed. It is about whether the company can achieve:
1. **durable recurring savings**, and
2. **capacity alignment**
**without** hard footprint reduction.

That is a finance-and-operations question, not a slogan question.

## Mechanically, what determines the answer
The joint auditor/model needs to quantify, by plant and by measure:

- current utilization
- variable and fixed cost per unit
- recurring savings from:
  - reduced shifts / working-time changes
  - redeployment
  - voluntary exits
  - retirement pathways
  - wage concessions / temporary labour-cost measures
  - internal mobility
  - procurement / overhead reductions
- one-off restructuring costs
- stranded fixed costs if a site stays open below economic scale
- impact on EBIT margin / return on sales through 2030

## Key restructuring logic
### 1) Soft measures can reduce costs, but they do not automatically fix overcapacity
Working-time changes, voluntary exits, and temporary labour concessions can help meaningfully. But if the problem is a **persistent mismatch between installed capacity and expected demand**, soft measures often only:
- buy time,
- reduce payroll cost at the margin,
- and improve utilization somewhat.

They do **not** always eliminate:
- duplicate fixed overhead,
- underfilled plant economics,
- logistics inefficiencies,
- or the need to close a line or site.

### 2) A plant can be “open” and still be uneconomic
If a plant remains structurally underutilized, the company may retain:
- maintenance cost,
- energy and utilities,
- site overhead,
- indirect labour,
- logistics complexity,
- and subscale depreciation burden.

In automotive, fixed-cost absorption is critical. A plant with low utilization can destroy margin even if it is not “losing cash” in a narrow sense.

### 3) Guaranteeing no closures can create a hidden financing problem
A blanket no-closure commitment is not just an industrial-relations issue. It can also:
- reduce the credibility of the restructuring,
- prolong margin pressure,
- weaken cash generation,
- and limit the funding capacity for product and EV investment.

That matters because this type of company is usually balancing:
- restructuring cash outflows,
- continuing capex,
- and earnings pressure.

If savings are delayed or diluted, the burden is shifted into future years.

## Tradeoff assessment
### If the non-closure path works
Then the best outcome is usually:
- negotiated workforce contribution,
- voluntary exits,
- redeployment,
- capacity rebalancing,
- and a tighter site network without formal closures.

This is preferable because it reduces conflict cost and preserves skills.

### If it does not work
Then keeping all sites open can mean:
- lower long-term margins,
- continuing subscale plants,
- recurring labour tension,
- and less investment headroom.

In that case, refusal to consider closures is not “protecting value”; it is **locking in cost overhang**.

## Practical constraint
Because of co-determination and blocking positions, the issue is not just what is economically optimal. It is what is **governable**.

That means:
- hard measures may be economically rational but procedurally blocked,
- and soft measures may be politically acceptable but financially insufficient.

This is why the audit is central: it is the only way to test whether there is a credible middle path.

## What I would look for in the audit
For a credible non-closure scenario, I would want to see:
- the exact number of jobs removed through voluntary means,
- how much cost comes out permanently versus temporarily,
- whether remaining plants can run at economic scale,
- whether savings exceed the annual drag from subscale capacity,
- and whether margin improvement is enough to finance future investment.

If the audit shows a small residual gap, a negotiated non-closure package may be feasible.  
If it shows a large structural gap, then the company likely needs at least one hard capacity action to restore economics.

## Bottom line
From my remit:
- **soft measures are real and important**
- **but they are not a guaranteed substitute for hard capacity reduction**
- **the decisive question is whether the recurring savings and utilization gains are enough to restore plant economics and margin by 2030**
