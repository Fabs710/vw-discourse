I appreciate the clarity from the works council and the union. Let me respond just as clearly.

## Where I agree

I agree that:
- this cannot be a cosmetic exercise,
- the company needs a durable industrial plan through 2030,
- workforce contributions should be part of any settlement,
- and a short-lived compromise that merely postpones the problem would be a failure.

I also agree that trust matters. A restructuring that is perceived as arbitrary or purely punitive will be harder to implement and more disruptive than necessary.

## Where I disagree

### 1) “No closures” as an absolute line is not compatible with the economics
I do not accept the claim that plant closures are off the table in principle.

If the company has structural overcapacity and a cost base materially above target, then every option has to be judged against one question: **does it actually restore competitiveness on a durable basis?** If a site cannot be made viable through alternatives, then refusing to consider closure simply protects inefficiency.

That is not a threat for its own sake. It is the consequence of the market reality we are facing.

### 2) “No compulsory redundancies” cannot be an unconditional guarantee
I want to minimise forced redundancies. I do not want them as a first resort. But I cannot accept a framework that rules them out categorically if they are required to achieve a sustainable structure.

If we bind ourselves to that line before we have even closed the gap, we are choosing preserve-the-status-quo over competitiveness. That is not responsible management.

### 3) Binding guarantees through 2030 cannot freeze the company
I understand why you want site guarantees. But unconditional guarantees for every German plant through 2030 would remove exactly the flexibility needed to adapt to a shrinking and changing market.

A guarantee can only be credible if it is part of a package that actually delivers the cost and capacity correction. Otherwise it becomes a promise to preserve an uncompetitive structure.

## On your demand for “proof”
The works council says management must prove that no combination of alternatives can solve the problem. Fair enough: I am prepared to put the numbers on the table.

But I want to be equally clear about what those numbers must be used for:
- to test alternatives seriously,
- not to rule out hard decisions in advance.

If redeployment, qualification, working-time changes, voluntary exits, and capacity adjustments can close the gap fully and durably, I am open to that.
If they cannot, then we have to face the fact that deeper structural measures are unavoidable.

## On burden-sharing
I agree with both of you that burden-sharing cannot fall only on labour.

Management, owners, and the broader corporate structure must all contribute. We should not pretend otherwise.

But burden-sharing is not a substitute for a structurally sound cost base. Even if management and owners contribute, the company still needs a workforce and plant structure that matches demand and competitiveness. That is the core issue.

## What I can support

I can support a package built around:
- capacity reductions,
- working-time measures,
- voluntary exit and retirement instruments,
- redeployment and qualification,
- labour-cost contributions,
- and, where necessary, plant rationalisation.

What I cannot support is a package that:
- forbids closures in advance,
- forbids compulsory redundancies in advance,
- and still expects management to sign off on a structure that does not materially improve margins.

That would not be a settlement. It would be a delay mechanism.

## To the union’s point on coercion
You say closure threats create pressure. Yes — but the company is under pressure too. The market is shrinking, competition is intense, and our current cost base is not acceptable. Negotiating under economic reality is not the same as negotiating in bad faith.

If the only acceptable outcome is one where nothing structural changes, then there is no basis for agreement.

## Where this leaves us
I am prepared to continue on two conditions:

1. We move from slogans to a **quantified restructuring case**
   - excess capacity,
   - savings by measure,
   - timeline,
   - effect on margins and return on sales.

2. We discuss **all instruments**, including hard ones
   - not as an ideological preference,
   - but as part of a credible path to durable competitiveness.

If the alternative package genuinely closes the gap, then it deserves serious consideration.
If it does not, then I will not endorse a cosmetic deal that leaves the company structurally uncompetitive.

So my message is simple:
We are open to a negotiated solution.  
We are not open to a negotiated freeze.
