From the perspective of dispersed capital-market shareholders, the decisive issue is not whether the package is “tough” or “socially balanced” in the abstract. It is whether it credibly restores **risk-adjusted value** through a **durable** improvement in margins, costs, and capital allocation.

So our position is straightforward:

## Where we agree

We agree with management, the family owners, and the state on one core point:  
**the current cost base and capacity structure are not acceptable** if the company is to remain competitive.

We also agree that:
- a cosmetic settlement would be value-destructive,
- repeated short-term patches would simply delay the same problem,
- the company needs structural savings, not optics,
- and some workforce contribution is likely unavoidable if the business is to regain pricing and margin discipline.

On that basis, we are **not** aligned with any outcome that merely preserves the status quo while calling it “partnership.”

## Where we disagree

### 1) We do not accept “no closures” as an absolute line
The works council and union are entitled to defend jobs, but from an investor standpoint an absolute prohibition on closures is not acceptable if it leaves the company structurally uncompetitive.

If management can prove that some plant rationalisation is necessary to restore margins and reduce overcapacity, then it should not be ruled out on principle. A company that keeps every site for political reasons while earnings keep eroding is not protecting value; it is destroying it.

That said, closures are not automatically the best solution. They are only justified if they are part of a **credible, quantified restructuring case** showing:
- lasting cost reduction,
- real capacity alignment,
- and a measurable return to target margins.

If that case is not made, closures become a blunt instrument rather than a value-creating one.

### 2) We do not support a settlement that avoids hard decisions but leaves costs structurally high
I hear the union’s call for alternatives: redeployment, working-time adjustments, voluntary exits, qualification, capacity measures. Those can absolutely be part of the solution.

But they are only acceptable if they deliver the same economic effect. If they merely buy time while preserving an uncompetitive labor and capacity structure, then they are not a solution. They are a delay mechanism.

From our perspective, the real test is simple:
**Do the measures close the gap on a durable basis, yes or no?**

If yes, we can support them.  
If no, then they are inadequate, regardless of how socially elegant they sound.

### 3) We are not persuaded by blanket job or site guarantees through 2030 if they undermine flexibility
Binding guarantees can be useful if they unlock a credible transformation plan. But unconditional guarantees that prevent restructuring are not value-neutral. They can become a promise to preserve inefficiency.

So if the price of “peace” is a guarantee that freezes the industrial footprint regardless of market conditions, we would view that as a negative for shareholders. It may reduce conflict in the short run, but it increases the risk of margin erosion and future underperformance.

### 4) We expect burden-sharing from all sides, but we will not treat fairness as a substitute for economics
The state and family owners are right that management and labor cannot be the only parties asked to move. But for shareholders, the primary question is not who feels the pain; it is whether the end result is financially credible.

If labor makes a contribution but the company still lacks a viable cost base, that is not enough.  
If management preserves all sites but still cannot return to target returns on sales, that is not enough.  
If the settlement is politically palatable but economically weak, we will not support it.

## What would move us

We could support an alternative to plant closures only if it contains all of the following:

1. **A quantified, durable cost reduction**
   Not promises, not aspirations — hard numbers tied to EBITDA, margins, and cost per unit.

2. **Meaningful capacity reduction**
   Whether through closures, line consolidation, or other measures, the structure must be brought in line with demand.

3. **A credible employee contribution**
   Temporary or permanent labor-cost savings are acceptable if they are real and enforceable.

4. **No open-ended delay**
   The package must be implemented on a clear timetable. Prolonged conflict and staged ambiguity destroy value.

5. **A believable path back to target margins**
   This is the key test. If the plan does not visibly improve the return-on-sales trajectory, we will not regard it as successful.

## Our bottom line

- We **support restructuring**.
- We **support durable cost and capacity cuts**.
- We **do not support cosmetic compromise**.
- We **do not support preserving an uncompetitive structure for the sake of industrial peace**.

So our message to all parties is:

Bring us a package that **actually restores competitiveness**, or do not expect capital markets to reward delay dressed up as consensus. If the company cannot get there without some closures or equivalent hard capacity cuts, then avoiding them for political reasons is not a shareholder-friendly outcome.

We will back whatever is **credible, durable, and margin-improving**.  
We will not back a settlement that leaves the cost base structurally impaired.
