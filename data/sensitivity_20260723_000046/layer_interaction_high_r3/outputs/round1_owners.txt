I will be equally direct.

I agree with the broad diagnosis from management, and I also agree with the state shareholder and the employee side on one essential point: a merely symbolic or short-lived fix would be a disaster. This company needs a durable, structural answer. If we protect the brand today only to lose competitiveness tomorrow, we destroy value for everyone — employees, state, and owners.

But I do **not** accept the implication in the works council’s and union’s opening lines that the baseline must be “no closures, no compulsory redundancies, no meaningful structural break.” If that were the starting point, we would be signing up to a slow decline. That would be irresponsible stewardship.

### To the works council and union
You are right that trust matters, and you are right that a one-sided burden on the workforce is not acceptable. But “no plant closures” and “no compulsory redundancies” as absolute red lines are not compatible with the scale of the competitiveness problem management describes.

A few points:

- **Job security guarantees through 2030** may be politically understandable, but as an unconditional promise they can easily become value-destructive if they block necessary restructuring.
- **Alternatives to closures** — redeployment, working-time adjustments, voluntary exits, qualifications — should absolutely be pursued first. But they must be judged by whether they actually close the cost and capacity gap.
- **Shared sacrifice** is necessary, yes — but it cannot mean that the company preserves every site, every legacy arrangement, and every guarantee while asking owners to absorb the economic consequences indefinitely.

So I reject any framing that makes workforce demands the starting presumption and management’s restructuring plan the thing that must justify its existence twice over. The business has to become competitive. That is not optional.

### To management
I support the direction of travel: there must be a credible reduction in cost and capacity. I also agree that labor costs must come down. But I want to be clear that value destruction can also come from a restructuring that is too abrupt, too public, or too inflexible.

If closures or forced redundancies become the first instrument rather than the last resort, you will trigger avoidable conflict, reputational damage, and possible governance deadlock. That would not strengthen competitiveness; it would weaken it.

So I expect management to do more than say “the gap is real.” I expect a **precise industrial case**:
- what capacity is excess,
- what savings come from each measure,
- what the alternatives deliver,
- and what the impact is on margins, product allocation, and investment.

If the numbers prove that some plant rationalisation is unavoidable, then say so plainly. But if you can achieve equivalent durable savings through a negotiated package with capacity reduction, labour-cost contribution, and voluntary workforce adjustment, then that is preferable — not out of sentiment, but because it may be the best way to preserve long-term value and labour peace.

### To the regional state government
I appreciate your insistence that German industrial capacity should not be treated casually. That is a legitimate concern. But I would caution against turning “no closures as a first resort” into a de facto veto on economically necessary decisions.

The company’s future cannot be built on political comfort alone. If the plant structure remains uncompetitive, the burden will eventually show up in underinvestment, weaker products, and lower shareholder value — which also harms the region in the long run.

So I can support a settlement that protects the industrial base **if** it is anchored in real competitiveness, not just in employment preservation.

### My position
The controlling owners’ priority is long-term enterprise value and governance stability. That means:

1. **We support a credible competitiveness programme.**
   Cost and capacity reductions must be real, durable, and sufficient.

2. **We do not support permanent impairment of the enterprise to preserve every existing structure.**
   That is unacceptable.

3. **We are open to negotiated alternatives to closures**
   — but only if they deliver measurable structural savings and restore margins.

4. **We want labour peace, but not at any price.**
   A settlement that merely postpones conflict or preserves the status quo under a nicer label is not a settlement we will back.

5. **We will not endorse any process that undermines governance control or turns necessary restructuring into a political hostage situation.**
   Co-determination and the state’s role matter, but they cannot be used to freeze the company in place.

So I am not here to bless a confrontation, but I am also not here to bless a no-change outcome dressed up as social responsibility.

If management brings a package that is credible on savings, and if labour responds with a serious contribution that preserves operational stability, then there is a basis for support. If not, I will not support a solution that protects every legacy claim while eroding the company’s competitiveness.
