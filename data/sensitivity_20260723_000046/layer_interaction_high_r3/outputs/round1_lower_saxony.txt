As the regional state shareholder, I need to be very clear: I understand the competitive pressure, and I am not denying that this company needs a serious restructuring. But I cannot support a path that treats German plants and jobs as the primary adjustment variable.

Where I agree with management:
- The cost and capacity problem is real.
- A purely cosmetic solution would only postpone the crisis.
- The company needs a durable industrial strategy through 2030 and beyond.
- Workforce contributions may be part of a settlement.

Where I disagree sharply:
- Plant closures in Germany must not be the starting point.
- Forced redundancies cannot be the default instrument.
- A restructuring that undermines regional employment and destroys trust would be economically and politically irresponsible.
- “Blanket” cuts that sideline co-determination and the state’s role are not acceptable.

To the works council and union: I share your core concern that this cannot become a one-sided burden on employees. I support a negotiated restructuring that protects sites, avoids compulsory redundancies, and uses alternatives like redeployment, qualification, working-time measures, voluntary exits, and capacity adjustments. That is the right direction.

But I also need to be honest: if the numbers only work by preserving every structure exactly as it is, then we will not solve the competitiveness problem. So I am not defending a status quo freeze. I am defending a restructuring that is socially responsible, reversible where possible, and anchored in a real industrial plan.

My position is therefore:

1. No closures as a first resort.  
   Management must present a credible alternative package before any closure is even considered.

2. No forced redundancies if avoidable.  
   Workforce reduction, if needed, should come through voluntary, negotiated, and socially managed instruments.

3. Binding site and investment commitments.  
   If employees are asked to contribute, the company must commit to production, product allocation, and investment at German sites.

4. Shared burden across all sides.  
   Management, owners, and the workforce all need to contribute. The burden cannot fall mainly on labour.

5. Co-determination remains essential.  
   The state’s role as anchor shareholder and the workforce’s rights are not obstacles to be bypassed; they are part of how durable decisions are made in this company.

So I am open to a settlement, but only if it protects the company’s role as a regional economic anchor and avoids permanent damage to employment and the industrial base in Germany. If the proposal goes too far toward closures and unilateral cuts, I will oppose it.
