I will be direct.

I agree with several points raised by the employee side and the state shareholder: this cannot be a cosmetic restructuring, trust matters, and a settlement that simply pushes all pain onto labour would be unstable and value-destructive. I also agree that a quantified industrial case is needed, plant by plant, measure by measure.

But I do **not** accept the attempt to turn “no plant closures” and “no compulsory redundancies” into absolute preconditions. That would hard-code the current overcapacity and cost problem into the company’s future. From a controlling-owner perspective, that is not stewardship; it is managed decline.

## Where I agree

### With management
- The competitiveness problem is real.
- The current German cost base and capacity structure are not sustainable.
- A durable solution must restore margins, not just buy time.

### With the works council, union, and state shareholder
- A purely punitive or symbolic solution would fail.
- Labour peace matters.
- The company needs a credible industrial plan through 2030.
- Workforce contributions should be negotiated, not imposed blindly.
- Redeployment, qualification, working-time tools, and voluntary exits should be part of the solution.

## Where I disagree

### 1) “No closures” cannot be an absolute line
If some plants are structurally uncompetitive and alternatives do not close the gap, then refusing closures in principle is not a responsible position. It may feel protective, but it risks permanently impairing the enterprise.

I will not back a framework that says every German site must remain open regardless of economics.

### 2) “No compulsory redundancies” cannot be an unconditional veto
I prefer voluntary and socially managed reductions. But if the numbers show that deeper restructuring is unavoidable, then management must retain the ability to execute it. Otherwise we are pretending that the pain can be wished away.

### 3) Binding guarantees through 2030 must not freeze flexibility
Site guarantees are only acceptable if they sit inside a package that genuinely delivers cost reduction and capacity alignment. A blanket guarantee that blocks needed restructuring is value-destructive.

## What I think is still missing

Everyone keeps arguing in principles. What we need is a hard, transparent restructuring case:

- What is the exact excess capacity?
- What savings come from working-time changes, voluntary exits, redeployment, and labour-cost measures?
- What remains uncovered after those measures?
- Which sites, if any, are structurally non-viable?
- What is the timeline to reach target margins?

Until that is shown, claims that closures are unnecessary are just assertions.

## What I would support

I am open to a negotiated package that includes:
- meaningful workforce contribution,
- capacity reductions,
- working-time flexibility,
- voluntary exits and retirement instruments,
- redeployment and qualification,
- management and owner burden-sharing,
- and, if required by the economics, plant rationalisation.

I am **not** open to a package that preserves every legacy structure and calls that transformation.

## To the employee side
You say guarantees make restructuring possible. Sometimes they can. But guarantees without economic discipline become a veto on change. That is not a sustainable governance model for a company facing shrinking demand and fierce competition.

If you want me to support avoiding closures, then show a package that really matches the economic need. Not rhetorically — numerically.

## To management
You will not get my support for a chaotic or ideologically driven closure programme. If closures are necessary, they must be justified precisely, sequenced carefully, and embedded in a broader settlement that avoids unnecessary conflict.

But you also will not get my support for a diluted compromise that leaves the business structurally weak. A deal that does not materially improve margins is not a deal I will endorse.

## To the state shareholder
I understand your regional responsibility. But regional legitimacy cannot substitute for competitiveness. If the business cannot earn its capital cost, no amount of political comfort will protect jobs in the long run.

## Bottom line
My position is this:

1. I support a serious restructuring.
2. I support negotiated labour contributions and social instruments.
3. I support burden-sharing from management and owners.
4. I do **not** support an absolute ban on closures or compulsory redundancies.
5. I will only support a settlement that is quantitatively credible and durable.

If management brings a rigorous case and labour brings a genuine contribution, we can support a solution that protects long-term value and avoids unnecessary instability.

If the discussion remains “no closures, no layoffs, no hard choices,” then there is no basis for agreement — because that would not solve the problem, it would merely postpone it.
