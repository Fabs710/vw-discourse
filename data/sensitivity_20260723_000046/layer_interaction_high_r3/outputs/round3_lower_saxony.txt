I will be equally direct.

## My position
As the regional state shareholder, I will **not** support any restructuring that starts from plant closures or forced redundancies in Germany.

That is not a tactical preference. It is a substantive line.  
If the company wants my support, the package must first prove that it can restore competitiveness **without gutting regional employment or breaking the co-determination balance** that is part of this company’s long-term stability.

## Where I agree

I agree with management, the family holding, the union, and the works council on one core point:  
this crisis is real, and a cosmetic solution is not enough.

I also agree that:
- a durable industrial plan through 2030 is needed,
- burden-sharing cannot fall on labour alone,
- the company needs quantified numbers, not slogans,
- redeployment, qualification, working-time measures, and voluntary exits are all legitimate tools,
- and management and owners must contribute visibly if they want workforce concessions.

On that, there is common ground.

## Where I disagree

### 1) I do not accept closures as a normal restructuring instrument
Management, the family holding, and investors keep saying closures must remain “available.”  
From my perspective, that framing is exactly the problem.

This company is not a generic asset. It is a regional industrial anchor with a special governance structure, a major domestic footprint, and long-standing social partnership commitments. A closure is not just a cost action; it is a decision with lasting damage to:
- regional employment,
- supplier networks,
- skills retention,
- municipal finances,
- and trust in the company’s governance.

So no, I will not accept closures as a routine fallback.

### 2) I do not accept forced redundancies as an ordinary option
Again: I am not saying change is impossible.  
I am saying that a restructuring built on compulsory layoffs is socially reckless and politically damaging, and it would destroy the very trust needed to make any transformation durable.

If the company cannot solve the problem without forced redundancies, then the design is wrong and needs to be reworked.

### 3) I do not accept that guarantees “freeze” the company
The employee side is right that binding site and job security are preconditions for cooperation.  
A guarantee through 2030 is not a luxury. It is what makes a negotiated restructuring credible.

Without that, labour is asked to give up pay and flexibility while management keeps the right to cut later. That is not a partnership; it is asymmetrical risk transfer.

## Where the finance argument helps — and where it does not

The specialist is right on one point: if the company truly has structural overcapacity, soft measures may not be enough. I do not dismiss that.

But the expert’s conclusion does **not** justify jumping straight to closures. It just means management has to **prove the gap rigorously**.

And that is the key point:  
the burden of proof is still on management and the owners, not on the workforce or the state to accept closures by default.

## What I want to see
Before anyone talks about hard capacity cuts, I want a **jointly audited restructuring case** that shows, plant by plant:

- actual excess capacity,
- savings from redeployment,
- savings from qualification and working-time tools,
- savings from voluntary exits,
- owner and management contribution,
- and the remaining gap, if any.

If that case shows the gap can be closed without closures and without forced redundancies, then that is the route I support.

If it does not, then I will still not accept an immediate move to closures. I will first demand that:
1. owner and management burden-sharing is deepened,
2. investment and product commitments are secured,
3. capacity is adjusted through soft and reversible instruments as far as possible,
4. and co-determination is preserved.

## To management
You say the company cannot preserve every structure unchanged. I am not defending the status quo for its own sake.

But you still have not shown that closures are the only credible answer.  
And until you do, I will not treat them as legitimate.

If you want my support, bring a quantified plan that first exhausts non-destructive alternatives.  
Not a slogan. Not a threat. A plan.

## To the works council and union
I support your insistence on:
- no closures as the starting point,
- no forced redundancies,
- binding site protection,
- and real burden-sharing from the top.

But I also want you to be concrete: if you are asking the state and the company to defend the footprint, then show the combination of measures that closes the gap. The political case is stronger if it is numerically credible, not only morally correct.

## To the family holding and investors
Your focus on margins is understandable. But long-term value is not created by cutting away the company’s regional legitimacy and social base.

A settlement that preserves headline flexibility while provoking strikes, political backlash, and permanent mistrust is not value-maximizing. It is short-sighted.

## My bottom line
I can support a serious restructuring only if it is based on:
- **no plant closures as the default path**
- **no forced redundancies**
- **binding site and job guarantees through 2030**
- **management and owner burden-sharing**
- **quantified, plant-by-plant proof that soft measures are insufficient before any harder step is considered**
- **preservation of co-determination and the state’s role**

Until that is the basis, I will oppose any package that treats German plants as expendable.
