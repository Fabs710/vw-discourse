## My position remains unchanged
As the controlling family holding, I will not back a settlement that hard-codes the current cost structure and leaves the company structurally uncompetitive.

That means I **do not accept**:
- an absolute ban on plant closures,
- an unconditional ban on compulsory redundancies,
- or guarantees that freeze restructuring flexibility through 2030.

Those are not stewardship-friendly constraints if the audit shows a real residual gap. They would protect the present footprint at the expense of long-term enterprise value.

## Where I agree
I agree with management, the expert, and the investor side on the central financial point: this company needs a **durable** solution, not a symbolic one.

I also agree with the works council, union, and state shareholder that:
- the problem has to be tested **plant by plant**,
- soft measures should be used first,
- burden-sharing cannot fall on labour alone,
- and management and owners must contribute visibly.

That is all sensible.

## Where I disagree with the employee side and the state shareholder
### 1) “No closures” is not a credible governance line
I understand the political and social appeal. But from an ownership perspective, a blanket refusal to consider closures is simply not responsible if a site is structurally non-viable.

If the audit shows persistent excess capacity, duplicated fixed costs, or a site that cannot be made competitive without endless subsidy, then refusing closure in advance means asking all shareholders to absorb avoidable value destruction.

That is not stewardship. That is denial.

### 2) “No compulsory redundancies” cannot be an absolute veto
I strongly prefer voluntary exits, redeployment, and retirement pathways. Those should absolutely be the first instruments.

But I will not endorse a framework that eliminates compulsory reductions as a fallback before we know the size of the residual gap. If the gap remains material after soft measures, management must retain the ability to act.

### 3) Guarantees are only valuable if they are conditional on competitiveness
A guarantee through 2030 sounds stabilising, but if it blocks necessary footprint or workforce adjustment, it simply postpones the problem and weakens the company’s future.

A guarantee is only acceptable if it sits inside a package that genuinely restores margins.

## On the works council and union
You are right to insist on numbers, and right to insist that labour should not carry the burden alone.

But I do not accept the repeated implication that closures and redundancies are somehow illegitimate just because they are painful. In an overcapacity case, painful measures may be the only value-preserving ones.

If you want to exclude them, then bring a quantified package that closes the gap durably. Not morally, not rhetorically — quantitatively.

## On the regional state shareholder
I respect the regional responsibility, but regional legitimacy cannot override economics forever.

If the company cannot earn its capital cost, then preserving every site in Germany becomes a way of spreading the cost of inaction across workers, suppliers, taxpayers, and shareholders. That is not a sustainable industrial policy.

I am open to state-backed support for a serious transition, but not to a framework that immunises the company from structural adjustment.

## On management
Management is right that we cannot solve this with slogans.  
But management should also be careful not to overstate its own case.

I support a **credible, independent joint audit** because it must test both possibilities:
- can soft measures close the gap, or
- does the residual gap require hard capacity action?

That audit has to be honest. If it shows the non-closure path works, fine — I can support that.  
If it shows it does not, then I will not support a deal that leaves restructuring blocked by red lines.

## What I support
I support:
- immediate soft measures,
- visible management and owner contribution,
- independent plant-by-plant audit,
- capacity rebalancing,
- redeployment and qualification,
- voluntary exits and retirement pathways,
- temporary labour-cost contributions,
- and investment commitments tied to a credible industrial plan.

I also support a **staged process**:
1. soft measures first,
2. audit and validation,
3. decision on any residual gap.

## What I do not support
I do **not** support:
- a preemptive closure taboo,
- an unconditional no-layoff veto,
- or a settlement that protects every site regardless of economics.

If the residual gap is real and material, then hard decisions must remain available.

## Bottom line
My position is straightforward:

- I support serious restructuring.
- I support soft measures first.
- I support burden-sharing from all sides.
- I do **not** support absolute bans on closures or compulsory redundancies.
- I will only back a settlement that restores competitiveness and protects long-term enterprise value.

If the non-closure path truly closes the gap, I can support it.  
If it does not, then I will not help lock in an uncompetitive structure for the sake of short-term comfort.
