We need to be very clear: the works council is right on the central principle — the workforce cannot be made to pay for this crisis alone. But I do **not** accept the current management framing that closures and forced redundancies remain normal instruments in a “serious” restructuring.

## Where I agree with the others

### With the works council
- No cosmetic reform.
- No one-sided burden on labour.
- Real site and job security must be part of any deal.
- Worker participation in shaping the transformation is essential.

### With the state shareholder
- This must be socially responsible and industrially credible.
- Redeployment, qualification, working-time measures, voluntary exits, and capacity adjustments are the right starting tools.
- A regional industrial base matters.

### With the fact pattern from management, owners, and investors
- There is a real cost and competitiveness problem.
- A short-term patch would be irresponsible.
- Some workforce contribution will likely be necessary.
- A durable solution must be quantified, not rhetorical.

## Where I disagree sharply

### 1) “Closures are not ruled out” is not an acceptable bargaining baseline
To be blunt: if plant closures stay on the table as a routine option, then the company is asking workers to negotiate under threat. That is not a fair bargaining position. It is coercive.

We have already signaled openness to labour-cost contributions and capacity reductions. That openness exists **because** we want a negotiated solution. It is not a blank cheque for management to keep closure threats alive while demanding concessions.

### 2) Forced redundancies remain unacceptable
Management and investors keep treating compulsory layoffs as a fallback. For us, that crosses the line. If the restructuring depends on forced redundancies, then the restructuring design is wrong. The company should exhaust all negotiated, socially managed instruments first.

### 3) “Shared sacrifice” cannot mean workers absorb the pain while owners stay protected
This is the core fairness issue. If the workforce is expected to give up pay, bonuses, or flexibility, then:
- management must contribute visibly,
- owners must accept a real burden,
- and there must be binding guarantees that the contribution serves jobs, sites, and future production — not just higher short-term earnings later.

### 4) A vague promise of future competitiveness is not enough
We keep hearing “durable savings,” “margin-improving,” and “value-creating.” Fine. Then show the numbers. If alternatives to closures are allegedly insufficient, that must be demonstrated in a transparent, plant-by-plant, measure-by-measure way — not asserted as a slogan.

## What we are prepared to discuss
We remain open to a serious package that includes:
- temporary labour-cost contributions,
- working-time measures,
- capacity reductions,
- voluntary exit and retirement instruments,
- redeployment and qualification,
- possibly a negotiated restraint on bonuses or certain allowances,
- and a jointly agreed industrial plan.

But this only makes sense if it is tied to:
1. **No compulsory redundancies**
2. **No plant closures**
3. **Protection of German sites**
4. **Binding investment and product commitments**
5. **A collective agreement framework that remains intact**
6. **Visible burden-sharing from management and owners**

## On the quantification question
Yes, we need the numbers. But numbers cut both ways.

If management claims a certain savings gap, then management must show:
- how much comes from working-time flexibility,
- how much from voluntary exits,
- how much from capacity adjustments,
- how much from management compensation and owner contribution,
- and what exact gap remains before anyone starts talking about closures.

Until that analysis is on the table, we reject the idea that closures are “necessary.”

## On escalation
Let me be equally explicit: the peace obligation has ended. Our members are mobilised. If the company persists in treating closure and forced redundancy as acceptable tools, then warning strikes will continue and intensify.

That is not our preferred path. But we will not let the collective wage framework and job security be dismantled by pressure.

## Bottom line
We are ready for a real settlement. But the settlement must be built on:
- fairness,
- binding site and job security,
- negotiated labour contributions,
- and shared burden across all sides.

What we will **not** accept is a restructuring where:
- workers give up pay and security,
- management keeps maximal discretion,
- owners stay insulated,
- and closures remain the threat hanging over the table.

If management wants agreement, bring a package with **no closures, no forced layoffs, and genuine burden-sharing**. Otherwise, expect resistance.
