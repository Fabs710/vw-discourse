We are aligned with management, and only partly with the employee side and the state, on what matters most: **the company must restore competitive margins through durable cost and capacity reduction**. That is the value test. Anything that fails that test is not a compromise; it is value destruction dressed up as social peace.

## Where we agree
- **The cost base is too high.**
- **Capacity is misaligned with demand.**
- **A cosmetic settlement would be unacceptable.**
- **The company needs a quantified, durable industrial plan through 2030.**
- **Some workforce contribution is necessary** if the business is to regain margin discipline.
- **Voluntary exits, redeployment, qualification, working-time tools, and capacity adjustment** should be part of the toolkit.

## Where we disagree
### 1) “No closures” cannot be an absolute red line
I do not accept the works council’s and union’s position that closures are off the table as a principle. If management’s numbers show that some sites are structurally non-viable, then refusing closures in advance is not responsible stewardship — it is a request to preserve overcapacity and let shareholders bear the cost of inaction.

That said, closures should not be a slogan. They must be justified by a **clear, plant-by-plant case** showing:
- excess capacity,
- durable savings,
- impact on unit costs and margins,
- and why alternatives do not close the gap.

If management cannot produce that case, then closures are not justified. But if it can, then they cannot be ruled out for political reasons.

### 2) “No compulsory redundancies” also cannot be an unconditional veto
We prefer voluntary and socially managed workforce reductions. But a categorical ban on compulsory redundancies can become a de facto veto on restructuring. If that blocks a sustainable cost base, shareholders will not support it.

The question is not whether forced redundancies are pleasant — they are not. The question is whether management has a credible path to target margins without them. If yes, use softer instruments. If no, then the company must retain the ability to act.

### 3) Binding guarantees through 2030 are only acceptable if they unlock competitiveness
The employee side keeps treating guarantees as the foundation of trust. We understand that. But guarantees that freeze the footprint regardless of market conditions are not value-neutral. They can lock in inefficiency and weaken the company’s ability to adapt.

A guarantee can be supported only if it is part of a package that **genuinely restores competitiveness**. Otherwise it is just a promise to preserve a structurally weak cost base.

### 4) “Shared sacrifice” is not a substitute for hard numbers
I agree with the state shareholder that burden-sharing must not fall on labour alone. But fairness is not the main metric for us. The main metric is **risk-adjusted return**.

So the real question is:
- How much do we save?
- How durably?
- By when?
- What does it do to margins and return on sales?

If the package does not get us back to target margins, it is not enough — no matter how balanced it sounds.

## What would move us
We would support a negotiated alternative to closures only if it contains all of the following:

1. **Quantified structural savings**
   Hard numbers, not rhetoric.

2. **Meaningful capacity reduction**
   If demand is lower, the structure must shrink.

3. **A real workforce contribution**
   Temporary or permanent labour-cost measures, if enforceable and material.

4. **No open-ended delay**
   The deal must be implemented on a clear timetable.

5. **A believable path back to competitive margins**
   This is the key issue. If the plan does not improve the return-on-sales trajectory, we will not treat it as acceptable.

## To management
You are right that this cannot be a short-term patch. But you still owe investors a precise case. We want the numbers, not just the diagnosis.

If alternatives to closures can fully close the gap, we would support them. If they cannot, then you should say so plainly and stop treating closure as taboo. Indecision destroys value faster than hard choices do.

## To the works council and union
Your insistence on social responsibility is understandable, but **social responsibility does not mean preserving an uncompetitive structure**. If your package delivers the same savings without closures or forced layoffs, show it. If it does not, then your red lines are making the company less investable.

## To the state shareholder
We respect the regional role, but regional legitimacy cannot override economic reality. A company that fails to earn its capital cost will not protect jobs in the long run. Stability comes from competitiveness, not from freezing the footprint.

## Bottom line
We are prepared to back:
- durable cost cuts,
- capacity reductions,
- workforce contributions,
- and even an alternative to closures,

**but only if** it is quantitatively credible and margin-improving.

We will **not** back:
- a cosmetic deal,
- an arrangement that keeps the cost base structurally uncompetitive,
- or a settlement that preserves every site while postponing the same crisis.

So the next step is not more principle-chasing. It is a **quantified restructuring case** showing exactly how the savings gap is closed. If that case requires closures or equivalent hard capacity cuts, then avoiding them for political reasons is not a shareholder-friendly outcome.
