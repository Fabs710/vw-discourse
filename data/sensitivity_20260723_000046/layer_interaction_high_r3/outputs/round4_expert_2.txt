From my remit — industrial capacity and restructuring finance — the key point is this:

## 1) The dispute is economically about residual structural overcapacity
What is still unresolved is not whether the company needs restructuring. It clearly does.

The unresolved question is whether **soft measures plus burden-sharing** can eliminate the **recurring cost gap** created by:
- excess installed capacity,
- underutilized plants,
- high fixed-cost absorption,
- and a cost base above competitive benchmark.

If the answer is no, then keeping every site open and forbidding forced reductions will almost certainly leave a **persistent margin drag**.

## 2) Soft measures can help materially, but they are not automatically sufficient
In automotive restructuring, these measures can produce real savings:
- voluntary exits
- retirement pathways
- working-time reductions
- redeployment
- temporary wage/labor-cost concessions
- internal mobility
- overhead reductions

But they usually have limits:
- they often reduce payroll, not fixed plant overhead
- they may not fully solve low utilization
- they can buy time rather than close the gap permanently
- they do not necessarily remove stranded logistics, maintenance, and depreciation burden

So the employee/state position is economically plausible only if:
1. the gap is modest, or
2. the company has significant non-labor overhead to remove, or
3. demand recovers enough to restore plant utilization.

If none of that is true, the non-closure path may be insufficient.

## 3) A plant can remain open and still be uneconomic
This is the central industrial reality.

A subscale plant can destroy margin even if it is not obviously cash-negative on a short-term basis, because fixed costs remain:
- site overhead
- utilities and maintenance
- indirect labor
- logistics complexity
- depreciation on underabsorbed assets
- inefficiency from fragmented production

So “keeping the plant open” is not the same as “preserving viable capacity.”

## 4) A blanket no-closure line is financially expensive unless the residual gap is small
If closures are ruled out ex ante, then the company may have only four ways to close a large gap:
- deeper wage/labor concessions
- bigger workforce reductions through voluntary means
- more investment/capex to lift productivity
- or accepting lower returns for longer

If the gap is large, one of those will usually prove insufficient.

That means the ownership/investor position is economically coherent: closures and compulsory redundancies should remain available **as fallback instruments** if the quantified residual gap is material.

## 5) But the governance constraint is real
Because of co-determination and blocking rights, the issue is not just economic efficiency. It is also what can actually be approved and executed.

So the realistic decision framework is:
- first test the non-closure scenario rigorously,
- then determine whether residual cost and capacity gaps remain,
- then decide whether the remaining gap is small enough to bridge without hard measures.

That is the only technically defensible way to proceed.

## 6) What the joint auditor should quantify
A serious plant-by-plant model should show:
- current utilization by site
- unit cost and fixed-cost absorption
- recurring savings from each soft measure
- one-off restructuring costs
- stranded cost if a site stays open below scale
- impact on EBIT / return on sales through 2030
- how much of the labor-cost gap is permanent versus temporary

Without that, everyone is arguing from preference, not economics.

## Bottom line
My assessment is:

- **Soft measures are necessary and can produce meaningful savings**
- **But they are not, by themselves, a reliable substitute for hard capacity reduction in a genuine overcapacity case**
- **If the residual gap after soft measures is still material, the non-closure path will likely leave the company structurally uncompetitive**
- **If the residual gap is small, a negotiated non-closure settlement may be financially viable**
