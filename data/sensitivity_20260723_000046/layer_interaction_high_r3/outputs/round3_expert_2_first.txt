My assessment, strictly on industrial capacity and restructuring finance, is this:

## Core answer
A **joint plant-by-plant audit** is the right next step, but the likely economic outcome is that **soft measures alone will only close part of the gap** if management’s description is accurate: namely, that the company has both **structural overcapacity** and a **German cost base above competitive levels**.

In that case, a package limited to:
- redeployment,
- qualification,
- working-time measures,
- voluntary exits,
- and temporary labour-cost concessions

can reduce cash cost and buy time, but it usually does **not** fully solve a durable capacity problem. For a large automotive manufacturer, the residual gap often remains unless there is some combination of:
- footprint rationalisation,
- permanent headcount reduction,
- line consolidation,
- or site/production transfer.

## What is economically plausible
### Soft measures can do three things
1. **Lower near-term cost**
   - wage restraint
   - reduced hours
   - lower premiums/allowances
   - delayed hiring
   - voluntary exits

2. **Smooth the transition**
   - avoid abrupt disruption
   - preserve skills in the short term
   - reduce industrial conflict

3. **Improve plant utilization modestly**
   - if capacity is reallocated internally

### But soft measures usually do not fully solve
- duplicate fixed overhead
- structurally underutilized plants
- subscale production lines
- excess indirect workforce
- persistent logistics inefficiency
- capex drag from maintaining too much footprint

If the core problem is **systemic overcapacity**, not a temporary volume dip, then soft measures often **defer** rather than **eliminate** the issue.

## What would make a no-closure solution credible
A non-closure package would be economically defensible only if the audit shows one or more of the following:

- the capacity gap is **modest**, not large
- there is **clear volume recovery** on the existing footprint
- one or more German sites are actually capable of absorbing more output at low incremental cost
- labour-cost concessions are **material enough** to restore margin
- overhead and logistics savings can be extracted without site closures
- product mix and platform allocation can be improved substantially

If those conditions are not met, then “no closures, no compulsory redundancies” is not a restructuring solution; it is a constraint that may leave the company with an unresolved cost problem.

## What the audit must quantify
To be useful, the audit must separate **one-off effects** from **recurring savings**.

For each plant and measure, it should show:

1. **Capacity**
   - nominal capacity
   - effective capacity
   - actual utilization
   - expected demand through 2030

2. **Cost base**
   - direct labour
   - indirect labour
   - fixed overhead
   - maintenance
   - logistics
   - utilities
   - depreciation/capital charge if relevant

3. **Savings by measure**
   - working-time changes
   - wage concessions
   - voluntary exits
   - redeployment
   - headcount reductions
   - line consolidation
   - closure scenarios

4. **Timing**
   - immediate cash impact
   - annual recurring EBITDA/EBIT impact
   - implementation cost
   - payback period

5. **Margin impact**
   - return on sales
   - break-even volume
   - cash flow through 2030

## Practical restructuring-finance conclusion
If management is right that:
- European demand is shrinking,
- German labour and overhead costs are structurally high,
- and the brand is missing its return-on-sales target,

then I would expect **soft measures plus voluntary exits** to close **some**, but usually not **all**, of the structural gap.

So, from an economic standpoint:

- **Soft measures first:** sensible and often necessary.
- **Soft measures only:** often insufficient in a true overcapacity case.
- **Hard capacity action reserved as fallback:** economically plausible, but only after a quantified test.

## Decision test
The right decision criterion is:

**After all soft measures and owner/management contributions are fully quantified, does the company still have a durable recurring gap that prevents it from reaching target margins and funding required investment?**

- If **no**, then a non-closure settlement is economically workable.
- If **yes**, then refusing any hard capacity action likely preserves an uncompetitive cost structure.

## Bottom line
My view is **not** that closures are automatically required.  
My view is that, **if the diagnosis of structural overcapacity is correct**, then it is **unlikely** that soft measures alone will be sufficient.

The next necessary step is a **credible, independent, plant-by-plant quantification** of:
- excess capacity,
- recurring savings from each soft measure,
- and the residual gap under 2030 scenarios.

Without that, all sides are still arguing preferences rather than economics.
