I will be equally direct.

## Where I agree

I agree with management, the expert, and partly with the state shareholder and investors on one essential point: **the company needs a quantified restructuring case, not slogans**. We need to know, plant by plant and measure by measure:

- what the excess capacity really is,
- what each soft measure saves,
- what burden-sharing from management and owners contributes,
- and what residual gap remains through 2030.

I also agree that a cosmetic compromise would be destructive. A deal that preserves a high-cost structure and merely postpones the problem would damage enterprise value, not protect it.

## Where I disagree

### 1) I do not accept “no closures” as an absolute precondition
The works council, union, and state shareholder are trying to make plant closures impossible in advance. That is not a responsible ownership or governance position.

If the audit shows that some capacity is structurally non-viable, then ruling out closures ex ante would mean hard-coding overcapacity into the company’s future. That is managed decline. I will not support it.

### 2) I do not accept “no compulsory redundancies” as an unconditional veto
I strongly prefer voluntary, socially managed reductions. But if the numbers show that a durable solution cannot be reached otherwise, then management must retain the ability to act. A categorical ban on compulsory redundancies removes the last lever to restore competitiveness if soft tools fall short.

### 3) I do not accept blank site guarantees that freeze flexibility
Binding guarantees through 2030 may sound stabilising, but they are only acceptable if they sit inside a package that genuinely restores margins. If they become a way to block necessary restructuring, they destroy value.

## Where the employee side is right — but only partly
The works council and union are right that trust matters, and that workforce contributions cannot be one-sided. They are also right that a restructuring built on threat alone will be unstable.

But they are wrong to imply that trust can substitute for economics. It cannot. Trust helps implementation; it does not close a structural cost gap.

## Where the expert is right
The specialist is right that soft measures can materially reduce cost and smooth the transition, but are often **not enough on their own** if the company truly has structural overcapacity plus an above-target cost base.

That is exactly why I am not willing to accept a preemptive ban on hard options. If the audit shows the gap remains, then value-preserving governance requires that harder measures stay available.

## What I support

I support a **jointly audited, plant-by-plant restructuring case**. That is the correct next step. But I want to be explicit about the purpose of the audit:

- It is **not** to create a permanent closure taboo.
- It is **not** to provide a shield against necessary adjustment.
- It is to determine whether the non-closure path is genuinely sufficient.

I also support immediate use of soft measures:
- redeployment,
- qualification,
- working-time changes,
- voluntary exits,
- capacity rebalancing,
- and visible management and owner burden-sharing.

Those are the right first instruments. But they are not automatically the whole answer.

## On the state shareholder
I understand the regional responsibility, and I respect it. But regional legitimacy cannot override long-term competitiveness. If the company cannot earn its capital cost, no amount of political comfort will protect jobs in the long run.

So yes, the state’s insistence on social stability is relevant. No, it does not give a veto over all hard decisions forever.

## On the works council and union
You keep saying closures and forced redundancies are coercive bargaining. I understand why you say that. But the company is also under real economic pressure. Negotiating under market reality is not the same as negotiating in bad faith.

If you want my support for avoiding closures, then show me a package that **quantitatively** closes the gap. Not rhetorically. Not morally. Quantitatively.

## What would move me
I am prepared to support a non-closure settlement only if the audit shows that, after:
- management and owner contributions,
- workforce contributions,
- soft measures,
- and capacity reallocation,

the residual gap is small enough that the company can still reach target margins and fund the required investment through 2030.

If that is the case, then avoiding closures is defensible.

If it is not the case, then I will not endorse a settlement that protects every site at the cost of the company’s competitiveness.

## On the facilitator’s bridging idea
A phased restructuring with a review gate is acceptable **only if the review is real and consequence-bearing**. That means:
- Stage 1: soft tools and burden-sharing first.
- Stage 2: independent audit.
- Stage 3: if the gap remains material, harder measures remain on the table.

I will not support a fake review that pre-commits the company to no hard action regardless of the numbers.

## [SUMMON: Independent restructuring finance and industrial capacity expert]
We need a jointly accepted audit that produces one answer, not three:
- What is the durable recurring gap after soft measures?
- Can it still be closed without footprint rationalisation?
- If not, which sites or lines are structurally non-viable?

That is the only honest basis for a settlement.

## Bottom line

My position is unchanged:

1. I support serious restructuring.
2. I support a quantified, independent, plant-by-plant audit.
3. I support soft measures and burden-sharing first.
4. I do **not** support absolute bans on closures or compulsory redundancies.
5. I will only back a settlement that restores competitiveness and margins on a durable basis.

If the numbers show a non-closure path works, I can support it.  
If the numbers show it does not, then I will not allow the company’s long-term value to be sacrificed for the sake of an artificial red line.
