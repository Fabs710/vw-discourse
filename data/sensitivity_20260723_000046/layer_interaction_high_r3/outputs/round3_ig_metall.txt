I will be equally clear.

We are not here to negotiate workers into accepting closures and forced redundancies as a “normal” part of transformation. That remains unacceptable. If management, owners, or investors want a settlement, they need to stop treating the workforce as the default shock absorber.

## Where I agree
I agree with several points made by others:

- The company faces a real competitiveness problem.
- A cosmetic compromise would be useless.
- A durable industrial plan through 2030 is necessary.
- The discussion must be quantified, not just rhetorical.
- Burden-sharing cannot fall on labour alone.
- Redeployment, qualification, working-time adjustments, voluntary exits, and capacity rebalancing are the right starting tools.

That is not controversial. The dispute is about the boundary line.

## Where I disagree sharply
### 1) Closures are not a neutral “option”
To management, the family holding, and investors: you keep saying closures must remain available. From our side, that means negotiating under threat. It is not a fair basis for bargaining, and it is not how you build the trust needed for a durable settlement.

If you claim a plant is structurally non-viable, then prove it — transparently, plant by plant, with the numbers. Until that case is made, closures are not a legitimate bargaining instrument. They are leverage.

### 2) Forced redundancies are not acceptable
I do not accept compulsory layoffs as a fallback. If a restructuring plan depends on them, then the plan is socially flawed and industrially reckless. The company should exhaust negotiated alternatives first.

### 3) A “conditional” site guarantee is not enough
To the state shareholder and the works council: I understand the demand for binding site security through 2030. I support that direction. A weak, conditional promise that can be undone at the next pressure point is not a guarantee.

If workers are asked to contribute, then they need certainty in return:
- no forced layoffs,
- no plant closures,
- protection of the collective wage framework,
- and concrete investment and product commitments.

### 4) “Shared sacrifice” must be real
To management and owners: if you want labour to contribute, then the sacrifice cannot be one-sided. The workforce should not give up pay, time, and protections while management retains full discretion and ownership remains insulated.

Visible burden-sharing from the top is not a cosmetic detail. It is the precondition for any deal members would accept.

## On the facilitator’s bridge ideas
### Phased restructuring with a review gate
This can only be discussed if the first stage is truly a soft-measures package:
- redeployment
- qualification
- working-time measures
- voluntary exits
- internal mobility
- temporary labour-cost contributions
- management/owner contribution

But I will not accept a “review gate” that serves as a pretext for later closures. If the numbers are being tested, the default assumption must remain: no closures, no compulsory redundancies.

### Site-specific guarantees with conditional flexibility
I do not support a loophole that quietly preserves closure rights in the background. If there is flexibility, it should be in working-time, capacity, and production mix — not in the right to shut plants down.

### Burden-sharing ladder
A burden-sharing ladder makes sense only if the sequence is correct:
1. management and owners contribute visibly,
2. soft instruments are used,
3. investment and product commitments are locked in,
4. then workforce contributions are discussed.

And even then, not in exchange for closures or forced layoffs.

## What we are prepared to discuss
We are prepared to negotiate a serious package that includes:
- temporary labour-cost contributions,
- working-time flexibility,
- capacity adjustments,
- voluntary exits and retirement pathways,
- redeployment and qualification,
- restraint on certain bonuses or allowances,
- and a jointly agreed industrial plan.

But that package must be built on these non-negotiables:
- **no compulsory redundancies**
- **no plant closures**
- **binding site and job security through 2030**
- **protection of the collective bargaining framework**
- **visible burden-sharing from management and owners**

## To management
You say the company cannot preserve every structure unchanged. Fine. But you have not shown that closures are the only credible answer. Until you do, we reject the attempt to keep them live as bargaining pressure.

If you want our cooperation, bring a quantified plan showing how the gap can be closed without layoffs or closures. If you cannot, then the issue is not workforce unwillingness. It is management’s failure to present a socially acceptable restructuring.

## To the works council
We are aligned on the core line. The union will back a serious, negotiated transformation — but not one that dismantles job security and the collective wage agreement. We can support hard contributions from labour only if management and owners take real responsibility and the sites are protected.

## To the state shareholder
Your support matters, but regional legitimacy is not enough if workers are asked to carry the full risk. If you want stability, then back a settlement that excludes closures and forced layoffs, and insists on real investment and product commitments.

## To the family holding and investors
If your only acceptable outcome is one where closures and redundancies remain available as a threat, then you should expect resistance, not cooperation. Members are mobilised. The peace obligation has ended. We are not bluffing.

## Bottom line
We are open to a hard but fair deal. We are not open to a deal that:
- makes workers pay alone,
- preserves maximal management discretion,
- keeps closures on the table,
- and treats compulsory redundancies as acceptable.

Bring a quantified, plant-by-plant alternative that closes the gap through soft measures and shared sacrifice. If you cannot, then you are not asking for transformation — you are asking the workforce to accept the loss of its protection without adequate reason.
