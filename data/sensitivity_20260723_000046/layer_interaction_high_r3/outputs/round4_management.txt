Thank you. I’ll respond directly.

## Where I agree

I agree with the union, works council, state shareholder, and experts on one point that matters: **we cannot solve this with slogans**.

We need a **joint, plant-by-plant, measure-by-measure quantified restructuring case**.  
We need to separate:
- one-off costs from recurring savings,
- temporary relief from durable cost reduction,
- and political wishful thinking from operational reality.

I also agree that:
- management and owners must contribute visibly,
- redeployment, qualification, working-time tools, voluntary exits, and capacity rebalancing should be on the table,
- and a cosmetic compromise would be unacceptable.

So yes: the process must be analytical, and it must be serious.

## Where I disagree — fundamentally

### 1) “No closures” cannot be an absolute red line
I do not accept a framework that excludes plant closures in advance.

If the audit shows that some German capacity is structurally non-viable, then refusing to consider closures would mean preserving overcapacity and locking in an uncompetitive cost base. That is not protecting the company. It is postponing the problem.

I am not saying closures are the first choice. They are not.  
I am saying they must remain available if the economics require them.

### 2) “No compulsory redundancies” cannot be unconditional
I prefer voluntary, socially managed reductions. But I cannot accept a categorical ban on compulsory redundancies before we know whether the required savings can be achieved otherwise.

If a sustainable structure needs deeper adjustment, management must retain the ability to execute it. Otherwise we are choosing rigidity over competitiveness.

### 3) Binding guarantees through 2030 cannot freeze the company
A blanket site and job guarantee through 2030 sounds reassuring, but if it blocks necessary restructuring, it simply hard-codes today’s cost problem into tomorrow’s business.

A guarantee is only credible if it sits inside a package that actually restores margins and funds investment.

## To the works council

You say a quantified case is not a license for closures. On that, we partly agree.

But the reverse is also true: soft measures are not automatically sufficient, and no one should pretend they are before the numbers are tested.

You are asking us to rule out the very instruments that may be needed if the gap is real and persistent. I cannot accept that.  
If the numbers show that non-coercive measures fully close the gap, I will support that outcome.  
If they do not, I will not sign up to a deal that preserves every site by weakening the entire company.

## To the union

You say closures and forced redundancies are coercive bargaining. I understand the concern, but I reject the implication that management is negotiating in bad faith simply because market reality is hard.

We have a shrinking European market, intense competition, and a cost base that is above target. That is not a rhetorical problem. It is an economic one.

I agree that labour should not carry the burden alone. I agree that management and owners must contribute first and visibly.  
What I do **not** accept is the claim that closures and redundancies can be removed from the table before the savings gap is proven.

Bring a package that closes the gap durably without those instruments, and I will engage seriously.  
But I will not pretend that economics disappears because the workforce objects.

## To the state shareholder

I respect the regional responsibility you carry. But regional legitimacy cannot override competitiveness.

A company that does not earn its capital cost will not protect jobs in the long run.  
If we want to preserve German industrial presence, then we need a structure that can actually compete.

So yes, I support social stability and investment commitments.  
No, I do not support a framework that makes necessary adjustment impossible.

## To the controlling family and investors

You are right about one thing: a cosmetic deal would destroy value. We agree there.

But “hard” is not the same as “good.” What matters is whether the package is **durable, quantified, and margin-improving**.

If soft measures can close the gap, they are preferable.  
If they cannot, then we must retain the ability to make harder structural changes.

That is not ideology. That is responsible governance.

## On the expert input

The expert has made the key point correctly: soft measures can materially help, but in a genuine structural overcapacity case they are often not enough on their own.

That is precisely why I am not willing to sign off on an ex ante ban on closures or compulsory redundancies.  
We need the audit to tell us whether the residual gap is small enough to manage without hard measures. If it is, fine. If it is not, then we cannot keep pretending otherwise.

## What I am prepared to support

I am prepared to support a serious package built around:

- redeployment
- qualification and retraining
- working-time adjustments
- voluntary exits and retirement pathways
- internal mobility
- temporary labour-cost contributions
- management and owner burden-sharing
- capacity rebalancing
- and a quantified industrial plan through 2030

I am also prepared to support a **staged process** — but only if it is real, not a delaying tactic.

## What I cannot support

I cannot support:

- an absolute ban on closures,
- an absolute ban on compulsory redundancies,
- unconditional site guarantees that eliminate flexibility,
- or a restructuring that leaves the company unable to restore competitiveness.

Those positions do not solve the crisis. They preserve it.

## My proposal for the next step

I support the expert call for a jointly accepted audit, but I want it to answer one central question:

**After all soft measures and all management/owner contributions are quantified, is there still a durable recurring gap that prevents the company from reaching target margins and funding the investment needed to 2030?**

If the answer is no, then a non-closure settlement is viable.

If the answer is yes, then I will not support a settlement that simply protects every site while leaving the company structurally uncompetitive.

## Bottom line

I am not here to sign a cosmetic deal.  
I am also not here to impose closures as an ideology.

I am here to restore competitiveness, reduce overcapacity, lower labour costs, and free up funds for future products and technology.

If the non-coercive package can close the gap durably, I will support it.  
If it cannot, then I will not accept a settlement that preserves every site at the expense of the company’s future.
