We are aligned on the **objective**, not on the **toolset**.

Our objective remains unchanged: **restore competitive margins on a durable basis**. If the company cannot credibly reduce costs and align capacity with demand, then it is not protecting value; it is deferring impairment.

## Where we agree
We agree with management, the family holding, and the expert on the core financial diagnosis:

- this is not a cosmetic problem,
- the cost base is too high,
- capacity is likely too broad for the market,
- and a durable restructuring is needed.

We also agree with the employee side and the state on one important process point:

- this must be quantified,
- plant by plant,
- measure by measure,
- with a real model of recurring savings and one-off costs.

That is the right next step.

## Where we disagree
### 1) A blanket “no closures” line is not acceptable
We do **not** support precluding plant closures in advance.

If the audit shows that some German sites are structurally non-viable, then ruling out closures would mean preserving overcapacity and leaving shareholders to absorb the cost of inaction. That is not a defensible capital-allocation outcome.

We understand the political and social resistance. We do not dismiss it. But we do not buy the argument that all sites must be protected regardless of economics.

### 2) An unconditional ban on compulsory redundancies is also not acceptable
We prefer voluntary exits, redeployment, and retirement routes. Those are better than forced cuts.

But if the residual gap remains material after soft measures, management must retain the ability to act. A categorical ban on compulsory redundancies can easily become a veto on restructuring.

### 3) Guarantees are only useful if they do not freeze the company
A site/job guarantee through 2030 is only acceptable if it is part of a package that actually restores competitiveness.

A guarantee that prevents the company from adapting its footprint would simply hard-code today’s cost problem into tomorrow’s business.

## On the works council and union
You argue that closures and forced layoffs are coercive bargaining. We understand why you say that. But from a shareholder perspective, the market is not suspending reality.

If soft measures can fully close the gap, then fine — we would support that.  
If they cannot, then your red lines are not protecting value; they are impairing it.

We are open to a serious negotiated package, but not to one that rules out the instruments needed to fix the economics.

## On the state shareholder
We respect the regional responsibility. But regional legitimacy cannot override long-term value creation.

A company that cannot earn its capital cost will not protect jobs in the long run. The state can and should insist on social order and investment commitments, but it cannot make an uncompetitive structure viable by decree.

## On management
Management is right to insist on a quantified, independent case. We support that.

But management should be careful not to rely on abstract threat alone. The numbers need to do the work. If the audit shows that non-coercive measures are enough, then great. If not, management must be prepared to use the full restructuring toolkit.

## What we would support
We would support a package that includes:
- immediate soft measures,
- visible burden-sharing from management and owners,
- a jointly audited plant-by-plant model,
- a clear timetable,
- and no ideological exclusion of closures or compulsory redundancies before the residual gap is proven.

## What we will not support
We will **not** support:
- a cosmetic compromise,
- a deal that preserves the full cost base,
- or a settlement that rules out necessary structural measures before the savings gap is actually demonstrated.

We need a jointly accepted model that translates the proposed measures into:
- recurring annual savings,
- one-off restructuring costs,
- impact on unit cost,
- residual gap by site,
- and return-on-sales through 2030.

Without that, everyone is defending a preference rather than a plan.

## Bottom line
Our line remains disciplined and clear:

- **soft measures first**
- **burden-sharing from all sides**
- **independent quantified audit**
- **no blanket veto on closures or compulsory redundancies**
- **only a settlement that credibly restores margins and competitiveness**

If the non-closure path truly closes the gap, we can support it. If it does not, then we will not back a settlement that preserves an uncompetitive structure for the sake of comfort or politics.
