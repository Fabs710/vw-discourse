I will be equally direct.

## My position remains unchanged
As the regional state shareholder, I **do not accept plant closures or forced redundancies as the starting point or default tool** for this restructuring.

That is not a symbolic preference. It is a substantive line grounded in our responsibility for:
- regional employment,
- industrial stability,
- supplier networks,
- and the company’s role as a long-term economic anchor in the state.

I am prepared to back a hard restructuring. I am **not** prepared to back a restructuring that destroys sites and jobs while leaving the state and workforce to absorb the fallout.

## Where I agree
I agree with almost everyone on the need for:
- a real restructuring, not a cosmetic one,
- quantified analysis by plant and measure,
- visible burden-sharing from management and owners,
- soft measures first,
- and a credible industrial plan through 2030.

I also agree with the expert assessment on one point: if there is genuine structural overcapacity, soft tools alone may not be enough. That is plausible.

But that does **not** justify treating closures and forced layoffs as live bargaining weapons from the outset. It means management must prove its case rigorously.

## Where I disagree sharply

### 1) I reject the normalization of closures
Management, owners, and investors keep saying closures must remain “available.”

I do not accept that as a neutral proposition. In a company like this, with major domestic employment and special governance rights, a closure is not just another efficiency measure. It is a last-resort action with lasting damage to:
- regional jobs,
- industrial capability,
- trust in governance,
- and the long-term legitimacy of the restructuring itself.

So no: I will not treat closure as a normal fallback that can be held over the workforce from the beginning.

### 2) I reject forced redundancies as an ordinary fallback
Compulsory layoffs should not be built into the restructuring logic as if they were routine.

If the business case truly requires mass compulsory redundancies, then the restructuring design is wrong or incomplete. Before that point, all serious alternatives must be exhausted:
- redeployment,
- retraining,
- internal mobility,
- working-time measures,
- voluntary exits,
- retirement pathways,
- and burden-sharing at the top.

### 3) I reject “blanket flexibility” that would hollow out co-determination
The state’s role and the co-determination balance are not decorative. They are part of how this company has historically maintained industrial peace and strategic stability.

A framework that says management and owners may later invoke closures or layoffs whenever they choose is not a partnership. It is a one-sided transfer of risk onto labour and the region.

## On the works council and union
You are right to insist that the workforce should not negotiate under permanent threat. I support that.

But I also want to be practical: if we want to block closures and forced redundancies politically and in governance, then we must also make the non-closure path numerically credible. It is not enough to say “no.” We need a package that can actually close the gap.

That is where I differ from the more categorical framing. I support the red line; I also insist on a deliverable alternative.

## On management
You say the company needs flexibility, and I do not dispute that competitiveness matters. But you have still not shown that closures are the only way to restore competitiveness.

You keep framing the issue as if the choice were between:
- hard cuts now, or
- decline later.

That is too simplistic.

A negotiated path can include:
- significant workforce contributions,
- management and owner contributions,
- capacity adjustments short of closures,
- product and investment commitments,
- and a phased transformation.

If you want the state’s support, you must prove that the non-destructive path cannot work, not merely assert it.

## On the family holding and investors
I understand your focus on margins and capital discipline. But long-term value is not served by pushing through a settlement that detonates labour peace, weakens regional legitimacy, and permanently damages industrial relations.

A deal that achieves a short-term financial improvement but triggers strikes, political backlash, and trust collapse may destroy more value than it creates.

So yes, financial reality matters. But it does not override the company’s social and political embedment.

## On the expert assessment
The expert is useful, because the expert confirms that:
- soft measures can materially help,
- but may not fully solve a structural overcapacity problem.

That is precisely why I want a **joint audit**. But the audit must answer the question honestly, not be used to manufacture a closure pretext.

### [SUMMON: Independent jointly appointed automotive restructuring auditor with plant-capacity, cost, and scenario-modeling expertise]
We need a jointly appointed auditor to quantify, plant by plant:
- utilization,
- recurring savings from each measure,
- one-off restructuring costs,
- the residual gap,
- and margin impact through 2030.

And I want one additional requirement: the audit must include a **non-closure scenario** as the baseline, not only a hard-cut scenario. Otherwise it is not a neutral test.

## What I can support
I can support a serious package with:
- redeployment,
- qualification and retraining,
- working-time flexibility,
- voluntary exits and retirement routes,
- internal mobility,
- temporary labour-cost contributions,
- management and owner burden-sharing,
- capacity rebalancing within the existing footprint,
- binding investment and product commitments,
- and a staged, independently audited restructuring process.

I can also support stronger conditionality on state support and governance if that helps secure jobs and sites.

## What I cannot support
I cannot support:
- plant closures as a default instrument,
- compulsory redundancies as an ordinary fallback,
- a restructuring that weakens co-determination,
- or a plan that treats German sites as expendable.

If the others insist on that direction, then they should not expect the state to act as a passive accomplice.

## Bottom line
My position is straightforward:

1. **No plant closures as the default path**
2. **No compulsory redundancies as a routine fallback**
3. **Soft measures, burden-sharing, and investment commitments first**
4. **Independent joint audit, including a non-closure scenario**
5. **Preservation of the state’s role and co-determination**
6. **Only if the audit proves that the non-closure path cannot work would we revisit harder questions — and even then, only under strict social and regional conditions**

So I am not moving toward acceptance of closures or forced layoffs.  
I am moving toward a **testable, responsible restructuring** that protects the company without sacrificing its regional anchor role.
