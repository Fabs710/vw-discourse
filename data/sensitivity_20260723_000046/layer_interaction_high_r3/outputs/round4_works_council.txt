Let me be equally direct in round 4.

## My position remains unchanged
For the works council, the core line is still:

- **no plant closures in Germany**
- **no compulsory redundancies**
- **binding site and job guarantees through 2030**
- **preservation of co-determination and the job-security framework**
- **restructuring through soft measures, voluntary instruments, redeployment, qualification, and negotiated burden-sharing**

I will not accept a package that keeps closures and forced layoffs as a standing threat over the workforce.

## Where I agree with others
I agree with the repeated point that this must be based on numbers, not slogans.  
I agree that burden-sharing cannot fall on the workforce alone.  
I agree that management and owners must contribute visibly.  
And I agree that a joint, independent audit is needed.

That said, an audit is only useful if it is used to test a **non-destructive solution**, not to manufacture a pretext for closures.

## Where I disagree with management, owners, and investors
You keep saying closures and forced redundancies must remain available “if needed.” From the workforce side, that is exactly the problem.

You are asking us to negotiate under a permanent threat:
- a plant can be closed later,
- jobs can be cut later,
- guarantees can be broken later,
- and the company can still call that a partnership.

That is not partnership. That is coercive bargaining.

If the company wants a real settlement, it must first show that it can solve the problem **without** destroying the job-security framework.

## On the expert’s argument
I respect the expert’s technical view that soft measures may not always close a structural gap. That is plausible in the abstract.

But the expert has not shown — because nobody has yet shown — that the gap here cannot be closed by a combination of:

- management and owner contributions
- labour-cost contributions short of closures and layoffs
- working-time adjustment
- redeployment
- qualification
- internal mobility
- voluntary exits
- capacity rebalancing within the existing footprint
- product and investment commitments that secure future volume

That is the test. Not whether hard measures are theoretically possible.

So yes, I support the independent audit.  
No, I do not accept the implied conclusion that hard capacity action is therefore legitimate.

## On the state shareholder
I appreciate the state’s position more than the others because it at least recognises that this is not a purely financial question. Regional employment, supplier networks, and industrial stability matter.

But I also need to be clear: saying you oppose closures and forced redundancies is not enough if, in the end, you allow management to keep them on the table as leverage.

If the state truly stands for regional responsibility, then it should back a framework that excludes:
- plant closures
- compulsory redundancies
- and unilateral dismantling of the job-security regime

## On the controlling family and investors
Your position is the least convincing from a governance point of view.

You say “we need flexibility.” What you really mean is that you want the workforce to bear the burden of uncertainty while capital keeps the right to shift the pain onto plants and employees later.

You also underestimate the cost of industrial conflict.  
A restructuring imposed through threat will not create trust, will not secure skills, and will not create durable performance. It will provoke strikes, public resistance, and long-term damage to the company’s reputation.

That is not value-preserving governance. That is short-termism.

## On management
You say a blanket guarantee freezes the company. That is only true if management has already decided that the only acceptable restructuring path is through closures and layoffs.

We are not rejecting change. We are rejecting **destructive change**.

If management is serious about restoring competitiveness, then it should put forward a quantified plan that:
1. rules out closures,
2. rules out compulsory redundancies,
3. uses the existing footprint intelligently,
4. and shares sacrifice fairly.

If you cannot do that, then the problem is not the workforce. The problem is the design of your restructuring.

## What we can support
We are prepared to negotiate a serious package including:

- redeployment
- qualification and retraining
- working-time flexibility
- voluntary exits and retirement pathways
- internal mobility
- temporary labour-cost measures
- capacity adjustments **within** the existing German plant network
- management and owner contribution
- binding investment and product commitments
- co-determination in shaping the transformation

That is a real contribution from labour. It is not obstruction.

## What we will not support
We will not support:
- plant closures
- compulsory redundancies
- a hollow “guarantee” that can be undone whenever management chooses
- a restructuring that treats the workforce as the shock absorber
- any plan that weakens co-determination or the historic job-security framework

## On sequencing
If the company wants a phased process, then fine — but only if the sequence is honest:

### Phase 1
Immediate use of soft measures:
- management and owner burden-sharing first
- labour contribution through negotiated, reversible instruments
- redeployment and qualification
- working-time measures
- voluntary exits only

### Phase 2
Independent audit of:
- excess capacity
- recurring savings
- plant-by-plant productivity
- margin impact through 2030

### Phase 3
Joint decision on the basis of the audit

But the decision logic must remain:
- if soft measures and burden-sharing close the gap, then no closures and no forced layoffs
- if they do not, then management must come back with **more non-destructive measures**, not jump immediately to plant closures and compulsory redundancies

I am not accepting a hidden staircase toward shutdowns.

## Bottom line
My red lines remain:
- **no closures**
- **no compulsory redundancies**
- **binding site and job guarantees through 2030**
- **preservation of co-determination**
- **fair burden-sharing from management and owners**

If the others want a durable settlement, they should stop asking the workforce to pre-accept its own loss of security. Bring a credible, quantified plan that works inside these limits. Until then, we remain opposed.
