We share the same core metric as management and the family owners: **this company has to get back to competitive margins on a durable basis**. Anything that does not materially reduce the cost base and align capacity with demand is not a settlement from our perspective; it is deferred value destruction.

## Where we agree
- The company’s cost base is too high.
- The current capacity structure is not obviously sustainable.
- A cosmetic deal would be unacceptable.
- Soft measures should be part of the package:
  - redeployment
  - qualification/training
  - working-time changes
  - voluntary exits
  - labour-cost contributions
  - capacity rebalancing
- A quantified, plant-by-plant restructuring case is necessary.
- Management, owners, and labour should all contribute.

## Where we disagree
### 1) “No closures” cannot be an absolute line
I do **not** support a framework that rules out closures in advance. If the audit shows some German sites are structurally non-viable, then refusing to consider closures would mean preserving overcapacity and forcing shareholders to absorb the cost of inaction.

That is not a responsible position, whether it comes from labour, the state, or anyone else.

### 2) “No compulsory redundancies” cannot be an unconditional veto
We prefer voluntary and socially managed workforce reduction. But if the numbers show that a sustainable cost structure cannot be reached otherwise, management must retain the ability to act. A categorical ban on compulsory redundancies can become a veto on restructuring.

### 3) Binding guarantees are only acceptable if they do not freeze the company
We understand why employees want site and job guarantees through 2030. But guarantees that eliminate flexibility before the economics are tested can hard-code inefficiency into the future.

A guarantee is only acceptable if it is part of a package that actually restores competitiveness.

## On the works council and union
You argue that closures and forced layoffs are coercive bargaining. We understand the concern, but we do not accept the conclusion that those instruments must be removed from the table.

If management’s case is weak, then prove it with numbers.  
If soft measures can fully close the gap, then fine — we would support that.  
But if they cannot, then red lines that block hard choices are not protecting value; they are impairing it.

## On the state shareholder
We respect the regional responsibility, but regional legitimacy cannot override economic reality. A company that does not earn its capital cost will not protect jobs in the long run.

## On the family holding
We agree that a cosmetic compromise would be value-destructive. But “hard” is not the same as “good.” What matters is whether the package is **quantitatively credible and margin-improving**.

## Where the expert’s input matters
The expert is right that soft measures can help materially, but in a genuine structural overcapacity case they are often not enough on their own. That is exactly why we insist on a quantified audit rather than a preemptive closure taboo.

## What we would support
We would support a package only if it includes:
1. **Independent, plant-by-plant audit**
2. **Immediate soft measures**
3. **Visible burden-sharing from management and owners**
4. **A clear timetable**
5. **A credible path back to target margins**
6. **No ideological exclusion of closures or compulsory redundancies before the numbers are tested**

## What we will not support
We will **not** back:
- a cosmetic deal,
- a deal that preserves the full cost base,
- or a settlement that rules out necessary structural measures before the savings gap is actually demonstrated.

We would like an independent, jointly accepted model that translates the proposed measures into:
- recurring annual savings,
- one-off restructuring costs,
- impact on unit cost,
- and return-on-sales through 2030.

That model should show whether the non-closure path truly closes the gap, or whether hard capacity action is still needed.

## Bottom line
Our position remains disciplined and straightforward:

- We support restructuring.
- We support soft measures first.
- We support burden-sharing from all sides.
- We do **not** support absolute bans on closures or compulsory redundancies.
- We will only support a settlement that is numerically credible and restores competitiveness.

If the non-closure package really works, we can back it. If it does not, then we will not help lock in an uncompetitive structure for the sake of political comfort.
