Management, owners, and investors are still trying to shift the burden onto the workforce first and then asking us to accept the result as “necessary.” I do not accept that framing.

Let me be equally clear on the points that matter:

## What we agree on
- Yes, the company faces a real competitive problem.
- Yes, the answer cannot be cosmetic.
- Yes, there must be a durable industrial plan through 2030.
- Yes, we are prepared to discuss workforce contributions, but only in a framework that protects jobs and sites.

## What we do not accept
### 1) Plant closures in Germany
You keep saying closures are “not ruled out” if they are necessary. From our side, that is exactly the problem: once closures are on the table as a normal instrument, trust is broken and the workforce is being prepared for a fait accompli.

We reject any restructuring logic that begins from closing German plants.  
If management claims there is excess capacity, then the burden is on management to prove — quantitatively and transparently — that no combination of alternatives can solve it.

### 2) Forced redundancies
Compulsory layoffs are not an acceptable restructuring tool.  
If the plan requires them, then the plan is socially unacceptable and industrially reckless.

### 3) Blank cheque on flexibility
You all talk about “flexibility” and “durable savings,” but what that often means in practice is: remove protections, weaken job security, and call it transformation. That is not a serious social partnership.

## Where I agree with the concern about economics
I do not dismiss the need for numbers. In fact, I welcome a quantified case — because if management is serious, it should be able to show:
- what the actual excess capacity is,
- what each measure saves,
- what can be achieved through working time, redeployment, qualification, and voluntary exits,
- and what investment is tied to each German site.

But let me say what must follow from that:
A quantified case does **not** automatically justify closures or forced layoffs. It simply means management must prove that its preferred remedy is the only one. Right now, I have not heard that proof.

## The condition for any workforce contribution
If management wants labour to contribute, then the company must first commit to:

1. **Binding site guarantees for all German plants through 2030**
2. **No compulsory redundancies**
3. **No plant closures**
4. **Protection of core production and investment at each site**
5. **Full co-determination in shaping the transformation**
6. **Use of soft instruments only**  
   - redeployment  
   - qualification  
   - working-time adjustments  
   - voluntary exits  
   - capacity rebalancing

That is the only credible basis for a settlement.

## On burden-sharing
I agree with the union and the state that owners and management must also contribute visibly. But I want to be precise: “visible burden-sharing” cannot mean a symbolic gesture while the workforce gives up binding protection. If the board, family owners, and executives want credibility, then they should start by ruling out closures and forced layoffs and by committing to long-term site security.

## On the arguments from investors and family owners
You say the company cannot preserve every existing structure. I say: what you are really defending is the right to make workers absorb management’s strategic mistakes.

You say the settlement must be “margin-improving.” I say: a settlement that destroys trust, triggers industrial conflict, and tears up historic job guarantees is not margin-improving in any durable sense. It is a recipe for permanent confrontation.

You say guarantees can block restructuring. From our side, guarantees are exactly what makes restructuring possible, because without them there is no trust and no willingness to cooperate.

## Our position for this round
We are not here to negotiate the principle of plant closures or forced redundancies. Those are off the table.

If management wants a real deal, then bring:
- a restructuring plan with **no closures**,
- **no compulsory redundancies**,
- binding guarantees for every German site through 2030,
- and a concrete investment and product commitment for each plant.

Only then can we discuss workforce contributions, and only within those limits.

If the answer remains that closures are “still possible,” then you should assume resistance, mobilisation, and escalation. We have already shown that we can do that, and we will do it again.
