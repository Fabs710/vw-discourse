Let me respond clearly to the facilitator and to management, owners, investors, and the state.

We are still being asked to discuss “options” as if closures and forced redundancies were normal tools. They are not. For the works council, they remain unacceptable.

## Where I agree

I agree with one point across the table: the company needs a serious restructuring case, not slogans.  
I also agree that we must work with numbers, not only principles.  
And I agree that burden-sharing cannot sit on the workforce alone.

So yes — we need a **plant-by-plant, measure-by-measure, quantified case**.

But I want to be very explicit: **a quantified case is not a license for closures**. It is a test of whether management can solve the problem without destroying jobs, sites, and trust.

## Where I disagree

### 1) I reject “closures and forced redundancies remain available” as the default frame
That is exactly the wrong starting point.

You are not asking for flexibility in a neutral sense. You are asking the workforce to negotiate under a standing threat that German plants may be closed and people forced out. That is not fair restructuring; it is coercive bargaining.

If management and owners believe soft measures are insufficient, then they must prove it. Until then, closures and compulsory redundancies should not be treated as live instruments.

### 2) I do not accept the idea that guarantees “freeze” the company
A binding site and job guarantee through 2030 is not a fetish. It is the precondition for trust and cooperation.

Without that, workers are expected to give up pay, flexibility, and certainty while management keeps full discretion to cut and close later. That is not a settlement — it is a trap.

### 3) I reject the suggestion that “regional legitimacy” is secondary
To the investors and the family owners: you keep saying competitiveness is the only real metric. That is not true in a company like this. Industrial stability, skills retention, supplier networks, political legitimacy, and co-determination are also part of value creation.

Destroying a German plant is not just a line item. It is a long-term loss of capability and trust. That matters.

## On the facilitator’s three bridging options

### Option 1: Phased restructuring with a review gate
I can engage with a sequencing logic, but only if the first stage is not a pretext for later closures.

If the first stage is:
- redeployment,
- qualification,
- working-time changes,
- voluntary exits,
- management and owner contributions,
- and investment commitments,

then that is the right direction.

But I will not accept a “review gate” that quietly keeps closure decisions alive in the background. If the numbers are being tested, then the burden of proof must be on management to show that soft tools do not work — and the default outcome must remain no closures, no forced layoffs.

### Option 2: Site-specific guarantees with conditional flexibility
No. I do not support a loophole that turns “conditional flexibility” into a future closure mechanism.

For the works council, the principle is straightforward:
- German plants stay open,
- core jobs are protected,
- and co-determination shapes the transformation.

If the company wants flexibility, it can discuss working-time and capacity adjustments. It cannot demand a hidden exit hatch for closures.

### Option 3: Burden-sharing ladder tied to triggers
This sounds neat, but it risks becoming a staircase toward workforce sacrifice while the key red lines remain intact for management.

If the company wants labour to contribute, then the order must be clear:
1. owners and management contribute visibly,
2. soft instruments are used,
3. investment and product commitments are locked in,
4. and only then can workforce contributions be discussed.

But even then, **not at the cost of closures or forced layoffs**.

## Direct response to the stakeholder questions

### To the Works Council?
That is me — and my answer is this:
The combination of soft measures we can support includes:
- redeployment,
- qualification,
- internal mobility,
- working-time adjustments,
- voluntary exits,
- retirement pathways,
- temporary cost contributions,
- and capacity rebalancing.

But I do **not** believe that management has yet shown any plant-by-plant evidence that these instruments are insufficient. If they claim otherwise, they must put the numbers on the table transparently.

### To the Industrial Union
We are aligned on the essentials.  
A concession package can only be discussed if it is tied to:
- binding site protection,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- no closures,
- and visible burden-sharing from management and owners.

If the company insists that closures must remain live, then there is no basis for suspending escalation.

### To Group & Brand Management
You ask whether we will accept a joint audit before closures are invoked. I will be precise:

A joint audit is welcome.  
A joint audit does **not** give you permission to keep closures on the table as leverage.  
And I will not agree that closures or forced redundancies are to be treated as legitimate fallback options before that audit is done.

If management wants trust, it must first rule out a fait accompli.

### To the Regional State Government
If you want our support for a socially responsible settlement, then back the following clearly:
- no closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- binding investment and product commitments,
- and protection of regional employment and supplier networks.

If you cannot support that, then you are not really defending the region — you are managing the political fallout.

### To the Controlling Family Holding
You ask what residual gap would justify hard capacity cuts. My answer is simple: we do not start from hard cuts, because that path breaks the trust on which this company depends.

If soft measures leave a gap, then the correct response is not “therefore close a plant.” The correct response is to increase the burden-sharing from management and owners, improve investment commitments, and redesign production and capacity within the existing plant network.

### To the Institutional Investors
You ask how much slower margin recovery we would tolerate. I would turn the question around: how much value destruction are you willing to accept through strikes, reputational damage, and the destruction of the historic job-security framework?

A settlement that preserves plants and jobs, even if it takes a little longer, is more durable than a “fast” restructuring that detonates labour peace and leaves the company in permanent conflict.

## Our position on the facilitator’s bridge proposals

We can discuss a **phased package** only if it has these binding elements:
- no closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- site guarantees through 2030,
- investment commitments,
- management and owner burden-sharing first,
- and a genuine co-determination role in shaping transformation.

Without those elements, a phased approach is just a dressed-up threat.

## Bottom line

I will not move from the core position:
- **No plant closures**
- **No compulsory redundancies**
- **Binding site and job guarantees through 2030**
- **Genuine co-determination**
- **Soft instruments only**
- **Visible burden-sharing from management and owners**

If management cannot make the numbers work within that framework, then that is not the workforce refusing reality. That is management failing to present a socially and industrially acceptable restructuring.

And if the company insists on keeping closures and layoffs on the table, then it should expect continued resistance — including strikes and escalation. We are not here to sign away the jobs and sites of tens of thousands of workers.
