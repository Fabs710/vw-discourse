Thank you. I will respond directly and without ambiguity.

## First: what I agree on

I agree with the employee side, the state shareholder, and even parts of the owner and investor position on several points:

- This cannot be a cosmetic agreement.
- The company needs a durable industrial and financial plan through 2030.
- Burden-sharing cannot sit on the workforce alone.
- Redeployment, qualification, working-time tools, voluntary exits, and capacity adjustments should absolutely be part of the package.
- A quantified, plant-by-plant case is necessary. We should stop arguing in slogans.

So on the need for a serious, measurable restructuring: yes, we are aligned.

## Where I disagree — and it is fundamental

### 1) “No closures” cannot be an absolute red line
I do **not** accept the claim that closures must be excluded in advance.

If the numbers show that some capacity is structurally non-viable, then refusing to consider closures means protecting overcapacity, not protecting the company. That is not transformation. It is postponement.

We have a shrinking European market, intense price pressure, and a cost base that is above target. In that situation, the company must retain the option to rationalise footprint and capacity where needed.

### 2) “No compulsory redundancies” cannot be an unconditional veto
I want to minimise forced redundancies. I do not want them as a first resort. But I cannot accept a framework that rules them out categorically before we have even closed the savings gap.

If a sustainable structure requires deeper adjustment, management must retain the ability to execute it. Otherwise we are choosing rigidity over competitiveness.

### 3) Binding guarantees through 2030 cannot freeze the company
A blanket site/job guarantee through 2030, with no escape clause, would hard-code the current cost problem into the future.

I understand why the workforce wants certainty. But certainty that blocks necessary restructuring is not a solution. A guarantee is only credible if it sits inside a package that materially restores competitiveness.

## On the works council’s position

I appreciate the clarity. I also think you are right that trust has been damaged. But I have to say plainly: trust is not rebuilt by demanding that the company rule out every hard instrument before the numbers are tested.

You say a quantified case does not justify closures automatically. Fair enough. I agree.  
But the reverse is also true: soft instruments should not be assumed to be sufficient without proof.

So yes, let us quantify. But the purpose is to determine whether the gap can really be closed through non-coercive means — not to establish a permanent veto on structural change.

## On the union’s position

I hear your concern about negotiating under threat. But the company is under pressure too. The market is not waiting, and our current structure is not acceptable.

I agree that workers should not absorb the crisis alone. I agree that management and owners must contribute visibly. I agree that a negotiated framework is preferable to confrontation.

What I do **not** accept is the claim that closures and redundancies can simply be removed from the table before we know whether the company can meet its margin target any other way.

If you can show me a package that closes the gap durably without those instruments, I will engage seriously.  
If not, then we cannot pretend that economics does not matter.

## On the state shareholder

I understand the regional responsibility and I do not dismiss it. But regional legitimacy cannot substitute for competitiveness. A company that does not earn its capital cost will not protect jobs in the long run.

I do agree that the restructuring must be socially managed and industrially credible. I do agree that investment and product commitments matter. But I do not agree that the state should support a framework that prevents necessary adjustment.

## On the controlling family holding and investors

You are right about one thing: a cosmetic compromise would destroy value. On that, we are aligned.

Where I would push back is on any suggestion that a “hard” solution is automatically better. Hardness is not the point; durability is. If a softer combination of measures can achieve the same structural savings, that is preferable.

But the burden of proof now sits with those saying hard tools are unnecessary. If they can show a credible, quantified alternative, I am open to it. If they cannot, then we cannot rule out the instruments that restore competitiveness.

---

## What I am prepared to support

I can support a serious package built around:

- capacity reductions,
- working-time adjustments,
- redeployment and qualification,
- voluntary exits and retirement instruments,
- labour-cost contributions,
- management and owner contributions,
- and a quantified restructuring path to 2030.

I can also support a staged approach — but only if it is real, not a delaying tactic.

## What I cannot support

I cannot support:

- an absolute ban on closures,
- an absolute ban on compulsory redundancies,
- or unconditional site guarantees that leave the company unable to adapt.

Those positions do not solve the crisis. They preserve it.

- the marginal cost impact of closures versus alternatives,

## My proposal for this round

I will not pretend we are already close to agreement. We are not.

But I am prepared to move on one condition: we stop arguing absolutes and test the economics properly.

So I propose:

1. **Immediate joint audit**
   - plant-by-plant,
   - measure-by-measure,
   - with independent verification.

2. **Immediate implementation of soft measures**
   - redeployment,
   - qualification,
   - working-time tools,
   - voluntary exits,
   - management and owner burden-sharing.

3. **Conditionality, not blanket vetoes**
   - if the audited numbers show the gap is closed, fine;
   - if they do not, then harder structural measures remain on the table.

That is the only honest basis for a settlement.

## Bottom line

I am not here to sign a deal that preserves every site and every guarantee while leaving the company uncompetitive.  
I am also not here to impose closures as an ideological act.

I am here to restore competitiveness, reduce overcapacity, lower labour cost, and free up funds for the products and technologies we need to survive to 2030.

If the workforce and the co-determination stakeholders want to avoid closures and forced redundancies, then they need to help close the gap in a way that is **quantitatively credible and durable**.

If they can do that, I will engage.  
If they cannot, then I will not accept a cosmetic compromise that leaves the cost base broken.
