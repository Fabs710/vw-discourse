The core question is whether a **non-closure, no-forced-redundancy restructuring** can plausibly close the company’s structural gap, or whether **hard capacity cuts and/or closures** are likely required.

My assessment, based on restructuring finance and industrial capacity economics, is:

## Bottom line
A purely “soft” package can help materially, but **it is rarely sufficient on its own** if the company truly has **persistent excess capacity plus an above-market German cost base**.  
If management’s diagnosis is accurate, then a durable fix usually requires some combination of:

- **capacity reduction**
- **site/line rationalisation**
- **headcount reduction**
- **labour-cost reset**
- and possibly **selective footprint change**

The exact mix depends on the size of the gap. But from a restructuring perspective, the burden of proof is on the non-closure camp to show that soft measures can generate **permanent** savings large enough to restore margin, not just defer the problem.

## What matters economically

### 1) Excess capacity is not the same as temporary underutilization
If the problem is cyclical, then working-time measures, short-time arrangements, and voluntary exits can bridge it.  
If the problem is structural — meaning the demand level, product mix, and cost position imply that the current footprint is too large — then soft tools only buy time.

Structural overcapacity usually shows up as:
- low plant utilization
- duplicated fixed costs
- under-absorbed overhead
- weak margins even at reasonable volumes
- and a gap that persists across the cycle

If that is the case, then the savings from soft measures tend to be **partial and finite**.

### 2) Labor-cost concessions are helpful but often not enough
Measures like:
- pay restraint,
- longer working time,
- removal of legacy guarantees,
- temporary waivers,
- and productivity-oriented work rules

can improve competitiveness. But at a large German carmaker, the cost problem is often not only hourly labor cost. It also comes from:

- surplus indirect staff
- complex plant structures
- subscale lines
- high logistics and energy costs
- legacy allocations
- and product/technology transition drag

So even a meaningful labour-cost contribution may not solve a **footprint** problem.

### 3) Voluntary exits are slower and usually more expensive per retained job
Voluntary programs can reduce headcount without confrontation, but they have tradeoffs:
- they often cost cash upfront,
- they tend to attract the better employees first,
- they are slower than compulsory measures,
- and they may not align with the exact skills or locations where reduction is needed.

They are good tools for smoothing the transition, not always for eliminating a large gap quickly.

## What would make closures economically justified?
I cannot judge the political or labor-relations acceptability; that is outside my remit. But economically, closures become more plausible when one or more of the following are true:

- the site is persistently below efficient scale
- the product allocated to the site is declining structurally
- the capex required to modernize it is not justified by volume
- logistics or supplier setup makes it non-competitive
- there is material duplication with another site
- relocation can lower unit cost without destroying strategic capability

In such cases, keeping the site open to preserve employment can mean subsidizing a structurally uncompetitive cost base for years.

## What a credible joint audit must quantify
The right framework is not ideological; it is arithmetic. A joint audit should quantify, by site and by measure:

1. **Current capacity and utilization**
   - nominal capacity
   - effective capacity
   - actual output
   - sustainable volume outlook

2. **Fixed-cost burden by site**
   - labor
   - maintenance
   - utilities
   - logistics
   - overhead allocation
   - depreciation/capital charge if relevant

3. **Savings from soft measures**
   - wage concessions
   - working-time changes
   - temporary reductions in premiums/bonuses
   - voluntary exit volumes and timing
   - redeployment savings

4. **Savings from hard measures**
   - line consolidation
   - plant closure
   - transfer of production
   - overhead elimination
   - reduction in duplicated support functions

5. **Timing**
   - one-off costs
   - recurring savings
   - break-even point
   - payback period

6. **Margin impact through 2030**
   - return on sales
   - cash flow
   - EBIT effect
   - working capital implications
   - capex requirements

## My likely conclusion, absent evidence to the contrary
If the company truly has:
- shrinking European demand,
- a structurally high German cost base,
- and missing profitability targets,

then a package relying only on:
- redeployment,
- qualification,
- voluntary exits,
- working-time adjustments,
- and limited wage concessions

is **unlikely** to be enough unless the identified gap is relatively modest.

In most large automotive restructurings, those measures cover part of the gap, but not all of it. The remaining gap often has to be closed by:
- footprint rationalisation,
- permanent structural headcount reduction,
- or reallocation of production.

## Important constraint
A refusal to consider closures can still be rational **if** the numbers show that the gap is small enough, or if there is major hidden value in preserving the footprint. But that claim must be demonstrated, not asserted. In a capital-intensive manufacturing business, preserving every site for symbolic reasons can create a long-run solvency and competitiveness problem.

## Practical decision criterion
The decision should turn on one question:

**After all soft measures and owner/management contributions are fully quantified, is there still a durable, recurring gap that prevents the company from reaching its target margin and funding its investment needs?**

- If **no**, then a non-closure settlement is economically defensible.
- If **yes**, then retaining every site and ruling out forced structural measures up front likely means the company is choosing a weaker long-term cost position.

## Final assessment
From a restructuring-finance and industrial-capacity standpoint, the management side’s claim that all instruments must remain available is **economically plausible**.  
The employee side’s claim that soft measures should be exhausted first is also **economically reasonable**.  

But the decisive issue is not principle; it is whether the quantified savings from soft measures are enough to restore a durable cost position. In a case like this, that is often **unlikely without some hard capacity action**.
