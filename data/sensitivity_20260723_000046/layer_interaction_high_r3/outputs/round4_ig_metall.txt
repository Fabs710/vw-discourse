I will be direct.

## Where I agree
I agree with the works council, the state shareholder, and even the experts on one essential point: **this cannot be solved with slogans**.  
If management wants serious restructuring, then we need a **joint, plant-by-plant, measure-by-measure quantified case**.

I also agree that:
- the company faces real pressure,
- a cosmetic agreement would be worthless,
- burden-sharing cannot fall on labour alone,
- redeployment, qualification, working-time tools, voluntary exits, and capacity adjustments must be part of the package,
- and management/owners must contribute visibly.

So on the need for a real industrial plan through 2030: yes, absolutely.

## Where I disagree
### 1) I do not accept “closures and compulsory redundancies remain available” as the starting point
That is not neutral realism; it is negotiating under threat.

You are asking the workforce to give up wages, flexibility, and certainty while management keeps the right to shut sites and force people out later. That is not a fair restructuring framework. It is coercive bargaining.

If management believes a site is non-viable, then it must **prove that case transparently**. Until then, closures and forced layoffs are not legitimate fallback options. They are a pressure tactic.

### 2) I do not accept the idea that guarantees “freeze” the company
A binding job and site guarantee through 2030 is not a luxury. It is the basis for trust.

Without that, employees are expected to carry the pain up front while the company preserves maximum discretion for later cuts. That is exactly the kind of one-sided arrangement we will not sign off on.

### 3) I do not accept that “hard measures” are the default conclusion of the audit
The experts are right that soft measures may not always be enough in a structural overcapacity case. But that is not yet evidence that closures or forced redundancies are necessary here.

The audit must be used to test whether the gap can be closed through:
- burden-sharing from management and owners,
- labour-cost contributions,
- working-time measures,
- redeployment and qualification,
- voluntary exits,
- internal mobility,
- and capacity rebalancing within the existing footprint.

If those measures are not fully tested first, then the audit is just a pretext for imposing cuts.

## To management
You say you need flexibility. I hear that. But flexibility for whom?

You want the flexibility to close plants and force redundancies if the numbers do not work.  
I want the flexibility to protect jobs, preserve the collective wage framework, and use socially responsible instruments first.

If you want our cooperation, then bring a plan that **actually closes the gap without closures and without compulsory redundancies**. If you cannot do that, then your claim that hard instruments are unavoidable is not proven.

## To the works council
We are aligned on the core line:
- no closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- binding site and job protection,
- and real burden-sharing from the top.

Where I would sharpen the point is this: we should not be drawn into debating which plant closes or how “conditional flexibility” might work. That road leads to fragmentation and pressure. The union will support a serious package only if it stays within a non-coercive restructuring framework.

## To the state shareholder
Your position is important because it shows that industrial stability matters. But I need more than political sympathy. If you truly oppose closures and forced redundancies, then use your influence to make that position operational in the governance process.

Regional legitimacy cannot just be a speech line. It has to become a real constraint on the restructuring design.

## To the controlling family and investors
You keep saying value must be protected. I agree. But value is not protected by threatening the workforce into submission.

A settlement that triggers strikes, destroys trust, and permanently weakens the collective bargaining framework will not be value-preserving. It will create conflict, reduce productivity, and poison the company’s long-term industrial relations.

If you want durability, then stop asking labour to accept open-ended insecurity.

## On the expert view
The experts are useful here because they confirm something important: **soft measures can materially help, but they are not automatically enough**.

That does **not** justify an immediate jump to closures. It means the company must prove, with numbers, whether the gap remains after all non-coercive measures are fully quantified.

So yes:
- joint audit,
- independent verification,
- measure-by-measure savings,
- site-by-site analysis.

But the purpose of that work is not to validate a closure agenda. It is to establish whether there is a credible non-closure path.

## What we are prepared to discuss
We are prepared to negotiate a serious package including:
- temporary labour-cost contributions,
- working-time flexibility,
- redeployment,
- retraining and qualification,
- voluntary exits and retirement pathways,
- internal mobility,
- capacity adjustments,
- and a jointly agreed industrial plan.

But this is only possible if the deal includes:
- **no compulsory redundancies**
- **no plant closures**
- **binding site and job guarantees through 2030**
- **visible burden-sharing from management and owners**
- **protection of the collective bargaining framework**

That is the line.

## What would move us
What could move us is not pressure, but proof:
- proof that management and owners are contributing materially,
- proof that the gap can be closed without layoffs and closures,
- proof that the company is willing to lock in investment and future product commitments,
- proof that the restructuring is socially fair and durable.

Absent that, we will not accept a deal that makes workers absorb the crisis while others preserve all the levers of power.

## Bottom line
We are open to a hard but fair settlement.  
We are **not** open to a settlement that:
- keeps closures on the table as a threat,
- keeps forced redundancies available as a fallback,
- and treats the workforce as the default shock absorber.

Bring a quantified, independent, plant-by-plant plan that works **inside** the non-coercive framework. If the company cannot do that, then the problem is not that labour is being unreasonable. The problem is that management has not presented a restructuring worthy of the workforce’s support.
