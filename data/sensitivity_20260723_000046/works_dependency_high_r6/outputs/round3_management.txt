I’ll be equally direct.

## First: what I agree with
I agree with all sides on one point: **this company needs a durable solution, not a short-lived truce**.  
I also agree that future investment in products and technology is essential. Without freeing up funds now, there will be no competitive brand in 2030.

And yes, I agree that any restructuring should use non-terminal tools where they can actually deliver savings:
- working-time flexibility,
- redeployment,
- qualification,
- attrition,
- retirement corridors,
- and a negotiated employee contribution.

So far, there is common ground on the *need* for restructuring and on the *principle* that alternatives should be examined seriously.

## Where I disagree
I disagree with the employee side and the state government on the main point: **you are trying to turn “no closures” and “no compulsory redundancies” into absolute rules before proving they can absorb the savings gap**.

That is not a restructuring strategy. It is a constraint that may well preserve the current structure and cost base, which we cannot afford.

### To the works council
You say plant closures are a choice, not a necessity. I say: if you want to make that claim, then show me a package that is **structurally equivalent** in effect.

Not symbolic savings. Not temporary relief. Not a deal that looks good for 12 months and then leaves us with the same problem.

I need to see:
- a real and durable reduction in overcapacity,
- a meaningful reduction in fixed labour costs,
- and a package that restores margins, not just industrial peace.

A blanket guarantee that all German plants remain open through 2030 is **not acceptable as a starting point** if it prevents us from achieving the required correction. It would freeze a problem we need to solve.

### To the union
You are right that the workforce should not pay alone. I agree.  
But I do **not** accept your framing that temporary wage moderation, bonus restraint, and flexibility are enough if they are not accompanied by deeper structural changes.

Temporary concessions help; they do not, by themselves, solve structural overcapacity.  
We need **lasting cost relief**. That means changing the economics of the German operations in a durable way.

Also, “fair and limited” is not a financial metric. I need a number that materially improves competitiveness.

### To the regional state government
I understand your political responsibility, but a blocking position cannot become a substitute for a solution. If the state wants to preserve industrial employment, then it must support a package that actually restores competitiveness.

I cannot accept a position that effectively says:
- protect every site,
- rule out compulsory redundancies,
- preserve the full job-security framework,
- and still expect the company to become fit for competition.

That does not add up.

### To the family holding and investors
I agree with you on the diagnosis: a cosmetic compromise would be value-destructive.  
But I also need you to be honest about what “shared sacrifice” means. If the burden is only shifted into temporary measures and vague promises, then we are not solving anything.

The company needs a **real structural reset**, not a pause.

## On closures and redundancies
Let me be precise.

I am **not** saying closures are the first choice. They are not.  
But I am saying they must remain on the table unless and until a non-closure package is shown to deliver **equivalent savings at scale**.

The same applies to compulsory redundancies. I prefer to avoid them. But I cannot remove them from the toolbox before the alternatives have been proven sufficient.

That is the real issue here: not ideology, but whether the numbers work.

## What would move me
I will move if — and only if — the employee side and co-determination stakeholders bring forward a package that is:
- **large enough**,
- **durable enough**,
- and **credible enough** to close the savings gap without closures.

That package would need to include, at minimum:
- meaningful and lasting labour-cost relief,
- real capacity reduction,
- internal redeployment and qualification,
- voluntary exit and retirement tools,
- and a binding implementation mechanism.

If those measures can truly deliver the required savings, I am prepared to look seriously at avoiding closures.

If they cannot, then I will not pretend otherwise.

## What I need next
We are still missing the basic facts. So I want the next round to answer three questions concretely:

1. **What exact savings gap must be closed, and by when?**  
2. **What exact non-closure measures close that gap structurally?**  
3. **What exact employee contribution is durable enough to matter?**

Until those are answered, the discussion is still stuck at the level of red lines.

## My bottom line
I am not here to dismantle the company. I am here to restore competitiveness.

That means:
- real capacity reduction,
- real labour-cost reduction,
- and real room for investment to 2030.

If the other stakeholders can deliver that without closures and without forced redundancies, I will consider it.

If not, then stronger measures remain necessary — because a cosmetic compromise that leaves the cost base uncompetitive is not acceptable.
