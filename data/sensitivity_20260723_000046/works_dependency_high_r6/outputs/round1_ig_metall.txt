We share the same core line on the essentials: **no plant closures and no compulsory redundancies**. That is not negotiable from the union’s side either.

I also agree that the crisis cannot be solved by simply forcing the workforce to pay for management mistakes, market shifts, and years of strategic misjudgment. If the company wants industrial peace, it needs to present a credible future for the German sites, not just a cost-cutting program.

That said, I want to be precise about what can be part of a real settlement:

- **Yes** to capacity reductions where they are done responsibly and without breaking the industrial base.
- **Yes** to working-time solutions, redeployment, qualification, and temporary flexibility.
- **Yes** to a fair employee contribution if, and only if, management and owners also contribute in a visible and binding way.
- **Yes** to serious investment commitments and product guarantees that secure the future of the plants.

What we will not accept is a “transformation” that in practice means:
- plant closures in Germany,
- forced layoffs,
- open-ended insecurity,
- or a permanent erosion of the collective wage framework.

If management is serious, then the burden must be shared. That means not only asking employees for concessions, but also putting real skin in the game on the management and ownership side. Otherwise the workforce is being asked to carry all the pain while others keep control and avoid consequences.

So our position is clear: **we are prepared to negotiate a durable, jointly borne restructuring — but only on the basis of site security, job security, and fairness.** If the proposal remains built around closures and compulsory redundancies, then we will oppose it with all available means, including industrial action.
