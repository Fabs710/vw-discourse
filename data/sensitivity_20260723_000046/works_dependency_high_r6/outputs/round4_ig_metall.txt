I will be equally direct.

## Where I agree
I agree with the works council on the essentials:

- the company needs a durable restructuring,
- the current cost base has to come down,
- the German sites need future investment,
- and a prolonged conflict would damage everyone.

I also agree with the expert assessments on one important point: **temporary measures alone are not enough if the structural gap is large**. If the economics are not fixed, we are just buying time.

## Where I disagree
What I do **not** accept is the attempt to turn that factual point into a blanket justification for closures or forced layoffs.

That is still not proven. It is still a choice, not an inevitability.

### 1) On plant closures
I do **not** accept plant closures in Germany as the normal restructuring tool here.

Not as a starting point.  
Not as a pressure tactic.  
Not as a “fallback” before the alternatives have been fully tested.

If management wants to argue that closures are unavoidable, then it must show, plant by plant and measure by measure:

- the exact savings gap,
- what each non-closure instrument contributes,
- where the gap remains,
- and why the gap cannot be closed without destroying sites.

Until that proof exists, closures remain a threat, not a necessity.

### 2) On compulsory redundancies
I do **not** accept compulsory redundancies as the response to this crisis.

We have not exhausted:
- redeployment,
- qualification,
- internal mobility,
- retirement corridors,
- attrition,
- working-time reductions,
- voluntary exits,
- and negotiated flexibility.

If management still reaches for forced layoffs before those tools are fully used, then it is choosing confrontation over restructuring.

### 3) On job security through 2030
I do **not** accept the claim that binding job and site security through 2030 is incompatible with transformation.

What is incompatible with transformation is asking employees to carry the burden alone while keeping their future uncertain.

If the company wants concessions, it must give real security in return:
- no forced layoffs,
- no closures,
- binding investment,
- and enforceable commitments.

## Where I differ from the works council
I support your red lines on closures and forced layoffs. But I want to be clear that the union will not accept a settlement that erodes collective wage standards over time under the label of “flexibility.”

So yes, we can discuss:
- temporary wage moderation,
- bonus restraint,
- working-time flexibility,
- capacity adjustments,
- voluntary exits,
- and qualification-linked deployment.

But those concessions must be:
- time-limited,
- reversible,
- and matched by binding guarantees.

No open-ended austerity.  
No permanent wage dumping.  
No collective agreement rewritten into a one-way sacrifice.

## On the expert input
The finance experts are right that closures can be stronger on steady-state fixed-cost removal and that a hybrid can be more realistic in a veto-heavy governance structure.

But that does **not** mean the labour side should concede closures in advance.

It means management has to do the hard work of proving that a non-closure package cannot close the savings gap.

And let’s be honest: the execution risk of closures here is not a footnote. It is central.

You are talking about:
- co-determination vetoes,
- state influence,
- warning strikes,
- supply chain disruption,
- lost production,
- and long-term damage to trust and skills.

That risk must be priced in. Once you do that, the case for closures is much less clean than capital keeps claiming.

## On the facilitator proposals
### Proposal 1: time-limited no-closure moratorium with review triggers
I do **not** support this as a substitute for a real agreement.

A moratorium is not security. It is just deferred pressure.

If workers concede now and closures come later after the resistance is softened, then the review clause has been used to disarm the workforce, not protect it.

### Proposal 2: hybrid package with targeted permanent capacity rationalization
This is closer to reality than a blanket closure program, but I want to be very clear: a “hybrid” must not become a backdoor closure strategy.

If “targeted rationalization” means silently hollowing out sites and then declaring them nonviable later, then the union will oppose it.

If a hybrid is to be discussed at all, it must preserve:
- no forced layoffs,
- strong site protection,
- and a credible job framework.

### Proposal 3: binding labour contribution matched by binding investment and protection
This is the only direction I would even consider — but only if the protection is real.

That means:
- no plant closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- binding job and site security,
- real investment commitments,
- and collective bargaining protections intact.

Without that, asking for employee contributions is just asking workers to finance uncertainty.

## What the union is prepared to consider
We are still open to a serious negotiated package that includes:

- temporary wage moderation,
- bonus restraint,
- working-time flexibility,
- redeployment,
- qualification,
- retirement corridors,
- voluntary exits,
- and capacity adjustments **within existing sites**.

But only if the package includes:

1. **no plant closures in Germany**,  
2. **no compulsory redundancies**,  
3. **binding job and site security**,  
4. **binding investment commitments**,  
5. **fair contributions from management and owners**,  
6. **preservation of the collective wage framework**.

That is the minimum basis for a fair settlement.

## What we will not accept
Let me make this unmistakable:

- No closures.
- No forced layoffs.
- No dismantling of the collective wage agreement.
- No “shared sacrifice” where employees bear the burden and capital keeps all options open.
- No restructuring that reduces the German base by stealth.

If management wants a deal, it has to be a real bargain, not a unilateral extraction.

## What is still missing
The decisive missing element is still the same: **decision-grade numbers**.

We need:
- exact savings required,
- exact savings from each measure,
- exact one-time costs,
- exact contributions from labour, management, and owners,
- and exact enforceable protections.

So I repeat the request: bring the quantified bridge. Show how the alternative package closes the gap without closures or forced layoffs.

## My bottom line
The union will **not** retreat from its core position.

We are prepared to contribute fairly.  
We are prepared to negotiate hard measures.  
We are prepared to help deliver a durable restructuring.

But we are **not** prepared to carry all the pain while management and owners preserve the right to close plants and impose layoffs later.

If that is the proposal, then there is no fair settlement.
