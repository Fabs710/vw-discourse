We agree with management and the family holding on the **economic diagnosis**: this cannot be a cosmetic settlement. If the company comes out of this with the same structurally uncompetitive cost base, shareholders will simply pay for delayed decline.

We also agree with the state and labour representatives on one important point: **prolonged conflict is value-destructive**. A long strike, supervisory-board deadlock, or endless renegotiation would damage margins, cash flow, and investor confidence. Deal certainty matters.

That said, our position is still straightforward:

## Where we agree
- The company needs **durable** cost reduction.
- The turnaround must create room for **future investment**.
- Any employee contribution should be **matched by real commitments** from capital and management.
- Alternatives to immediate closures or compulsory redundancies should be explored **if** they deliver equivalent savings.

## Where we disagree
### 1) “No closures” as a blanket rule
We do **not** accept an absolute no-closure principle.

If plant closures are necessary to restore competitiveness and capacity discipline, they cannot be ruled out in advance. Shareholders care about the economic result, not the symbolism. An outcome that protects every site but leaves margins impaired is not acceptable.

### 2) “No compulsory redundancies” as an absolute
We do **not** insist on forced layoffs as a principle, but we also will not support a framework that makes meaningful restructuring impossible.

If the same cost reset can be achieved through voluntary exits, attrition, redeployment, early retirement, working-time tools, and negotiated concessions, that is preferable. But if those tools do not close the gap, management must retain the ability to act.

### 3) Job guarantees to 2030
A blanket guarantee for all jobs to 2030 is not compatible with a serious structural reset if overcapacity is real. It may feel stabilizing, but it can also freeze an uncompetitive structure and defer the burden into later years. That would be worse for shareholders.

## What would move us
We are open to a **non-closure package** only if it is:
- **quantified**,
- **durable**,
- **large enough to matter**,
- and **credible in implementation**.

That means we would need to see:
1. the exact savings gap,
2. the exact labour contribution,
3. the exact owner/management contribution,
4. the exact investment commitments,
5. and the exact governance/enforcement mechanism.

Without that, claims of “alternatives” remain just claims.

## On the employees’ position
We understand the workforce wants protection. But a package built on:
- no closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- no meaningful wage or flexibility contribution,
- and full preservation of legacy guarantees

would be value-destructive. We would not support that. It would leave the company structurally impaired and likely depress the share price further.

## On the state government
We respect the political and regional constraints, but the blocking-minority role cannot be allowed to turn into a veto on all serious restructuring. If the state wants to preserve industrial employment, it should support a package that restores competitiveness, not one that preserves the status quo.

## Our bottom line
We are prepared to support **tough restructuring** if it credibly restores margins and protects future investment. We are also open to **alternatives to closures** if they deliver equivalent savings.

But we are **not** open to a cosmetic compromise.

So our standard is simple:
- **Yes** to durable structural cost reduction.
- **Yes** to non-closure solutions if they are economically equivalent.
- **No** to preserving uncompetitive structures.
- **No** to a settlement that leaves margins structurally impaired.

If the parties can table a package with real numbers and binding mechanisms, we can support it. If not, we would side with whatever delivers the necessary cost reset.
