Below is a restructuring-finance comparison framework for the three paths the parties have asked about. Because no plant-level cost base, headcount, capex, or volume data has been provided, this cannot be a decision-grade model yet. I can, however, set out the economically correct structure, the likely direction of outcomes, and the key breakpoints.

## 1) First, the governing financial logic

For a large automotive manufacturing group with German plants, the choice is usually between:

1. **Non-closure package**
   - working-time changes
   - wage moderation / bonus restraint
   - attrition and retirement corridors
   - redeployment and qualification
   - internal capacity reduction without site exit

2. **Hybrid package**
   - non-closure measures plus
   - targeted permanent capacity exit at the weakest sites / functions
   - limited plant rationalization rather than a broad closure program

3. **Closure-based restructuring**
   - plant closure(s)
   - larger fixed-cost removal
   - larger one-time cash costs and execution risk

The crucial point: **steady-state savings are not the same as net present value**. The highest nominal savings option can still be inferior if it carries very large one-time costs, long delay, or a high probability of disruption.

---

## 2) What can be said with reasonable confidence

### A. Non-closure packages
These usually have:
- **lower upfront cash cost**
- **faster implementation if negotiated**
- but **less certain and less durable savings**

Typical strengths:
- avoid immediate severance and shutdown costs
- preserve industrial peace if agreed
- reduce execution risk if labour accepts them

Typical weaknesses:
- savings can decay over time
- may not remove enough structural fixed cost
- can be too slow if the business needs immediate margin repair
- may not solve permanent overcapacity

### B. Closure-based restructuring
Usually has:
- **highest permanent fixed-cost removal**
- but also
- **highest one-time cost**
- **highest conflict risk**
- **highest operational disruption risk**

Typical one-time costs:
- severance / social plan costs
- decommissioning
- write-downs / impairment
- transfer and relocation costs
- logistics and supply-chain reconfiguration
- possible customer disruption and production loss

### C. Hybrid
Often the most rational in a veto-heavy governance setting because it:
- captures some permanent structural savings
- avoids the full shock of a blanket closure program
- may be more executable than a pure closure strategy
- can be structured as a negotiated exchange

In practice, a hybrid often wins on **risk-adjusted NPV** even if it does not maximize headline savings.

## 3) Plant-by-plant comparison: how to think about it

Without actual plant data, the proper model is not “which side is right,” but:

### For each plant/function:
- current fixed cost
- variable cost per unit
- capacity utilization
- logistics adjacency
- labor productivity
- wage level / collective agreement structure
- future product allocation
- capex needed to remain competitive
- redundancy of the site within the network
- social/industrial disruption risk
- closure cost
- ongoing subsidy or support needs, if any

### Decision rule:
A plant becomes a closure candidate when:
- it has structurally poor economics,
- low strategic adjacency,
- high future capex needs,
- and poor chance of achieving competitiveness through internal measures.

A plant becomes a retention candidate when:
- it has good product adjacency,
- high productivity potential,
- and can be brought to target economics through labor and process measures.

## 5) Execution-risk adjustment is essential

The parties have correctly highlighted this.

### What must be discounted:
- strike days
- warning strikes
- supplier disruption
- lost vehicle output
- delayed model launches
- legal/co-determination delays
- management distraction
- reputational damage affecting labor relations and recruiting

A closure program may show a large gross savings number, but after:
- delay,
- severance,
- production loss,
- and implementation friction,

its **risk-adjusted NPV** can fall materially.

### Important implication
In a governance structure with:
- co-determination vetoes,
- family control,
- state blocking rights,
- and organized labour resistance,

the probability of clean closure execution is materially lower than in a normal shareholder-controlled company. That means:
- pure closure logic is economically weaker than it looks on paper,
- and hybrid solutions often dominate on expected value.

## 6) Likely breakpoint logic

A non-closure package becomes inferior when one or more of the following are true:

1. **The savings gap is too large**
   - labour flexibility and working-time changes cannot bridge it

2. **The measures are too temporary**
   - savings expire before the cost base is structurally fixed

3. **The plants are genuinely uneconomic**
   - no realistic path to productivity competitiveness

4. **The one-time costs of preserving sites become excessive**
   - repeated concessions, support, and deferred restructuring exceed closure costs

5. **Execution risk makes the “soft” package uncertain**
   - if non-closure savings depend on continuous labor compliance and management discipline, they may be less reliable than the parties assume

A hybrid becomes inferior to closure when:
- the hybrid preserves too much excess capacity,
- yet still triggers much of the conflict cost,
- so you end up with neither full savings nor full industrial peace.

## 8) My provisional financial judgment

On the facts provided, and without plant data:

- **A pure closure strategy is likely to maximize gross structural savings, but also carries the highest execution risk and highest one-time cost.**
- **A pure non-closure strategy is likely to have the lowest upfront cost but the weakest certainty of closing a large structural savings gap.**
- **A hybrid is the most plausible risk-adjusted solution** in this governance environment, because it can combine:
  - meaningful permanent capacity reduction,
  - significant labor contribution,
  - and lower conflict intensity than a full closure program.

That does **not** mean the hybrid is guaranteed to work. It means it is the option most likely to survive the combination of:
- economics,
- governance,
- labour resistance,
- and implementation constraints.

---

## 9) Bottom line

From a restructuring-finance perspective:

- **Closures are economically strongest on permanent cost removal**
- **Non-closure measures are strongest on preserving industrial peace and avoiding upfront cash cost**
- **Hybrid is usually strongest on risk-adjusted value**

The decisive question is not philosophical. It is:

> Can the non-closure package, after realistic execution-risk discounting, produce enough durable savings to restore margins and fund future investment?

If the answer is no, then the debate shifts to **how much targeted permanent capacity exit is necessary**, not whether restructuring is needed.
