Let me respond directly, because we are now at the point where slogans have to give way to arithmetic.

## First: what I agree with
I agree with everyone on one basic point: **this company needs a durable solution, not a short-lived truce**.

I also agree that the future of the German sites matters, and that investment in future products and technology is essential. If we do not free up funds now, we will not have a competitive company in 2030.

And yes: I agree that alternatives to immediate closures and compulsory redundancies should be examined seriously. I have said that from the beginning.

## But I do not accept the core premise of the employee side
What I do **not** accept is the idea that management is simply “choosing” closure and layoffs out of convenience.

We are facing:
- structural overcapacity,
- a shrinking European market,
- and German factory costs far above target.

That is not a rhetorical claim. That is the economic reality we have to fix.

So if the workforce says “no closures, no redundancies, no change to guarantees, no erosion of the cost base,” then I have to say plainly: **that is not a restructuring proposal**. It is a demand to preserve the current structure while expecting the company to become competitive anyway. That will not work.

## To the works council
You say:
- all German plants must stay open,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- job security to 2030,
- and binding guarantees.

That combination is **not compatible** with the scale of correction we need unless you bring forward a package that produces genuinely equivalent savings.

And I need to stress this: “capacity adjustments” and “working-time flexibility” are not enough by themselves unless they are large, durable, and verifiable. We are not looking for a cosmetic settlement that merely delays the cost problem.

If you want me to move away from plant closures, then show me a package that delivers:
- substantial capacity reduction,
- durable labour-cost reduction,
- and structural improvement in margins.

Not promises. Not intentions. Numbers.

## To the union
You are right about one thing: the burden cannot fall only on employees. It must be shared.

But you are wrong if you think the company can get to the required savings through temporary measures alone:
- temporary wage moderation,
- bonus waivers,
- working-time changes,
- retirement corridors.

Those may help. They do not, on their own, solve a structural overcapacity problem.

So I will not pretend that a one-year or two-year concession package is enough. I need **lasting** cost relief. That means a real reduction in the fixed cost base, not just a deferral.

And I will also say this clearly: if the union wants to avoid sharper measures, then it has to come forward with a contribution that is large enough to matter. “Fair and limited” is not a financial metric. We need something that changes the economics.

## To the regional state government
I understand your political responsibility, but I have to be equally clear: **a blocking position is not a restructuring strategy**.

You say closures should be the last resort. I can accept that principle. What I cannot accept is a position that effectively removes closures from the toolbox before the alternatives have been shown to work at scale.

If the state wants to help preserve industrial employment, then it should help secure a package that actually restores competitiveness — not one that merely protects the appearance of stability.

## To the controlling family
I agree with you on one essential point: we cannot accept a slow decline masked as social peace.

Where I differ from the employee side is that I do not believe long-term enterprise value is protected by freezing the current structure. It is protected by making the company fit for the market again.

So yes, owners and management must contribute. But the most important contribution right now is to change the economics of the German operations. That requires structural action, not symbolic support.

## On the trade-off
Let me be very explicit:

I am **open** to avoiding plant closures if — and only if — the employee side presents a package that is structurally equivalent in effect. That means:
- meaningful and durable labor-cost reductions,
- genuine and lasting capacity reductions,
- no forced redundancies,
- and a level of savings that restores competitiveness.

If that can be demonstrated, I will look at it seriously.

But I will not accept:
- no closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- no real wage contribution,
- and no meaningful reduction in overcapacity.

That combination leaves the company uncompetitive, and I will not endorse it.

## What I need next
If we are going to move forward, I want the next round to answer three questions concretely:

1. **How much savings are required, by when?**  
2. **What exact alternative measures deliver those savings structurally?**  
3. **What binding employee contribution is on the table, and how durable is it?**

Until those are answered, we are not negotiating a viable restructuring. We are just exchanging red lines.

## My bottom line
I am not here to dismantle the company for the sake of it. I am here to restore competitiveness.

That means:
- real capacity reduction,
- real labor-cost reduction,
- and real room for investment to 2030.

If the workforce can help deliver that without closures, then I am prepared to consider it.

If not, then the stronger measures remain on the table — because I will not sign up to a cosmetic compromise that leaves the cost base uncompetitive.
