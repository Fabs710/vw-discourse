I’ll respond plainly.

## Where I agree
I agree with the moderators and with the experts on one essential point: **we need decision-grade numbers, not slogans**.  
I also agree that a durable solution must be **structural**, not temporary. And I agree that prolonged conflict would damage the company, its customers, and the investment case.

I further agree that a **non-closure package can work in principle**. The finance experts are right on that. Working-time changes, redeployment, attrition, retirement corridors, bonus restraint, and productivity measures can all contribute.

## Where I disagree
I do **not** agree with the works council and union when they turn **“no closures”**, **“no compulsory redundancies”**, and **“job security through 2030”** into absolute preconditions.

That is not a restructuring framework. It is a demand to preserve the current structure while asking management to somehow make the economics work anyway.

That is not realistic.

### To the works council
You say closures are a choice, not a necessity. That may be emotionally satisfying, but it is not yet economically proven.  
You keep repeating that alternatives may be sufficient. Fine — **show it**.

Right now, I still do not see a package that:
- removes enough fixed cost,
- cuts capacity enough,
- and does so quickly and durably enough
to restore the brand’s return on sales.

A company with structurally too much capacity cannot be healed by goodwill and internal transfers alone.

### To the union
You say temporary concessions are acceptable if they are time-limited and matched by guarantees. I hear that. But temporary concessions do **not** solve the core problem if the underlying cost base remains too high.

We need **lasting** improvement in labor cost and capacity.  
Not just a one-year breathing space.

If the offer is:
- wage moderation for a limited period,
- bonus restraint,
- some flexibility,
- and a promise to revisit later,

then that is **not enough**. It postpones the problem. It does not solve it.

### To the regional state government
You keep framing closures as politically unacceptable. I understand your role, but as an anchor shareholder you also have a responsibility to the long-term health of the company.  
A blocking position is not a license to freeze all industrial rationalization.

If the state insists that every German site must be preserved regardless of economics, then it is not acting as a responsible owner. It is protecting sentiment at the expense of competitiveness.

### To the controlling family holding
You are right about one thing: a cosmetic compromise would destroy value.  
But I need the same clarity from you that you demand from labour.

If you want to rule out closures only after a credible alternative is shown, then let’s be honest: we are not there yet. So closures must remain on the table.  
And if non-closure measures turn out to be too slow or too weak, then yes, stronger measures are necessary.

### To institutional investors
You are correct that this is a value test. I agree.  
But value is not protected by preserving excess capacity and calling that stability. Value is protected by restoring margins, reducing fixed cost, and freeing investment capacity.

A “hybrid” is only acceptable if it is **economically real**. If it merely spreads pain without actually fixing the cost base, then it is not a solution.

## On the expert assessments
The experts have helped clarify the issue:
- **Closures**: strongest on permanent fixed-cost removal, but high upfront cost and high conflict risk.
- **Non-closure**: lower upfront cost, but slower and more uncertain.
- **Hybrid**: often best risk-adjusted outcome in a veto-heavy governance setting.

That last point is important. It does **not** mean “no closures forever.” It means a selective, disciplined restructuring may be the most executable route.

And that is exactly where I stand:
- I am **not** demanding a full closure program as the only route.
- But I am **also not** accepting an absolute no-closure rule.

If the non-closure package is genuinely equivalent, show it.  
If it is not, then we need some permanent capacity exit.

## My position
Let me state it clearly:

- I am prepared to consider a **hybrid package**.
- I am prepared to consider **targeted permanent capacity rationalization**.
- I am prepared to consider **non-closure measures** if they are quantified, binding, and large enough.
- I am **not** prepared to accept:
  - a blanket no-closure rule,
  - a blanket no-layoff rule,
  - or a guarantee that blocks necessary restructuring.

## What I need next
We are still missing the decisive bridge. I want a plant-by-plant and function-by-function comparison of:

1. current fixed costs and overcapacity,
2. savings from working-time, redeployment, attrition, retirement, and wage measures,
3. one-time costs of closure vs. hybrid vs. non-closure,
4. timing of savings,
5. execution risk and strike risk,
6. the resulting NPV.

If the non-closure route is truly superior, it should be able to survive that comparison.  
If it cannot, then we should stop pretending otherwise.

## My bottom line
I will not support a cosmetic deal that leaves the company uncompetitive.

I will support a **serious restructuring** that:
- cuts overcapacity,
- reduces labor cost materially,
- improves margins durably,
- and protects the company’s investment capacity to 2030.

If that requires targeted closures or other permanent capacity exits, then I will not rule them out.  
If a credible non-closure package can do the same job, I am open to it.

But what I will **not** do is accept a settlement that preserves the current cost base and calls that transformation.
