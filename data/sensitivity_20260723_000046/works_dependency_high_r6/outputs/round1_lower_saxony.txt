I will be equally clear from the outset:

**The regional state government will not support a restructuring that closes German plants, forces mass layoffs, or strips the workforce of its long-standing protections without a convincing, jointly negotiated alternative.**

I agree with management on one point: the company cannot afford a purely symbolic solution. If costs are structurally too high, then we need structural answers. But I reject the implication that the only credible answer is plant closure and compulsory redundancies.

### Where I agree
I do agree that:
- the company needs a durable competitiveness solution,
- the current cost base must come down,
- and future investment in products and technology is essential.

I also agree with the works council and the union that any employee contribution must be **matched by real commitments from management and owners**. The burden cannot fall on one side alone.

### Where I disagree
I do **not** accept that “real restructuring” necessarily means closing German plants. That is a very narrow definition of credibility, and it is politically and economically dangerous.

This company is not just another balance-sheet exercise. It is a major industrial anchor for the region, with supply chains, skilled employment, and social stability tied to these sites. Once a plant is shut, the damage is often irreversible:
- jobs are lost directly,
- suppliers lose volumes,
- regional know-how erodes,
- and the company weakens its long-term industrial base.

That is precisely why plant closures must be the last resort, not the starting point.

### On the works council and union position
The workforce representatives are right to insist that this cannot become a one-sided rescue plan paid for by employees alone. Their red lines are understandable and legitimate.

At the same time, I would encourage them to be serious about alternatives that can genuinely produce savings:
- capacity reductions through smarter allocation,
- working-time flexibility,
- qualification and redeployment,
- temporary wage moderation,
- and targeted adjustments in legacy guarantees.

Those tools are not “cosmetic” if they are done at scale and with binding commitments on investment and future product allocation.

### On management’s position
Management is right to warn against simply preserving the status quo. But I must say plainly: **management cannot present closures as the default solution and then call everyone else unreasonable when they resist.**

A credible restructuring must show:
1. how savings are achieved without destroying the industrial base,
2. how the company invests in future products,
3. how jobs are protected as far as possible,
4. and how the burden is shared fairly across capital, management, and labour.

### The state’s line
As a shareholder with a blocking minority and board seats, I will use that role responsibly. That means:
- I will not agree to any plan that guts regional employment,
- I will not accept a settlement that sidelines co-determination,
- and I will not support a restructuring that turns the state into a passive bystander while strategic decisions are imposed from above.

I am prepared to back a negotiated restructuring that is tough but fair, and that preserves sites and jobs while restoring competitiveness. But that requires management to move beyond a closure logic and engage seriously with an alternative package.

### What I am looking for
If there is to be agreement, it must include:
- **no forced redundancies**,
- **no plant closures as part of the baseline plan**,
- binding **investment commitments** for future technologies and products,
- a fair **employee contribution**,
- and a restructuring that keeps the company as a **regional industrial anchor**.

If management can table such a package, I will engage constructively.  
If not, I will not be the actor that enables plant closures and social damage in the name of “competitiveness.”
