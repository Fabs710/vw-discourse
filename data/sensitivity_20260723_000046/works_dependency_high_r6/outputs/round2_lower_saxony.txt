I will be equally direct.

The state government recognizes the hard economic reality management has described: there is a structural competitiveness problem, and the company needs a durable solution, not a cosmetic one. On that point, I agree.

But I **do not** agree that the only credible path is to keep plant closures and compulsory redundancies permanently on the table as the default threat. That is not a neutral economic necessity; it is a choice with severe regional consequences. And for us, those consequences are not abstract.

## Where I agree
I agree with management, the family holding, and investors that:

- the company needs **real structural savings**,
- the current cost base must come down,
- and future investment in products and technology must be secured.

I also agree with the works council and union that:

- the workforce must not be asked to carry the burden alone,
- any employee contribution must be matched by binding commitments from management and owners,
- and industrial peace has real value if it is based on fairness and durability.

## Where I disagree

### On plant closures
I must be very clear: **plant closures in Germany must not be the baseline solution**.  
They are not a normal restructuring instrument in a company that is a regional industrial anchor and has a special governance structure with co-determination and a state blocking minority.

If closures are treated as the starting point, then the burden of proof is being placed in the wrong place. Management has to show why alternatives do not work — not simply assume that closure is the cleanest way to force acceptance.

I am not saying closures can never be discussed in the abstract. I am saying they are **politically, socially, and economically the last resort**, not the opening position.

### On compulsory redundancies
I also reject compulsory redundancies as the first response.  
A serious restructuring should first exhaust:
- redeployment,
- working-time adjustments,
- qualification,
- natural attrition,
- early retirement corridors,
- and negotiated flexibility.

If those tools are insufficient, then we are still not at the point where one should jump to forced layoffs. The company has not yet demonstrated that every non-destructive alternative has been used seriously and at scale.

### On “no closure/no layoff” being incompatible with restructuring
Management keeps saying that a real restructuring cannot be compatible with site protection and job security. I do not accept that framing.

A restructuring can absolutely be tough and still preserve the company’s industrial base if it is designed properly. The question is not whether savings are needed. The question is **how they are achieved** and **who bears the risk**.

The regional state government will not endorse a plan that defines competitiveness purely as the right to cut jobs and shut sites.

## What I can support
I am prepared to support a negotiated package if it meets all of the following conditions:

1. **No baseline plant closures in Germany**
   - Closures are not the starting point of the plan.
   - If any site changes are contemplated, they must be justified only after all alternatives have been exhausted and must be subject to the strongest possible safeguards.

2. **No compulsory redundancies**
   - Workforce reduction must be managed through voluntary and social instruments first.

3. **A serious, quantified employee contribution**
   - Yes, the workforce may need to contribute through working-time flexibility, temporary wage moderation, or adjustments to legacy arrangements.
   - But that contribution must be clearly time-limited, proportionate, and matched by commitments from capital and management.

4. **Binding investment commitments**
   - We need written commitments on future products, technologies, and allocation of work to German sites.
   - Otherwise the restructuring becomes a pure cutback exercise.

5. **Protection of co-determination and the state’s role**
   - No arrangement that sidelines the supervisory board, weakens employee voice, or turns the special statute into a formality.

## To management
You asked for numbers and mechanisms. That is fair. But numbers must not be used to force a predetermined outcome.

If you want my support, put forward a package that shows:
- the exact savings gap,
- the exact contribution expected from labour,
- the exact contribution from owners and management,
- and the exact investment package for the German sites.

But do not present “no closure” as unreasonable while treating closure as the only serious instrument. That is not a balanced negotiation.

## To the works council and union
I hear your red lines, and I understand them.

I cannot, however, simply endorse an unlimited guarantee if the economic case shows a genuine need for adjustment. What I can support is a **hard no to forced redundancies and a strong preference for site protection**, combined with serious negotiated flexibility if it preserves the industrial base.

That means:
- yes to capacity adjustments,
- yes to working-time solutions,
- yes to qualification and redeployment,
- yes to a fair employee contribution,
- but only if the outcome preserves German employment and gives the workforce a real future.

## To the family holding and investors
I appreciate the concern for competitiveness and enterprise value. But long-term value is not protected by hollowing out the company’s regional base. A settlement that destroys trust, destabilizes labour relations, and damages the company’s role in the region will also create long-term value destruction.

You are right that the answer cannot be cosmetic.  
You are wrong if you think the only alternative is to threaten closures until resistance breaks.

## The state’s line
Because we have a special statute and blocking rights, I will use them responsibly and explicitly:

- I will **not** support any restructuring that guts regional employment.
- I will **not** agree to a deal that removes the state’s co-determination or veto role in practice.
- I will **not** accept a plan that makes the state a passive witness to industrial withdrawal.

But I **will** support a negotiated restructuring that:
- preserves the German sites as far as possible,
- avoids forced redundancies,
- secures future investment,
- and restores competitiveness through a shared burden.

## What I need next
The next step must be concrete:

1. Management should quantify the savings target and the time horizon.
2. The workforce side should specify what voluntary and flexible tools it is prepared to use.
3. Owners should specify what binding investment and governance commitments they will make.
4. And everyone should stop treating plant closure as the only serious option before the alternatives have been properly tested.

If that is the basis, I am ready to broker a tough but fair settlement.  
If not, then I will not be moved by repetition of “competitiveness” as a justification for dismantling regional industrial capacity.
