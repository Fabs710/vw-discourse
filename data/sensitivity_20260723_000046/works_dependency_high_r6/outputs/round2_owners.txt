I will be direct.

I agree with the broad diagnosis from management and the capital side: **this company needs a durable structural reset, not a symbolic peace deal**. If we preserve an uncompetitive cost base and simply defer the problem, we will destroy value over time. That is unacceptable for the family holding as long-term controlling owner.

At the same time, I do **not** accept the labor side’s attempt to turn “no closures” and “no compulsory redundancies” into absolute starting points. That may be understandable from their perspective, but it is not a sufficient basis for governing a large industrial company under real market pressure.

## Where I agree
- The company needs **real and lasting cost reduction**.
- Future investment in products and technology is essential.
- A prolonged conflict would be value-destructive.
- The burden cannot fall on employees alone.
- Alternatives to closures should be explored seriously if they are structurally credible.

## Where I disagree

### 1) “No plant closures” as a blanket rule
I cannot support an absolute ban on closures.

If a site is structurally redundant or permanently uneconomic, keeping it open forever is not stewardship — it is value erosion. The family holding will not endorse a framework that rules out necessary industrial restructuring in advance.

That said, I am also **not** endorsing closures as the first choice. They are a last resort, and management must prove why alternatives do not work at scale.

### 2) “No compulsory redundancies” as an absolute
I am open to avoiding compulsory redundancies if the same savings can be achieved through voluntary exits, redeployment, qualification, attrition, working-time changes, and retirement corridors.

But I will not accept a settlement that makes meaningful workforce adjustment impossible. If the alternatives do not close the gap, management must retain the ability to act.

### 3) “Job security to 2030” for all jobs and all sites
A blanket guarantee through 2030 for every job and every German plant is not realistic if the company truly has structural overcapacity.

I understand the desire for certainty, but an unconditional guarantee can become a mechanism for freezing a broken structure. That would harm the enterprise and, in the end, everyone connected to it.

## Where I am prepared to engage
I would support a tough but socially responsible package if it delivers all of the following:

- **substantial and durable capacity reduction**
- **measurable labor-cost relief**
- **no immediate forced redundancies if alternatives can achieve the same result**
- **credible investment commitments for future products and technologies**
- **a binding implementation framework**, so the agreement does not become another round of endless renegotiation

## To management
You need to stop speaking only in warnings and start showing the economics clearly.

Bring forward:
- the exact savings gap,
- the required time horizon,
- what alternatives have been tested,
- and what level of employee contribution is actually sufficient.

If the non-closure package is truly equivalent, I will consider it. If it is not, then I will not block the stronger measures simply because they are unpopular.

## To the works council and union
You are right that the burden should be shared. You are wrong if you think shared burden means the company can be made competitive without serious structural adjustment.

If you want to avoid closures and forced cuts, then the alternatives must be **large, durable, and enforceable**. Not slogans. Not temporary optics.

I would expect a serious proposal to include:
- capacity reductions,
- working-time flexibility,
- wage moderation or temporary concessions,
- redeployment and qualification,
- adjusted legacy guarantees,
- and a real employee contribution tied to binding company and owner commitments.

But if your position remains “no closures, no layoffs, no deep structural change,” then there is no viable restructuring path.

## To the regional state government
I respect your responsibility to the region. I also understand why you resist closures.

But a blocking position is not the same as a solution. If you want to preserve regional employment, then you need to help deliver a package that genuinely restores competitiveness, not just preserve the current footprint.

I agree that closures should be a last resort. I do **not** agree that they can be removed from the discussion before alternatives are shown to work.

## Bottom line
The family holding’s position is this:

- **Yes** to a durable restructuring.
- **Yes** to serious alternatives to closures if they are economically equivalent.
- **Yes** to shared sacrifice.
- **No** to preserving the current structure regardless of economics.
- **No** to any outcome that permanently weakens the company’s competitiveness or governance control.

So the next step is not more slogans. It is numbers and mechanisms:
1. the exact savings target,
2. the exact alternative measures,
3. the exact contribution from labor,
4. the exact investment commitments,
5. and the binding governance structure to enforce the deal.

If a non-closure package can truly match the economics, I am open to it. If not, then I will not support pretending otherwise.
