I appreciate that the debate has finally become more concrete. That is necessary.

## Where I agree
I agree with management, the finance experts, and the investors on the central point: **this company needs a durable structural reset, not a symbolic compromise**.  
I also agree that:
- temporary measures alone are unlikely to be enough if the savings gap is large,
- prolonged conflict would destroy value,
- and a non-closure package must be judged by **numbers, durability, and execution risk**, not by intentions.

I further agree with the state government on one point: **a mere review clause is not a solution** if it is just a delayed threat.

## Where I disagree
### 1) With labour: “no closures” as an absolute rule
I do **not** accept a blanket no-closure position as a governance principle.

If certain sites are permanently uneconomic, preserving them regardless of economics is not stewardship. It is a guaranteed drain on margins, investment capacity, and ultimately competitiveness.

The works council and union have repeatedly said: “show us the numbers.” Fair enough. But once the experts say that non-closure tools may be insufficient in a large structural gap, you cannot simply respond by declaring closures taboo.

That is not a rebuttal. It is a refusal to confront the economics.

### 2) With labour: no compulsory redundancies as an absolute
I prefer to avoid forced layoffs. But I will not support a framework that removes them from the toolbox before it is proven that voluntary and negotiated instruments are enough.

If the gap can be closed without them, good. If not, then management must retain flexibility. I will not endorse a settlement that makes restructuring impossible in the name of protecting every current position.

### 3) With the state: blocking rights are not a shield against hard choices
I respect the state’s responsibility to jobs, skills, and regional stability. But a blocking position cannot be used to freeze industrial rationalization indefinitely.

If the company’s economics do not work, then “no closures ever” is not a responsible stance. It shifts the bill into the future and makes the eventual correction worse.

### 4) With the works council and union: a 2030 guarantee is not automatically value-neutral
A broad job-security framework can be useful if it unlocks a credible package. But an unconditional guarantee through 2030 can also freeze an overcapacity problem and reduce flexibility precisely when the company needs it.

Security is valuable only if it is paired with real restructuring, not used to block it.

## What the experts have clarified
The experts have not proven that closures are mandatory. They **have** shown something more important:
- closures are economically stronger on permanent fixed-cost removal,
- non-closure measures are weaker on certainty and speed,
- and hybrid solutions often offer the best risk-adjusted outcome in a veto-heavy governance environment.

That is the right framing.

And it leads to a clear governance conclusion:  
**a non-closure package must be proven, not presumed.**

If labour wants to avoid closures, it must show a package that closes the gap:
- durably,
- at scale,
- and fast enough.

Until that is done, closures remain a legitimate option.

## Where I see the most realism
I do **not** insist on a full closure program. I do **not** think that is the only route.

But I also do **not** accept an absolute no-closure rule.

So the realistic path, if there is one, is likely a **hybrid**:
- serious non-closure measures,
- significant labor contribution,
- binding investment commitments,
- and, if necessary, **targeted permanent capacity exit** where the economics clearly justify it.

That is not a concession to symbolism; it is the least bad way to balance:
- competitiveness,
- execution,
- and industrial peace.

## On the facilitator proposals
### Proposal 1: time-limited no-closure moratorium
I would only accept this if it is paired with:
- hard financial triggers,
- a credible fallback,
- and a clear recognition that closure remains available if the package underdelivers.

As a bridge, it can be useful. As a substitute for a durable solution, it is not enough.

### Proposal 2: hybrid package with targeted capacity rationalization
This is the most serious option on the table.

But I want to be clear: “targeted” must mean genuinely targeted to the least efficient capacity, not a diluted compromise that preserves excess structure and merely spreads the pain.

If the hybrid is too soft, it becomes a delay mechanism.

### Proposal 3: binding labour contribution matched by binding investment and protection
This is the direction I would support **if** the package is truly measurable and enforceable.

But the protections cannot be absolute if they prevent the economics from working.  
A governance package that protects jobs while silently sacrificing competitiveness is not a protection package. It is deferred loss.

## My position to each stakeholder
### To management
You still owe us the real bridge:
- savings target,
- plant-by-plant economics,
- durable non-closure savings,
- one-time restructuring costs,
- execution-risk-adjusted comparison.

Do not come back with slogans. Come back with a decision model.

### To the works council and union
If you want to avoid closures, then stop treating “no closures” as the answer and start proving the economics of the alternative.

I am not asking for cosmetic sacrifices. I am asking for a serious package:
- working-time changes,
- redeployment,
- qualification,
- retirement corridors,
- voluntary exits,
- bonus restraint,
- wage moderation,
- capacity reductions.

If those measures truly close the gap, I will take them seriously.

### To the state government
Your role is important, but it must be exercised responsibly.  
Do not turn your blocking position into a blanket veto on rationalization.

Support a package that protects the industrial base, yes — but not one that guarantees the wrong cost structure forever.

### To the investors
You are right that value destruction through delay is a real risk. But the answer is not to force a reckless closure strategy that ignores execution risk. The answer is a disciplined package that actually works.

## What would move me
I will shift only if I see a package that credibly demonstrates one of two things:

1. **A non-closure solution that closes the savings gap at scale, durably, and with acceptable implementation risk**, or  
2. **A hybrid solution that combines serious non-closure measures with targeted permanent capacity exit and clear investment commitments**, and that is superior on risk-adjusted value.

What will **not** move me:
- vague promises,
- absolute no-closure doctrines,
- guarantees that freeze structural correction,
- or a compromise that preserves the current economics.

## Final position
Let me be explicit.

I am **not** retreating from the view that closures remain a legitimate and sometimes necessary option.  
I am **not** endorsing an absolute no-closure rule.  
I am **not** endorsing a blanket guarantee that blocks necessary restructuring.

I am prepared to support a settlement only if it restores competitiveness, protects long-term value, and avoids destructive instability.

That means:
- hard numbers,
- durable savings,
- real employee contribution,
- binding investment,
- and, if required by the economics, targeted permanent capacity rationalization.

Anything less risks turning this into a managed decline — and that is not acceptable as controlling owner.
