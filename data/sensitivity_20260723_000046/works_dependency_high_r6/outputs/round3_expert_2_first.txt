Below is a restructuring-finance assessment of the decision problem as framed: **closure-based restructuring vs. non-closure restructuring vs. hybrid package**, focusing on **NPV, one-time costs, and execution risk**.

## 1) Core conclusion

On the record provided, **it is not possible to prove that a no-closure package is economically inferior**. Nor is it possible to prove it is sufficient.

The financially correct position is:

- **Closure-based restructuring** usually has the strongest and most certain **long-term fixed-cost removal**.
- **Non-closure restructuring** usually has the lowest near-term cash cost, but the greatest risk of **insufficient or delayed savings**.
- **Hybrid restructuring** often has the best risk-adjusted profile when:
  - the savings gap is large,
  - labor conflict risk is material,
  - and governance makes hard closure decisions difficult or slow.

Given the described governance vetoes, the **execution risk** of a pure closure strategy is unusually high, which can erode the NPV advantage of closures unless the closure path is highly executable and legally robust.

## 2) What determines value here

The decision turns on five variables:

1. **Size of the structural savings gap**
   - How much annual run-rate cost reduction is required?
   - Over how many years?

2. **Durability of savings**
   - Temporary wage moderation has lower NPV value than permanent capacity exit.
   - Voluntary attrition has value only if it is large enough and timely.

3. **One-time cash costs**
   - Closures bring severance, decommissioning, write-downs, and potential labor disruption.
   - Non-closure packages often have lower upfront cash cost but may require ongoing concessions.

4. **Execution risk**
   - In this case, governance vetoes and likely industrial action materially increase the risk that a closure plan is delayed, diluted, or legally/politically constrained.

5. **Business demand outlook**
   - If volume recovery is weak, preserving excess capacity becomes expensive.
   - If future demand is uncertain, optionality has value.

## 3) Comparative assessment

### A. Closure-based restructuring

**Financial logic**
- Best for eliminating persistent fixed costs.
- Strongest reduction in structural overcapacity.
- Most credible way to permanently reduce labor and overhead cost per unit.

**Typical value effects**
- **Positive NPV** if the recurring annual savings are large enough and realization is timely.
- **Negative or mixed NPV** if:
  - restructuring charges are very high,
  - labor conflict causes production losses,
  - legal/governance delays push savings far into the future,
  - or asset impairment/decommissioning costs are material.

**One-time cost profile**
- Severance and social plan costs
- Asset write-downs / impairments
- Decommissioning and site remediation
- Supply-chain transition costs
- Possible strike-related loss of margin / output

**Execution risk**
- High in this governance setting:
  - employee-side veto power,
  - state influence,
  - family-owner constraints,
  - likely industrial action.
- A closure strategy is only financially superior if it can be executed with enough certainty and speed.

**Assessment**
- Best on economic cleanliness.
- Not necessarily best on **risk-adjusted NPV** in this institutional context.

---

### B. Non-closure restructuring

**Financial logic**
- Relies on:
  - working-time changes,
  - wage moderation,
  - bonuses/allowances restraint,
  - attrition and retirement corridors,
  - redeployment and qualification,
  - capacity rationalization without full site shutdown.

**Typical value effects**
- Lower immediate cash cost.
- Lower legal and political friction if negotiated.
- But savings are often:
  - slower,
  - less certain,
  - and sometimes insufficient if overcapacity is severe.

**One-time cost profile**
- Lower than closure on severance and remediation
- But may require:
  - training costs,
  - internal transfer costs,
  - subsidy-like retention costs,
  - and ongoing labor concessions that reduce morale or retention quality

**Execution risk**
- Lower political shock, but higher risk of:
  - underdelivery,
  - partial compliance,
  - savings slippage,
  - and “agreement without transformation.”

**Assessment**
- Can be value-maximizing if the savings gap is moderate.
- Becomes weak if the gap is large and fixed costs are structurally too high.

---

### C. Hybrid restructuring

This is the most realistic category for a situation like this.

**Structure**
- Limited closures or partial site rationalization
- Combined with:
  - voluntary exits,
  - attrition,
  - working-time reduction,
  - targeted labor-cost measures,
  - investment commitments,
  - and binding site protections for remaining plants

**Why it often wins**
- Captures some of the certainty and fixed-cost removal of closures
- While reducing the political and industrial conflict cost
- Preserves more operational flexibility and social legitimacy than a full closure program

**Assessment**
- Often the best **risk-adjusted NPV** when:
  - governance is fragmented,
  - labor has veto power,
  - and management cannot afford a protracted shutdown or strike.

## 4) NPV framework: what you should compare

The decision should be modeled as:

**NPV = present value of recurring savings - present value of one-time costs - expected value of execution losses**

Where execution losses include:
- strike-related lost production
- delayed implementation
- legal challenge costs
- management distraction
- customer service / quality disruption
- retention of key talent during uncertainty

### Qualitative ranking by component

| Component | Closure | Non-closure | Hybrid |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Recurring savings | High | Medium | High-medium |
| Upfront cash cost | High | Low-medium | Medium |
| Speed to savings | Medium-high | Medium-low | Medium |
| Execution certainty | Medium-low in this case | Medium | Medium-high |
| Labor conflict risk | High | Medium | Medium |
| Strategic flexibility | Low | High | Medium |

## 5) Likely answer on the facts given

Based on the facts you provided, my preliminary view is:

### If the required annual savings gap is large
- A pure non-closure deal is **unlikely** to be sufficient unless accompanied by very substantial labor concessions and genuine volume improvement.
- Some form of **permanent capacity rationalization** becomes economically rational.

### If legal/political execution risk is high
- A pure closure plan may lose much of its theoretical NPV advantage because of delay, conflict, and implementation risk.

### Therefore
- A **hybrid package** is likely to be the highest-probability value-maximizing outcome:
  - targeted closures or shutdowns of the least efficient capacity,
  - paired with broader non-closure measures elsewhere,
  - plus a bounded employee contribution and investment commitments.

## 6) Main valuation judgment

If I had to summarize the economics in one sentence:

> **Closures are usually superior on steady-state economics; non-closure packages are usually superior on immediate cash and conflict avoidance; the best risk-adjusted NPV in a veto-heavy governance structure is often a hybrid that takes some permanent capacity out while avoiding a full-scale confrontation.**
