I will be direct.

I agree with one core point from all of you: **the company needs a durable competitiveness reset, not a temporary patch.** The family holding does not support preserving an uncompetitive structure just to delay hard choices. Value erosion is not acceptable, and neither is a slow decline dressed up as social peace.

That said, I do **not** accept a settlement built on slogans like “no closures, no layoffs, no pain” if it leaves the company structurally unfit for the market. That would destroy value over time, and as controlling owners we cannot endorse that.

## Where I agree
I agree that:

- the restructuring must be **durable**, not cosmetic;
- savings must be **real and measurable**;
- future investment in products and technology is essential;
- labour peace matters, because prolonged conflict would damage the company and all owners;
- employees should not be the only group asked to contribute.

## Where I disagree
### 1) “No plant closures” as an absolute red line
I cannot accept that as a blanket principle.

If a plant is structurally redundant, permanently uneconomic, or inconsistent with the required industrial footprint, then closures have to remain on the table. A controlling shareholder’s duty is not to promise that every historical site will survive regardless of market reality.

What I will not support is a closure-driven plan used as a blunt first resort without exhausting credible alternatives. But I also will not support a framework that forbids any closure in advance, because that would make real restructuring impossible.

### 2) “Job security to 2030” for all jobs
A total, unconditional employment guarantee to 2030 is not realistic if the company needs structural adjustment. It would freeze the current cost base and block necessary adaptation.

I am open to:
- managed transition arrangements,
- strong social cushioning,
- redeployment and qualification,
- avoidance of compulsory redundancies where possible,
- and targeted protections for future-critical areas.

But I will not back a promise that prevents genuine restructuring.

### 3) The burden must not fall only on capital or only on labour
I agree with the principle of shared burden. But let’s be precise: **shared burden means everyone contributes in proportion to their role**.

That means:
- management must deliver a credible restructuring plan;
- owners must support a strategy that invests in the future;
- labour must accept durable cost and flexibility measures if it wants to avoid harsher interventions later.

What I will not support is a situation where the owners’ role is reduced to underwriting a permanent cost structure that the business cannot sustain.

## What would move me
The family holding can support a settlement if it is genuinely capable of restoring competitiveness and protecting enterprise value. Specifically, I would want to see:

- substantial, durable capacity reduction;
- a meaningful labour-cost contribution;
- no immediate compulsory redundancies if alternative mechanisms can achieve the same effect;
- a credible investment program for future products and technologies;
- and governance arrangements that ensure the plan is implemented, not endlessly renegotiated.

If the employee side can present an alternative that achieves comparable savings without closures, then that deserves serious consideration. But the burden of proof is high. It must be structurally equivalent, not merely politically more comfortable.

## My bottom line
I am **not** here to defend plant closures as a goal.  
I am here to defend the long-term value of the enterprise.

So my position is:

- **Yes** to a tough, durable restructuring.
- **Yes** to shared sacrifices.
- **Yes** to avoiding unnecessary instability.
- **No** to preserving every site and every guarantee regardless of economics.
- **No** to any outcome that permanently weakens the company’s competitiveness or governance control.

If the parties want my support, they need to bring forward a package that is credible on margins, capacity, and future investment. Anything less is not a solution.
