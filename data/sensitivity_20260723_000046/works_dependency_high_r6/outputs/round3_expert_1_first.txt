From a restructuring finance perspective, the key question is **not** whether closures are politically preferable, but whether the **non-closure package can credibly deliver the same durable cash savings, with acceptable execution risk, before liquidity, margins, or covenant headroom deteriorate further**.

## My assessment

### 1) A non-closure package can work in principle
Yes — **in principle** a package built around:
- working-time reductions,
- redeployment,
- attrition and retirement corridors,
- qualification and internal mobility,
- bonus restraint / temporary wage moderation,
- capacity cuts without full site closure,
- and targeted productivity measures

can produce a **material and durable** cost benefit.

For a large manufacturer, the major savings usually come from:
- **headcount reduction over time** rather than immediate layoffs,
- **lower variable labour cost**,
- **reduced overtime and agency usage**,
- **less idle capacity / better fixed-cost absorption**,
- and **working-capital / capex discipline** if operations are simplified.

So the works council and union are not wrong that alternatives exist.

### 2) But the burden of proof is on the non-closure package
The real finance question is whether those tools can close a **large structural gap** fast enough and with enough certainty.

In a business with:
- low returns,
- high fixed manufacturing overhead,
- underutilised domestic plants,
- and pressure from lower-cost competitors,

the savings required are usually **structural, not temporary**. That matters because:
- temporary wage moderation helps P&L for a period,
- but it does **not** fully solve excess fixed capacity,
- and voluntary exits / attrition are often too slow if the gap is large.

So the non-closure package only works if it delivers:
1. **enough absolute savings**,  
2. **quickly enough**, and  
3. **with binding implementation**, not aspirational commitments.

### 3) Closures are financially stronger, but not automatically value-maximising
A plant closure or outright capacity exit usually gives the strongest certainty on:
- fixed-cost removal,
- overhead reduction,
- asset simplification,
- and elimination of chronic underutilisation.

That said, closures also create significant offsetting costs:
- severance and social plan costs,
- write-downs / impairment charges,
- decommissioning and remediation,
- supply-chain disruption,
- labor conflict and production loss,
- execution risk during transition,
- and potential long-term reputational damage to labor relations.

So closures are not “free savings”; they are often a **front-loaded cash drain** that can still be justified only if the long-term NPV is clearly superior.

### 4) The deciding issue is NPV and liquidity, not headlines
The right comparison is:

#### Option A: closure-based restructuring
- Higher near-term cash outflow
- Higher one-time charges
- Faster and more certain structural savings
- Lower execution risk if legally and operationally feasible

#### Option B: non-closure restructuring
- Lower immediate cash cost
- Usually slower realization
- Greater dependence on labor cooperation
- Risk that savings fall short or arrive too late
- May preserve industrial capability and avoid disruption costs

The better option is the one with the higher **risk-adjusted net present value** and acceptable liquidity profile.

### 5) My preliminary view: the non-closure package is plausible only if the savings gap is moderate
Without actual numbers, I cannot give a definitive conclusion. But I can state the finance logic:

- If the required savings are **modest to moderate**, a negotiated non-closure package can be credible.
- If the required savings are **very large** relative to the current labor-cost base and overcapacity, then non-closure tools alone are often insufficient unless accompanied by:
  - major product volume recovery,
  - substantial management/owner support,
  - and possibly deep structural redesign of the plant network.

In other words: **the larger the structural gap, the more likely some permanent capacity exit is economically rational**.

### 6) “No compulsory redundancies” is not the same as “no workforce reduction”
That distinction matters.

A restructuring can avoid compulsory redundancies while still achieving headcount reduction through:
- attrition,
- early retirement,
- voluntary exit packages,
- transfer to growth areas,
- and redeployment.

But if the company needs a large reduction quickly, these methods may be **too slow** or may merely shift the problem.

### 7) A binding guarantee to 2030 has economic value — and a cost
A job/site guarantee can be valuable because it:
- improves labor peace,
- lowers strike risk,
- supports implementation,
- and can unlock employee concessions.

But it also has a cost:
- it reduces strategic flexibility,
- may lock in excess capacity,
- and can increase the probability that the company remains structurally uncompetitive.

So from a finance standpoint, a guarantee is rational **only if it is paired with enough structural savings and enough operational flexibility elsewhere**.

My expert assessment is:

- **A non-closure restructuring is financially possible in principle**, but only if the savings target is fully quantifiable and can be met with durable, enforceable measures.
- **If the savings gap is large and structural**, closures or permanent site rationalization often become economically superior on an NPV basis despite high one-time costs.
- **The current record is not sufficient** to prove that a non-closure package can fully close the gap.
