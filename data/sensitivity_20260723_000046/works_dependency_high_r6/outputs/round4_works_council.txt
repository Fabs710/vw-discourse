Let me respond directly to the points on the table.

## Where I agree
I agree with the expert and with the moderator on one thing: **we need numbers, not slogans**.  
And I agree that the company must become more competitive, secure future investment, and avoid a long conflict if possible.

I also acknowledge one factual point from the finance experts: **temporary measures alone do not automatically solve a structural gap**. That is true.

## Where I disagree
What I **do not** accept is the attempt by management, the family holding, and investors to convert that factual point into a political conclusion: that closures and compulsory redundancies therefore remain “necessary” in practice.

That is still an assumption, not a proven necessity.

You have all said, in different words, that:
- a non-closure package may be slower,
- may need discipline,
- may require binding implementation,
- and may need a strong employee contribution.

Fine. But none of that proves that German plant closures are required. It only proves that management wants more room to force through its preferred solution.

## To management
You keep saying “structural reset,” “durable savings,” and “equivalent effect.” Good. Then show the actual bridge.

Right now, what I hear is:
- “closures might be needed,”
- “forced layoffs must remain possible,”
- “blanket guarantees are unacceptable,”
- and “the workforce must prove alternatives first.”

That is backwards.

If you want the workforce to make concessions, then you must first show:
1. the exact savings gap,
2. what each plant contributes,
3. what each alternative measure contributes,
4. and why the combination of working-time, redeployment, qualification, attrition, retirement corridors, bonus restraint, and capacity adjustments cannot close the gap.

You have not shown that.

So I reject the logic that closures are the default fallback. They are not. They are a choice.

## To the finance specialists
Your input is useful, but I want to be very clear about what it does and does not establish.

Yes, you say closures can be stronger on steady-state fixed-cost removal.  
Yes, you say a hybrid may be best in a veto-heavy environment.

But that does **not** mean the works council should accept closures as a given. It means the company side has not yet done the work needed to prove that a non-closure package cannot work.

And crucially: the experts also say the risk-adjusted outcome depends on **execution risk**. In this company, execution risk of closures is not a small footnote — it is huge:
- supervisory-board veto constraints,
- state influence,
- workforce resistance,
- warning strikes,
- possible production disruption,
- supplier damage,
- and long-lasting trust destruction.

Those are not abstract risks. They are immediate and real. So if you claim closures are economically superior, you must discount for that. And once you do, the gap is far less clear than management pretends.

## To the controlling family holding and investors
You both keep repeating that value destruction will follow if the structure is preserved. I disagree with the framing.

Value is also destroyed by:
- tearing up trust,
- undermining co-determination,
- forcing closures through conflict,
- and wrecking the skills base of the German plants.

A company is not only a balance sheet. It is a production system and a social system. You cannot strip out the latter and expect the former to survive in any meaningful way.

And no, I do not accept the argument that a job-security framework through 2030 “freezes the problem.” A binding guarantee is precisely what makes a serious transformation possible without destroying the workforce that must carry it.

## To the regional state government
You have said the right thing more clearly than the capital side: closures must not be the baseline, and forced layoffs are not the first response.

But I need to challenge you on enforcement. A “conditional framework” or “review clause” is only worth something if the default is genuinely protection, not a delayed threat.

I do not want a model where:
- workers concede now,
- management gets flexibility now,
- and then closures come later once resistance has been softened.

That is not a compromise. That is staged pressure.

If you are serious about your blocking role, then use it to defend:
- no plant closures in Germany,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- binding job and site security,
- and real investment commitments.

## To the union
I support the union’s red lines:
- no closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- no dismantling of the collective framework.

But I want to make one thing equally clear: temporary concessions are only discussable if they are genuinely temporary and if they are matched by hard guarantees. Otherwise you are just being asked to finance uncertainty.

## On the facilitator proposals
### Proposal 1: time-limited no-closure moratorium
No. Not acceptable as a substitute for a real commitment.

A moratorium is not a guarantee. It simply postpones the same threat. If management’s preferred outcome is closure, then a moratorium just buys time to repackage it.

We do not need a delayed ultimatum. We need a binding commitment.

### Proposal 2: hybrid package with targeted permanent capacity rationalization
I hear the logic, but I do not accept it as a starting point.

“Targeted permanent capacity reduction” is often just a polite phrase for opening the door to closures by another name. If a site can be reduced today, it can be closed tomorrow once enough capacity has been hollowed out.

And once that logic starts, the claim that German plants are protected is meaningless.

### Proposal 3: binding labour contribution matched by binding investment and protection
This is the only direction worth discussing, but only if the protection is real:
- no closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- binding job security through 2030,
- enforceable site guarantees,
- meaningful co-determination in implementation.

Without that, there is no balanced exchange.

## What I am prepared to consider
I remain open to:
- working-time reductions,
- internal transfers,
- qualification,
- retirement corridors,
- voluntary exits,
- temporary bonus restraint,
- temporary wage moderation,
- capacity adjustments **within existing sites**,

but only if they are paired with:
- a binding no-closure commitment,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- job and site guarantees through 2030,
- and genuine co-determination over implementation.

That is the minimum.

## What I am not prepared to accept
Let me restate the red lines clearly:

- **No plant closures in Germany.**
- **No compulsory redundancies.**
- **No weak “review clause” instead of a real guarantee.**
- **No hybrid that quietly prepares later closures.**
- **No restructuring that shifts all risk onto workers while capital retains all optionality.**

## What is still missing
The decisive missing element is still the same: a **quantified non-closure package**.

So I am asking management, and anyone else who claims closures are unavoidable, to produce the hard figures:
- annual savings required,
- savings from each proposed measure,
- one-time costs,
- and the exact reason the non-closure package fails.

Not general assertions. Not “we think.” Not “we believe.”

If you cannot prove that the alternatives fail, then closures remain unjustified.

## My bottom line
I do not move from my position.

The works council will continue to insist on:
- **keeping every German plant open**,
- **no compulsory redundancies**,
- **preserving co-determination**,
- and **maintaining the job-security framework**.

If management wants a settlement, it must be built on those foundations.  
If not, then the responsibility for escalation lies with those who are trying to make closures sound inevitable before they have proven it.
