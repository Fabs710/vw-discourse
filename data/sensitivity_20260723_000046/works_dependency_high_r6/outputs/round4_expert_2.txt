Yes. Within my remit, the key question is **not** “closures vs no closures” in the abstract, but **which package has the highest execution-risk-adjusted NPV** after one-time costs.

## Preliminary valuation view
With no plant-level data, the financially correct conclusion is:

- **Pure closure strategy**
  - Highest potential steady-state savings
  - Highest one-time cost
  - Highest execution/disruption risk
  - Can be NPV-superior only if:
    - the savings gap is large,
    - the sites are structurally uneconomic,
    - and the probability of clean execution is high enough.

- **Pure non-closure strategy**
  - Lowest upfront cost
  - Lowest immediate conflict risk if accepted
  - But savings are often smaller, slower, and less durable
  - Usually inferior if the structural overcapacity gap is material.

- **Hybrid strategy**
  - Usually the best risk-adjusted candidate in a veto-heavy governance setting
  - Captures some permanent fixed-cost reduction without paying the full closure penalty
  - Often dominates on expected NPV if full closures face heavy strike, veto, and delay risk.

## Sensitivity breakpoints
A non-closure package becomes financially inferior when one or more of these hold:

1. **Savings shortfall**
   - Non-closure measures cover too little of the required structural gap.

2. **Savings decay**
   - Concessions are temporary, while the cost problem is permanent.

3. **High implementation leakage**
   - Working-time, wage, or productivity gains are not actually realized.

4. **Conflict cost rises**
   - If preserving sites requires repeated concessions, the cumulative cost can exceed a one-time rationalization.

5. **Investment drag**
   - If the company cannot fund future products because the restructuring is too soft, NPV deteriorates even if the sites stay open.
