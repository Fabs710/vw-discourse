From the perspective of dispersed institutional shareholders, the central issue is not preserving any particular site or legacy guarantee. It is whether the final package credibly restores **competitive margins, sustainable cash generation, and investment capacity**.

## Where we agree
We agree with management and the family holding on one essential point: **a cosmetic settlement is unacceptable**. If the outcome leaves the company with an uncompetitive cost base, then it will continue to underperform and destroy shareholder value. That is not a viable “peace deal.”

We also agree that the company needs:
- **durable** savings, not one-off optics;
- **real capacity adjustment**, not just promises;
- and enough room to fund future products and technology.

On that basis, we do not object in principle to difficult restructuring measures, including plant rationalisation, if they are genuinely necessary and economically justified.

## Where we disagree
We do not share the view that closures or forced redundancies are the only credible route. They may be part of the answer, but what matters to us is the **economic result**, not the instrument.

At the same time, we also do not accept the employee side’s position if it amounts to:
- no closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- no meaningful wage or cost concessions,
- and a guarantee structure that simply freezes the current cost base.

That would be value-destructive. It would leave the company structurally impaired and likely push the burden into future years, which is worse for shareholders.

## Our position on the trade-off
We are prepared to support **alternative measures** to closures if, and only if, they deliver savings that are:
- **quantifiable**,
- **durable**,
- **large enough to matter**,
- and **credible in implementation**.

That means we would look positively on:
- working-time flexibility,
- capacity reductions,
- redeployment and qualification,
- targeted wage moderation,
- adjustment of legacy guarantees,
- and any negotiated employee contribution that produces real margin improvement.

But those measures must be substantial. If they merely soften the headlines while preserving the same fixed-cost structure, we would not consider that acceptable.

## On job guarantees and “no forced redundancies”
We understand why the workforce wants certainty, but an unconditional guarantee to 2030 for all jobs is not compatible with a serious restructuring if the company truly has structural overcapacity.

We would not insist on forced redundancies as a principle. In fact, if the same savings can be achieved through attrition, redeployment, early retirement, working-time tools, or other managed mechanisms, that is preferable. It lowers disruption and reduces execution risk.

But if the alternative package is too weak, then management must be able to act. Shareholders cannot support an arrangement that preserves jobs today only to impair the company’s survival and valuation tomorrow.

## On the regional state and co-determination
We recognize the reality of the governance structure: employee veto power, the state’s blocking minority, and the family’s control. That means the final deal must be **deal-certainty driven**.

What we would strongly oppose is prolonged conflict that drags on margins, depresses sentiment, and delays the turnaround. Industrial peace has value, but only if it leads to a credible financial outcome. A drawn-out standoff would be a poor result for shareholders.

## What would move us
We would support a package if it clearly includes:
- a **credible path back to competitive margins**;
- **durable structural cost reduction**;
- meaningful reduction in excess capacity;
- protection of future investment capacity;
- and a settlement that is stable enough to execute.

We are open on the method. We are not open on the economic standard.

## Bottom line
Our position is straightforward:

- **Yes** to tough restructuring.
- **Yes** to alternatives to closures if they are economically equivalent.
- **No** to a cosmetic compromise that protects the current cost base.
- **No** to a settlement that leaves margins structurally impaired.

If the other stakeholders can produce a package that genuinely restores competitiveness, we can support it. If not, we would side with whatever delivers the necessary cost reset, even if that means sharper restructuring.
