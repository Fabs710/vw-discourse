I hear the red lines you are setting. I also need to be equally clear:

**I do not accept a cosmetic solution that leaves the company structurally uncompetitive.**  
If we simply preserve every site unchanged, freeze the current cost base, and call that a “transformation,” then we will fail — commercially and strategically. That would eventually put even more jobs at risk.

Let me respond directly.

### On plant closures and compulsory redundancies
You say “no closures, no compulsory redundancies.” I understand the force of that position, but I cannot accept it as an absolute.  
We are facing **persistent overcapacity, a shrinking European market, and factory costs that are far above target**. That means we need **real capacity reduction and real labour-cost reduction** — not just temporary measures.

If the employee side is willing to bring forward a **credible, durable package** that achieves comparable savings through other structural means, then I am prepared to look at alternatives to closures. But the burden is on you to show that such a package is not just softer language for the same cost base.

### On job security to 2030
A blanket guarantee “for every job” and “for every plant” to 2030 is not compatible with the scale of restructuring we need. I will not sign up to a framework that locks in today’s structure and prevents us from correcting excess capacity.

What I can consider is:
- a **managed transition** rather than a chaotic one,
- **socially responsible reductions** instead of abrupt compulsory cuts,
- redeployment and qualification where they genuinely create future value,
- and targeted protections where they do not undermine the restructuring.

But the outcome must still deliver **structural savings**.

### On employee contribution
I agree that any contribution must be shared. But let’s be honest: the main pressure point here is the cost base.  
We need a **genuine labour-cost contribution** that is large enough to matter — not symbolic gestures. That can include pay moderation, working-time changes, the removal or adjustment of legacy guarantees, and other durable measures.

If the workforce wants a credible no-closure path, then it must help create the economics for it.

### On investment and future products
This point matters, and I do agree with it: restructuring must not be about shrinking for its own sake. We need to **free funds for future products and technology through 2030**.  
But that requires the savings to be real. Capital cannot be invested twice. If we keep excess capacity and high fixed costs, we will not have the room to fund the next generation of products.

### Where I draw the line
I will be very explicit:  
- I will not endorse a settlement that is only a short-term peace deal.  
- I will not accept a package that preserves uncompetitive structures and simply defers the problem.  
- I will not let management’s mandate be reduced to managing decline.

### Where I am prepared to engage
If the works council and the union are prepared to put forward a **durable alternative** that delivers:
- substantial capacity reduction,
- measurable and lasting labour-cost savings,
- no immediate forced redundancies,
- and sufficient room for future investment,

then I am willing to negotiate that seriously.

But if the answer is simply “no closures, no layoffs, no pain,” then there is no credible restructuring path on the table. And in that case, we will have to pursue the stronger measures that remain available.

So my position is straightforward:  
**I am open to alternatives — but only if they are structurally equivalent, durable, and economically credible.**
