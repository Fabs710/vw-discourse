I will be equally clear.

## Where I agree
I agree with the works council on the essentials:
- this company needs a durable restructuring,
- there must be real savings,
- and the future sites need investment in products and technology.

I also agree with the state government on one point: this cannot become a pure cutback exercise where workers pay and the company merely shrinks itself into weakness.

And I agree with management, the family holding, and investors on one point as well: we do need a solution that actually changes the economics. A cosmetic peace deal is not acceptable.

## Where I disagree
### 1) Plant closures
I do **not** accept plant closures in Germany as a normal restructuring tool here.  
Not as a baseline, not as the opening move, and not as a pressure tactic.

If management wants to claim closures are necessary, then it must prove that:
- non-closure measures cannot deliver comparable savings,
- and that every serious alternative has been exhausted.

That proof has not been made.

### 2) Compulsory redundancies
I do **not** accept compulsory redundancies as the response to this crisis.  
We have not exhausted:
- redeployment,
- qualification,
- attrition,
- retirement corridors,
- working-time reductions,
- temporary concessions,
- and internal flexibility.

If management insists on forced layoffs before those tools are fully used, then it is choosing confrontation, not restructuring.

### 3) Job security through 2030
I do **not** accept the argument that a binding job and site security framework is incompatible with transformation.  
What is incompatible with transformation is trying to force employees to bear the whole adjustment while keeping the company’s social and industrial future uncertain.

If workers are asked to contribute, then they need real security in return. Otherwise there is no fair deal.

## What I support
I am prepared to negotiate a serious package that includes:
- temporary wage moderation,
- bonus restraint or waiver,
- working-time flexibility,
- capacity adjustments,
- redeployment,
- qualification,
- retirement corridors,
- and a negotiated employee contribution,

**if and only if** it is paired with:
- no plant closures in Germany,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- binding site guarantees,
- and binding investment commitments.

That is not a wish list. That is the minimum basis for a fair settlement.

## To management
You keep saying the alternative package must be “structurally equivalent.” Fine. Then show the numbers.

I want a quantified answer to these questions:
1. What exact savings gap must be closed?
2. How much comes from labor-cost measures?
3. How much comes from management and owners?
4. What investment commitments are being made for future products and technologies?
5. Why do you claim closures are still needed if alternatives are available?

Until that is answered, closures remain a threat, not a necessity.

And let me be very direct: if management uses the current crisis to force through permanent deterioration of collective wage standards, then it is not negotiating restructuring — it is attacking the collective bargaining framework.

That is unacceptable.

## To the works council
I support your insistence on no closures and no compulsory redundancies.  
But I also want to make one thing clear: employee concessions can only be asked for if they are **time-limited, reversible, and matched by binding commitments**.

A temporary wage contribution, a bonus waiver, or working-time flexibility can be part of a fair package. A permanent erosion of pay and protections cannot.

So yes:
- temporary concessions,
- yes;
- open-ended austerity,
- no.

## To the regional state government
Your role matters here. A blocking minority is only meaningful if it protects a real settlement, not if it becomes a passive witness while management pushes the workforce toward forced concessions.

If you want to preserve industrial employment, then back a package that:
- protects sites,
- avoids forced redundancies,
- and secures future investment.

Do not accept a logic in which “last resort” quietly becomes the starting point after enough pressure is applied.

## To the family holding and investors
You are right that value cannot be protected by preserving an uncompetitive cost base.  
But value is also destroyed if management breaks trust, undermines collective bargaining, and turns structural pressure into a job-cutting machine.

A company’s productive strength includes:
- skills,
- trust,
- industrial peace,
- and a stable workforce.

You cannot cut all of that away and then call the result competitiveness.

## On the numbers and mechanism
The moderator is right: we need specifics. But specifics must be shared fairly.

So here is the union’s position:

- We are open to a **quantified employee contribution**.
- We are open to **durable cost measures**.
- We are open to **working-time and capacity solutions**.
- We are open to **temporary and negotiated wage moderation**.

But only if the package contains:
1. **no plant closures in Germany**,
2. **no compulsory redundancies**,
3. **binding site and job security**,
4. **binding investment commitments**,
5. **fair contributions from management and owners**.

## The line we will not cross
Let me be unmistakable:

- No closures.
- No forced layoffs.
- No dismantling of the collective wage framework.
- No “shared burden” where only employees actually carry the burden.

If those conditions are not met, then there is no settlement from our side.

And if management tries to impose a closure- or layoff-based restructuring, then it will face industrial resistance.

## Final point
We are not refusing responsibility. We are refusing one-sided sacrifice.

If the company wants durable competitiveness, then it needs a fair deal:
- employees contribute,
- management contributes,
- owners contribute,
- and German sites and jobs are protected.

That is the only acceptable basis for agreement.
