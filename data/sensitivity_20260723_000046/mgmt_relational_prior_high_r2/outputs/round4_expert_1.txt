Yes. From a restructuring-finance and industrial-footprint perspective, the key unresolved question is not “closures or no closures” in the abstract, but whether the **no-closure package can match the economics of footprint reduction on a present-value basis, with acceptable execution risk**.

## What I would say as the footprint / valuation expert

### 1) The decision should be tested plant by plant
A credible comparison must, for each German site, quantify:

- **Current utilization**
- **Direct labor and overhead cost**
- **Allocated fixed cost burden**
- **Capex needs to keep the site viable**
- **One-time restructuring charges**
- **Run-rate savings under each option**
- **Timing of savings**
- **Residual fixed cost after restructuring**
- **Execution risk / labor conflict risk**
- **NPV impact over a defined horizon**

Without that, the debate stays political rather than economic.

---

### 2) Closure is not automatically superior
Factually, closure tends to deliver the **largest and most certain fixed-cost reduction** per site, but it also usually brings:

- high one-time charges
- asset write-downs
- severance and transfer costs
- supply-chain disruption
- loss of operational flexibility
- possible brand/reputation damage
- and, if labor conflict is severe, delayed realization of savings

So closure is usually the **cleanest** structural tool, but not always the **best value** tool once all costs and timing are included.

---

### 3) No-closure packages can work, but only under conditions
A no-closure alternative can be economically equivalent only if it achieves enough of the following:

- material and durable **headcount reduction**
- **capacity reduction** through shifts, lines, and product allocation
- lower wage or bonus burden
- reduced overtime / working-time cost
- management and overhead cuts
- voluntary exits at sufficient scale
- credible constraints on future fixed cost
- enforceable implementation and time-bound savings

If the package only redistributes pain without lowering the fixed-cost base enough, it will be a delay, not a restructuring.

---

### 4) The real threshold is the structural gap
A useful way to frame the test is:

- **Small gap**: non-closure measures may be enough.
- **Moderate gap**: hybrid package is most likely.
- **Large gap**: footprint reduction becomes hard to avoid.

The missing input is the actual size of the gap by plant and by product line.

---

### 5) What “equivalent” should mean
A no-closure plan should be judged against closure on four criteria:

1. **NPV of savings**  
   Does the present value of savings over 5–10 years match closure?

2. **Durability**  
   Are the savings structural, or do they unwind after a few wage rounds?

3. **Execution certainty**  
   Can the package be implemented without repeated renegotiation or strike risk?

4. **Investment capacity**  
   Does the solution preserve enough cash for the EV / technology transition?

If it loses on two or more of these materially, it is not equivalent.

---

### 6) What I would request next
To answer this properly, I would need:

- plant-level fixed cost and utilization data
- product allocation assumptions
- labor cost by site
- restructuring charges for each site option
- forecast volumes by model / platform
- one-time and recurring savings from non-closure levers
- timing assumptions for implementation
- sensitivity analysis for lower volumes and labor conflict delays

---

## Bottom line
My expert assessment would be:

- **You cannot assume closures are necessary without the plant-level numbers.**
- **You cannot assume a no-closure package is sufficient without proving durable fixed-cost reduction and NPV equivalence.**
- In most cases, the likely outcome is a **hybrid**: some footprint correction plus substantial internal measures, unless the no-closure package is unusually deep and enforceable.
