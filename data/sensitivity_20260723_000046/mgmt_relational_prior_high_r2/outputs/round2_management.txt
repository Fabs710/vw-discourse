Let me respond plainly.

### First: on the core diagnosis
I agree with everyone on one point: **the company cannot continue as it is**.

- The German cost base is too high.
- We have structural overcapacity.
- The European market is shrinking.
- Competitors are undercutting us.
- The brand is missing its return-on-sales target.
- And we need funding for the next product cycle and the 2030 technology transition.

So no, I am not asking for change because I enjoy confrontation. I am saying the current structure is not competitive, and if we do not fix it now, we will destroy far more jobs and value later.

### Where I agree with labour and the state
I agree that:
- a one-sided solution is not sustainable,
- the company needs a credible industrial plan,
- investment in future products matters,
- and prolonged strikes would damage output, reputation, and trust.

I also agree that alternatives to pure plant closure should be examined seriously.

### Where I disagree — fundamentally
I do **not** accept the repeated attempt to turn “no closures, no compulsory redundancies, no guarantee changes” into a starting premise.

That is not a restructuring strategy. It is a demand that management solve a structural cost problem with one hand tied behind its back.

If you want to keep every plant open and rule out all compulsory measures in advance, then you must show — concretely, not rhetorically — how we get to a cost base that is competitive with lower-cost rivals and a market that is shrinking.

So far, I have not heard that answer.

### To the Works Council
You say closures are just a threat and alternatives must be exhausted first. I hear that. But you are still not answering the central question: **how do we remove enough cost and capacity, fast enough and durably enough, without touching the footprint?**

Internal reallocation, working-time flexibility, and voluntary exits are useful tools. They are **not** enough on their own if the structural gap is as large as our analysis shows. A cosmetic shift of volumes around the network does not fix an oversized base.

I am not prepared to sign up to “preserve all sites” as a principle if that leaves the business structurally uncompetitive. That would be a failure of stewardship.

### To the union
You keep saying “show us the numbers.” Fair. Then let me be equally direct: the numbers we have justify a durable reduction in cost and capacity. That means:
- lower fixed labour cost,
- lower overhead,
- and a smaller industrial footprint in Germany.

If you want an alternative that avoids closures, then it must deliver **equivalent, verified, durable savings**. Not temporary concessions. Not a one-year bridge. Not attrition dressed up as reform.

And to be explicit: I do **not** accept a deal that preserves the current structure and simply postpones the pain into 2027 or 2028.

### To the state government
I appreciate the concern for regional employment. But the state cannot demand competitiveness and simultaneously veto the instruments required to achieve it. If the outcome protects every site regardless of economics, then it is not a restructuring — it is a subsidy to inefficiency.

We will respect co-determination and the governance framework. But co-determination is not a right to freeze the footprint. It is a right to negotiate the transition. That transition must still be real.

### To the controlling family
I agree with you that value erosion must stop. Where I differ is this: “all options on the table” is not a threat for its own sake. It reflects the fact that we need a serious restructuring, and a serious restructuring may require difficult measures.

I also agree that we should not rush blindly into closures. But let’s be honest: if alternatives do not close the structural gap, then closures remain necessary. Keeping them off the table in advance is not prudent governance.

### To investors
You are right to focus on durable margin restoration and execution certainty. I share that focus.

But I also need to say this clearly: if the final outcome is a diluted compromise that avoids hard decisions and leaves the cost base uncompetitive, then the market will punish the company anyway. You will not get long-term value from preserving every legacy arrangement.

### What we need now
I want to shift this discussion from slogans to substance.

If labour wants to avoid closures, then bring forward a package that credibly delivers:
1. **durable cost reduction**
2. **measurable capacity reduction**
3. **a real labour-cost contribution**
4. **implementation certainty**
5. **restored competitiveness by 2030**

That package must be substantial enough that I can honestly say it solves the problem. If it does, I will examine it seriously.

If it does not, then I will continue to argue for deeper structural measures, including closures if necessary.

### My bottom line
I am open on the instrument mix. I am **not** open on the outcome.

The outcome must be:
- structurally lower cost,
- structurally lower overcapacity,
- and a business that can fund its future.

If that requires closures, then we must be honest about that. If labour can deliver equivalent savings without closures, then show it. Until then, I will not pretend that symbolic protection of every site is an acceptable substitute for competitiveness.
