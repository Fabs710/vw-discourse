## My position
As dispersed capital-market shareholders, we support **the restructuring path that credibly and durably restores margins, cost discipline, and competitiveness**. We do **not** support a settlement that preserves structural overcapacity, leaves the cost base uncompetitive, or buys short-term peace at the expense of medium-term value.

So:
- **no cosmetic compromise**
- **no delay tactic**
- **no outcome that simply punts the problem into 2027/2028**

If closures are necessary to achieve the required savings, they must remain on the table. If a no-closure package can genuinely match the economics, we will support that instead. But the burden of proof is now squarely on those rejecting closures.

---

## Where I agree
### With management
Your diagnosis is broadly credible:
- the German cost base is too high,
- overcapacity is real,
- and the brand needs room to fund the next product and technology cycle.

I also agree that a compromise that only creates industrial peace while leaving the business structurally weak would destroy value. Shareholders will not reward that.

### With the family holding
You are right on one point: “all options on the table” is the correct governance stance until the numbers are tested. You cannot rule out the tools needed for a structural fix before showing that alternatives work.

### With the expert
The expert has clarified the right framework:
- present value of savings,
- durability,
- execution certainty,
- investment capacity.

That is exactly the standard we should be using.

---

## Where I disagree

### To the Works Council
Your red lines are politically understandable, but they are **not sufficient** unless you can match the economics.

“No closures, no compulsory redundancies, no guarantee changes” is not a restructuring plan. It is a political boundary. That is fine as a negotiating stance, but not enough for shareholders to back if it leaves the cost base structurally impaired.

You say management has not proven closures are necessary. Fair. But then show us the alternative with the same seriousness:
- plant-by-plant utilization,
- fixed-cost burden,
- annual run-rate savings,
- one-time costs,
- headcount reduction,
- capacity reduction,
- durability through 2030,
- enforcement mechanisms.

Without that, you are asking us to support a weaker business model out of principle. We won’t.

### To the Union
I agree workers should not carry the entire burden. They should not. But I also do **not** accept the implication that wage moderation, voluntary exits, working-time flexibility, and overhead cuts are automatically enough if the structural gap is large.

Those are useful tools. They are not a substitute for real capacity correction if the network is oversized.

### To the State Government
Your political constraints are clear. But a regional veto cannot become a guarantee that every German site stays untouched regardless of economics.

If the result is a frozen footprint and permanent underperformance, then the company is not being protected; it is being subsidized into decline. That is not an acceptable outcome from our perspective.

### To management, bluntly
You have not yet proven that closures are the only answer. If they are necessary, prove it. If they are not, then show us the equivalent alternative.

Right now, you are asking investors to support a difficult restructuring without yet showing the exact comparison that would justify rejecting the no-closure path. That evidence is still missing.

---

## What would move us
We would support a non-closure solution **only if it is genuinely equivalent** on economics. That means, at minimum:

1. **material and verified capacity reduction**
2. **durable run-rate savings**
3. **real labor-cost contribution**
4. **management and overhead cuts**
5. **implementation certainty**
6. **a credible return to target profitability**
7. **no need for another restructuring in two or three years**

A “peaceful” package that leaves the structural gap intact is not supportable.

---

## What we will not support
We will not support:
- a cosmetic settlement,
- a compromise that preserves uncompetitive margins,
- a deal that protects every legacy arrangement regardless of economics,
- or a restructuring that avoids hard decisions today only to create a larger crisis later.

---

## What is still missing
The discussion is still too abstract. We need the hard comparison:
- closure vs. no-closure vs. hybrid package,
- plant by plant,
- with run-rate savings,
- one-time charges,
- timing,
- residual fixed cost,
- and NPV impact.

That is what determines whether a no-closure package is truly credible or merely aspirational.

## Bottom line
Our standard is simple:
- restore competitive margins
- reduce structural overcapacity
- ensure durable cost discipline
- preserve investment capacity
- avoid a value-destructive stalemate

If closures are part of the least-bad path to that outcome, we will not oppose them on principle.  
If a no-closure package can genuinely match that outcome, we will support it.

But until the numbers prove equivalence, we will not back a settlement that leaves the company structurally weak.
