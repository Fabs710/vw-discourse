I will be just as direct in round 3.

## My position has not changed
We do **not** accept:
- **German plant closures**
- **compulsory redundancies**
- **breaking the existing job-security framework**
- **any restructuring that turns co-determination into a formality**

If the company needs restructuring, then it must be a restructuring **with** the workforce, not **against** it.

## Where I agree
I agree with the expert, management, the state, and even investors on one point:  
**the current structure is not sustainable.**

That is precisely why we are not defending the status quo. We are defending **sites, jobs, and the company’s long-term industrial base**. Those are not the same thing.

I also agree that numbers matter. Good. Then let us stop hiding behind slogans like “all options on the table” and “hard decisions.” Put the alternatives on the table and test them honestly.

## Where I strongly disagree

### To management
You still have not proven that closures are necessary rather than preferred.

The expert said something important: if the gap is moderate, a no-closure package may be feasible; if the gap is large, closures become more likely. Fine. But that is not a blank cheque for management to jump straight to closures. It means **you must prove the size of the gap and compare options properly**.

So far, what I hear is:
- “We need lower cost.”
- “We need lower capacity.”
- “We need execution certainty.”
- “Therefore closures may be necessary.”

That is not an analysis. That is a conclusion looking for evidence.

If you want to claim that non-closure options are insufficient, then show:
- plant-by-plant utilization,
- fixed-cost burden,
- the exact savings from each measure,
- and why those savings cannot be achieved through internal reallocation, working-time changes, voluntary exits, overhead cuts, and product allocation.

Until then, closures are not justified. They are a threat.

### To the family holding
You keep saying “all options remain on the table.” In practice, that means all the pressure stays on the workforce while capital keeps its escape routes open.

That is not stewardship. That is leverage.

If you genuinely care about the company’s future, then back a solution that preserves industrial capability and labour peace. Do not hide behind the word “discipline” to force through an industrial retreat.

And let me be clear: “mathematically equivalent” savings are not something labour has to conjure out of thin air while management controls the balance sheet and product allocation. If you want equivalence, you need to engage on the **actual levers**:
- management compensation and overhead,
- investment sequencing,
- internal transfer of volume,
- redesign of work organization,
- and owner-side support for the transition.

### To investors
You are correct that a cosmetic compromise is useless. But your current standard is too narrow: you treat every site as a line item and every safeguard as friction.

A package that destroys trust, triggers strikes, and permanently damages the company’s labour relations is not “certainty.” It is a risk premium with a high probability of materializing. So yes, I understand your desire for durable savings. What I do **not** accept is the assumption that closures are automatically the best or only credible route.

The expert explicitly said the most realistic outcome is often a **hybrid package**. Good — but a hybrid does **not** mean closures by default. It means combining:
- working-time flexibility,
- voluntary instruments,
- redeployment,
- product allocation,
- and management/owner contributions.

If you want to argue for closures, then prove that no-closure alternatives fail on the same metrics, not just that they are less convenient.

### To the state government
You are right to reject closures and mass layoffs. But I need more than political support. I need the state to use its leverage to insist on:
- binding site security,
- binding investment commitments,
- and a restructuring that does not hollow out plants by stealth.

Soft wording about a “substantial core” is not enough. We have been through that game before. It becomes gradual erosion dressed up as compromise. We will not accept that.

## On the expert’s analysis
The expert made the central issue clear:  
**the right test is whether the alternative package delivers the same present value of savings, with similar durability and acceptable risk.**

We accept that test. But we reject the lazy assumption that only closures can meet it.

Why? Because the company has multiple instruments before it touches the irreversible one:
- capacity reduction through shifts and volume changes,
- voluntary exits,
- temporary working-time measures,
- wage moderation by agreement,
- overhead and management cost cuts,
- redeployment and retraining,
- product allocation and platform planning.

If management says these are not enough, then the burden is on management to quantify that honestly.

## What we are prepared to discuss
We are prepared to discuss a **quantified no-closure package** that includes:
- voluntary rather than compulsory workforce reduction,
- internal redeployment,
- shifts and working-time flexibility,
- temporary employee contributions,
- management and overhead contributions,
- and binding investment commitments.

But “discuss” does **not** mean “accept closures if the numbers are inconvenient.” It means: show us a plan that preserves every German plant, and we will evaluate it on its merits.

## What we are not prepared to discuss
We are **not** prepared to negotiate on the premise that:
- plant closures are legitimate starting points,
- forced redundancies are an acceptable fallback,
- or job-security guarantees are just obstacles to competitiveness.

They are not obstacles. They are the foundation of trust and long-term industrial stability.

## Co-determination is not a side issue
Let me be very clear here:  
co-determination is not something management and owners can preserve in form while emptying it of substance.

If restructuring decisions are predetermined by shareholder preference and then “consulted” with labour afterward, that is not co-determination. That is theatre. We will not participate in that.

## Bottom line
Here is the real choice:

- **Either** the company brings a genuine restructuring plan that preserves **all German plants**, avoids **compulsory redundancies**, and distributes the burden fairly across management, owners, and capital,
- **or** there is no basis for agreement from our side.

We are ready to work on the first option.  
We will actively resist the second.

And because the stakes are so high, I am not interested in further vague assurances. Bring the quantified alternative. Bring the binding guarantees. Bring the real burden-sharing.

If you cannot do that, then you should stop pretending closures are the only serious answer.
