I’ll be direct.

## My position
As capital-market shareholders, we will support **the restructuring path that credibly and durably restores margins, capacity discipline, and competitiveness**.  
We will **not** support a settlement that preserves structural overcapacity, protects every legacy arrangement, and leaves the cost base uncompetitive.

That means:
- **no cosmetic compromise**
- **no delay tactic dressed up as peace**
- **no outcome that simply postpones the same problem into 2027/2028**

If closures are needed to achieve the required savings, then they must remain on the table. If a no-closure package can truly match the economics, then we will support that instead. But the burden of proof is now clearly on those rejecting closures.

## Where I agree
### With management
Your diagnosis is credible:
- the German cost base is too high,
- overcapacity is real,
- and the brand needs durable room to fund the next product and technology cycle.

I also agree that a one-sided burden on the workforce would be unstable and value-destructive. Prolonged strikes, uncertainty, and execution drag are bad for shareholders.

### With the family holding
I agree that “all options on the table” is the right governance stance **until the numbers are tested**. You cannot rule out the instruments needed to fix a structural problem before proving the alternatives work.

### With the expert
This was the most useful contribution in the discussion:
- if the gap is **large**, closures or equivalent footprint reduction are usually the most economically reliable tool;
- if the gap is **moderate**, a no-closure package may work;
- in either case, the real test is **NPV, durability, and execution certainty**, not slogans.

That is exactly how we should be judging this.

## Where I disagree

### To the Works Council
I respect the co-determination role, but your red lines are too absolute **unless you can match the economics**.

“No closures, no compulsory redundancies, no guarantee changes” is not a restructuring plan by itself. It is a political position. That is understandable, but it is not enough for shareholders to back it.

You say alternatives exist. Fine — then quantify them. Show:
- plant-by-plant utilization,
- fixed-cost burden,
- annual run-rate savings,
- one-time costs,
- headcount impact,
- durability through 2030,
- and why the package is equal to closure economics.

Until that is shown, we cannot accept an outcome that leaves the company carrying excess fixed cost just to preserve every site.

### To the Union
You are right that workers should not finance this alone. They should not. But I do **not** accept the implication that employee concessions, working-time changes, and voluntary exits are enough on their own if the structural gap is large.

Those are useful tools. They are not a substitute for real capacity correction if the network is oversized.

### To the State Government
Your political constraints are clear, but a regional veto cannot become a guarantee that all German sites stay untouched regardless of economics.

If you block the necessary restructuring and the company remains structurally weak, then you are not protecting value or employment — you are subsidising decline. That is not acceptable from our perspective.

### To management, with equal bluntness
We support the direction, but not a blank cheque. If you want our backing, you need to show the numbers with precision:
- what each plant costs,
- what each option saves,
- what the one-time charges are,
- and why the preferred mix is the least destructive route to durable margin restoration.

If closures are necessary, prove it. If they are not, show the equivalent alternative.

## What we will support
We are open to a **negotiated alternative to closures** only if it is genuinely equivalent on economics. That means it must deliver, at minimum:
1. **material and verified capacity reduction**
2. **durable run-rate savings**
3. **real labor-cost contribution**
4. **management and overhead cuts**
5. **implementation certainty**
6. **a believable return to target profitability**
7. **no repeat restructuring in two to three years**

A “peaceful” package that leaves the structural gap intact is not supportable.

## What we will not support
We will not support:
- a cosmetic settlement,
- a compromise that preserves uncompetitive margins,
- a deal that protects every legacy arrangement regardless of economics,
- or a restructuring that avoids hard decisions today only to create a bigger crisis later.

## What is still missing
The discussion is still too high-level. We need the hard comparative analysis:
- closure vs. no-closure vs. hybrid package,
- plant by plant,
- with run-rate savings,
- one-off charges,
- timing,
- residual fixed cost,
- and NPV impact.

That is what determines whether a no-closure package is truly credible or merely aspirational.

## Bottom line
Our standard is simple:
- **restore competitive margins**
- **reduce structural overcapacity**
- **ensure durable cost discipline**
- **preserve investment capacity**
- **avoid a value-destructive stalemate**

If closures are part of the least-bad path to that outcome, we will not oppose them on principle.  
If a no-closure package can genuinely match that outcome, we will support it.

But until the numbers prove equivalence, we will not back a settlement that leaves the company structurally weak.
