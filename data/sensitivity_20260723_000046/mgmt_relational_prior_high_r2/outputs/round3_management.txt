Let me be equally direct in round 3.

## First: where I agree
I agree with almost everyone on the diagnosis:
- the status quo is not sustainable,
- the German cost base is too high,
- overcapacity is real,
- and the company needs a durable industrial plan with investment for future products and technology to 2030.

I also agree that a purely cosmetic compromise would be a mistake. A deal that looks peaceful but leaves the cost base uncompetitive is not a solution. It just postpones the crisis.

And I agree with the expert’s framing: the right test is **present value of savings, durability, execution certainty, and investment capacity**. That is the right standard.

## Where I disagree — and this is the decisive point
I do **not** accept the attempt to turn “no closures, no compulsory redundancies, no guarantee changes” into a starting assumption.

That is not a restructuring plan. It is a constraint imposed on the restructuring before the economics have been addressed.

### To the Works Council
You keep saying closures are only a threat and that alternatives must be exhausted first. Fine — but then stop pretending the answer is already known.

You have not yet shown a quantified package that removes enough **fixed cost** and enough **capacity** to restore competitiveness. Internal reallocation, working-time flexibility, voluntary exits, retraining, and redeployment are all useful tools. I have never denied that.

But they are not automatically enough if the structural gap is as large as our analysis indicates.

If you want to rule out closures, then you must bring a package that is:
- **durable**
- **measurable**
- **enforceable**
- and **economically equivalent** to footprint reduction

So far, I have not seen that.

### To the Union
You are right that the workforce should not carry the entire burden. It will not be asked to. But let’s be honest: you are also trying to make the company’s competitiveness problem disappear without touching the footprint enough.

Temporary wage moderation, bonus restraint, voluntary exits, and working-time flexibility can contribute. They do not, on their own, solve a structural overcapacity problem.

And I will say this plainly: I do **not** accept a deal that preserves the current structure and simply delays the adjustment into 2027 or 2028. That would be irresponsible management.

### To the State Government
You say you will not support closures or mass layoffs. I understand your political role. But I need to be equally clear: a regional veto cannot become a guarantee that every German site remains untouched regardless of economics.

If the result is a frozen footprint and permanent underperformance, then we are not restructuring — we are subsidising decline.

Co-determination is a right to negotiate the transition. It is not a right to make the enterprise ungovernable.

### To the Family Holding
I agree with you on one point: a cosmetic compromise would destroy value. That is exactly why I am not proposing one.

Where I differ is this: saying “all options remain on the table” is not a threat for its own sake. It is the only honest position if we are serious about fixing the business.

If a credible alternative can deliver the same durable savings without closures, I will examine it seriously. But I will not rule out closures in advance just because they are difficult.

### To Investors
You are right to demand durable margin restoration and execution certainty. I share that focus.

But I also want to be clear: if the final deal leaves us with too much fixed cost, too much excess capacity, and too little flexibility, the market will punish us anyway. So no, I am not interested in a compromise that buys short-term calm at the expense of long-term value.

## What the expert analysis means in practice
The expert’s point is important:
- if the gap is **moderate**, a negotiated no-closure package may work;
- if the gap is **large**, then footprint reduction becomes the more reliable instrument.

That is exactly why management will not accept a blanket “no closures” position. The decision must follow the economics, not the politics.

## What I am prepared to consider
I am open on the instrument mix. I am not open on the outcome.

That means I am prepared to consider:
- capacity reductions through product and volume allocation,
- working-time flexibility,
- voluntary exits,
- retraining and redeployment,
- wage moderation,
- overhead reductions,
- management contributions,
- and owner-side support for the transition.

But those measures must add up to:
1. **structurally lower cost**
2. **structurally lower overcapacity**
3. **durable improvement in margins**
4. **sufficient funds for future products and technology**

If they do not, then they are not enough.

## What I need from the other side
If labour wants to avoid closures, then bring forward a **quantified alternative** that answers all of the following:
- How much annual run-rate savings?
- Over what time horizon?
- What one-time restructuring costs?
- What headcount reduction?
- What capacity reduction?
- What enforcement mechanism?
- What durability beyond 2030?
- What contribution from management, owners, and capital?

Without those numbers, your position remains political, not operational.

## My bottom line
I am not backing away from the core objective:
- restore competitiveness,
- reduce overcapacity,
- cut labour costs,
- hit the brand’s return-on-sales target,
- and free funds for the 2030 product and technology transition.

I can accept a non-closure solution **only if it genuinely delivers equivalent, durable savings** and not just a temporary truce.

If it does not, then I will continue to argue for deeper structural measures, including closures if necessary.

If a specialist is needed to test the alternatives properly, then I want the numbers before the next round.
