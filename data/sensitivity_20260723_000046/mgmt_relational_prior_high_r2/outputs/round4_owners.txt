## My position
The family holding supports a restructuring that **durably restores competitiveness, margins, and investment capacity**. We will **not** support a settlement that merely preserves the current footprint, postpones the problem, or leaves structural overcapacity in place.

We also will **not** support a value-destructive confrontation. A prolonged strike, reputational damage, or operational paralysis would hurt the company and the owners alike. So our standard remains:

- real structural savings
- durable capacity and cost reduction
- a credible industrial plan
- labor peace if it can be achieved on serious terms
- no erosion of ownership governance

## Where I agree
### With management
Your diagnosis is credible:
- the German cost base is too high
- overcapacity is real
- the brand needs headroom to fund the next product cycle and technology transition

I agree that a cosmetic compromise would be a mistake. A deal that looks peaceful but leaves the business structurally uncompetitive is not a solution.

### With the expert
The expert has clarified the right test well:
- present value of savings
- durability
- execution certainty
- investment capacity

That is the correct framework. We should be judging the options against economics, not slogans.

### With investors
You are right that the market will not reward a deal that preserves structural weakness. A settlement must be economically real.

### With labour and the state, partially
I also agree that a one-sided solution is unacceptable. If all the burden falls on employees, the package will be unstable and likely fail.

## Where I disagree

### To the Works Council and Union
Your red lines are politically understandable, but they do not solve the economic problem by themselves.

You keep saying:
- no plant closures
- no compulsory redundancies
- no guarantee changes

But you have **not yet demonstrated** how that combination can deliver the same durable savings as footprint reduction if the gap is large.

Working-time flexibility, voluntary exits, redeployment, and wage moderation are useful tools. I do not dismiss them. But if they do not materially reduce fixed cost and capacity, they become a delay mechanism.

So let me be explicit: I do **not** accept a restructuring defined in advance by prohibitions on the instruments most likely to fix the problem.

### To the State Government
I respect the state’s role and the regional responsibility you carry. But a blocking minority is not a right to freeze industrial reality.

If the result is permanent overcapacity and declining competitiveness, then we are not protecting value or employment — we are postponing decline. That is not stewardship, and it is not a serious use of governance rights.

### To management, where necessary
I support the direction, but support is not a blank cheque. If you want shareholder backing for difficult measures, you still need to show the numbers with precision:
- which plants carry what fixed cost
- what each option saves
- what the one-time cost is
- and why the preferred mix is the least destructive one available

You have not yet proven that closures are the only answer. If they are necessary, prove it. If they are not, then show me the equivalent alternative.

## What would move us
We are open to **a negotiated alternative to closures** only if it is not a symbolic compromise but a genuinely equivalent restructuring.

That would require, at minimum:

- material and verified capacity reduction
- durable labor-cost contribution
- management and overhead cuts
- binding implementation mechanisms
- a time horizon long enough to matter
- and a credible return to target profitability

If a package can truly deliver that, then it deserves serious consideration. If it cannot, then I will not pretend otherwise.

## What I cannot accept
I cannot accept:
1. a deal that preserves structural overcapacity
2. a deal that protects every legacy arrangement regardless of economics
3. a deal that weakens owner governance while expecting the family to underwrite the risk
4. or a compromise that buys peace today and another crisis in two years

**Can a no-closure package really match the economic effect of closures, with similar durability and implementation certainty?**

If the answer is no, then we should stop pretending that all plants can be preserved without cost.

## Bottom line
Our family’s priority is long-term enterprise value. That means:
- competitiveness
- margin restoration
- investment capacity
- and industrial peace where possible

But long-term value is not served by wishful thinking or by preserving every site at any price. The company must be restructured seriously. If labour and the state want to avoid closures, they must bring a package that is economically equivalent, durable, and enforceable.

Until then, closures remain on the table.
