### Expert response: what the current discussion means financially and industrially

The core issue is now well-defined:

**A no-closure package can only replace closures if it delivers comparable present value of savings, comparable capacity reduction, and similar durability with acceptable execution risk.**  
That is the correct test.

## 1) What closures do better
A plant closure removes several cost layers at once:
- direct labor
- plant overhead and local support
- maintenance, utilities, and site services
- some indirect overhead through simplification
- sustaining capex and future reinvestment needs
- underutilization and complexity costs

So closures are usually the strongest tool for reducing **fixed cost** and **latent overcapacity**.

But closures also create large one-time costs:
- severance / social plan costs
- decommissioning and remediation
- transfer and ramp-up costs
- supply disruption risk
- labor conflict risk
- political and execution risk

So closures are high-savings, high-friction measures.

## 2) What non-closure measures can do
A no-closure package can still deliver material savings through:
- voluntary exits / early retirement
- working-time changes
- shift reductions or shift reconfiguration
- wage moderation or deferred pay increases
- bonus restraint
- overhead and management cost cuts
- redeployment and retraining
- product and volume reallocation across plants
- procurement/logistics redesign

These tools can be very effective if the problem is mainly cost, and only moderately effective if the problem is structural overcapacity.

## 3) The key economic distinction
The issue is not whether non-closure measures save money — they do.  
The issue is whether they can remove enough of the **fixed-cost base** and enough of the **capacity excess** to restore competitiveness.

### In practice:
- **Moderate gap:** no-closure solutions can often capture a large share of closure economics.
- **Large gap / chronic underutilization:** no-closure solutions usually fall short, because the site cost remains and the excess capacity persists.

## 4) My judgment based on the dialogue so far
Given the language used by management and capital providers, the implied problem sounds like **more than just a temporary labor-cost issue**. It sounds like a combination of:
- structural overcapacity
- high fixed industrial cost
- and weak margin performance

In that setting, **a no-closure package is possible, but only if it includes real capacity removal, not just wage or workforce adjustments**.

If the package relies mostly on:
- temporary concessions,
- voluntary exits,
- and working-time flexibility,

then it is likely to be a **delay mechanism**, not an equivalent restructuring.

## 5) What would make a no-closure plan credible
A credible alternative to closures would need to show, plant by plant:
- current utilization
- fixed cost burden
- annual run-rate savings
- one-time restructuring costs
- headcount impact
- capacity reduction
- durability through 2030
- enforcement mechanisms
- impact on investment capacity

It would also need:
- binding management and overhead cuts
- product allocation changes
- multi-year labor-cost contribution
- a clear funding source for future investment
- and a mechanism if volumes or productivity miss targets

Without those elements, it is not economically equivalent to closure.

## 6) Practical conclusion
### If the overcapacity gap is moderate:
A negotiated no-closure package could be competitive with closures on a net present value basis.

### If the gap is large:
Closures or equivalent footprint reduction are usually the more reliable path to durable competitiveness.

### What is still missing:
The discussion still lacks the essential comparison:
- **closure scenario**
- **no-closure scenario**
- **hybrid scenario**

All three should be modeled plant by plant on:
- run-rate savings
- one-time charges
- residual fixed cost
- timing
- execution risk
- and NPV

## 7) Bottom line
At this stage, I would say:

- **The parties are correct to demand quantified alternatives.**
- **Management is correct that alternatives must be economically equivalent, not merely politically preferable.**
- **Labour is correct that closures are not automatically justified without proof.**
- **But labour has not yet shown that a no-closure package can fully replicate closure economics.**

So the decision remains open, but the burden now is on the sides rejecting closures to provide a **fully quantified, enforceable, durable no-closure alternative**.
