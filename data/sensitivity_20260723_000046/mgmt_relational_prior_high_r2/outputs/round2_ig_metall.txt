I’ll be very clear.

### Where I agree
I agree with the works council on the essentials:
- **no plant closures**
- **no compulsory redundancies**
- **no dismantling of co-determination**
- **no one-sided restructuring where workers pay for management’s mistakes**

I also agree with management, the owners, and investors on one point: **the status quo is not sustainable**. The company does need a credible industrial plan, lower costs, and investment in future products. So I am not defending inaction.

### Where I disagree
What I do **not** accept is the repeated insistence that closures and forced layoffs must remain on the table as the default answer. That is not a neutral “option mix.” It is a threat that puts all the pressure on the workforce before any serious alternative has been properly tested.

I also do **not** accept the idea that “durable savings” automatically means plant shutdowns. That is simply not proven. If management claims there is no other way, then management has to show the numbers, not just assert them.

### My position
Our bargaining position remains:

1. **No plant closures**
2. **No compulsory redundancies**
3. **No weakening of the collective bargaining framework**
4. **Fair burden-sharing across all sides**
5. **A binding industrial future for the German sites**

We are open to a serious contribution from employees — but only if it is part of a balanced deal. That can include:
- wage moderation or temporary concessions,
- reduction or suspension of bonuses,
- working-time flexibility,
- voluntary exit instruments,
- training and redeployment,
- and capacity reduction through shifts and volume changes.

But I will be explicit: **employees will not carry the whole burden** while management preserves its own room to maneuver and owners demand “discipline” from a distance.

### To management
You say alternatives may not be enough. Then bring a quantified comparison:
- What savings do you actually need?
- Over what time horizon?
- What is the savings impact of each measure?
- What is the cost of strikes, conflict, and loss of trust if you insist on closures?

Right now, you are asking the workforce to accept irreversible damage without proving necessity.

### To the controlling family
I agree that the company needs long-term stewardship. But long-term stewardship does **not** mean keeping every hard option open until the workforce folds. It means finding a durable solution that preserves industrial capability and social stability. If you push too hard on closures, you may “win” a restructuring and lose the company’s labour peace, capability, and legitimacy.

### To the state government
I appreciate your support for site protection. But “substantial core employment” is not enough as a phrase. We need **binding safeguards** and a clear structure for how work, volumes, and investment will be distributed across the sites.

### To investors
You are right that prolonged conflict destroys value. Exactly. That is why a one-sided closure strategy is risky, not prudent. If you want execution certainty, then back a settlement that the workforce can actually support. You do not get certainty by escalating until there is a strike.

### What could move us
A settlement could be possible if it includes:
- **binding site security through 2030**
- **no forced redundancies**
- **a negotiated employee contribution**
- **management and owner-side concessions**
- **real investment commitments**
- **capacity adjustments instead of closures**
- **a durable restoration of the collective agreement**

If management comes back with a package built on those principles, we are ready to negotiate seriously. If the package is still built around closures and coercion, then there is no basis for agreement and the conflict will intensify.

### Bottom line
We are prepared to contribute to saving the company.  
We are **not** prepared to finance restructuring alone.  
And we are **not** prepared to trade away jobs and collective standards for promises that can be withdrawn later.
