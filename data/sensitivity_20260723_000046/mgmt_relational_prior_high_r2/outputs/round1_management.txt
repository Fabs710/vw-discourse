Let me be equally clear.

I hear the rejection of closures and compulsory redundancies. I understand why those red lines are being drawn. But I cannot accept a framework that starts and ends with “no closures, no layoffs, no guarantee changes” while leaving the company with an uncompetitive German cost base and excess capacity. That would not be a restructuring. It would be postponing the crisis.

The reality is this:

- European demand is shrinking.
- Competition is intensifying, including from lower-cost producers.
- Our German factory costs are materially above target.
- The core brand is missing its return-on-sales objective.
- We need cash and margin to invest in the next product cycle and the 2030 technology transition.

So yes, the company needs change. But it has to be **structural change**, not symbolic adjustment.

Where I agree:
- We do need an industrial plan.
- We do need investment in future products and technologies.
- We do need a negotiated solution rather than unilateral escalation if at all possible.
- We do need alternatives to pure mass layoffs where they can achieve durable savings.

Where I do not agree:
- I do **not** accept a position that rules out plant closures in advance.
- I do **not** accept a blanket promise of no compulsory redundancies if the savings required cannot otherwise be delivered.
- I do **not** accept preserving every site and every legacy guarantee as a sacred principle while the business model deteriorates.

If the workforce wants to argue for keeping every plant open, then it must also explain how the company gets to a sustainable cost base. “Capacity adjustments” and “voluntary instruments” may help at the margin, but they are not enough if the scale of overcapacity is as serious as our analysis shows. We are talking about a structural gap, not a temporary dip.

On burden-sharing: I agree that this cannot be one-sided. Management, the owners, and the broader stakeholder group all have responsibilities. But burden-sharing does not mean the same burden for everyone. The workforce impact is real, but the priority has to be restoring competitiveness. If we fail there, we will lose far more jobs later.

So my position is:

1. **We need a durable reduction in cost and capacity.**
2. **We are open on the instrument mix** — closures, production shifts, working-time measures, and employee contributions can all be part of the package.
3. **We need a real labour-cost contribution**, not a cosmetic one.
4. **We need binding commitments on investment and future products**, but those commitments must be matched by real structural relief.
5. **A deal that leaves the cost base uncompetitive is not acceptable.**

If the union and works council want to avoid closures, then bring forward a package that produces equivalent savings with the same durability. If that package is credible, we will examine it seriously. But if the answer is simply to veto the hard measures and hope the numbers improve, I will not support that.
