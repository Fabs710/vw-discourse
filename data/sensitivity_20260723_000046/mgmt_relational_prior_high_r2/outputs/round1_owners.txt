I will be direct.

I agree with one core point from management: **the status quo is not sustainable**. The company needs a durable improvement in cost and competitiveness, and it needs room to invest in the next product cycle and the technology transition. If the German base remains structurally uncompetitive, value erosion will continue. That is not acceptable to us as long-term owners.

I also agree with the union, works council, and state government on one important point: **a one-sided solution is the wrong solution**. If the entire adjustment burden lands on the workforce while everyone else protects their position, the result will be conflict, loss of trust, and ultimately weaker enterprise value. That would be irresponsible stewardship.

Where I disagree is the attempted hard line against all closures and all compulsory redundancies.

### On plant closures
I do **not** accept an automatic veto on restructuring instruments before the facts are tested. If there is persistent overcapacity, then some sites, lines, or volumes may have to change materially. Pretending otherwise would simply delay the pain and deepen the eventual damage. What I cannot support is a posture that makes every German plant untouchable regardless of economics.

That said, I am not endorsing closures as an ideological starting point. I am saying the company must be allowed to consider them if — and only if — they are genuinely required for a credible turnaround and there is no equivalent alternative delivering durable savings.

### On compulsory redundancies
I am open to avoiding forced layoffs **if** management and labour can produce a package that is genuinely sufficient and durable. But I will not rule out compulsory measures in advance if that becomes the only way to restore the business. A promise that sounds humane today but condemns the company to weaker margins and future decline is not stewardship.

### On guarantees and co-determination
I support the governance structure and the special role of co-determination. I am not interested in bypassing it or triggering a constitutional fight. But that structure is not meant to freeze the industrial footprint forever. It exists to force serious negotiation and balance, not to prevent adaptation.

So my position is:

1. **The company needs structural change, not cosmetic savings.**
2. **All options should remain on the table**, including closures if necessary.
3. **But alternatives must be fully explored first** and measured against the same savings and durability standard.
4. **There must be a real employee contribution**, but not a symbolic one.
5. **Management, owners, and capital must also bear responsibility** — through restrained capital allocation, governance discipline, and support for a credible restructuring plan.
6. **Any solution must preserve long-term enterprise value and labour peace**, because endless confrontation destroys both.

### What would move us
A credible package would need to show, in concrete terms:
- how much cost is removed,
- how long the savings last,
- how investment in future products is protected,
- and why the chosen mix is better than closures.

If the union and works council can put forward an alternative that truly matches the scale of the problem, I will take it seriously. But a package that only says “no closures, no layoffs” without a mathematically credible substitute will not move me.

And to be equally clear to management: if you come back with a plan that treats plant closure as the easiest answer rather than the last resort, you should expect strong resistance. This is a major industrial asset, not a disposable cost center. We will not support value destruction in the name of speed.

So: **I am open to a tough restructuring, but not to a blind rush into closures.** Give me a plan that restores competitiveness with durability and stability. If you cannot, then the burden of proof remains on management.
