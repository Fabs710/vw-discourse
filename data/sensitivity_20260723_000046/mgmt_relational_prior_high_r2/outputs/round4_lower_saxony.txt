I will be equally direct.

## My position
The regional state government **does not support German plant closures, mass layoffs, or any restructuring that weakens co-determination and our veto role**. That remains our line. It is not tactical language; it reflects our responsibility as an anchor shareholder and as a political actor for the region.

At the same time, we are **not** defending the status quo. The company needs a real restructuring, lower structural costs, and investment in future products. But the adjustment must preserve the company as a regional economic anchor, not hollow it out.

## Where I agree
### With management
I agree with the diagnosis:
- the current German cost base is too high,
- overcapacity is real,
- and the company needs durable savings and investment to remain competitive.

I also accept the expert’s warning that temporary or reversible measures alone may be insufficient if the gap is large. That is a fair point.

### With labour
I agree that a one-sided solution is unacceptable. Workers should not be the only adjustment variable. I also agree that any workable package needs real investment, real burden-sharing, and a future industrial plan.

### With the family holding and investors
I agree that a cosmetic compromise is not enough. If the company remains structurally weak, value destruction continues. So yes, this must be serious, quantified, and durable.

## Where I disagree
### To management
You have still not proven that closures are unavoidable. Repeating that the gap is structural does not automatically justify plant shutdowns. The expert said the right test is whether a no-closure package can match the economics on a present-value basis. Fine — then prove that closures are necessary, not merely convenient.

You need to show:
- plant-by-plant utilization,
- fixed cost burden,
- the savings from each option,
- and why internal reallocation, working-time changes, voluntary exits, overhead cuts, and product allocation changes are insufficient.

Until that is done, closures remain an unproven choice, not a necessity.

### To the family holding
“All options on the table” sounds balanced, but in practice it often means putting the social and political burden on the workforce and the regions first. That is not stewardship. If you want discipline, then show discipline on your side too: management cost, overhead, product allocation, and investment sequencing are all part of the equation.

### To investors
You are right to reject a cosmetic settlement. I agree with that. But I do not accept the implication that closures are automatically the only serious answer. The expert explicitly said a hybrid package is often the realistic outcome. A hybrid does not mean “close plants by default”; it means combining multiple tools while still protecting the regional industrial base.

## What the expert analysis means for me
The expert’s assessment is useful because it makes the real question plain:
- if the gap is **moderate**, a no-closure package may be feasible;
- if the gap is **large**, closures become more attractive economically.

That does **not** mean the state now accepts closures. It means management has to prove the scale of the gap and compare the alternatives honestly.

And I will be equally clear: a package that “works” only if it removes jobs and sites in the state is not acceptable to us, because the social and regional damage is not a side issue. It is part of the decision.

## What I am prepared to support
I am prepared to work for a **quantified, enforceable restructuring package without German plant closures**, provided it includes:
- durable cost reductions,
- measurable capacity reduction,
- voluntary instruments rather than compulsory redundancies,
- working-time flexibility,
- redeployment and retraining,
- management and overhead contributions,
- owner-side support for the transition,
- and binding investment commitments for future products and technologies.

That is the middle ground I can defend. Not because it is easy, but because it protects the company’s future **and** its regional role.

## What I cannot support
I cannot support:
1. German plant closures,
2. compulsory redundancies as the default tool,
3. a restructuring that sidelines co-determination,
4. or a deal that treats the state’s governance rights as an obstacle rather than a legitimate part of the company’s structure.

## My message to management, bluntly
If you want our support, bring a **seriously quantified no-closure alternative** and show why it is not merely a delay tactic. If you cannot do that, then you have not met the burden of proof for closures either, because the state will not vote for them.
