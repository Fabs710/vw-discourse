I will be direct.

## My overall position
I support a **serious restructuring**. I do **not** support a restructuring that is built around **German plant closures or compulsory redundancies**. As anchor shareholder and regional government, my responsibility is to protect the company’s long-term competitiveness **and** its role as a regional economic anchor. Those two goals are linked, not opposed.

## Where I agree
I agree with management, the family holding, the investors, and the expert on one important point: **temporary measures alone will not solve a structural problem**. If the company has an overcapacity and cost-base issue, then the solution must be durable.

I also agree that:
- open conflict is costly,
- the company needs savings that are real,
- and the German industrial base must remain competitive.

## Where I disagree
I disagree very clearly with management’s insistence that **closures and compulsory redundancies must remain on the table as normal restructuring tools**.

You have not shown that they are the only credible route. You have shown urgency, not necessity. That matters.

The works council and union are right to insist that:
- internal redeployment,
- voluntary departures,
- retirement corridors,
- working-time tools,
- qualification,
- productivity measures,
- and a fair employee contribution

must be exhausted first.

That is not avoidance. That is responsible restructuring in a co-determined company.

## To management
You keep saying the footprint must shrink materially. Possibly. But **“must shrink” does not automatically mean “must close plants.”** That is the leap I reject.

If you want the state and workforce to accept serious concessions, then put the numbers on the table:
- site-by-site capacity utilization,
- fixed-cost burden by plant,
- savings from each alternative,
- expected strike and delay costs,
- and the 2030 employment and investment path.

Without that, the closure argument is incomplete.

And let me be clear: if the only way management can make its case is by threatening plant closures before proving all other options insufficient, then it is putting pressure ahead of analysis. I will not endorse that.

## To the Works Council
I agree with your core line: **no plant closures in Germany and no compulsory redundancies**.

That is not a sentimental position. It is the right position for a company that is a regional anchor and a co-determined enterprise. The burden of adjustment cannot be shifted first and foremost onto workers and the region.

I also agree that the workforce can discuss a **limited, temporary, and fair contribution** — but only if it is matched by real concessions from management and owners, and only if it comes with binding site and investment guarantees.

What I would press you on is not whether the red line exists — it does — but how you quantify the alternative path. If you can show how capacity reduction, redeployment, and voluntary instruments deliver the needed savings, that strengthens the no-closure case. If not, management will keep arguing that the alternative is not serious.

## To the union
I agree that a contribution must not become permanent wage erosion. That is an important line.

A fair package could include:
- temporary bonus waivers,
- moderate wage restraint,
- working-time flexibility,
- solidarity contributions,
- and voluntary instruments.

But it must also include:
- no forced layoffs,
- no closures,
- preservation of the collective bargaining framework,
- and binding commitments for future work in Germany.

The workforce cannot be expected to underwrite the restructuring alone. If management and ownership want contributions, they must also contribute materially.

## To the family holding
I understand the pressure for competitiveness and I respect the need for realism. But I do not accept the idea that closures are a routine, acceptable tool in this company.

Once that door is opened too easily, the burden of strategic failure falls on regions and workers first. That is not long-term value preservation; it is social and industrial short-termism.

If the family really wants to protect enterprise value, then it should back a solution that keeps industrial capacity in Germany, preserves skills, and avoids an irreversible damage to trust.

## To the investors
You are right to insist on a credible path back to margins. I do not dispute that.

But you are underestimating the cost of a “hard” solution if it destroys social peace, industrial know-how, and local legitimacy. A deal that looks neat in the spreadsheet but triggers lasting conflict is not value-maximizing.

If you want a stable return, then support a stable industrial base. That means a negotiated restructuring that preserves sites and jobs where possible, not a simplistic closure-first logic.

## On the expert input
The expert’s analysis was useful. It confirmed that:
- temporary measures alone are insufficient,
- the company needs structural savings,
- and a hybrid package may be economically sensible.

I accept that.

What I do **not** accept is the conclusion that because closures can sometimes be cheaper in NPV terms, they should automatically remain available here. NPV is not the only criterion. In this company, governance stability, regional employment, skills retention, and industrial peace are material value factors too.

If we include those properly, then a negotiated no-closure restructuring can be the better long-term choice.

## What would move me
I would support a negotiated package if it delivers all of the following:
1. **material and durable cost reduction**
2. **meaningful capacity reduction without plant closures**
3. **a real workforce contribution that is temporary and limited**
4. **binding investment and product commitments in Germany**
5. **protection of co-determination and the state’s blocking role**
6. **a credible 2030 competitiveness path**

If a proposal meets those conditions, I am prepared to support it. If it does not, then it is not an acceptable restructuring, regardless of how often it is called “necessary.”

## What is not acceptable
Not acceptable are:
- plant closures in Germany,
- compulsory redundancies,
- dismantling the job-security framework,
- or a settlement that treats the regional economy as disposable.

## Bottom line
My position remains firm:

- **Yes** to real restructuring
- **Yes** to capacity adjustment
- **Yes** to shared sacrifice
- **No** to closures
- **No** to forced layoffs
- **No** to sidelining co-determination or the state’s veto role

Bring a quantified alternative that protects sites, jobs, and the company’s industrial future. That is the standard I will judge by.
