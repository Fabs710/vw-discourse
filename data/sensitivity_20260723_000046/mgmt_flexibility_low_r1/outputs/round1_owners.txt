I agree with the basic diagnosis: the company needs a real restructuring, not a cosmetic one. The cost base, capacity structure, and competitiveness gap are not sustainable if we want to protect long-term value.

But I need to be equally clear on what I do **not** accept.

### Where I agree
- The company cannot drift.
- Structural savings have to be real and durable.
- A purely symbolic contribution from any side will not solve the problem.
- The future industrial base in Germany matters, because this company’s value depends on it.

### Where I disagree
I do **not** accept the position that the only credible route is to keep every site, avoid every closure, and preserve all existing guarantees unchanged.

If that is the starting point, then we are not discussing restructuring — we are discussing postponement of decline.

From an ownership perspective, that is not acceptable. The family holding’s responsibility is to protect the enterprise’s long-term value, not to defend every legacy structure if it has become structurally uncompetitive.

### On plant closures
I am not endorsing closures as a first choice. But I am also not willing to rule them out in advance.

If management concludes that capacity must come out of the system and that some sites are structurally non-competitive, then that reality has to be faced. A blanket prohibition on closures would weaken the credibility of the restructuring and, in the end, risk more value destruction across the whole group.

### On compulsory redundancies
Here I am more cautious. I do not see forced redundancies as the preferred route, and I would expect management to exhaust alternatives:
- voluntary departures,
- retirement corridors,
- redeployment,
- qualification,
- working-time adjustments,
- and site-level flexibility.

But I will not say that no employment reduction can occur. If the economics require it, then a serious agreement must reflect that. The question is not whether there is pain, but whether the pain is structured intelligently and fairly.

### On the union and works council position
I respect the insistence on social responsibility, but I cannot support a line that effectively says:
- no closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- no meaningful labour-cost adjustment,
- and strong guarantees on top.

That combination is not a restructuring package. It is a request that the owners absorb all the economic burden while the operating model remains largely intact.

That is not a sustainable basis for value creation.

### On the state government
I understand the regional and political responsibilities, and I share the interest in stability. But a blocking position used to freeze necessary adjustment is not stewardship. If the company’s competitiveness is not restored, the state’s stake is damaged too.

So I would say this plainly: the state’s concern for employment is legitimate, but it cannot become a veto over all meaningful change.

### What would move me
I would support a settlement only if it delivers:
1. **Clear and durable cost reduction**
2. **Meaningful capacity adjustment**
3. **A credible competitiveness gain by 2030**
4. **Labour peace and operational stability**
5. **A protected path for future investment in Germany**

If those conditions can be met through alternatives to broad closures, I am open. But if the alternatives are too weak to fix the economics, then they are not enough.

### Bottom line
My position is straightforward:
- I support a serious restructuring.
- I support management having real options, including closures if necessary.
- I do **not** support a settlement that preserves the current structure by making the company less competitive.
- I do support a negotiated solution if it is economically credible and socially stable.

So I would ask the other parties to stop treating “no closures” as a moral absolute. The only absolute I recognize is this: we cannot accept an outcome that permanently weakens the enterprise.
