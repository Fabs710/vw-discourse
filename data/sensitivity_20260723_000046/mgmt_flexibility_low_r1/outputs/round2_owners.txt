Let me respond plainly.

## Where I agree
I agree with management, the investors, and even the labour side on one essential point: **this must be a real restructuring, not a cosmetic one**. If the company’s German cost base and capacity are structurally out of line, then temporary gestures alone will not restore competitiveness.

I also agree that **prolonged conflict destroys value**. A drawn-out strike, uncertainty over output, and public escalation would be bad for everyone, including the family holding.

And I agree that **Germany remains central** to this company’s long-term value. The industrial base, engineering depth, and brand value are not optional.

## Where I disagree
I do **not** accept the labour-side and state-side position that:

- **all German plant closures must be ruled out in advance**, and
- **compulsory redundancies must be excluded categorically**, regardless of the economics.

That is not a restructuring framework. It is a constraint that may preserve the present structure, but risks preserving the underlying problem as well.

From an ownership perspective, I cannot support a settlement that simply freezes the existing footprint and cost structure while asking the company to somehow become competitive anyway. That would be value-destructive over time.

## On management’s position
Management is right that there is a structural problem and that the company needs durable savings. Where I will not follow management automatically is in assuming that closure is the default answer.

I want to be very clear:
- **closures are not my preferred route**
- **forced redundancies are not my preferred route**
- but **neither can be excluded if they are genuinely necessary**

What I need from management is not rhetoric about urgency, but a **quantified, site-by-site business case** showing:
- what savings are needed,
- what each alternative delivers,
- what capacity can be removed without closures,
- what the wage-cost contribution looks like,
- and what the 2030 margin path is under each scenario.

Without that, the argument for sweeping measures is incomplete.

## On the Works Council and union
I respect the insistence on social responsibility, but I cannot support a line that effectively says:

- no closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- no major labour-cost adjustment,
- and binding guarantees on top.

That combination would place almost all adjustment pressure on the owners and the company while leaving the operating model largely intact. That is not a balanced restructuring.

I am open to alternatives to closures if they are real and scaled:
- voluntary exits,
- retirement corridors,
- redeployment,
- working-time flexibility,
- qualification,
- site specialization,
- and a negotiated labour contribution.

But they must be sufficient in magnitude and durable enough to fix the economics. A temporary contribution that merely delays the issue is not enough.

## On the state government
I understand the political responsibility and the regional interest. But I do not accept the idea that the state’s blocking position should be used to prevent all meaningful restructuring.

A blocking right is a governance safeguard, not a licence to preserve uneconomic structures indefinitely. If the company cannot restore competitiveness, then the state’s stake — and the region’s jobs — are damaged anyway.

So I would urge the state to focus less on blanket prohibitions and more on whether the final package actually improves the enterprise’s long-term value.

## On the institutional investors
You are right to insist on competitive margins and a credible return to target. I share that objective.

Where I would push back is if “credible” is interpreted as “whatever hurts least politically.” The market will not reward a deal that looks polite but leaves the structure impaired. If the final package does not materially improve the economics, it will fail, regardless of how it is presented.

## On value and stability
My position is simple:
- I support **serious restructuring**
- I support **durable cost and capacity reduction**
- I support **labour peace if it can be achieved on a credible basis**
- I do **not** support an agreement that permanently weakens enterprise value by protecting every legacy structure

That means I am open on the method, but not on the objective. The objective must be a company that is competitive again by 2030.

## What would move me
I would support a negotiated solution if it delivers all of the following:
1. **material and durable cost reduction**
2. **meaningful capacity reduction**
3. **a credible competitiveness path**
4. **stability and labour peace**
5. **continued investment in Germany**
6. **a structure that does not permanently impair the enterprise’s value**

If those conditions can be met **without** closures, that is better. If they cannot be met without some hard decisions, then those hard decisions cannot be ruled out simply because they are uncomfortable.

## What is not acceptable
What I cannot support is:
- a settlement that keeps every site open at all costs,
- preserves the full legacy framework unchanged,
- and calls that a restructuring.

That would be postponing decline, not fixing it.

So my bottom line remains:
- **No cosmetic compromise**
- **No preservation of the status quo**
- **Yes to a serious, quantified restructuring package**
- **No automatic veto on closures or workforce reduction if the economics require them**

If the other parties want support from the controlling shareholder, they need to bring a package that truly restores competitiveness, not just one that is easier to defend publicly.
