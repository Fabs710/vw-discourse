Yes. Here is the decision-relevant comparison from a labour-economics and restructuring-cost perspective.

## Bottom line
A **no-closure restructuring can be cheaper than plant closures** if it can eliminate a large share of the cost gap through:
- voluntary exits,
- retirement corridors,
- working-time reductions,
- redeployment,
- shift/line rationalization,
- temporary wage moderation,
- and productivity measures,

**and** if it avoids major strike disruption and legal/political delay.

But if the overcapacity is large and durable, or if the no-closure package only shifts costs into temporary measures without materially reducing fixed costs, then **closures and compulsory redundancies usually deliver higher long-run recurring savings** — albeit with much higher one-off costs, higher industrial-relations risk, and often a slower or more uncertain implementation.

So the economically correct answer is not “closures are always cheaper” or “no-closure is always cheaper.” It depends mainly on:
1. **the size of the structural overcapacity**,  
2. **how much of it can be removed without closing sites**,  
3. **the one-off cost of closures vs voluntary measures**, and  
4. **the expected delay/strike risk under each path**.

---

## 1) Cost logic: closures vs alternatives

### A. Plant closure / compulsory redundancy path
Usually involves:

**One-off costs**
- severance packages,
- social plan costs,
- transfer company / outplacement,
- pension bridging,
- decommissioning and site-remediation,
- write-downs of assets and tooling,
- logistics/network reconfiguration,
- legal and consultation costs,
- possible penalties if supply contracts are disrupted.

**Recurring savings**
- elimination of site fixed costs,
- lower overhead,
- lower labour cost,
- lower maintenance/energy/utilities,
- less capex needed for that site,
- simpler network and management structure.

**Main economic advantage**
- Largest and most durable recurring savings if the site is genuinely redundant.

**Main economic disadvantage**
- Very high one-off cost and high risk of delay, especially in Germany with co-determination, works council veto power, bargaining conflict, and likely strike action.

---

### B. No-closure / negotiated alternatives
Usually involves:

**One-off costs**
- voluntary exit bonuses,
- early retirement top-ups,
- qualification and redeployment costs,
- temporary wage concessions negotiated as offsets,
- administration of work-time changes,
- possible internal transfer costs,
- support for short-time work or partial utilization gaps.

**Recurring savings**
- lower labour cost,
- fewer employees through attrition,
- reduced hours,
- shift reductions,
- productivity gains,
- some overhead reduction if lines are rationalized.

**Main economic advantage**
- Lower conflict risk, lower legal delay, preserve know-how, preserve optionality, usually much lower one-off cash outlay than closure.

**Main economic disadvantage**
- Savings may be smaller and slower, and the fixed-cost base may remain too high if sites are kept open with insufficient volume.

---

## 2) Which is usually cheaper in net present value terms?

### No-closure is usually cheaper when:
- the cost gap is **moderate**, not extreme;
- there is substantial scope for **voluntary attrition** and early retirement;
- sites can be **reconfigured** to run with fewer shifts or lower headcount;
- there is meaningful **internal redeployment** to growth areas;
- employees accept a **temporary and measurable contribution**;
- industrial peace is maintained;
- and the company values preserving skills and future capacity.

In such cases, the no-closure route often has:
- lower cash burn,
- lower disruption,
- faster implementation,
- and better risk-adjusted value.

### Closure is usually cheaper when:
- the site is **structurally underutilized**;
- demand is unlikely to recover;
- the site has weak product allocation beyond 2030;
- fixed costs are high relative to output;
- and internal measures cannot eliminate enough capacity.

Then the recurring savings from closure can outweigh the high one-off cost over a 3–5 year horizon, especially if the plant would otherwise continue to operate below efficient scale.

---

## 3) The German-specific constraint
In Germany, the economics are heavily shaped by industrial-relations and governance constraints:

- **co-determination** makes closures slower and harder;
- works councils and unions can raise the cost of conflict;
- strikes can materially reduce output and customer confidence;
- site closures often trigger protracted negotiation over social plans;
- and a blocked restructuring can create a very expensive “delay cost.”

That means a closure that looks cheaper on paper can become more expensive in reality if it takes 12–24 months to execute and causes lost volume, delayed model launches, or supply chain disruption.

So in German carmaking, the relevant comparison is not just:
> closure savings vs alternative savings,

but:
> closure savings vs alternative savings, **minus delay, conflict, and execution risk**.

---

## 4) What matters most in the comparison model
If I were building a decision model, the critical variables would be:

1. **Current utilization by site**  
   A low-utilization plant is a closure candidate; a well-utilized plant is not.

2. **Fixed-cost burden by site**  
   Maintenance, utilities, depreciation, supervision, logistics, and local overhead.

3. **Headcount reducibility without closure**  
   How much can be removed through voluntary exits, retirement, transfer, and work-time changes?

4. **One-off restructuring cost**
   - closure severance/social plan cost,
   - vs voluntary exit/early retirement cost.

5. **Implementation delay**
   - months to agreement,
   - months to execution,
   - lost production and launch delays.

6. **Strike and legal risk**
   - expected output loss,
   - supplier disruption,
   - reputational/customer effects.

7. **Know-how loss**
   - especially in body, paint, final assembly, and launch functions.

8. **Future product allocation**
   - whether the site has confirmed platform/program volume.

## 5) Likely workforce effects by option

### Closure-led restructuring
- Largest job loss, concentrated at one or more sites.
- High risk of compulsory redundancies if voluntary exits are insufficient.
- Often requires extensive redeployment elsewhere in the group.
- Skill loss can be substantial, especially for specialized production lines.

### No-closure restructuring
- Job reduction can still be large, but usually via:
  - attrition,
  - retirement,
  - transfers,
  - reduced hours,
  - and voluntary departure.
- Less abrupt, more socially manageable.
- But if volume remains low, hidden underutilization may persist.

### Mixed package
Usually the most realistic in practice:
- no immediate closure,
- some site shrinkage,
- targeted headcount reduction,
- selective consolidation,
- and a fallback review if financial targets are missed.

This is often the best risk-adjusted compromise if the governance structure makes a pure closure strategy hard to execute.

---

## 6) Practical cost comparison: a simple rule of thumb

### No-closure is likely cheaper if:
- the company can close **most of the savings gap** with internal measures,
- the required workforce contribution is modest and temporary,
- and strike risk is contained.

### Closure becomes cheaper if:
- the company cannot reduce fixed costs enough without removing a site,
- or if keeping sites open requires permanent concessions that nearly equal the cost of closure but without eliminating the structural overcapacity.

In other words:
- **temporary concessions are cheaper than closure**,
- but **permanent overstaffing is more expensive than closure**.

---

## 7) Key uncertainty in this case
The most important unknown is the **site-level economics**:
- Which plants are underutilized?
- How much capacity can be cut without closing?
- How much headcount can be removed by voluntary means?
- How much is the margin target still short after those measures?

Without that site-by-site data, nobody can honestly prove that closures are either necessary or avoidable.

## 8) My expert assessment
From a restructuring-cost standpoint, the economically credible sequence is usually:

1. **Voluntary exits / early retirement**
2. **Working-time and shift reductions**
3. **Redeployment and qualification**
4. **Temporary wage moderation / workforce contribution**
5. **Targeted site rationalization**
6. **Only then closure, if the residual gap remains material**

That sequence minimizes one-off cost and industrial conflict while preserving the option to escalate if the no-closure package fails.

So the answer to the dispute is:

- **If the restructuring gap is bridgeable with negotiated measures, no-closure is likely cheaper in risk-adjusted terms.**
- **If it is not bridgeable, closure-led restructuring will usually produce larger long-run savings, but only at a much higher immediate cost and with materially higher execution risk.**
