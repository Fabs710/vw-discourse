I will respond directly.

## First: what I agree with
I agree with one important point repeated by the expert, the investors, and even parts of labor: **this must be a real restructuring, not a cosmetic exercise**.

Temporary measures alone will not solve:
- excess capacity,
- an uncompetitive German cost base,
- and a brand that is missing its return-on-sales target.

So yes, we are aligned on the need for **durable, structural change**. That much is not in dispute.

## Where I fundamentally disagree
I do **not** accept the core labor-state line that:
- **all German plant closures must be ruled out**, and
- **compulsory redundancies must be excluded categorically**.

That is not a restructuring mandate. That is a pre-emptive veto on the instruments needed to restore competitiveness.

You keep asking management to “prove” closures are necessary. We are doing exactly that through the business reality:
- demand in Europe is shrinking,
- competition is intensifying,
- our cost base is too high,
- and we have overcapacity in Germany.

If the footprint remains unchanged, the economics do not improve enough. That is the point you are all avoiding.

## To the Works Council
You say closure is a high-risk move and that voluntary tools have not been exhausted. I agree that closures are serious. I do **not** agree that this means they should be ruled out.

Voluntary exits, redeployment, qualification, working-time changes, and productivity measures will be used. But if they do not deliver the scale required, then they are not a substitute for real restructuring. They are only part of it.

You also demand:
- every German plant stays open,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- protection of the job-security framework through 2030,
- and binding guarantees first.

That combination would leave us with very limited room to adjust costs and capacity. I cannot accept a deal that preserves the current footprint and then expects management to somehow deliver competitiveness anyway. That is not a viable operating model.

## To the union
I hear your point that a contribution must not become permanent wage dumping. Fair enough. But your definition of “limited” is too narrow for the scale of the problem.

Temporary bonus waivers and modest wage moderation are not enough if the company still carries too much structural cost. A real employee contribution must be:
- material,
- durable,
- and measurable in the P&L.

If it is only temporary, then it does not solve the problem — it only delays it.

And let me be clear: a warning strike may signal resolve, but it does not improve the company’s cost base. It damages output, adds urgency, and increases the pressure for harder decisions. It does not change the underlying economics.

## To the Regional State Government
You say your blocking position should prevent closures. That is a political position; it is not an economic answer.

I respect the regional responsibility. But if the state uses its blocking power to freeze all meaningful adjustment, then it is not protecting jobs — it is protecting an uncompetitive structure until the market forces an even worse outcome.

If the goal is to keep industrial value in Germany, then the state should support a solution that actually restores profitability, not one that merely postpones the adjustment.

## To the Controlling Family Holding
You are right to insist this cannot drift. You are also right that Germany remains central to the long-term value of the company.

But I need to be very clear: if ownership backs the idea that closures and headcount reduction cannot be excluded, then it must also back management when that reality becomes necessary. We cannot have a situation where everyone agrees in principle that change is needed, but nobody will accept the instruments that make change possible.

A serious ownership stance means accepting that:
- some sites may have to shrink materially,
- headcount must fall,
- and the old guarantee regime cannot remain untouched if the business is to recover.

## To the Investors
You are right to demand a quantified path to margin recovery. That is exactly what management is trying to achieve.

But I need to push back on the idea that a deal avoiding explicit closures is automatically preferable. What matters is not the label. What matters is whether the package:
- reduces fixed cost,
- lowers capacity,
- improves labor productivity,
- and gets the brand back to a competitive return on sales.

If a non-closure package cannot do that, then it is not a solution — it is a delay.

## On the expert input
The expert was useful in one respect: it confirmed that **temporary measures alone are not sufficient**.

But the expert also made clear something the labor side keeps glossing over:
- if the gap is large and persistent, then **deep workforce and capacity adjustment is unavoidable**.

That is exactly our position.

A negotiated package can be part of the answer only if it is real:
- capacity reduction with teeth,
- meaningful headcount reduction,
- material labor-cost relief,
- and implementation certainty.

What it cannot be is a way to preserve every legacy structure and call that “socially responsible restructuring.”

## What would move management
I will be precise. Management can accept alternatives to closures only if they deliver all of the following:

1. **Substantial and durable cost reduction**
2. **Meaningful and lasting capacity reduction**
3. **A real workforce reduction at scale**
4. **A material labor-cost contribution**
5. **Implementation certainty**
6. **A credible path to the 2030 margin target**

If a proposal meets those conditions without closures, we will consider it. If it does not, then it is not serious.

## What is not acceptable
Let me state the red line on our side just as clearly as others have stated theirs:

- A package that protects every site and keeps the current footprint largely intact is unacceptable.
- A package that relies on temporary concessions while leaving the structural cost issue unresolved is unacceptable.
- A package that treats the old guarantees as untouchable while asking management to restore competitiveness is unacceptable.

## On the “no closures” demand
The works council and union keep repeating that no plant closures are the minimum basis for talks. I understand the political logic. I do not accept it as a business principle.

If a site is structurally redundant, if no viable future product allocation exists, and if the cost base cannot be corrected otherwise, then closure remains a legitimate restructuring tool. That is not ideology. That is economics.

## Bottom line
We are not here to negotiate the preservation of the status quo. We are here to restore a company that can compete through 2030.

So my position remains:

- **No cosmetic compromise**
- **No settlement that leaves the German cost base uncompetitive**
- **No blanket exclusion of closures or compulsory redundancies**
- **Yes to a durable restructuring package that materially reduces capacity and cost**
- **Yes to investment in future products and technology, but only on the basis of a viable cost structure**

If the labor side wants to avoid closures, it needs to bring a package that truly matches the savings. If it cannot, then it is not presenting an alternative — it is merely rejecting the necessary adjustment.

If a specialist is needed to quantify the gap under current assumptions, I would support that, but the core issue is already clear: **the company needs deeper structural change than the labor side is prepared to concede.**
