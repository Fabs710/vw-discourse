I will be direct.

## My overall position

I support a **serious restructuring**. I do **not** support a settlement that preserves an uncompetitive footprint and merely postpones the problem.

From an ownership perspective, the objective is not to protect every legacy structure. The objective is to restore the company’s competitiveness, margins, and long-term value. If we fail to do that, then everyone loses — workers, the region, and the owners.

At the same time, I also do **not** support an unnecessarily destructive path. A prolonged conflict, a public escalation spiral, or a closure-led approach that is not economically proven would destroy value rather than create it.

So my position is:

- **no cosmetic compromise**
- **no preservation of the status quo**
- **no open-ended instability**
- **yes to structural change that actually fixes the economics**

## Where I agree

I agree with management, investors, and the expert on one key point: **temporary measures alone are not enough**.

A few voluntary exits, some wage moderation, and a short-term productivity push will not solve:
- excess capacity,
- a structurally high cost base,
- and a brand that is missing its return target.

That is just delaying the adjustment.

I also agree with labor and the state on one important point: **a solution that cannot be implemented is worthless**. If a package triggers endless blockade, it destroys value.

And I agree that Germany remains central to this company’s future. This is not about abandoning the industrial base. It is about making it viable.

## Where I disagree with labor and the state

I do **not** accept the categorical position that:

- **all German plant closures must be ruled out in advance**, and
- **compulsory redundancies must be excluded regardless of the economics**.

That is not a restructuring framework. It is a veto on the instruments that may be needed to restore competitiveness.

You keep asking management to prove necessity. That is fair. But the burden is not one-sided. If you want to rule out certain instruments entirely, you need to show that the alternatives can close the gap in a durable way.

So far, I have not seen that proof.

## To management

Management is right to say the company has a structural problem. I agree.

What I do **not** give you automatically is a blank cheque for closures. A closure is not a virtue in itself. It is only justified if:
- the site is structurally redundant,
- alternatives are insufficient,
- and the full business case still works after all execution risks are included.

So I want the numbers, not generalities.

I want a **site-by-site business case** showing:
- current capacity and utilization,
- fixed and variable cost by site,
- savings from each alternative,
- savings from closure scenarios,
- strike and delay risk,
- product allocation through 2030,
- and the margin trajectory under each package.

Without that, your argument is incomplete.

## To the Works Council

I respect your insistence on social responsibility. I also understand why “no closures” is your red line.

But I cannot support a position that effectively says:
- no closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- no lasting labor-cost adjustment,
- and binding guarantees on top.

That would leave almost all adjustment pressure on management and owners while preserving an overcostly structure. That is not a viable restructuring.

You say voluntary tools and internal redeployment have not been exhausted. Fine — they should be. But I will not pretend that makes closures impossible in principle. They remain a legitimate option if the economics require them.

## To the union

You are right that a contribution must not become permanent wage erosion. I agree with that.

But your definition of “limited” must still be large enough to matter. A symbolic contribution does not solve a structural problem.

If there is a deal, I would expect:
- temporary and measurable labor-cost moderation,
- working-time flexibility,
- productivity-linked adjustments,
- and voluntary instruments at meaningful scale.

What I cannot support is a package that protects the wage framework entirely while asking the company to absorb all restructuring pain elsewhere.

## To the state government

I respect the state’s political role and its responsibility for regional employment. But a blocking position is not a substitute for competitiveness.

If the state uses its influence to freeze every meaningful adjustment, it risks protecting jobs in the short term while weakening the enterprise in the medium term. That is not responsible stewardship.

I am open to a negotiated solution that protects the industrial future in Germany. But protecting the future does not mean preserving every site unchanged.

## To the institutional investors

You are right to focus on margins and return on sales. That is the correct question.

Where I would caution you is against treating “no closures” as automatically better or worse. The real issue is whether the final package actually restores competitiveness.

If a no-closure package achieves:
- durable cost reduction,
- meaningful capacity reduction,
- and a credible 2030 margin path,

then it can be acceptable.

If it does not, then it is not a solution.

## On the expert’s analysis

The expert has clarified the key point: **closures can produce the largest recurring savings, but they also carry high upfront cost and high execution risk**. That is exactly why I am not endorsing a closure-first approach by default.

The economically correct answer is not ideological. It is conditional.

- If a no-closure package can truly close the gap, that is preferable.
- If not, then hard decisions cannot be ruled out just because they are uncomfortable.

That is the standard I will apply.

## What would move me

I would support a negotiated package if it delivers all of the following:

1. **material and durable cost reduction**
2. **meaningful capacity reduction**
3. **a real workforce contribution**
4. **implementation certainty**
5. **a credible path to the 2030 margin target**
6. **no permanent impairment of the enterprise’s value**

That is the test.

If the labor side wants to avoid closures, then it must bring a package that truly matches the savings. If it cannot, then it is not offering an alternative — it is simply rejecting the adjustment.

## What is not acceptable

I will not support:

- a settlement that preserves every site at all costs
- a package that leaves the German cost base structurally uncompetitive
- a deal that is socially quiet but economically weak
- a prolonged conflict that destroys value
- or any outcome that permanently impairs the enterprise’s value or governance control

## Bottom line

I am open on the instrument mix. I am **not** open on the objective.

The objective must be a company that is competitive again by 2030, with durable margins and a stable industrial future in Germany.

If that can be achieved without closures, good. If it cannot, then closures cannot be excluded simply because they are difficult. But I will only support them if the business case is real, quantified, and unavoidable.
