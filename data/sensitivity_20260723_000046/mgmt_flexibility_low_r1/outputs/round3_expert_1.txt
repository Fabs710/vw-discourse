Yes. Here is a neutral restructuring-finance / labour-economics comparison of the main options, framed for a German co-determined automotive group.

## Short answer

- **Plant closures and compulsory redundancies** usually deliver the **largest recurring savings**, but also the **largest one-off restructuring costs**, the **highest execution risk**, and the **greatest industrial-relations/govemance delay**.
- A **no-closure package** can be cheaper **if** enough savings can be achieved through:
  - capacity reductions short of closure,
  - shift and working-time changes,
  - voluntary exits / retirement corridors,
  - redeployment and qualification,
  - wage moderation or temporary concessions,
  - and productivity improvements.
- In practice, the cheaper path depends on the **size and persistence of the cost gap**.  
  If the gap is large and structural, no-closure measures often become too expensive or too slow.  
  If the gap is moderate and the workforce is highly protected, negotiated alternatives can outperform closure on a **5-year NPV** basis because they avoid heavy upfront cash costs and implementation disruption.

## What matters economically

You should separate:

### 1) One-off restructuring costs
These are immediate or near-term cash costs, for example:
- severance payments,
- social plan costs,
- outplacement,
- early retirement subsidies,
- decommissioning / site exit costs,
- asset write-downs,
- contract break costs,
- transfer company costs,
- retraining and redeployment costs,
- legal / advisory / implementation costs,
- restart costs at remaining plants if production is consolidated.

For a German plant closure, these can be very large. In automotive, it is not unusual for one-off costs to reach **hundreds of millions to well over €1 billion** for a major site, depending on headcount, tenure, pension obligations, asset base, and social-plan design.

### 2) Recurring operating savings
These are the actual annual benefits:
- lower payroll,
- lower overhead,
- lower maintenance and utilities,
- fewer shifts,
- better utilization at remaining sites,
- reduced logistics complexity,
- lower fixed cost absorption losses.

Closures usually create the biggest recurring savings because they remove an entire cost base, not just labor.

## Why closures are often financially attractive on paper

A closure-led plan tends to win on pure operating economics when:
- a plant is structurally underutilized,
- product allocation will not fill it in the medium term,
- fixed costs are high,
- and the remaining network can absorb production without major inefficiency.

Then the model often looks like this:

- **Year 0–2:** high cash outflow from severance, social plan, site exit, and transfer costs
- **Year 2 onward:** meaningful recurring savings from eliminated fixed cost
- **NPV result:** positive if the savings are large enough and implementation is not blocked

However, that “on paper” result can be materially weakened by:
- strike costs,
- delayed implementation,
- legal/co-determination blockage,
- lost volume during transition,
- loss of know-how,
- supplier disruption,
- reputational and political costs,
- and the need to overinvest in remaining plants to make consolidation work.

## Why no-closure packages can still be cheaper

A negotiated alternative can outperform closure if it achieves most of the savings at much lower upfront cost.

Common tools:

### 1) Working-time reductions / short-time work
- Cuts labor cost quickly.
- Preserves employment and skills.
- Best when demand weakness is cyclical or temporary.
- Less effective if the company has a permanent overcapacity problem.

### 2) Voluntary exits / retirement corridors
- Lower social conflict than forced redundancies.
- Often cheaper than large-scale severance if structured well.
- But savings come more slowly and may not be large enough if the gap is large.

### 3) Redeployment and qualification
- Avoids layoffs and preserves capability.
- Economically useful if the business can actually absorb the workers.
- It is not a pure cost reduction; it often delays savings and adds training cost.

### 4) Shift reduction / capacity rationalization
- Can save cost without site closure.
- But if a plant remains open with too little volume, unit costs can worsen.
- The savings are limited if fixed site costs remain high.

### 5) Temporary wage moderation / bonus waivers / labor-cost contribution
- Improves the near-term P&L.
- Usually not enough by itself for a structural problem.
- Only useful if it is material, time-bound, and linked to other measures.

### 6) Internal transfers / site specialization
- Can improve network efficiency.
- Requires strong product planning and capex commitments.
- Often slower than closure, but less disruptive.

## The key trade-off: magnitude versus implementation risk

From a labour-economics and restructuring-finance perspective, the choice is usually between:

### Closure-led strategy
**Pros**
- largest recurring savings,
- strongest capacity reduction,
- clearer long-term cost base.

**Cons**
- highest one-off cost,
- highest strike risk,
- governance veto risk,
- high delay risk,
- skill loss,
- political backlash.

### Negotiated no-closure strategy
**Pros**
- lower one-off cash cost,
- lower industrial-relations conflict,
- lower disruption,
- less political and legal resistance,
- better skill retention.

**Cons**
- savings may be insufficient,
- slower to implement,
- can leave structural overcapacity in place,
- may require repeated concessions,
- may not solve the cost gap if it is too large.

## German co-determination makes timing especially important

In Germany, closure or major downsizing in a co-determined setting often means:
- works council consultation and bargaining,
- social plan negotiations,
- potential collective bargaining conflict,
- supervisory-board constraints,
- and, in some cases, political influence through regional stakeholders.

That means the **time value of money** matters a lot:
- A saving that arrives in 4 years is worth much less than one that starts next quarter.
- A “cheaper” solution in nominal terms can be more expensive in NPV terms if delayed by strikes or legal blockage.

## In practical NPV terms

A simple comparison framework is:

**NPV = present value of recurring savings – present value of one-off restructuring costs – expected disruption costs**

Where disruption costs include:
- lost production from strikes,
- lower utilization during transition,
- premium logistics,
- temporary outsourcing,
- rework and quality problems,
- delayed product launches.

This is why a closure can fail to beat a no-closure package if:
- the strike probability is high,
- the site cannot be closed quickly,
- or the remaining plants need expensive reinforcement.

## When closures are likely to be cheaper

Closures are more likely to win economically if:
- the plant is clearly redundant,
- the product pipeline will not support it,
- labor cost reductions alone are too small,
- voluntary exits cannot close the gap,
- and the company can implement quickly with limited disruption.

In other words: **large structural gap + low implementation friction = closure advantage**.

## When no-closure alternatives are likely to be cheaper

A negotiated alternative is more likely to win if:
- the overcapacity is modest or temporary,
- utilization can be improved through internal reallocation,
- workforce reductions can come mainly through attrition and voluntary exits,
- the social plan for closure would be extremely expensive,
- or industrial action would heavily erode the closure case.

In other words: **moderate gap + high execution risk = negotiated alternative advantage**.

## The likely workforce effect

### Closure-led scenario
- Larger job losses, often concentrated at specific sites.
- More compulsory or socially negotiated exits.
- Greater age/profile distortion in the workforce.
- Possible preservation of jobs at remaining plants, but with higher intensity and less local stability.

### No-closure scenario
- Fewer immediate layoffs.
- More internal redeployment, reduced hours, and voluntary exits.
- Slower workforce adjustment.
- Greater preservation of know-how and industrial peace.
- But if the cost gap is not closed, the company may return to the table later with harsher demands.

## Bottom line

If you ask purely from a restructuring-cost standpoint:

- **Closure is usually the cheapest long-run instrument only when the plant is truly redundant and the company can implement quickly.**
- **A no-closure package is usually cheaper in the short run and can also be cheaper over 5 years if it closes most of the gap with low disruption.**
- The decisive variables are:
  1. size of the structural cost gap,
  2. probability and duration of strikes,
  3. severance/social-plan costs,
  4. legal and governance delay,
  5. ability to redeploy labor productively,
  6. remaining network efficiency after consolidation.
