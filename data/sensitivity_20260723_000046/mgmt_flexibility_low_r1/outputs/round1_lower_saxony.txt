I will be equally direct from the perspective of the state shareholder and political actor.

We agree with management on one point: **the company cannot simply drift through this crisis and expect the market to forgive it**. A real restructuring is necessary. But we do **not** accept the premise that the only credible restructuring is one built on German plant closures, forced redundancies, and the erosion of the company’s social partnership model.

That would be a strategic mistake, an economic mistake, and a political mistake.

### Where I agree
I agree that:
- the cost base is too high,
- the company needs durable competitiveness,
- capacity has to be adjusted,
- future investment and product allocation must be secured,
- and purely cosmetic measures are not enough.

I also agree that the burden cannot be shifted entirely away from the workforce. A negotiated contribution from employees can be part of a serious package.

### Where I disagree
I **strongly disagree** with the idea that:
- plant closures must be on the table as a normal restructuring tool,
- compulsory redundancies are unavoidable,
- or that the company should present job cuts in Germany as the price of survival.

That is not a conclusion I accept lightly. As a long-standing shareholder and elected state government, I have a responsibility not only to profitability, but to **regional stability, employment, and the industrial future of the state**. We are not a passive capital provider. We have a legitimate interest in preventing lasting damage to the regional economy.

And let me be very clear: **any outcome that guts regional employment or sidelines the state’s co-determination and veto role is unacceptable.**

### What I would consider workable
A settlement can be discussed if it meets three conditions:

1. **No plant closures in Germany**
   - I am not prepared to back a restructuring that dismantles sites in the state.
   - Capacity reductions, yes.
   - Site closures, no.

2. **No compulsory redundancies**
   - Job reductions, if unavoidable, must come through voluntary instruments, retirement corridors, redeployment, qualification, and working-time solutions.
   - The company cannot force the social costs onto workers while claiming this is the only way.

3. **A credible industrial future in Germany**
   - Binding commitments on future products, investment, and technology allocation.
   - Not slogans, but enforceable commitments that preserve the company as a regional anchor.

### On management’s “structural savings” argument
I do not dismiss the need for savings. But I reject the false choice between “closures or failure.”

There are other options that can deliver meaningful structural relief:
- better capacity utilization,
- product and shift optimization,
- negotiated working-time flexibility,
- temporary labor-cost moderation,
- internal redeployment,
- site specialization,
- and targeted productivity improvements.

Those are harder than closing a plant, yes. But they are also more responsible, more durable politically, and more consistent with the company’s role in Germany.

### On labor’s position
I support the workforce’s core line: no closures and no compulsory redundancies. That is not simply a trade-union slogan; it is a legitimate line in a company that depends on social partnership and co-determination.

At the same time, I would encourage the workforce and union to stay open to **fair, limited contributions** if — and only if — management, owners, and the company itself also make real concessions. A deal must be balanced. That means:
- not one-sided wage destruction,
- not symbolic “contributions” from management,
- not asking employees to pay for strategic failures alone.

### Bottom line
So my position is this:

- **I support restructuring.**
- **I do not support German plant closures.**
- **I do not support compulsory redundancies.**
- **I will not accept a settlement that weakens the state’s co-determination and blocking rights.**
- **I am open to a negotiated alternative that achieves savings through capacity adjustment, flexibility, and shared sacrifice — but only if it preserves jobs, sites, and the company’s long-term industrial base in Germany.**

Management should come back with a package that is economically serious **without crossing those red lines**. If it cannot, then it is not a serious package for us.
