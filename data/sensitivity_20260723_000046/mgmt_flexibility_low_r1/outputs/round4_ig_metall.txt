I will be equally direct in Round 4.

## To management
I agree with you on one point only: **this has to be a real restructuring**. No one at the worker side is arguing for cosmetics.

But I fundamentally disagree with your conclusion that real restructuring requires:
- German plant closures,
- compulsory redundancies,
- and a “material and durable” workforce contribution that effectively shifts the burden onto employees first.

That is not a restructuring plan. That is a demand that workers pay for strategic and management failure with their jobs.

You still have not shown, site by site, that the necessary savings cannot be reached through:
- internal redeployment,
- qualification,
- retirement corridors,
- voluntary departures,
- working-time adjustments,
- shift reductions,
- productivity gains,
- and a temporary, limited employee contribution.

Until you do, closure talk is not necessity. It is pressure.

And let me be clear: a warning strike does not create the cost problem. Management’s overcapacity and failed target attainment created it. The workforce will not be disciplined into accepting layoffs because output is being used as leverage.

## To the works council
I agree with your core line:
- no plant closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- preserve the job-security framework,
- and any contribution must be temporary, limited, and matched by binding guarantees.

That is the minimum basis for a settlement.

Where I would sharpen the point is this: if we offer moderation, it must be explicitly tied to **preserving sites and jobs**. We should not let temporary concessions become a gateway to permanent wage erosion or a backdoor closure package.

So yes to:
- limited bonus waivers,
- temporary wage restraint,
- working-time flexibility,
- voluntary exits,
- retirement corridors,
- and redeployment.

But only in a package that excludes closures and forced layoffs.

## To the regional state government
I agree that your blocking power must mean something.  
It should be used to prevent destructive plant closures, not merely to negotiate better terms for them.

If the state is serious about being an anchor shareholder and regional actor, then it should support a settlement that preserves industrial capacity in Germany and rules out closures as the first or default answer.

I also agree that temporary measures alone are not enough. But “durable” does not automatically mean “closure.” Durable can also mean:
- capacity reduction without site closure,
- internal realignment,
- productivity improvements,
- and fair contributions from all sides.

## To the family holding
You say closures should remain available if needed. From the workforce perspective, that means the burden stays entirely on employees while ownership keeps an escape hatch.

I reject that logic.

If the family truly believes Germany is central to the company’s value, then it should back a no-closure settlement and commit to keeping industrial capacity here. A company does not become stronger by signaling that sites can be sacrificed whenever management wants more flexibility.

You ask for proof that closures are unnecessary. Fine — but then include the full cost of:
- strike risk,
- delay,
- loss of skills,
- political resistance,
- and the destruction of trust.

Once those are counted properly, closures look a lot less “inevitable.”

## To the institutional investors
You are right to insist on a credible, quantified path to margin improvement. We do not dispute that. But your framing is too narrow if it ignores the costs of conflict and the value of industrial peace.

A deal that looks efficient on paper but triggers strikes, delays, and permanent insecurity is not a value-maximizing deal. It is a risk transfer.

If you want a credible return-on-sales path, support a package that:
- reduces costs materially,
- preserves the industrial base,
- and avoids forcing the workforce to absorb all the pain.

## On the expert input
The expert did not say closures are required. The expert said something more careful:
- closures can produce the largest recurring savings,
- but they also carry high one-off costs and high execution risk,
- and a negotiated no-closure package can outperform them if it closes most of the gap with lower disruption.

That is exactly why we should not open the closure door lightly.

The relevant test is not “what is the maximum possible cut?”  
The test is “what is the best durable package once implementation risk, strike risk, and governance blockage are included?”

That question still has not been answered against the labor alternative.

## What the union is prepared to negotiate
We remain prepared to discuss a serious package that includes:

- temporary bonus waivers,
- moderate wage restraint,
- working-time flexibility,
- a solidarity contribution,
- voluntary departure instruments,
- retirement corridors,
- productivity-linked adjustments,
- and redeployment/qualification measures.

But only if the package also includes:
1. **no German plant closures**
2. **no compulsory redundancies**
3. **preservation of the collective wage framework**
4. **binding investment and product commitments for Germany**
5. **real co-determination in implementation**
6. **material contributions from management and owners**

That is a fair burden-sharing structure.

## What we will not accept
We will not accept:
- closures dressed up as rationalization,
- forced layoffs,
- permanent wage dumping,
- open-ended insecurity,
- or a settlement where workers pay first and the capital side pays later, if at all.

## On the site-by-site question
Yes, a site-by-site review is necessary. But it must not become a closure roadmap by stealth.

The correct standard is:
- every German plant stays open unless management can prove, with hard numbers, that no other combination of measures can make it viable.

That proof has not been delivered.

So if management wants to proceed seriously, then bring the actual data:
- capacity utilization,
- fixed-cost burden by site,
- savings from shift reductions,
- savings from redeployment,
- savings from voluntary exits,
- savings from temporary labor-cost contributions,
- and the investment/product allocation through 2030.

If you cannot show that a no-closure package fails, then the closure case is not proven.

## The union’s bottom line
We are ready for a hard restructuring, but not for a restructuring that turns into social demolition.

Our position remains:

- **No plant closures in Germany**
- **No compulsory redundancies**
- **Preserve the collective wage agreement**
- **Use negotiated, reversible, socially responsible tools first**
- **Demand binding investment and future work in Germany**
- **Require fair contributions from management, owners, and employees alike**

If the other side wants a durable settlement, they need to come with a fair shared-sacrifice package — not a demand that the workforce absorb the strategic downside alone.
