I will be equally direct in Round 4.

## My position has not changed
I support a **real restructuring**.  
I do **not** support a package that leaves the German cost base structurally uncompetitive.

That means I cannot accept the repeated labor demand that:
- **all German plant closures must be excluded**, and
- **compulsory redundancies must be ruled out categorically**.

Those are not neutral principles. They are a pre-emptive veto on the instruments that may be necessary to restore competitiveness.

---

## Where I agree

### With the expert
I agree with the expert on the central economic point: this is **not** a cosmetic issue. Temporary measures alone will not fix:
- overcapacity,
- an uncompetitive German cost base,
- and the brand’s failure to meet return-on-sales targets.

I also agree that **implementation risk matters**. A restructuring that triggers prolonged strikes, legal blockage, or huge delay can destroy value.

### With the investors
I agree that what matters is a **credible, quantified path to margins and return on sales**.  
That is exactly what management is trying to secure.

### With the family holding
I agree that Germany remains central to the company’s value. That is precisely why we need a structure that can fund future products and technology through 2030.

---

## Where I disagree

### With the works council
You keep saying: “keep every German plant open, no compulsory redundancies, preserve the job-security framework, and use only reversible tools.”

That would be acceptable only if those tools could close the cost gap. So far, you have not shown that they can.

You also claim closures are simply a threat or leverage. No — closures are a legitimate restructuring tool when a site is structurally redundant. If you want to rule them out, then you must prove that the alternatives fully solve the economics. You have not done that.

Your position is also internally inconsistent:
- you say you want a real restructuring,
- but you preclude the instruments that may be necessary to achieve it.

That is not a viable business position.

### With the union
You are right that contributions must not become permanent wage dumping. I accept that point.

But your “temporary and limited” framing is too weak for the scale of the problem. If the company still carries too much structural cost after the first round, then symbolic concessions will not be enough.

You say warning strikes are not the cause of the problem. True — but they do increase the pressure and the cost of delay. That only makes a stronger restructuring case, not a weaker one.

### With the state government
I respect the state’s role, but I reject the idea that its blocking power should be used to prevent any meaningful structural adjustment. Protecting employment in the short term by freezing the cost base is not the same thing as protecting the industrial base.

If the state insists on no closures regardless of the numbers, then it is choosing political comfort over long-term competitiveness.

### With the family holding
You say you do not want unnecessary destruction, but you keep closure as an option only in the abstract. That is not enough. Either ownership is prepared to support the kind of deep structural change the company needs, or it is not.

If ownership wants competitiveness restored, it must accept that:
- some sites may shrink materially,
- headcount must fall,
- and the old guarantee regime cannot remain untouched.

### With the investors
You are correct to demand quantified savings and implementation certainty. But I want to push back on one point: a no-closure package is not automatically better just because it sounds smoother.

What matters is whether the package:
- reduces fixed cost,
- lowers capacity,
- improves productivity,
- and gets the company back to an acceptable margin path.

If it does not, then it is not a solution.

---

## On the central false argument in this round
Labor keeps presenting the issue as:

1. closures and forced layoffs, or  
2. preserving the status quo.

That is false.

There is a third route, and management is pursuing it seriously:
- capacity reduction,
- internal redeployment,
- qualification,
- voluntary departures,
- working-time changes,
- temporary employee contribution,
- and, where unavoidable, site rationalization.

But I am not prepared to accept a model where every German site is protected first and competitiveness is supposed to appear afterward by magic.

---

## What I need from labor before any further movement
If you want me to consider a no-closure path as credible, you need to show, site by site, how it closes the gap.

Not slogans. Not moral claims. Numbers.

I want the following quantified:
- current capacity and utilization by site,
- fixed-cost burden by plant,
- expected savings from shift reductions,
- expected savings from redeployment,
- expected savings from voluntary exits,
- expected savings from working-time changes,
- expected savings from temporary labor-cost contribution,
- and the impact on the 2030 margin target.

Without that, “no closures” is just a demand, not a plan.

---

## What is not acceptable
Let me be equally clear about management’s red line:

- A package that keeps the current footprint largely intact is **not acceptable**.
- A package that preserves every guarantee while expecting competitiveness to return is **not acceptable**.
- A package that creates a socially quiet stalemate is **not acceptable**.
- A package that delays hard decisions until the market forces them more brutally is **not acceptable**.

I am not here to sign off on delay.

We need a **site-by-site model** comparing:
- no-closure restructuring,
- partial rationalization,

including:
- strike risk,
- delay,
- social-plan costs,
- know-how loss,

## Bottom line
I remain prepared to support an alternative to closures **if** it delivers:
1. material and durable cost reduction  
2. meaningful capacity reduction  
3. real workforce reduction at scale  
4. measurable labor-cost contribution  
5. implementation certainty  
6. a credible path to the 2030 margin target  

If it does not meet those conditions, then it is not a serious restructuring proposal.

So I will say it plainly:  
**No cosmetic compromise. No cost base that remains uncompetitive. No blanket exclusion of closures or compulsory redundancies.**

If labor wants to avoid those instruments, it must bring a package that actually matches the savings. Otherwise, it is not offering an alternative — it is only rejecting the adjustment.
