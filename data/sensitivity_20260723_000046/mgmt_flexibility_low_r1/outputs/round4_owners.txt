I’ll be direct.

## My position
I support a **serious restructuring**. I do **not** support preserving an uncompetitive footprint or allowing the company to drift into permanent margin erosion.

At the same time, I do **not** support a value-destructive path: prolonged industrial conflict, governance breakdown, or a closure-led approach that is not clearly proven superior after all costs and risks.

So my line remains:

- **no cosmetic compromise**
- **no status quo settlement**
- **no open-ended instability**
- **yes to structural change that restores competitiveness**

---

## Where I agree

### With management
I agree that this is a **structural** problem. Temporary measures alone will not fix:
- overcapacity,
- a cost base that is too high for the market,
- and weak return-on-sales performance.

I also agree that **implementation risk matters**. A plan that cannot be executed in this governance environment is not a plan.

### With investors
You are right to insist on a **credible path back to margins**. That is the correct financial question.

### With the expert
The expert’s central point is right: **closures can generate the largest recurring savings, but they also bring the highest one-off cost and highest disruption risk**. That trade-off cannot be ignored.

---

## Where I disagree

### With the works council
I do **not** accept the categorical position that:
- every German plant must stay open, and
- compulsory redundancies are off the table regardless of the economics.

That is not a restructuring framework. It is a pre-emptive veto on the instruments that may be necessary to restore competitiveness.

You keep saying management has not proven the need for closures. Fair enough. But you have not yet proven that a no-closure package can close the gap either. As long as that proof is missing, closures cannot be ruled out in principle.

### With the union
I agree that temporary worker contributions must not become permanent wage dumping.

But a contribution that is too small or too short-lived will not solve a structural issue. If workers are asked to help, the contribution must be **material enough to matter**, while still time-bound and reversible.

### With the state government
I respect the regional responsibility, but a blocking position is not a substitute for competitiveness.

If the state uses its veto to prevent any meaningful structural adjustment, it risks protecting jobs short term while weakening the enterprise medium term. That is not stewardship.

### With the family holding
I agree that Germany remains central to long-term value. That is exactly why I am not endorsing an emotional closure-first approach.

But I also do not accept the idea that all closure risk can be excluded in advance. If the footprint is structurally too large, some sites may need to shrink materially, and the old guarantee regime cannot remain untouched forever.

---

## On the central false choice
What I reject is the framing that the only options are:

1. closures and forced layoffs, or  
2. preserving the status quo.

That is false.

There is a third route, and management should be pursuing it seriously:
- capacity reduction,
- internal redeployment,
- qualification,
- voluntary departures,
- retirement corridors,
- working-time changes,
- productivity gains,
- and a meaningful employee contribution.

But that route must be tested against the numbers, not defended as a principle.

---

## What I need before any further movement
I want a **site-by-site quantified model**. Not slogans. Numbers.

I want to see, for each German site:
- current capacity and utilization,
- fixed-cost burden,
- savings from shift reductions,
- savings from redeployment,
- savings from voluntary exits,
- savings from working-time changes,
- savings from temporary labor-cost contribution,
- one-off closure and social-plan costs,
- strike and delay risk,
- and the impact on the 2030 margin target.

Without that, “no closures” is a demand, not a plan.

## What is not acceptable
I will not support:
- a package that leaves the German cost base structurally uncompetitive,
- a deal that preserves every legacy structure and calls it reform,
- a settlement that creates a socially quiet stalemate,
- or any outcome that permanently impairs enterprise value or governance control.

That is the limit.

---

## Bottom line
I am open on the instrument mix. I am **not** open on the objective.

The objective must be a company that is competitive again by 2030, with durable margins and a stable industrial future in Germany.

If that can be achieved without closures, good.  
If it cannot, then closures cannot be excluded simply because they are politically difficult.

But I will only support hard measures if the business case is real, quantified, and unavoidable.
