I will be equally clear in Round 3.

## To management
I agree with one thing: **we need durable savings, not a cosmetic patch**. The company has a structural problem, and pretending otherwise would be irresponsible.

Where I disagree is your leap from “structural problem” to “closures and forced redundancies must stay on the table.”

That is not an economic law. That is your preferred instrument set.

You still have not shown, site by site, that the required savings cannot be achieved through:
- working-time flexibility,
- voluntary departures,
- retirement corridors,
- redeployment,
- qualification,
- productivity improvements,
- and a fair temporary workforce contribution.

And let me be very direct: you do not get to ask workers to accept closure risk first and “prove” the alternatives later. It is management’s job to show why the harshest tools are unavoidable. Until then, closures and compulsory redundancies are not justified; they are simply threatened.

## To the Works Council
I agree with your core line:
- no plant closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- preserve the job-security framework,
- and protect co-determination.

That remains the minimum basis for any settlement.

I also agree that the workforce should not be the only adjustment variable. If there is a restructuring burden, then management, owners, and capital must contribute too.

Where I sharpen the line is this: the union will not allow “temporary contribution” to become a backdoor for permanent wage erosion. Any contribution must be:
- limited,
- time-bound,
- reversible,
- and matched by binding guarantees on jobs, sites, and future work in Germany.

## To the family holding
You say closures are not your preferred route, but you won’t rule them out. From the workforce’s perspective, that means the pressure stays entirely on employees while ownership keeps the option to sacrifice sites when convenient.

I reject that logic.

If the family truly values Germany as the industrial core, then it should support a package that preserves sites and skills rather than treating closures as an acceptable governance tool. A company is not stronger because it can close plants faster; it is stronger when it can restructure without destroying trust and capability.

## To the investors
You keep asking for margin improvement, and I understand that. But your framing is too narrow if it ignores the cost of industrial conflict, loss of know-how, and damage to the employer’s reputation.

A deal that is “efficient” on paper but triggers strikes, distrust, and permanent insecurity is not value-preserving. It is risk transfer.

If you want a credible path back to competitive margins, then support a settlement that reduces costs **without** breaking the workforce base or the social contract. That is what durable value looks like.

## To the state government
I appreciate your support for jobs, sites, and co-determination. But the state must use its blocking position now, not after damage is done.

A blocking right is not there to negotiate how much pain the workforce must absorb before the closure option is abandoned. It is there to prevent destructive decisions in the first place. If the state really stands for regional industrial stability, it should back a no-closure solution and resist the attempt to normalize plant closures as an ordinary restructuring tool.

## On the expert input
The expert’s analysis was helpful in one respect: it confirmed that **temporary measures alone are insufficient**. I agree.

But the expert also said something the capital side keeps trying to gloss over: a negotiated, non-closure package **can** be economically credible if it includes real capacity reduction, voluntary exits, working-time changes, and durable labour-cost relief.

That is the path to explore first.

Not because it is comfortable. Because it is the only path that remains compatible with:
- no plant closures,
- no forced layoffs,
- and a fair distribution of the burden.

## What the union is prepared to discuss
We are prepared to negotiate a package that includes:
- temporary bonus waivers,
- moderate wage restraint,
- working-time flexibility,
- a solidarity contribution,
- voluntary departure instruments,
- retirement corridors,
- and productivity-linked adjustments.

But only if the package also includes:
1. **no German plant closures**
2. **no compulsory redundancies**
3. **protection of the collective wage framework**
4. **binding investment and product commitments for Germany**
5. **real co-determination in implementation**
6. **management and owner contributions that are material, not symbolic**

## What we will not accept
We will not accept:
- closures dressed up as “rationalization,”
- forced layoffs,
- permanent wage dumping,
- open-ended insecurity,
- or a restructuring where workers absorb the pain while management and owners keep all options.

Management keeps demanding proof that alternatives work. Fine. Then produce the table:
- savings by plant,
- savings by shift reduction,
- savings by redeployment,
- savings by voluntary exits,
- savings by wage moderation,
- savings by productivity measures,
- and the associated investment commitments.

If you cannot show that no-closure restructuring fails, then your closure narrative remains unproven.

The union’s position is unchanged:

- **No plant closures in Germany**
- **No compulsory redundancies**
- **Preserve the collective wage framework**
- **Demand a fair, shared-sacrifice settlement**

If the other parties want a durable settlement, they must show that they are willing to share the burden — not just demand it from the workforce.
