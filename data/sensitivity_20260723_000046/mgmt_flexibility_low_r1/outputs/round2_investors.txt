I’ll be direct.

From the capital markets’ perspective, the discussion is still circling around the right issue but not yet delivering the one thing that matters to us: **a quantified, credible path back to competitive margins**.

## Where I agree
I agree with management, the family holding, and the expert on one central point: **temporary measures alone are not enough**. If this is just wage moderation, bonus waivers, voluntary exits, and some redeployment while the underlying cost base and footprint remain too large, then the company has not solved its problem. It has postponed it.

I also agree that **prolonged conflict destroys value**. Strikes, output losses, and uncertainty are not a side issue. They are margin erosion in real time.

And I agree with labor and the state on one practical point: **a solution that cannot be implemented will not create value**. A theoretically perfect plan that triggers endless blockade is worthless to us.

## Where I disagree
I do **not** support the current labor and state position as stated if it amounts to:
- no plant closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- no meaningful labor-cost adjustment,
- and strong guarantees on top.

That combination is not a restructuring. It is a request that shareholders, management, and the company absorb the adjustment while the structural cost problem remains largely intact. We will not support that, because it leaves margins structurally impaired.

At the same time, I do **not** support management if it treats closures and broad layoffs as the only credible answer without showing the numbers. “We need it” is not enough. We need a business case, not a slogan.

## What we need to see
The debate has now reached the point where we need **site-by-site and measure-by-measure quantification**. Specifically:

1. **Current and target cost gap**
   - How far above competitor levels are German operations, by plant and function?

2. **Savings by instrument**
   - What is the NPV of:
     - plant closure,
     - capacity reduction without closure,
     - voluntary exits,
     - working-time changes,
     - wage moderation,
     - bonus waivers,
     - productivity measures?

3. **Implementation risk**
   - What is the expected delay, strike risk, and legal risk for each option?

4. **Margin impact**
   - What does each package do to return on sales by 2027 and 2030?

Without this, every side is still arguing from principle rather than economics.

## On plant closures
We are not ideologically attached to closures. But we are also not prepared to rule them out in advance if the economics require them.

A credible solution may still avoid closures if alternatives deliver:
- sufficient fixed-cost reduction,
- meaningful capacity cuts,
- durable labor-cost relief,
- and implementation certainty.

But if “no closures” means “the same industrial footprint with some temporary concessions,” then we will not back it. That would be a cosmetic compromise, and we are explicitly opposed to that.

## On compulsory redundancies
Our preference is clear:
- avoid forced layoffs if possible,
- use voluntary departures, retirement corridors, redeployment, and qualification first.

But if voluntary tools are too small or too slow, then management and ownership need to be honest about whether the remaining gap can still be closed. We are not going to bless an overstaffed structure just because no one wants to say the hard part out loud.

## On labor’s proposal
A fair employee contribution can be part of the answer, but it must be:
- **real**
- **durable**
- **measurable**
- and large enough to matter to the P&L

What we cannot accept is a contribution that is mainly symbolic or temporary while the structural cost issue stays untouched.

## On management’s position
Management is right to reject a cosmetic settlement. But it still needs to show discipline in how it frames the problem.

Do not ask investors to endorse a restructuring by defaulting immediately to the most politically explosive tools. Present a hierarchy of options, quantify them, and show why the chosen package is the best risk-adjusted route to margin recovery.

That is what capital can support.

## On the family and state
We respect your governance influence, but governance is not a substitute for economics.

- If the state blocks all meaningful adjustment, it increases the risk of a lower-valued company.
- If the family insists on preserving the enterprise, it must accept that preservation may require hard choices.
- But neither should force through a solution that is politically convenient and economically weak.

## Our threshold
We can support a deal only if it clearly delivers:
1. **durable cost reduction**
2. **meaningful capacity reduction**
3. **credible margin improvement**
4. **deal certainty and industrial stability**
5. **a viable path to return on sales target**

If those are met without closures, that is acceptable.
If they are not met without closures, then the closure option cannot be excluded just because it is painful.

## Bottom line
Our position is unchanged but now sharper:

- **No cosmetic compromise**
- **No prolonged conflict without economic resolution**
- **No settlement that leaves the cost base structurally uncompetitive**
- **Yes to a quantified, durable restructuring package**
- **Open to whichever mix of measures actually restores margins**

Bring the numbers. Until then, capital markets will remain skeptical.
