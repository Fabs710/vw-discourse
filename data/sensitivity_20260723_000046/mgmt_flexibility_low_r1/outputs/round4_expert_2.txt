## Executive view

At this stage, **no one can prove** whether a closure-led package or a negotiated no-closure package is superior without site-level data. The expert testimony already points to the key conclusion:

- **Closure-led restructuring** tends to generate the **largest recurring savings** if a site is genuinely redundant.
- But it also brings the **highest one-off costs**, the **highest delay risk**, and in Germany a material risk of **strike-related output loss** and governance blockage.
- **No-closure restructuring** can be financially superior if it removes a large enough share of overcapacity through voluntary exits, work-time changes, redeployment, and productivity measures, while avoiding prolonged conflict.

So the right comparison is not “closure vs no closure” in the abstract. It is:

> **Net present value of durable savings minus one-off costs, minus delay, minus conflict risk, minus loss of output, by site and over 5 years.**

---

## 1) What a 5-year comparison should measure

For each site, compare three scenarios:

### Scenario A: No-closure negotiated package
Typical tools:
- voluntary exits
- early retirement corridors
- redeployment
- reduced hours / shift reductions
- productivity measures
- temporary labor-cost contribution
- qualification/training

### Scenario B: Partial rationalization
Typical tools:
- no immediate closure
- deeper capacity reduction
- line or shift consolidation
- possible site shrinkage
- targeted workforce reduction
- selective outsourcing or internal consolidation

### Scenario C: Closure-led restructuring
Typical tools:
- plant closure
- asset write-down
- severance and social plan
- logistics reconfiguration
- transfer of production elsewhere

For each scenario, the model should include:

**Cash outflows**
- severance
- social plan cost
- early retirement top-ups
- retraining / redeployment
- capex for consolidation
- decommissioning / remediation
- legal/consultation cost
- strike cost / lost output
- delay cost
- logistics reconfiguration
- asset impairment

**Cash inflows / savings**
- labor cost reduction
- fixed-cost reduction
- maintenance/utilities reduction
- overhead reduction
- lower capex requirement
- productivity gains
- avoided future losses from underutilized capacity

## 2) The economics in plain terms

### When no-closure is likely to win
A negotiated no-closure package is often better on a 5-year NPV basis if:

- capacity overhang is **moderate**
- a large fraction of jobs can exit through **voluntary means**
- work-time and shift tools can cut costs meaningfully
- production can be rebalanced across sites
- strike risk is contained
- and the cost gap can be closed without keeping major fixed-cost excess in place

In those cases, the no-closure route usually has:
- lower cash cost
- faster implementation
- less output disruption
- less know-how loss
- lower governance delay

### When closure is likely to win
Closure-led restructuring tends to win if:

- one or more sites are **structurally underutilized**
- demand is unlikely to recover
- the site has weak product allocation beyond 2030
- internal measures only shift costs rather than remove them
- and the residual fixed-cost burden remains too high

Then the recurring savings from closure can exceed the large upfront cost over 5 years.

---

## 3) A simple NPV structure

A practical way to compare is:

\[
NPV = \sum_{t=1}^{5} \frac{(Savings_t - OngoingCosts_t - DelayLoss_t - StrikeLoss_t)}{(1+r)^t} - OneOffCosts
\]

Where:
- **Savings_t** = labor, fixed-cost, logistics, overhead savings
- **OngoingCosts_t** = any residual support costs, temporary measures, retained overhead
- **DelayLoss_t** = lost savings because implementation starts late
- **StrikeLoss_t** = volume loss, overtime, supplier disruption
- **OneOffCosts** = severance, social plans, write-downs, closure/decommissioning, redeployment

The option with the **highest risk-adjusted NPV** is the economically better restructuring package.

## 4) What would materially change the result

The ranking will change materially if any of the following is true:

### A. Higher-than-assumed strike duration
If industrial action is prolonged, the no-closure package can become much more expensive than expected. Conversely, if closure triggers major strikes, the closure case can deteriorate sharply.

### B. Larger one-off closure costs
If severance, social plan, remediation, and write-downs are very large, closure may underperform even if recurring savings are strong.

### C. Higher utilization at affected sites
If sites are more productive and better loaded than management claims, closure economics weaken.

### D. Stronger redeployment opportunity
If workers can be redeployed into growing internal functions, no-closure economics improve.

### E. Product allocation after 2027/2030
If a site has confirmed future volume or platform assignment, closure becomes harder to justify financially.

### F. Governance delay
In this governance structure, delay itself can be expensive. A closure that takes too long to execute may destroy much of its theoretical benefit.

## 6) Qualitative ranking under the facts given

Based only on the provided facts, a cautious qualitative assessment is:

### No-closure package
Most attractive if:
- management can secure real labor flexibility
- some workforce reduction can happen voluntarily
- there is meaningful temporary cost contribution
- strike risk is contained

### Partial rationalization
Likely the most realistic compromise if:
- the company needs material cost reduction
- but full closure is too disruptive or blocked
- and some site shrinkage is still required

### Closure-led restructuring
Likely strongest only if:
- specific sites are truly redundant
- internal tools cannot remove enough capacity
- and the cost gap is large and durable

In a German co-determined structure with effective blocking positions, the **risk-adjusted NPV** of closure-led restructuring can be badly damaged by delay and industrial conflict. That means a closure that is theoretically optimal may still be inferior in practice.

---

## 7) Decision implication

On the current record, the most defensible financial stance is:

- **do not assume closures are automatically the cheapest option**
- **do not assume a no-closure package is automatically sufficient**
- require a **site-by-site NPV model** that includes:
  - one-off costs,
  - recurring savings,
  - strike risk,
  - delay,
  - product allocation,
  - and utilization.

That is the only way to determine whether the best 5-year outcome is:
1. no closure,
2. partial rationalization,
3. or closure-led restructuring.
