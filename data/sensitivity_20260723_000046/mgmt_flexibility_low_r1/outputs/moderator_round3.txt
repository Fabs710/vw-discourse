Core impasse, as it stands:

## 1) Where the deadlock lies
The central blockage is **not** over whether restructuring is needed. All sides now accept that it is.

The impasse is over **the permitted instrument set**:

- **Labor, the works council, and the state** want a restructuring that is **no-closure / no-forced-layoff by default**, with capacity reduction achieved through internal, reversible, negotiated tools.
- **Management, the family holding, and investors** want enough flexibility to ensure **durable, measurable cost and capacity reduction**, including the option of closures and compulsory redundancies if needed.
- The **expert input** says both positions have merit economically, but the final answer depends on site-level numbers, execution risk, strike risk, and one-off costs.

So the conflict is basically:
**“Can the company be restructured without closures and forced layoffs, or are those instruments necessary to close the gap?”**

A second, closely related blockage is **trust and sequencing**:
- Labor wants **binding guarantees first**, then flexibility.
- Capital wants **credible adjustment first**, then commitments.

That sequencing mismatch is preventing convergence.

---

## 2) Possible bridge positions
Below are facilitator-style compromise frameworks that could bridge the main divide, without taking a side.

### Proposal A: “No-closure as first resort, closure as conditional last resort”
A staged agreement could say:

1. The parties jointly adopt a **no-closure restructuring package** as the first implementation phase.
2. The package includes:
   - voluntary exits,
   - retirement corridors,
   - reduced hours,
   - redeployment,
   - qualification,
   - temporary labor-cost contribution,
   - productivity measures.
3. A **predefined review trigger** is set after a fixed period, based on agreed metrics:
   - capacity utilization,
   - savings achieved,
   - demand development,
   - investment delivery,
   - and cost gap remaining.
4. Only if the agreed target gap is still materially unmet would the parties reopen discussion on more drastic measures.

Why this may help:
- Labor gets a real no-closure starting point.
- Management gets a defined fallback if the package fails.
- Investors and the family get a metrics-based review rather than an open-ended veto.

---

### Proposal B: “Site-by-site differentiated settlement”
Instead of one uniform rule for all German plants:

1. Each site is assessed separately on:
   - utilization,
   - product pipeline,
   - fixed cost,
   - capex need,
   - logistics role,
   - and future viability.
2. Sites with clear future product allocation receive **binding protection and investment commitments**.
3. Sites with weakest economics enter a **special adjustment track**:
   - downsizing,
   - partial consolidation,
   - or, only if unavoidable and proven, closure discussion.
4. The parties agree that **no closure decision is made without a quantified site business case** including strike and delay risk.

Why this may help:
- It reduces the all-or-nothing conflict.
- It lets the parties distinguish between structurally viable and structurally weak sites.
- It gives capital the disciplined evidence it is asking for, while limiting generalized closure pressure.

---

### Proposal C: “Shared sacrifice package with hard governance guardrails”
A broader compromise could combine:

- temporary wage moderation / bonus waivers,
- working-time flexibility,
- management and owner contribution,
- investment commitments for Germany,
- and a time-limited industrial peace agreement.

In return:
- no immediate closures,
- no compulsory redundancies during the agreement period,
- and a joint monitoring committee with co-determination, state involvement, and investor reporting.

A key feature would be:
- **if targets are missed**, the parties pre-agree on a second-round negotiation with defined escalation steps.
- That avoids immediate closure decisions while preserving credibility that underperformance will not be ignored.

Why this may help:
- It shares the burden more visibly.
- It converts the dispute from principle to performance.
- It reduces the chance of a first-round breakdown.

## 3) One pointed question to each stakeholder

### To management
**Which specific German site or sites are objectively non-viable after all alternatives are fully modeled, and what quantified savings gap remains if closures are excluded?**

### To the works council
**What level of measurable cost and capacity reduction would you accept as sufficient to prove that a no-closure package is economically credible through 2030?**

### To the industrial union
**What temporary workforce contribution, in concrete and quantified terms, is the union prepared to place on the table if it is matched by binding site and investment guarantees?**

### To the regional state government
**Will you support a site-by-site review with hard metrics even if it leads to differentiated outcomes across plants, provided that closures are not treated as the starting assumption?**

### To the controlling family holding
**Would you commit to backing a no-closure package for a defined period if it meets agreed financial and operational thresholds, rather than keeping closure risk permanently open?**

### To the institutional investors
**What minimum 5-year return, margin, or cost-reduction threshold would make you accept a negotiated no-closure settlement as financially credible?**

### To the expert
**Which single variable most decisively changes the NPV ranking between no-closure and closure-led scenarios in this case: strike duration, severance cost, utilization, or governance delay?**
