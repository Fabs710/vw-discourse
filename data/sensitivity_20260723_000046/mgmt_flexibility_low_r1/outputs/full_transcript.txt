[Expert: labour-economics / restructuring-cost sp — Round 2]
From a labour-economics and restructuring-cost perspective, the central question is:

**Can the company achieve materially comparable cost savings without plant closures and compulsory redundancies, and if so under what conditions?**

Short answer: **sometimes yes, but usually only if the required savings are moderate, time horizon is long enough, and all parties accept real concessions.** If management needs very large, fast, and durable cost cuts, then **purely flexible alternatives often become more expensive in total than a closure-led restructuring**, once you account for the compensation, transition, and governance costs of keeping excess capacity open. But closures are also typically the most expensive option upfront, and in Germany they are legally and industrially difficult.

## 1) Cost logic: what is actually being compared

You need to distinguish:

### A. One-off restructuring costs
These include:
- severance and exit packages,
- transfer company / bridging arrangements,
- pension adjustments,
- site wind-down costs,
- redundancy-related legal and negotiation costs,
- training and redeployment costs,
- production disruption during transition,
- possibly litigation and strike-related losses.

### B. Recurring operating savings
These come from:
- lower wage bill,
- fewer shifts / less overtime,
- lower fixed overhead,
- eliminated depreciation/maintenance on an idled site,
- less energy and logistics cost,
- better capacity utilization at remaining plants,
- lower social-security and fringe-cost exposure.

A plant closure usually produces **large recurring savings** but at a **high one-time cost**.  
Flexible alternatives usually produce **lower recurring savings** but are **cheaper upfront** and politically easier to implement.

## 2) Relative economics of the main options

### Option 1: Plant closure / large-scale workforce reduction
**Economics:**
- Highest immediate cash outflow.
- Highest legal and industrial-relations complexity in Germany.
- But potentially the **largest and most durable** savings if a site is structurally redundant.

**When it is cheapest in net present value terms:**
- the plant is persistently underutilized,
- there is no credible product allocation for the site,
- fixed-cost burden is substantial,
- labor-cost gap is large,
- and management can keep the plant closed without long litigation or strikes.

**When it becomes very expensive:**
- if closure triggers a long strike,
- if works council and state block implementation,
- if site-specific skills are lost that must later be rebuilt,
- if capacity has to be recreated elsewhere,
- if the closure damages production continuity or launch timing.

### Option 2: Flexible alternatives without closure
Examples:
- reduced working time,
- wage moderation,
- temporary bonus waivers,
- voluntary departures,
- retirement corridors,
- internal transfers,
- short-time work,
- redeployment and qualification,
- shift reductions,
- productivity and organization measures.

**Economics:**
- Lower immediate cost.
- Preserves industrial footprint and skills.
- Avoids major wind-down costs.
- But savings are usually **less than from closure**, unless concessions are unusually deep and broad.

**When it can work:**
- the overcapacity problem is moderate,
- there is time to phase adjustment,
- product demand may recover or shift,
- and employees accept meaningful labour-cost contributions.

**When it is not enough:**
- if the company needs a permanent step-change in fixed cost,
- if the surplus capacity is large,
- if concessions are temporary only,
- or if the remaining German cost base stays above competitor levels.

## 3) German industrial-relations constraints that matter for cost

In Germany, the “cheap” option is not always the one with the lowest severance cheque. Legal and institutional constraints can make closure expensive and slow:

- **Co-determination / supervisory board dynamics** can block major restructuring.
- **Works councils** have strong rights around reorganization, transfers, social plans, and staffing changes.
- **Collective bargaining peace** limits strike action only during the agreement; once expired, industrial action becomes a real cost factor.
- **Transfer and social-plan negotiations** often add significant cash cost.
- **State and anchor-shareholder politics** can increase the effective negotiation price of closure.

So even if closure has the best long-run cost profile, the **implementation risk premium** may be substantial.

## 4) Typical cost comparison: qualitative ranking

For a large German OEM, the ranking is usually:

### Upfront cash cost
1. **Plant closure with severance/social plan** — highest
2. **Voluntary exits + retirement corridors** — medium
3. **Wage moderation / bonus waivers / working-time changes** — lowest

### Recurring savings
1. **Plant closure** — highest
2. **Deep headcount reduction without closure** — medium to high
3. **Temporary concessions / working-time tools** — lowest to medium

### Implementation risk
1. **Plant closure** — highest
2. **Forced redundancies** — high
3. **Negotiated voluntary packages** — medium
4. **Shared-sacrifice deal with guarantees** — lower

## 5) What “no closures” can realistically deliver

A no-closure package can be economically credible if it contains several elements at once:

- **real capacity reduction** through shorter shifts or fewer lines,
- **voluntary headcount reduction** at meaningful scale,
- **temporary labour-cost concessions** that are not symbolic,
- **product and investment commitments** to keep the fixed assets productive,
- **internal mobility** so excess labour is not simply retained idle,
- **productivity measures** so the unit labour cost actually falls.

If those measures are combined, savings can be substantial. But they usually require:
- multi-year duration,
- hard numerical targets,
- and some form of employment or site security in return.

## 6) The key economic trade-off

The real trade-off is:

- **Closure-led restructuring**: better long-run cost correction, but high one-off cost and high conflict risk.
- **Flexible alternative restructuring**: lower one-off cost and better social acceptability, but often weaker savings and a risk of only delaying the problem.

In practice, the cheaper option depends on the scale of the gap:

- **Small-to-moderate structural gap**: flexible alternatives can be cheaper overall.
- **Large, persistent structural gap**: closure or very deep workforce reduction is often cheaper over the medium term, even if painful upfront.

- If the German cost gap is **modest to moderate**, a negotiated no-closure package can be the lower-risk, lower-cost option.
- If the gap is **large and persistent**, closure-led restructuring becomes more likely to win on medium-term NPV.

### 2. Capacity utilization by site
If a plant is running well below efficient utilization and has no secure future product allocation, closure economics improve materially.

### 3. Volume outlook through 2030
If European demand remains weak and EV transition volume is not sufficient, the case for capacity reduction strengthens.

### 4. Execution risk
If closure triggers prolonged strikes, delay, or legal blockage, the NPV can deteriorate fast. In Germany, that risk is not trivial.

### 5. Redeployment absorbency
If the company can absorb surplus labour internally, non-closure packages improve materially. If not, they may just defer the problem.

### 6. Wage-concession durability
A temporary wage moderation package has much lower value than a durable labour-cost reset.

---

## 5) Practical economic judgement

### A negotiated non-closure package is economically credible if it includes all of the following:
- meaningful headcount reduction through voluntary instruments,
- real capacity reduction,
- durable labour-cost relief,
- productivity improvement,
- and product/investment commitments that keep remaining sites efficient.

If those elements are present, the NPV can be competitive with closure-led restructuring, especially once closure risk and disruption are included.

### A closure-led package is economically stronger if:
- one or more sites are structurally redundant,
- non-closure tools cannot create sufficient fixed-cost reduction,
- and the company needs a step-change in cost base by 2030.

In that case, closures may be the lower-cost medium-term answer despite higher upfront cost.

---

## 6) What I would not assume without data
I would not assume:
- that closures are automatically cheapest,
- that no-closure packages are automatically too weak,
- or that the “best” package is the one with the lowest initial cash cost.

The correct metric is **5-year NPV including implementation risk**.

To produce a defensible site-by-site comparison, I would need:

- capacity and utilization,

### By restructuring option

- one-time and recurring savings.

### Financial inputs
- discount rate / WACC,
- tax treatment,
- asset write-down assumptions,
- inflation and wage growth assumptions,
- volume forecast by model and region.

## 8) What would materially change the result
The conclusion would change materially if any of the following were true:
- one plant has **no credible future product allocation** and cannot be retooled economically;
- the company needs **very large fixed-cost reduction quickly**;
- labour concessions can be made **durable and deep** without closure;
- or the governance/industrial-relations environment makes closure execution so costly that the NPV advantage disappears.

Yes. Here is a neutral restructuring-finance / labour-economics comparison of the main options, framed for a German co-determined automotive group.

## Short answer

- **Plant closures and compulsory redundancies** usually deliver the **largest recurring savings**, but also the **largest one-off restructuring costs**, the **highest execution risk**, and the **greatest industrial-relations/govemance delay**.
- A **no-closure package** can be cheaper **if** enough savings can be achieved through:
  - capacity reductions short of closure,
  - shift and working-time changes,
  - voluntary exits / retirement corridors,
  - redeployment and qualification,
  - wage moderation or temporary concessions,
  - and productivity improvements.
- In practice, the cheaper path depends on the **size and persistence of the cost gap**.  
  If the gap is large and structural, no-closure measures often become too expensive or too slow.  
  If the gap is moderate and the workforce is highly protected, negotiated alternatives can outperform closure on a **5-year NPV** basis because they avoid heavy upfront cash costs and implementation disruption.

## What matters economically

You should separate:

### 1) One-off restructuring costs
These are immediate or near-term cash costs, for example:
- severance payments,
- social plan costs,
- outplacement,
- early retirement subsidies,
- decommissioning / site exit costs,
- asset write-downs,
- contract break costs,
- transfer company costs,
- retraining and redeployment costs,
- legal / advisory / implementation costs,
- restart costs at remaining plants if production is consolidated.

For a German plant closure, these can be very large. In automotive, it is not unusual for one-off costs to reach **hundreds of millions to well over €1 billion** for a major site, depending on headcount, tenure, pension obligations, asset base, and social-plan design.

### 2) Recurring operating savings
These are the actual annual benefits:
- lower payroll,
- lower overhead,
- lower maintenance and utilities,
- fewer shifts,
- better utilization at remaining sites,
- reduced logistics complexity,
- lower fixed cost absorption losses.

Closures usually create the biggest recurring savings because they remove an entire cost base, not just labor.

## Why closures are often financially attractive on paper

A closure-led plan tends to win on pure operating economics when:
- a plant is structurally underutilized,
- product allocation will not fill it in the medium term,
- fixed costs are high,
- and the remaining network can absorb production without major inefficiency.

Then the model often looks like this:

- **Year 0–2:** high cash outflow from severance, social plan, site exit, and transfer costs
- **Year 2 onward:** meaningful recurring savings from eliminated fixed cost
- **NPV result:** positive if the savings are large enough and implementation is not blocked

However, that “on paper” result can be materially weakened by:
- strike costs,
- delayed implementation,
- legal/co-determination blockage,
- lost volume during transition,
- loss of know-how,
- supplier disruption,
- reputational and political costs,
- and the need to overinvest in remaining plants to make consolidation work.

## Why no-closure packages can still be cheaper

A negotiated alternative can outperform closure if it achieves most of the savings at much lower upfront cost.

Common tools:

### 1) Working-time reductions / short-time work
- Cuts labor cost quickly.
- Preserves employment and skills.
- Best when demand weakness is cyclical or temporary.
- Less effective if the company has a permanent overcapacity problem.

### 2) Voluntary exits / retirement corridors
- Lower social conflict than forced redundancies.
- Often cheaper than large-scale severance if structured well.
- But savings come more slowly and may not be large enough if the gap is large.

### 3) Redeployment and qualification
- Avoids layoffs and preserves capability.
- Economically useful if the business can actually absorb the workers.
- It is not a pure cost reduction; it often delays savings and adds training cost.

### 4) Shift reduction / capacity rationalization
- Can save cost without site closure.
- But if a plant remains open with too little volume, unit costs can worsen.
- The savings are limited if fixed site costs remain high.

### 5) Temporary wage moderation / bonus waivers / labor-cost contribution
- Improves the near-term P&L.
- Usually not enough by itself for a structural problem.
- Only useful if it is material, time-bound, and linked to other measures.

### 6) Internal transfers / site specialization
- Can improve network efficiency.
- Requires strong product planning and capex commitments.
- Often slower than closure, but less disruptive.

## The key trade-off: magnitude versus implementation risk

From a labour-economics and restructuring-finance perspective, the choice is usually between:

### Closure-led strategy
**Pros**
- largest recurring savings,
- strongest capacity reduction,
- clearer long-term cost base.

**Cons**
- highest one-off cost,
- highest strike risk,
- governance veto risk,
- high delay risk,
- skill loss,
- political backlash.

### Negotiated no-closure strategy
**Pros**
- lower one-off cash cost,
- lower industrial-relations conflict,
- lower disruption,
- less political and legal resistance,
- better skill retention.

**Cons**
- savings may be insufficient,
- slower to implement,
- can leave structural overcapacity in place,
- may require repeated concessions,
- may not solve the cost gap if it is too large.

## German co-determination makes timing especially important

In Germany, closure or major downsizing in a co-determined setting often means:
- works council consultation and bargaining,
- social plan negotiations,
- potential collective bargaining conflict,
- supervisory-board constraints,
- and, in some cases, political influence through regional stakeholders.

That means the **time value of money** matters a lot:
- A saving that arrives in 4 years is worth much less than one that starts next quarter.
- A “cheaper” solution in nominal terms can be more expensive in NPV terms if delayed by strikes or legal blockage.

## In practical NPV terms

A simple comparison framework is:

**NPV = present value of recurring savings – present value of one-off restructuring costs – expected disruption costs**

Where disruption costs include:
- lost production from strikes,
- lower utilization during transition,
- premium logistics,
- temporary outsourcing,
- rework and quality problems,
- delayed product launches.

This is why a closure can fail to beat a no-closure package if:
- the strike probability is high,
- the site cannot be closed quickly,
- or the remaining plants need expensive reinforcement.

## When closures are likely to be cheaper

Closures are more likely to win economically if:
- the plant is clearly redundant,
- the product pipeline will not support it,
- labor cost reductions alone are too small,
- voluntary exits cannot close the gap,
- and the company can implement quickly with limited disruption.

In other words: **large structural gap + low implementation friction = closure advantage**.

## When no-closure alternatives are likely to be cheaper

A negotiated alternative is more likely to win if:
- the overcapacity is modest or temporary,
- utilization can be improved through internal reallocation,
- workforce reductions can come mainly through attrition and voluntary exits,
- the social plan for closure would be extremely expensive,
- or industrial action would heavily erode the closure case.

In other words: **moderate gap + high execution risk = negotiated alternative advantage**.

## The likely workforce effect

### Closure-led scenario
- Larger job losses, often concentrated at specific sites.
- More compulsory or socially negotiated exits.
- Greater age/profile distortion in the workforce.
- Possible preservation of jobs at remaining plants, but with higher intensity and less local stability.

### No-closure scenario
- Fewer immediate layoffs.
- More internal redeployment, reduced hours, and voluntary exits.
- Slower workforce adjustment.
- Greater preservation of know-how and industrial peace.
- But if the cost gap is not closed, the company may return to the table later with harsher demands.

## Bottom line

If you ask purely from a restructuring-cost standpoint:

- **Closure is usually the cheapest long-run instrument only when the plant is truly redundant and the company can implement quickly.**
- **A no-closure package is usually cheaper in the short run and can also be cheaper over 5 years if it closes most of the gap with low disruption.**
- The decisive variables are:
  1. size of the structural cost gap,
  2. probability and duration of strikes,
  3. severance/social-plan costs,
  4. legal and governance delay,
  5. ability to redeploy labor productively,
  6. remaining network efficiency after consolidation.

## Bottom line
A **no-closure restructuring can be cheaper than plant closures** if it can eliminate a large share of the cost gap through:
- voluntary exits,
- retirement corridors,
- working-time reductions,
- redeployment,
- shift/line rationalization,
- temporary wage moderation,
- and productivity measures,

**and** if it avoids major strike disruption and legal/political delay.

But if the overcapacity is large and durable, or if the no-closure package only shifts costs into temporary measures without materially reducing fixed costs, then **closures and compulsory redundancies usually deliver higher long-run recurring savings** — albeit with much higher one-off costs, higher industrial-relations risk, and often a slower or more uncertain implementation.

So the economically correct answer is not “closures are always cheaper” or “no-closure is always cheaper.” It depends mainly on:
1. **the size of the structural overcapacity**,  
2. **how much of it can be removed without closing sites**,  
3. **the one-off cost of closures vs voluntary measures**, and  
4. **the expected delay/strike risk under each path**.

## 1) Cost logic: closures vs alternatives

### A. Plant closure / compulsory redundancy path
Usually involves:

**One-off costs**
- severance packages,
- social plan costs,
- transfer company / outplacement,
- pension bridging,
- decommissioning and site-remediation,
- write-downs of assets and tooling,
- logistics/network reconfiguration,
- legal and consultation costs,
- possible penalties if supply contracts are disrupted.

**Recurring savings**
- elimination of site fixed costs,
- lower overhead,
- lower labour cost,
- lower maintenance/energy/utilities,
- less capex needed for that site,
- simpler network and management structure.

**Main economic advantage**
- Largest and most durable recurring savings if the site is genuinely redundant.

**Main economic disadvantage**
- Very high one-off cost and high risk of delay, especially in Germany with co-determination, works council veto power, bargaining conflict, and likely strike action.

---

### B. No-closure / negotiated alternatives
Usually involves:

**One-off costs**
- voluntary exit bonuses,
- early retirement top-ups,
- qualification and redeployment costs,
- temporary wage concessions negotiated as offsets,
- administration of work-time changes,
- possible internal transfer costs,
- support for short-time work or partial utilization gaps.

**Recurring savings**
- lower labour cost,
- fewer employees through attrition,
- reduced hours,
- shift reductions,
- productivity gains,
- some overhead reduction if lines are rationalized.

**Main economic advantage**
- Lower conflict risk, lower legal delay, preserve know-how, preserve optionality, usually much lower one-off cash outlay than closure.

**Main economic disadvantage**
- Savings may be smaller and slower, and the fixed-cost base may remain too high if sites are kept open with insufficient volume.

## 2) Which is usually cheaper in net present value terms?

### No-closure is usually cheaper when:
- the cost gap is **moderate**, not extreme;
- there is substantial scope for **voluntary attrition** and early retirement;
- sites can be **reconfigured** to run with fewer shifts or lower headcount;
- there is meaningful **internal redeployment** to growth areas;
- employees accept a **temporary and measurable contribution**;
- industrial peace is maintained;
- and the company values preserving skills and future capacity.

In such cases, the no-closure route often has:
- lower cash burn,
- lower disruption,
- faster implementation,
- and better risk-adjusted value.

### Closure is usually cheaper when:
- the site is **structurally underutilized**;
- demand is unlikely to recover;
- the site has weak product allocation beyond 2030;
- fixed costs are high relative to output;
- and internal measures cannot eliminate enough capacity.

Then the recurring savings from closure can outweigh the high one-off cost over a 3–5 year horizon, especially if the plant would otherwise continue to operate below efficient scale.

---

## 3) The German-specific constraint
In Germany, the economics are heavily shaped by industrial-relations and governance constraints:

- **co-determination** makes closures slower and harder;
- works councils and unions can raise the cost of conflict;
- strikes can materially reduce output and customer confidence;
- site closures often trigger protracted negotiation over social plans;
- and a blocked restructuring can create a very expensive “delay cost.”

That means a closure that looks cheaper on paper can become more expensive in reality if it takes 12–24 months to execute and causes lost volume, delayed model launches, or supply chain disruption.

So in German carmaking, the relevant comparison is not just:
> closure savings vs alternative savings,

but:
> closure savings vs alternative savings, **minus delay, conflict, and execution risk**.

If I were building a decision model, the critical variables would be:

1. **Current utilization by site**  
   A low-utilization plant is a closure candidate; a well-utilized plant is not.

2. **Fixed-cost burden by site**  
   Maintenance, utilities, depreciation, supervision, logistics, and local overhead.

3. **Headcount reducibility without closure**  
   How much can be removed through voluntary exits, retirement, transfer, and work-time changes?

4. **One-off restructuring cost**
   - closure severance/social plan cost,
   - vs voluntary exit/early retirement cost.

5. **Implementation delay**
   - months to agreement,
   - months to execution,
   - lost production and launch delays.

6. **Strike and legal risk**
   - expected output loss,
   - supplier disruption,
   - reputational/customer effects.

7. **Know-how loss**
   - especially in body, paint, final assembly, and launch functions.

8. **Future product allocation**
   - whether the site has confirmed platform/program volume.

## 1. Decision context
The company faces a genuine restructuring crisis: weak European demand, elevated German cost structures, excess capacity, and pressure to restore durable competitiveness and return-on-sales. ## 3. Final positions
### Group & Brand Management
Management insists that a real restructuring must materially reduce cost and capacity, and that closures and compulsory redundancies cannot be ruled out in advance. It accepts that alternatives should be tested, but only if they produce a quantified, durable savings gap closure and a credible path to margin recovery by 2030.

### Works Council
The works council rejects German plant closures and compulsory redundancies outright, and treats them as incompatible with the company’s social contract and industrial legitimacy. It is open to internal adjustment tools, but only if every German site remains open and any workforce contribution is temporary, limited, and matched by binding investment and product commitments.

### Industrial Union
The union aligns closely with the works council on the red lines: no closures, no forced layoffs, and no permanent wage erosion. It is prepared to negotiate temporary, measurable labor-cost concessions, but only as part of a package that preserves sites, jobs, and the collective framework.

### Regional State Government
The state supports real restructuring but opposes a closure-led or redundancy-led solution as the default path. It emphasizes regional employment, industrial stability, and the state’s responsibility as anchor shareholder, while supporting quantified alternatives and binding commitments for Germany.

### Controlling Family Holding
The family backs a tough restructuring and rejects a mere status quo settlement, but it will not preclude closures if the numbers justify them. Its position is that the company needs structural change strong enough to restore competitiveness, even if that means material shrinkage and a reduced legacy guarantee regime.

### Institutional Investors
Investors want a risk-adjusted, credible return to competitive margins and accept neither cosmetic compromise nor indefinite protection of the footprint. They are open to a no-closure package only if it is materially durable, measurable, and clearly superior in NPV or risk-adjusted value; otherwise they will not rule out closures.

### Expert
The expert’s position is conditional and analytical: a no-closure package can be cheaper if it removes enough of the gap through voluntary exits, working-time reductions, redeployment, and productivity gains, especially if strike and delay risk are contained. But if overcapacity is large and durable, closure-led restructuring usually yields higher recurring savings despite substantial one-off costs and execution risk.

## 4. Genuine conflicts
1. **Closure as permissible instrument vs categorical red line**  
   Management, family, and investors keep closures available; works council, union, and state reject them in principle.

2. **Compulsory redundancies**  
   Capital-side actors treat them as a fallback; labor and state reject them as incompatible with legitimacy and governance.

3. **Burden of proof**  
   Labor says management must prove closures are unavoidable after exhausting internal tools; management says labor must prove a no-closure path can close the gap.

4. **Sequencing and trust**  
   Labor wants binding guarantees first; management wants adjustment first and commitments later.

5. **Definition of a “real” restructuring**  
   Labor defines it as durable savings without site destruction; management and investors define it as durable savings that may require site rationalization or closure.

## 5. Areas of convergence
- All sides now accept that **restructuring is necessary** and that cosmetic measures are insufficient.
- All sides agree that **durable cost reduction** is needed.
- All sides recognize that **open conflict, strikes, and delay destroy value**.
- There is shared support for a **site-by-site quantitative model**.
- Most parties accept that **voluntary departures, redeployment, retraining, working-time measures, and some employee contribution** should be explored first.
- Several parties want a **credible industrial future in Germany**, including investment and product allocation commitments.

## 6. Key trade-offs for the decision-maker
- **Risk vs savings**: closures may offer greater recurring savings, but with high one-off costs, litigation/consultation burden, and strike risk.
- **Social legitimacy vs managerial flexibility**: no-closure packages preserve trust and governance stability but may leave residual overcapacity.
- **Speed vs durability**: closures can be decisive if executable; negotiated alternatives may be slower but more stable.
- **Employment security vs competitiveness**: stronger guarantees support social peace but can constrain structural adjustment.
- **Governance feasibility vs economic optimum**: a theoretically best financial path may be blocked in practice by co-determination and shareholder-state politics.

## 7. Integrative pathways
1. **Phased restructuring with conditional review**  
   Start with a no-closure package, paired with measurable savings, capacity reduction, and a predefined review trigger if targets are missed.

2. **Partial rationalization instead of binary closure/no-closure**  
   Use site shrinkage, line consolidation, and product reallocation to achieve meaningful savings without full plant closures.

3. **Shared-sacrifice package**  
   Combine temporary labor-cost moderation and flexibility with binding investment, product commitments, and management/owner contributions.

4. **Site-by-site differentiated settlement**  
   Assess each plant separately and tailor outcomes to utilization, product pipeline, and fixed-cost burden, rather than applying one uniform rule.

## 8. Open questions
- Which German sites are actually structurally non-viable?
- How much of the savings gap can be closed through voluntary and internal measures?
- What is the quantified cost of strike disruption, delay, and legal blockage under each option?
- How much capacity reduction is possible without closures?
- What employee contribution is material enough to matter but temporary enough to be acceptable?
- What binding investment and product allocation commitments can credibly offset labor’s risk?
- What thresholds would trigger a second-stage review or fallback mechanism?

## 9. Recommended next step
Proceed to a **site-by-site quantitative decision table** before any final bargaining move. It should compare, for each plant, the 5-year NPV of:
1. no-closure restructuring,
2. partial rationalization,
3. closure-led restructuring,

including one-off costs, recurring savings, utilization, strike risk, delay, social-plan cost, and product allocation through 2030. This is the only way to move the debate from abstract red lines to evidence-based bargaining.
