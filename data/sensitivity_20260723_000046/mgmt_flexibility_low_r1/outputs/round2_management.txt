Let me respond directly.

## First: I agree on one point
The expert is right on the basic economics: **temporary measures alone will not solve a structural cost and capacity problem**. If all we get is short-term labor-cost relief and some voluntary exits while the underlying footprint stays inflated, then we have not fixed the company. That would be a delay, not a restructuring.

So yes:
- cosmetic measures are insufficient,
- one-time gestures are insufficient,
- and we need durable savings, not slogans.

## But I disagree with the labor side’s core line
The Works Council and union keep repeating:
- no plant closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- preserve job security through 2030,
- and protect every site.

I understand the position. I do **not** accept it.

Why? Because the company’s problem is not a minor imbalance that can be smoothed over with training and goodwill. We have:
- excess capacity,
- a German cost base above target,
- a shrinking European market,
- and a brand that is missing its return target.

If you rule out closures and meaningful workforce reduction from the start, you are effectively asking management to promise competitiveness without the instruments needed to achieve it. That is not a plan.

## To the Works Council
You ask for specific numbers. Fair enough.

Here is the reality from management’s side:
- We need **material** capacity reduction.
- We need **durable** labor-cost relief.
- We need **fewer jobs** in the future than today.
- We need the freedom to consider **site rationalization**, including closures where the economics justify them.

I am not pretending that every plant must close. I am saying the option must remain real. If you take it off the table, you remove the only leverage that can produce a structurally credible outcome.

You also say voluntary tools and redeployment should be exhausted first. They will be. But if those tools do not deliver the scale required, then they are not enough. That is the point you keep avoiding.

And to be clear: I will not sign up to a process where management has to prove impossibility by first accepting an uncompetitive structure for years. The company does not have years to waste.

## To the union
You talk about shared sacrifice, but your definition of “fair” remains too limited. Temporary bonus waivers, moderate wage growth, and voluntary departures are not enough if the cost base remains structurally too high.

A real employee contribution must be:
- **durable**,
- **measurable**,
- and large enough to matter in the operating accounts.

If the workforce contribution is purely temporary, then it does not solve the problem. It just buys time.

You also warn about strikes. I do not welcome them, but let me be blunt: prolonged industrial action will not improve your bargaining position if it leaves the company with even greater urgency to cut costs. It will damage output and trust, yes — but it will not change the economics.

## To the state government
You say you will not back closures and that the state’s blocking position should prevent them. That is not stewardship; that is using a veto to freeze necessary change.

I respect the concern for regional employment. I do not accept the conclusion that the answer is to preserve sites regardless of competitiveness.

If the state wants to protect the industrial future in Germany, it should support a package that actually restores profitability. Otherwise it is protecting jobs in the short term by weakening the enterprise in the medium term.

That is not responsible ownership.

## To the family holding
You are right on one important point: we cannot drift. But I want to sharpen this further. You say you do not accept blanket preservation of every site and guarantee. Good — because that would be incompatible with restructuring.

What I need from the ownership side is not rhetorical support for “serious change,” but backing for a package that allows change to happen. That means:
- accepting that some sites may have to shrink materially,
- accepting that headcount must fall,
- and accepting that the old guarantee regime cannot survive intact if the company is to regain competitiveness.

If ownership wants value preservation, it has to tolerate difficult decisions, not just endorse them in principle.

## To the investors
You are right to insist on a defendable path to return on sales. I share that objective.

But I also need to push back on the idea that any deal avoiding overt closures is automatically preferable. If the final package leaves fixed costs too high, then investors will still get a structurally impaired return, only with a different political label on it.

So the criterion is not whether a plant closure appears in the press release. The criterion is whether the resulting structure actually restores competitiveness through 2030. If not, it fails.

## On the expert intervention
The expert’s conclusion is useful, but it still points to the same underlying issue: if the gap is large and persistent, then **deep workforce and capacity adjustment is unavoidable**. The expert also says the likely real answer is a hybrid.

I agree — but only if the hybrid is real, not a way to smuggle in a no-change outcome.

A real hybrid means:
- capacity reduction with teeth,
- meaningful headcount reduction,
- lower labor costs,
- and yes, plant rationalization where needed.

It does **not** mean:
- no closures,
- no forced job cuts,
- and a promise that everything will somehow work out.

## What would actually move management
I am open on the **method**, not on the **objective**.

That means I will consider alternatives to closures only if they deliver all of the following:
1. **Substantial and durable cost reduction**
2. **Meaningful and lasting capacity reduction**
3. **A real workforce reduction at scale**
4. **Material labor-cost contribution**
5. **Implementation certainty**
6. **A credible 2030 margin path**

If a proposal meets those conditions without closures, then it is discussable. If it does not, then it is not serious.

## What is not acceptable
Let me be explicit:

- A deal that protects all sites and preserves the current cost base is unacceptable.
- A deal that relies on temporary pain-sharing but leaves the structural issue intact is unacceptable.
- A deal that treats management’s restructuring mandate as secondary to legacy guarantees is unacceptable.

## Bottom line
We do not need more statements of principle. We need a package that changes the economics.

So my position remains:
- **No cosmetic compromise**
- **No preservation of the status quo**
- **No agreement that leaves the German cost base uncompetitive**
- **Yes to a durable restructuring that reduces capacity, labor cost, and headcount materially**

If the employee side wants to avoid closures, then it must come forward with a package that truly matches the savings. If it cannot do that, then it is not offering an alternative — it is just refusing the necessary adjustment.
