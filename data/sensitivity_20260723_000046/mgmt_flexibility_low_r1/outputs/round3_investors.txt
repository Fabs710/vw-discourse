## Where we agree
We agree with management, the family holding, and the expert on the core diagnosis:

- this is a **structural** problem, not a temporary dip;
- **temporary measures alone are not enough**;
- the company needs **durable cost and capacity reduction**;
- and **prolonged conflict destroys value**.

We also agree with labor and the state on one practical point: a restructuring that cannot be implemented will not create value. So the final package has to be executable, not just theoretically neat.

## Where we disagree
We do **not** support the labor/state position as stated if it means:

- no plant closures under any circumstances,
- no compulsory redundancies regardless of economics,
- and meaningful guarantees on top.

That combination would leave the company with too little room to correct the cost base. For shareholders, that is not a restructuring; it is margin impairment with a better public narrative.

At the same time, we also do **not** support management if it treats closures and layoffs as the default answer without proving the numbers. “Urgency” is not a business case.

## What matters to us
From the capital markets’ perspective, the only question that matters is:

**Does the package produce a credible, quantified path back to competitive margins and return-on-sales by 2027/2030?**

If yes, we can support a difficult compromise.
If no, we will not back it, no matter how politely it is framed.

## On closures
We are **not ideologically attached** to closures. But we are also **not prepared to rule them out in advance**.

A no-closure solution is acceptable only if it delivers:
- meaningful fixed-cost reduction,
- real capacity reduction,
- durable labor-cost relief,
- and implementation certainty.

If “no closures” really means “same footprint plus temporary concessions,” then that is not enough. That would leave the cost base structurally uncompetitive, which is exactly what we cannot accept.

## On compulsory redundancies
Our preference is to avoid forced layoffs if possible, because they raise execution risk and can trigger prolonged conflict. But they cannot be excluded automatically if voluntary tools are insufficient.

The sequence should be:
1. voluntary departures,
2. retirement corridors,
3. redeployment,
4. working-time and pay moderation,
5. productivity measures,
6. and only then a hard look at whether the remaining gap can still be closed.

If the answer is still no, then the company must have the freedom to use stronger measures.

## On labor’s contribution
We are open to a **real, measurable, time-bound employee contribution**. But it must be:
- material enough to matter to the P&L,
- durable enough not to evaporate after one cycle,
- and not become permanent wage dumping.

A symbolic gesture is not a restructuring.

## On management’s role
Management needs to stop arguing at the level of principle and bring the model.

We want the following quantified, site-by-site:
- current capacity and utilization,
- fixed-cost burden by plant,
- savings from closure,
- savings from partial rationalization,
- savings from voluntary exits and working-time changes,
- strike/delay risk,
- and the 5-year NPV of each option.

Without that, the board and the market are being asked to endorse a conclusion without evidence.

## On the state and the family
We respect the governance reality, but governance is not a substitute for economics.

- The **state** should not use its blocking role to freeze all adjustment if the company remains structurally uncompetitive.
- The **family holding** should not use its control to preserve legacy structures that impair long-term value.

Both should focus on the same thing we do: a credible route to sustainable margins.

## Our threshold
We can support a deal only if it clearly delivers:

1. **durable cost reduction**
2. **meaningful capacity reduction**
3. **credible margin improvement**
4. **deal certainty**
5. **a viable path to the return-on-sales target**

If those are achieved without closures, fine.
If they are not, then closures cannot be ruled out just because they are politically difficult.

## What we would not support
We will not support:
- cosmetic compromise,
- a settlement that preserves an uncompetitive footprint,
- or a “socially acceptable” deal that leaves shareholders with structurally impaired returns.

## Bottom line
We are prepared to back a tough restructuring if it is real, quantified, and durable. We are not prepared to back a settlement that protects every site at the expense of competitiveness.

If management wants support, bring the numbers. If labor wants to avoid closures, show that the alternative truly closes the gap. Otherwise, the market will assume the company is choosing delay over value creation.
