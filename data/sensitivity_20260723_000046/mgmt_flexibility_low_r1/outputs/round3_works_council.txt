## First: what I agree with
I agree with the moderator and the expert on one point: **we need hard numbers, not slogans**. If management wants to claim that its only credible route is closures and forced layoffs, then it must prove that with site-by-site data, not with pressure tactics.

I also agree that **temporary measures alone are not enough**. No one here is asking for cosmetic change. We need real restructuring.

But let me be clear: **real restructuring does not automatically mean plant closures or compulsory redundancies**. That is management’s preferred instrument set, not an economic law.

## Where I disagree with management
Management keeps repeating “durable savings” as if that phrase justifies any instrument. It does not.

You say the company needs:
- material capacity reduction,
- durable labor-cost relief,
- fewer jobs,
- and the freedom to close sites.

I reject the last two as negotiated defaults. If the company has a structural problem, then management must first prove that it cannot solve it through:
- internal redeployment,
- qualification,
- working-time reductions,
- voluntary departures,
- retirement corridors,
- productivity improvements,
- and a fair, temporary employee contribution.

That list has not been exhausted. So no, you do **not** get to jump straight to closures and call it “responsible management.”

And let me say something else plainly: if management is asking the workforce to accept closures, then it is asking us to pay for strategic failure with jobs and livelihoods. That is not a restructuring mandate; that is social demolition.

## Where I disagree with the family holding
You say closures are not your preferred route, but you won’t rule them out. That sounds balanced, but in practice it means the burden stays on workers while ownership keeps the option to sacrifice sites when convenient.

I do not accept that logic.

If the family truly sees Germany as central to long-term value, then it should back an approach that **preserves industrial capacity and the workforce base**, not one that treats sites as disposable if the spreadsheet gets uncomfortable.

You ask for a business case showing alternatives cannot work. Fine — but that cuts both ways. You have not shown that closures are superior once you include:
- strike risk,
- governance blockage,
- loss of skills,
- damage to industrial peace,
- and the cost of rebuilding capacity later.

So no, closures are not some obviously rational baseline. They are a high-risk move that may destroy more value than it creates.

## Where I disagree with investors
You say you want a credible path to competitive margins. So do we. But your argument is still too narrow.

A margin target is not credible if it is bought by:
- breaking co-determination trust,
- hollowing out key plants,
- and provoking a prolonged labor confrontation.

That is not “value creation.” That is pushing costs and risks onto the workforce in the hope the market will reward the brutality. Sometimes markets punish exactly that.

You also keep demanding “quantified” savings as if the only uncertainty lies with labor. But your preferred route — closures — carries its own hidden costs:
- political resistance,
- implementation delay,
- social-plan expense,
- disruption,
- and reputational damage.

If you want NPV discipline, include all of that.

## Where I disagree with the state government
I appreciate the state’s support for sites, jobs, and co-determination. But I want to sharpen the point: **a blocking position is only meaningful if it is used to prevent closures, not just to negotiate around them.**

You say you won’t support sacrificing plants as the first answer. Good. Then stand by that. Do not turn this into a bargaining process where workers are asked how much pain they will tolerate before the veto is used.

The state should not be looking for a “balanced” closure deal. It should be defending the regional industrial base. That is the whole point of its role.

## Where I disagree with the union if it softens on the red line
The union is right on the core principle:
- no plant closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- preserve the wage framework,
- protect the job-security framework.

That remains the minimum basis for any settlement.

What I will not accept is any drift toward “we can discuss closures if the package is good enough.” No. That would be crossing a red line that should not be opened in the first place.

Temporary wage moderation, bonus waivers, and working-time flexibility can be discussed **only** if they are:
- limited,
- temporary,
- balanced,
- and matched by binding guarantees on sites and jobs.

But let me be precise: those are not concessions we make to buy closure risk. They are contributions to stabilize the company **without closures**. That distinction matters.

## On the expert input
The expert has been useful, but the conclusion is not “closures might be cheaper, therefore closures should be on the table.” That is too simplistic.

The actual conclusion is:
- closures may have higher recurring savings,
- but they also have higher upfront cost,
- higher implementation risk,
- and higher political and industrial-relations damage.

The expert also said a negotiated no-closure package can be economically credible if it includes real capacity reduction, voluntary exits, working-time changes, and durable labor-cost relief.

That is the path we should be pursuing — **not because it is easy, but because it is the only path compatible with keeping every German plant open**.

## What must be quantified now
Management keeps saying the German footprint is too large. Then show us, concretely:

- Which plant is supposedly surplus?
- What is the actual capacity utilization?
- What savings come from shift reductions?
- What savings come from internal redeployment?
- What savings come from voluntary departures?
- What savings come from temporary cost contributions?
- What investment and product commitments are tied to each site through 2030?

If management cannot produce that table, then its closure talk is not a business plan. It is a threat.

And until that proof exists, the default position in this company must be:
**every German plant stays open, no compulsory redundancies, and the burden is shared through negotiated alternatives.**

If you are asking us to retreat from that, you are asking us to abandon our responsibility to the workforce. I will not do that.

We are ready to engage on a serious restructuring package. But the parameters are fixed:

- **No German plant closures**
- **No compulsory redundancies**
- **Preserve co-determination and job security**
- **Use negotiated, reversible, socially responsible tools first**
- **Demand a real investment and product future for Germany through 2030**

Bring a quantified alternative that respects those conditions. If you cannot, then the problem is not the works council’s position — it is the failure of management to produce a viable restructuring plan without breaking the social contract.
