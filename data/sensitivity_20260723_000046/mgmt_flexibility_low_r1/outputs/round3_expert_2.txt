## 1) What can be concluded now

The expert input is directionally correct:

- **Closure-led restructuring** usually gives the **largest recurring cost reduction**.
- It also usually carries the **largest one-off cash cost**, the **highest implementation friction**, and the **highest risk of delay/disruption** in a German co-determined setting.

  1. the **size of the structural overcapacity/cost gap**,
  2. the **speed and certainty of execution**,
  3. the **scale of one-off restructuring costs**,
  4. the **probability and duration of industrial action**,
  5. and the **extent of product/capacity reallocation to remaining sites**.

## 3) Simple 5-year scenario framework

I’ll use a simplified framework to show how the comparison works.

### Discounting
- 5-year horizon
- illustrative discount rate: **8%**
- present value factors:
  - Year 1: 0.926
  - Year 2: 0.857
  - Year 3: 0.794
  - Year 4: 0.735
  - Year 5: 0.681

### Scenarios
I’ll model three cases:

1. **Closure-led**
   - one or more plants closed
   - compulsory redundancies possible
   - large recurring savings
   - high one-off cost
   - higher disruption risk

2. **Partial rationalization**
   - no immediate closure
   - one or more sites materially downsized
   - voluntary exits + working-time changes + selective redeployment
   - moderate recurring savings
   - moderate one-off cost
   - lower disruption risk than closure

3. **No-closure negotiated package**
   - all sites remain open
   - savings from hours, redeployment, wage moderation, voluntary exits, and productivity
   - lowest one-off cost
   - highest risk of insufficient structural savings if the gap is large

## 5) How to interpret that result

In this illustration, the **no-closure package has the best 5-year NPV**, not because it creates the biggest operating improvement, but because it avoids large upfront restructuring cash and transition losses.

However, that result is highly sensitive to the assumptions:

- If the underlying overcapacity is larger,
- if labor-cost relief is small,
- if voluntary exits are insufficient,
- or if a no-closure package triggers repeated concessions later,

then the no-closure NPV falls quickly.

So this is not a general endorsement of no-closure. It is a statement that **within a 5-year window**, a negotiated solution can look superior if it is cheaper to implement and if it delivers enough savings.

## 6) Sensitivity points that would change the result materially

### A. Size of annual savings
If closure-led recurring savings rise from €500m to €650m per year, the closure case improves materially and may overtake the others.

### B. One-off closure cost
If closure social-plan, severance, and decommissioning costs exceed €1.5bn rather than €1.1bn, closure-led NPV weakens sharply.

### C. Strike / delay duration
If a closure package triggers 6–9 months of production loss or major launch delays, the economic case can be heavily eroded.

### D. Utilization and product allocation
If remaining plants can absorb volume efficiently, closure economics improve.
If they require expensive retooling, extra logistics, or overtime, closure economics worsen.

### E. Political / governance delay
In a setting with board vetoes and co-determination constraints, any added implementation year reduces NPV sharply.

### F. Workforce contribution size
A meaningful, durable labor-cost contribution can materially improve a no-closure package.
A symbolic concession does not move the economics enough.

## 8) Practical decision rule

A sensible finance test would be:

### Closure-led is justified if:
- a site is clearly redundant,
- closure cost is contained,
- strike/delay risk is manageable,
- and the remaining network can absorb volume without major extra capex.

### No-closure is justified if:
- savings from capacity reduction, hours, voluntary exits, and wage moderation close most of the gap,
- one-off costs are materially lower,
- and industrial conflict is meaningfully reduced.

### Partial rationalization is usually the middle case if:
- some capacity must leave the network,
- but a full closure is too costly or too politically blocked,
- and the company can preserve productive sites while reducing fixed cost.
