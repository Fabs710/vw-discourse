I will respond directly.

## To the Works Council and Union
I acknowledge one thing clearly: you are right that the company needs a **durable** solution, not a headline fix. You are also right that a prolonged conflict would damage the company. On that, we agree.

But I reject your central premise: **“no plant closures, no compulsory redundancies, no meaningful change to guarantees”** is not a restructuring answer to a structural cost crisis.

You keep asking management to prove why your preferred outcome should be changed. I’ll turn that around: **if you rule out the instruments that remove overcapacity and reset the cost base, then you are asking the company to stay uncompetitive and call it protection**. That is not responsible.

The expert has now confirmed the basic industrial reality:
- no-closure solutions can work only if they deliver **comparable permanent savings**,
- otherwise they become a delay tactic,
- and if capacity is truly excessive, closure or equivalent consolidation is usually the more reliable route.

That supports management’s core point, not yours.

## Where I agree with the expert
The expert is correct that this is not about moral preference. It is about whether the package produces:
1. enough permanent fixed-cost reduction,
2. enough capacity reduction,
3. enough labor-cost relief,
4. and enough speed.

That is exactly why we are pressing for deep structural measures.

## Where I disagree with the labour side
You keep framing closures as if they were an ideological choice. They are not. They are a possible consequence of a cost structure that is too heavy for the market we are in.

If you want to avoid closures, then the alternative must be **economically equivalent**, not merely politically easier. That means:
- material and durable wage moderation,
- working-time flexibility with real cost effect,
- headcount reduction at scale,
- productivity gains that actually stick,
- and capacity reduction that genuinely changes the economics.

So far, what you have put forward is still too vague on scale and enforceability. “No closures” is not a savings plan.

## On compulsory redundancies
I am not pretending that forced layoffs are desirable. They are the last resort. But I will not accept a framework that makes them impossible in advance. If the required reduction cannot be achieved through voluntary instruments alone, then the company must have the ability to adjust.

That is not a threat; it is an economic reality.

## On job-security guarantees
Historic guarantees cannot remain untouchable if they block the turnaround. I hear your concern about trust, but trust does not pay for underutilized plants. We cannot protect every legacy commitment and still claim to have solved the structural problem.

## To the State Government
I understand your political responsibility to the region. But your position cannot be: “protect the industrial base” and simultaneously preserve every German site regardless of economics.

A regional anchor shareholder should support a solution that keeps the company viable long term. If that requires tough choices, then those choices cannot be vetoed simply because they are difficult.

That said, your point is important: closures should not be treated as the default first move. Fine. They are not the first move. But they must remain available if the numbers require them.

## To the Controlling Family Holding
You are right about one thing: a destructive conflict would damage long-term value. That is precisely why we need a settlement that actually works.

But I disagree with any suggestion that the company can preserve enterprise value while avoiding the core adjustment. Value is also destroyed by keeping excess cost, weak margins, and chronic underinvestment in place. A company cannot invest its way out of a broken cost base.

## To Investors
You are right to demand economic credibility. But that means you should not support a cosmetic compromise dressed up as social peace.

If a no-closure package truly delivers equivalent savings, then fine — test it. But if it doesn’t, then we should not pretend otherwise. Investors should want the same thing management wants: a real improvement in return-on-sales and a durable cost reset.

## What management is prepared to accept
We are open to a negotiated solution that avoids closures **if** it is structurally sufficient. That means the package must include:
- significant labor-cost contribution,
- meaningful capacity reduction,
- enforceable productivity improvements,
- and binding site specialization or consolidation where necessary.

But we will not accept:
- a deal that leaves the cost base fundamentally unchanged,
- a short-lived concession package,
- or a “no closures” settlement that merely postpones the same crisis to 2027 or 2028.

## Bottom line
Management’s position remains:
- **the restructuring must be structurally sufficient**;
- **overcapacity has to come out**;
- **labor cost must come down materially**;
- and **closures remain on the table** if that is what is required to achieve a durable solution.

If the employee side wants to avoid closures and forced redundancies, then it needs to put forward a package that is not just socially preferable, but economically equivalent in lasting effect. Until then, I will not retreat from the core restructuring mandate.
