Let me be equally direct in round 2:

**The workforce will not accept plant closures in Germany or compulsory redundancies as the price of this restructuring.**  
That is not a bargaining chip for us. It is a red line.

I heard management, investors, and the family holding all say that closures “remain on the table” unless the employee side produces an “economically equivalent” alternative. I reject that framing. It puts the burden of proving innocence on the workforce while management retains the power to threaten irreversible damage. That is not genuine negotiation; it is pressure through ultimatum.

## Where I agree
I do agree on a few points:

- The company has a real competitiveness problem.
- A cosmetic plan will not work.
- The solution must be durable through 2030, not just a one-year patch.
- A prolonged conflict would hurt everyone.

But agreement on the diagnosis does **not** mean agreement on the method.

## Where I disagree
I disagree with the claim that the only serious restructuring is one that includes:
- closures,
- forced layoffs,
- and tearing up job-security guarantees.

That is precisely the kind of approach that destroys trust, weakens the industrial base, and undermines the company’s long-term ability to perform. Once a German plant is closed, the loss is not “efficiency”; it is the destruction of skills, supplier networks, training capacity, and social stability.

And let me be very clear: **historic guarantees cannot simply be declared obsolete because management wants more flexibility.** If management wants to change the social contract, it must first justify why employees should carry the full risk for decisions they did not make.

## What we are prepared to discuss
We are prepared to discuss a restructuring **only on the basis of keeping every German plant open** and **avoiding compulsory redundancies**. That means:

- capacity adjustments through working-time tools,
- internal redeployment,
- qualification and retraining,
- voluntary exit pathways only,
- temporary and fair employee contributions,
- and binding investment commitments for the German sites.

If the company genuinely needs cost relief, then the burden must be shared. That means management and owners must also contribute visibly — not just ask workers to make sacrifices while preserving every strategic option for capital.

## What is required for any serious settlement
If management wants the works council to even consider a deal, it must put forward:

1. **Binding site guarantees to 2030**
   - every German plant remains open;
   - no hidden closure through underinvestment or production transfer.

2. **No compulsory redundancies**
   - not as a promise “if possible,” but as a hard commitment.

3. **Real investment commitments**
   - products, platforms, tooling, and future production volume in Germany.

4. **Transparent numbers**
   - what savings are actually required;
   - what alternatives have been tested;
   - what the impact is under each option.

5. **Co-determination in implementation**
   - no fait accompli;
   - the workforce representatives must have a real role in shaping the turnaround.

## On employee contribution
We have never said “no contribution.” We have said **no one-sided sacrifice**.

If there is to be an employee contribution, it must be:
- temporary,
- proportionate,
- negotiated,
- and matched by commitments from management and owners.

What we will **not** accept is using wage concessions or flexibility demands as a bridge to closures later. That is why trust is so low: we have seen how “temporary” measures become permanent pressure.

## To management
If you believe there is no path to competitiveness without closures, then show the numbers transparently and compare them honestly against alternatives. But do not expect the works council to bless a closure-first strategy simply because you call it “structural necessity.”

You have to prove:
- why the burden cannot be shared differently,
- why all non-closure options are insufficient,
- and why the social and industrial destruction would be justified.

So far, I have not seen that case made convincingly.

## To the state government
Your caution is right. You said closures should not be the default answer. I agree. But caution is not enough. Use your position to insist on a package that protects sites and jobs, not one that opens the door to permanent downsizing in Germany.

## To investors and the family holding
You keep saying “we need economically sufficient measures.” Fine. Then support a real negotiated package that preserves the company’s productive base instead of betting on irreversible site destruction. A structurally healthier company is not one that shrinks by breaking its own workforce.

## Bottom line
The works council’s position remains:

- **No plant closures**
- **No compulsory redundancies**
- **No dismantling of job-security and co-determination guarantees**

If management comes with a package that respects those principles, we can negotiate seriously on the details of capacity, flexibility, and contributions. If not, then there will be resistance — in the supervisory board, in collective bargaining, and on the shop floor.

If management wants a durable solution, it must stop treating the workforce as the adjustment variable of last resort.
