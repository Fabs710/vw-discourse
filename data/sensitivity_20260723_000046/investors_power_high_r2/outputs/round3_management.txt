I will be direct, because we are now in the phase where clarity matters more than rhetoric.

## To the Works Council and Union

I agree with you on **one essential point**: this company needs a **durable** solution, not a cosmetic one. I also agree that a prolonged conflict would damage everyone.

Where I disagree is on your attempt to turn **“no closures, no compulsory redundancies, no meaningful change to guarantees”** into a fixed starting framework. That is not a restructuring framework. It is a request to preserve the current cost structure and hope the market becomes more forgiving. It will not.

You keep saying management must prove necessity before anything irreversible happens. I reject the implication that management has to prove the obvious from scratch: **the German cost base is too high, capacity is too high, and the brand is missing its return target**. That is the reality we are paid to fix.

### On closures
You say closures are never acceptable. I cannot accept that as an absolute. If a plant network is structurally oversized, then closures are not ideology — they are one of the few instruments that can permanently remove fixed cost and simplify the network.

The expert has already said what matters:
- a no-closure package is only credible if it delivers **comparable permanent savings and capacity reduction**;
- otherwise it is just delay;
- and if the network remains overextended, the cost problem remains.

That is the core issue. Not the label “closure,” but whether the economics are fixed.

### On compulsory redundancies
I would prefer voluntary measures too. But I will not accept a framework that makes workforce reduction impossible in advance. If the required adjustment cannot be achieved voluntarily, then the company must retain the ability to act. Otherwise we are pretending that structural overcapacity can be managed by goodwill alone.

### On job-security guarantees
Historic guarantees cannot be treated as untouchable if they block the turnaround. Guarantees that made sense in a different market context cannot be used to freeze the company in place while competitors move faster and cheaper.

You are asking us to preserve protections without accepting the structural consequences. That is not sustainable.

## To the State Government

I appreciate your caution, and I agree that closures should not be treated as a reflex. But I do **not** accept the idea that the state can insist on industrial continuity while also ruling out the instruments needed to achieve it.

If the no-closure alternative is truly economically equivalent, then fine — let’s test it. But if it is not, then the state should not support a settlement that preserves political comfort and postpones the crisis.

Your role should be to ensure a viable industrial base, not to block every difficult adjustment before the numbers are tested.

## To the Controlling Family Holding

You are right that a cosmetic compromise would destroy value. On that we agree.

Where I push back is your reluctance to confront the fact that value is also destroyed by **keeping too much cost and capacity in the system**. A company cannot invest its way out of a structurally uncompetitive network. If the cost base stays too high, the brand misses its margin target and future investment gets squeezed.

So yes: preserve value. But that means accepting real restructuring, not shielding every legacy structure from change.

## To Investors

You are right to demand economic sufficiency and measurable outcomes. I agree.

But I am concerned by your repeated framing that closures remain the credible fallback unless labour proves otherwise. That sounds neat, but it is too easy to say and too hard to operationalize without hard data.

What we still need is a **plant-by-plant comparison** using the same assumptions:
- fixed cost,
- utilization,
- capacity removed,
- labor-cost relief,
- transition cost,
- and 3–5 year margin impact.

Until that is available, “economically equivalent” remains a slogan on both sides.

## My position

Let me state it plainly:

- I do **not** accept a cosmetic no-closure deal that leaves the cost base basically unchanged.
- I do **not** accept a temporary peace deal that kicks the restructuring problem into 2027 or 2028.
- I do **not** accept a settlement where employee contributions are real but structurally insufficient.
- I do **not** accept a framework that rules out the instruments needed to restore competitiveness.

What I **do** accept is a serious negotiated solution if it delivers:
- substantial and durable labor-cost relief,
- real capacity reduction,
- site specialization and simplification,
- a meaningful headcount adjustment path,
- and a credible path back to competitiveness and investment capacity through 2030.

That is the standard.
