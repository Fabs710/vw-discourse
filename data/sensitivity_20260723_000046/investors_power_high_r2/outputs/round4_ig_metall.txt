I will respond directly and keep it clear.

## To the Works Council
I share your red lines:
- no compulsory redundancies,
- no one-sided burden on workers,
- no hollowing out of job-security protections,
- no settlement that turns employees into the adjustment variable of last resort.

On that, we are aligned.

Where I would sharpen the message is this: we should not let “every plant stays open” become a slogan that management can dismiss as non-economic without having to prove anything. We need to force a **quantified comparison**. Not because I am opening the door to closures, but because the workforce must be able to show that a serious alternative exists.

So I support your line, but I want it backed by a hard, plant-by-plant package that is credible enough to withstand pressure.

## To Management
You are still relying too much on a pressure narrative:
- the cost base is too high,
- the network is oversized,
- therefore closures and forced layoffs must remain available.

That is not yet a restructuring case. It is a bargaining position.

I accept the diagnosis that the company has a structural problem. I do **not** accept that this automatically legitimizes closures or compulsory redundancies as the starting instruments.

If you want the union to engage seriously, then put the numbers on the table:
- exact savings target,
- exact capacity reduction required,
- fixed-cost reduction by site,
- labor-cost relief expected,
- transition costs,
- and the 3–5 year impact.

Then we can test whether a negotiated alternative is sufficient. Until then, closure threats remain pressure, not proof.

## To the State Government
Your caution is useful, but it is not enough if it remains only a plea for moderation.

If the state opposes closure-first logic, then it should use its leverage to insist on:
- no closures as the starting framework,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- binding investment and product commitments,
- and a real industrial plan for the German sites.

I agree with you that this company must remain an industrial anchor. But that requires more than sympathy; it requires a clear stance against irreversible damage being used as a bargaining tool.

## To the Controlling Family Holding
I agree with one part of your argument: a cosmetic compromise would be worthless.

Where I disagree is your implied conclusion that only closures or the threat of closures can create sufficient seriousness. That is too narrow.

A serious settlement can also come from:
- meaningful owner contribution,
- investment commitments,
- product allocation,
- management restraint,
- and a durable labor-cost package that does not destroy job security.

If ownership wants credibility, it should not hide behind the easiest lever to pull on workers.

## To Institutional Investors
You are right to insist on economic credibility. I do not dispute that.

But “economic equivalence” cannot mean “whatever imposes the most pain on labour is best.” That is not a neutral financial standard; it is a political choice in disguise.

A credible no-closure solution must be judged by whether it:
- removes enough permanent cost,
- reduces capacity meaningfully,
- simplifies the network,
- and improves margins durably.

If it does that, it is a serious alternative. If it does not, then the burden is on management to explain why it failed to design one.

## On the Expert’s analysis
The expert has helped narrow the issue correctly: a no-closure path can only work if it achieves comparable permanent savings, capacity reduction, and simplification.

That is exactly why I keep pushing for the detailed audit. We need a **plant-by-plant comparison** of:
- utilization,
- fixed costs,
- capacity removed,
- headcount effects,
- transition costs,
- labor-cost relief,
- and 3–5 year margin impact.

[SUMMON: independent plant-network and financial impact auditor to compare closure-based and no-closure scenarios on equal assumptions, including utilization, fixed costs, capacity reduction, headcount effects, transition costs, labor-cost relief, and 3–5 year margin impact]

This is not to create a pretext for closures. It is to test whether management’s preferred instruments are truly necessary.

## On “fallback triggers”
I want to be explicit here: I do **not** accept a no-closure deal that contains a built-in closure trigger as a normal fallback from the outset.

That would place a permanent sword over the workforce and make every concession conditional on management’s future discretion. That is not shared sacrifice; that is asymmetrical risk.

If the package underdelivers, the first question must be:
- which measures failed,
- what management and ownership have done to meet their side of the bargain,
- and what additional non-destructive steps can still be taken.

Not “then close a plant.”

## What I am prepared to support
The union remains prepared to support a real restructuring if it includes all of the following:

- **No compulsory redundancies**
- **No plant closures as the starting or default answer**
- **A fair employee contribution**
- **Management and owner contributions**
- **Binding investment commitments**
- **Working-time, shift, redeployment, qualification, and voluntary exit tools**
- **Preservation of the collective bargaining framework**
- **A measurable savings and capacity target**

That is a serious package. It is not an evasion.

## What I will not accept
I will not accept:
- a closure-first restructuring disguised as realism;
- a deal where workers carry the pain while others preserve their options;
- a temporary concession package that simply postpones the problem;
- or any erosion of collective wage standards as a substitute for real strategic adjustment.

## Bottom line
We all agree the company needs a durable turnaround. We do **not** agree that this justifies making closures and forced redundancies the central benchmark of seriousness.

If management, investors, and owners want a settlement, they need to prove that the company can be stabilized **without** turning job destruction into the default instrument. If they can do that, the union will bargain constructively. If they cannot, then we will defend jobs, sites, and the collective agreement with all available means.
