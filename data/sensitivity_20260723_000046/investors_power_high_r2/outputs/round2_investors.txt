From the capital markets’ perspective, the discussion has become much clearer in round 2, and that is useful.

## Where we agree

We agree with management and the expert on the central financial point:

- the company has a **real structural cost problem**;
- the current cost base is **too high for the market reality**;
- a **cosmetic settlement is worthless**;
- and the outcome must deliver **durable, not temporary, margin repair**.

We also agree with the state government and, in part, with labour representatives on one procedural point:

- a **prolonged industrial war destroys value** quickly;
- a negotiated settlement is better than open-ended conflict.

That said, the agreement ends there.

## Where we disagree

### 1) “No closures” is not a financial answer by itself
The works council and union keep repeating that closures are unacceptable. I understand the social logic, but we do **not** invest on the basis of social logic alone.

What matters is whether the final package:
- removes enough fixed cost,
- reduces capacity enough,
- improves return on sales enough,
- and does so with sufficient durability and certainty.

If “no closures” still leaves the company with an uncompetitive cost base, then we cannot support it.

### 2) Employee red lines cannot override economic reality
The labour side is asking for:
- no closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- no meaningful break with legacy guarantees,
- and strong site protection.

Those are understandable demands, but they are **not automatically investable**. The expert has now made the key point: a no-closure package is only credible if it achieves **equivalent permanent savings**.

That means the burden is not on shareholders to bless a preferred social outcome. The burden is on the whole negotiating table to produce a package that actually fixes the economics.

### 3) Management still has to prove the numbers, not just the threat
I also want to be explicit that management cannot hide behind “closures remain on the table” as a substitute for a quantified restructuring case.

The expert has correctly said the comparison must be on the same basis:
- fixed cost reduction,
- capacity reduction,
- labor-cost relief,
- complexity reduction,
- transition costs,
- and execution risk.

So far, we still do not have enough hard numbers in the public discussion to judge whether the no-closure alternative is truly equivalent. That is a problem.

## What would move us

We would support a no-closure package **if and only if** it is proven to achieve all of the following:

1. **Durable cost reduction**
   - substantial enough to restore competitive margins.

2. **Real capacity reduction**
   - not just re-labelling spare capacity or shifting it around.

3. **Verified labour-cost relief**
   - wage moderation, bonus restraint, working-time changes, or comparable measures that stick.

4. **Enforceable headcount reduction path**
   - through voluntary exits, redeployment, retirement, attrition, or other reliable mechanisms.

5. **Network simplification and site specialization**
   - because keeping every plant broadly intact while cutting a bit around the edges is not enough.

6. **A credible fallback**
   - if the package misses its targets, then further restructuring must be possible.

## What we will not support

We will not support any of the following:

- a deal that keeps all sites open but leaves the economics basically unchanged;
- a temporary peace deal that postpones the same crisis to 2027 or 2028;
- labour contributions that are symbolic rather than financially meaningful;
- or governance arrangements that block adaptation indefinitely.

That is the line. Anything that leaves the company structurally uncompetitive is unacceptable from an investor perspective.

## On plant closures

We are **not ideologically attached** to closures. If a no-closure solution truly delivers the same permanent economic result, that is preferable because it reduces disruption.

But if the alternatives do not close the gap, then closures must remain on the table. Not as punishment, but as a necessary instrument to remove overcapacity and reset the cost base.

### Important nuance
We do **not** need an immediate closure announcement today. But we do need the option to remain credible until the numbers show otherwise.

## On compulsory redundancies

We prefer avoiding compulsory redundancies because they are disruptive and can deepen conflict. But we cannot accept a framework in which workforce adjustment becomes impossible.

If the savings target is real and voluntary measures are insufficient, then the restructuring must still be able to proceed. The company cannot be locked into a cost structure it cannot carry.

## On the state government’s position

The state is right to warn against irreversible damage to the industrial base. But that caution cannot become a veto on necessary change.

If the no-closure route is financially equivalent, excellent. If it is not, then the state should not support a settlement that prioritizes political comfort over enterprise viability.

## On the works council and union

You are right about one thing: the workforce should not be treated as a dumping ground for management failure.

But you are wrong if you think that statement alone is sufficient. Workers are not the only stakeholders whose interests matter, and a structurally uncompetitive company eventually damages everyone — including workers.

So if you want our support, bring a **measurable package**:
- quantified savings,
- actual capacity reduction,
- durable cost relief,
- and clear evidence that margins will recover.

## Bottom line

Our position is unchanged but sharper after hearing the expert:

- **We will back a serious restructuring.**
- **We will back a credible no-closure alternative only if it is economically equivalent.**
- **We will not back a cosmetic settlement that preserves too much cost and capacity.**
- **We will not tolerate prolonged conflict that destroys value without producing durable savings.**

If management wants investor support, it must bring a plan that proves the math. If labour wants to avoid closures, it must be prepared to deliver the same economic effect through other means. Until then, no side gets a blank cheque.
