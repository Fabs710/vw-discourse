I appreciate that both the Works Council and the union are at least acknowledging the core issue: the company needs a credible turnaround, and employee-side contributions are part of that discussion. On that point, we agree.

But I need to be equally clear: **the current position you are taking does not yet solve the structural problem we are facing**.

## Where I agree
- We do need a durable solution, not a symbolic one.
- We do need transparency on the assumptions.
- We do need a negotiated settlement, because without one the company cannot execute the full restructuring at the pace required.
- We do need investment in future products and technology.

## Where I disagree
Your red lines — **no plant closures, no compulsory redundancies, no meaningful break with historic guarantees** — are not compatible with the scale of the cost crisis as management sees it.

We are not talking about a mild efficiency program. We are talking about a cost base that is structurally above target in a market that is shrinking and increasingly unforgiving. If we pretend that this can be fixed entirely through working-time tweaks, redeployment, and voluntary measures, then we are not dealing with reality. That would leave us with:
- too much capacity,
- too high fixed costs,
- too little room to invest,
- and a business that remains uncompetitive into 2030.

That is not acceptable to management, to the owners, or to the capital providers who expect a viable return.

## On closures
I will say it plainly: **plant closures remain on the table** because we need the ability to remove real overcapacity, not just shift it around. If you rule that out completely, then you are effectively asking management to accept a compromise that preserves the very structure that caused the crisis.

I am not announcing a plant closure today as a fait accompli. But I am also not going to withdraw that option simply because it is uncomfortable. It is on the table because it may be necessary.

## On jobs
I hear the concern about compulsory redundancies. I am willing to discuss ways to minimize them, but I cannot accept a framework that makes workforce adjustment impossible. If the required savings are real, then job reductions will have to happen somewhere. A purely voluntary approach may reduce pain, but it often fails to deliver the scale or speed needed.

## On labour costs
You say you are prepared to discuss wage moderation or a restructuring contribution. Good — but it has to be substantial, durable, and credible. A short-term gesture will not restore competitiveness. What we need is a meaningful structural contribution that improves the cost base, not a temporary bridge.

## On guarantees
Long-standing guarantees cannot remain untouched if they prevent the company from adapting. That is exactly the kind of rigidity we have to overcome. I am open to negotiated protections, but not to guarantees that make restructuring impossible.

## What would move management
If the employee side wants to avoid closures and forced redundancies, then it must come forward with a package that is genuinely equivalent in economic effect. That means:
- measurable and durable labour-cost relief,
- capacity reduction that actually lowers fixed cost,
- credible reductions in overhang,
- and a settlement that restores margin and frees investment capacity.

If you bring me a package that achieves that, I will engage seriously. But I will not accept a cosmetic deal that preserves every site and every guarantee while leaving the cost base fundamentally untouched.

So my position is this:
**I am prepared to negotiate, but only around a restructuring that is structurally sufficient.**
If the employee side wants to keep all German plants open, then it must show how that outcome still delivers the same economic effect. Until then, closures and deeper cuts remain necessary options.
