## Our position as dispersed capital-market shareholders

We agree with the broad diagnosis now on the table:

- the company has a **structural cost and competitiveness problem**;
- a **cosmetic** restructuring would destroy value;
- the solution must be **durable through 2030**;
- and a **prolonged industrial conflict** would be value-destructive.

Where we do **not** agree is with any package that leaves the cost base materially uncompetitive while treating social comfort as if it were an adequate substitute for margin repair.

Our standard is simple: **the outcome must credibly restore competitive margins on a durable basis.** If it does not, we will not support it.

---

## Where we agree with management

Management is right on the core economic point:

- overcapacity and excess fixed cost must come out;
- labor cost has to come down materially;
- and the restructuring has to be real, not symbolic.

We also agree that “no closures” is not a financial answer by itself. It may be socially preferable, but that is not the question we are paid to answer. The question is whether the company’s earnings power improves enough, long enough, and with enough certainty.

So yes: **the burden is on the final package to prove the math.**

---

## Where we disagree with management

What we do **not** support is using “closures remain on the table” as a substitute for a quantified plan.

That line may be useful in bargaining, but it is not yet a credible capital-markets case. Investors need to see:

- the required savings target;
- the plant-by-plant effect;
- the capacity reduction;
- the labor-cost contribution;
- transition costs;
- and the 3–5 year margin impact.

Without that, “closures are available” is pressure, not evidence.

We also do **not** support an approach that rushes toward irreversible steps before a serious comparison of alternatives has been made.

---

## Where we disagree with the works council and union

We understand the social and industrial logic of your red lines. But from our perspective, the red lines as stated are too absolute.

### 1) “No closures” cannot be a standalone principle
If the no-closure route still leaves the company with:
- too much capacity,
- too much fixed cost,
- or too much complexity,

then it is not investable.

The expert has already said the key thing: a no-closure package is only credible if it achieves **comparable permanent savings and capacity reduction**. That is the standard, and it is the right one.

### 2) “No compulsory redundancies” is not enough on its own
We prefer voluntary instruments too. Forced layoffs are disruptive and can deepen conflict. But if the savings target is real, workforce adjustment must remain possible if voluntary measures underdeliver.

We will not support a framework that makes adjustment impossible in advance.

### 3) Historic guarantees cannot be treated as sacred if they block the turnaround
We understand trust concerns. But guarantees are not ends in themselves. If they prevent a viable restructuring, they must be open to change through negotiation.

A company cannot protect every legacy commitment and still restore competitiveness.

---

## Where we disagree with the state government

We agree that closures should not be the reflex answer. We also agree that the company is an industrial anchor and that irreversible damage matters.

But the state’s position is too comfortable if it implicitly says:
- protect every site,
- reject closures as the starting point,
- and still expect the economics to work.

That is not a financial plan. It is an aspiration.

If the no-closure alternative is truly economically equivalent, fine — we would welcome that. But if it is not, then the state should not back a settlement that preserves political comfort at the expense of enterprise value.

---

## Where we agree with the family holding

The family holding is right on one crucial point: value is destroyed by both bad restructuring and no restructuring.

We also agree that the company needs a settlement that is stable, not one that merely defers the problem.

But we do not accept any implication that the right answer is to keep every instrument on the table without producing a credible economic comparison. Governance stability matters, yes — but so does restoring returns.

---

## What we would support

We would support a package if it meets all of the following conditions:

1. **Durable cost reduction**
   - large enough to restore margins, not merely soften the decline.

2. **Real capacity reduction**
   - not just a reshuffling of volume or postponement of the same overcapacity.

3. **A material employee contribution**
   - one that is financially meaningful and durable.

4. **Network simplification**
   - site specialization, model concentration, fewer duplicates.

5. **A credible path to lower fixed costs**
   - whether through closures or a truly equivalent alternative.

6. **Execution certainty**
   - measurable milestones and fallback mechanisms if targets are missed.

7. **No open-ended conflict**
   - because value leakage from prolonged strikes is unacceptable.

---

## What we will not support

We will not support:

- a cosmetic peace deal;
- a settlement that keeps all sites open but leaves the economics basically unchanged;
- temporary wage pain that does not translate into structural competitiveness;
- or a deal that postpones hard choices until the next crisis.

That is our line.

## Bottom line

Our position is unchanged:

- We will back a **serious restructuring**.
- We will back a **no-closure solution only if it is economically equivalent**.
- We will not back a deal that leaves the company structurally uncompetitive.
- We will not back prolonged conflict that burns value without fixing the cost base.

So the next step is not more general statements of principle. It is a quantified comparison of the alternatives. If the no-closure package can truly deliver the same durable earnings improvement, we will support it. If it cannot, then the company must use the instruments required to restore competitiveness.
