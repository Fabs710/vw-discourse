As the independent restructuring expert, I can address the core economic question:

## Short answer
A **no-closure restructuring can only be judged equivalent** to a closure-based scenario if it delivers, on a present-value and recurring basis:

1. **Comparable permanent fixed-cost reduction**
2. **Comparable capacity reduction**
3. **Comparable productivity improvement**
4. **Comparable margin uplift over 3–5 years**
5. **Lower or at least manageable transition risk**

If it does **not** achieve those outcomes, then it is **not economically equivalent** — regardless of its social acceptability.

---

## The basic economics

### Closure-based scenario
Plant closures are typically the most direct way to remove:
- fixed overhead,
- duplicate logistics,
- underutilized assets,
- maintenance burden,
- and persistent labour cost tied to excess capacity.

But closures also create high one-time costs:
- severance and social plan costs,
- dismantling and remediation,
- relocation or requalification costs,
- supply-chain disruption,
- and often lower short-term execution certainty because of industrial conflict.

### No-closure scenario
A no-closure package can work **only if** it compensates with some combination of:
- capacity reductions inside existing plants,
- shift eliminations and working-time reductions,
- specialization of sites,
- voluntary exits,
- redeployment,
- wage moderation or temporary labour-cost relief,
- management/overhead cuts,
- product allocation changes,
- and investment to raise productivity.

The problem is that many of these measures reduce cost **less permanently** than closure does. They often preserve more of the fixed-cost base than management would like.

---

## What “equivalent” would mean in practice
For a no-closure package to be economically credible, it should usually meet something like the following test:

### 1. Recurring savings
The package must generate enough **annual recurring savings** to close most of the structural gap, not just buy time for 1–2 years.

### 2. Capacity removal
It must remove enough production capacity to bring utilization up to a healthy level. In automotive manufacturing, that generally means getting away from chronically low utilization, because low utilization is what keeps unit costs high.

### 3. Fixed-cost reset
It must materially reduce site-level fixed costs. If all plants remain open but only marginally smaller, the cost base often remains too high.

### 4. Productivity gain
It must improve output per worker and per plant through specialization, line balancing, and better product allocation.

### 5. Execution certainty
It must be implementable without open-ended industrial conflict. A theoretically elegant no-closure package that takes years to execute may not be worth much.

---

## My judgment on feasibility
In German automotive restructuring, **no-closure solutions are possible**, but they are usually harder to make fully equivalent because:

- German plants are often constrained by legacy agreements and social commitments;
- fixed costs are sticky;
- productivity gains from labor concessions alone are usually insufficient;
- and preserving every site often preserves too much duplication.

So, in economic terms:

- **If the cost gap is modest**, a no-closure deal can work.
- **If the cost gap is large and structural**, no-closure packages often underdeliver unless they include very deep concessions and strong product/investment commitments.
- **If the network is genuinely overbuilt**, closures usually remain the cleanest way to remove capacity and fixed cost.

---

## The critical risk in no-closure scenarios
The main risk is **partial restructuring**:
- sites stay open,
- cost reductions are spread thin,
- utilization improves only slightly,
- and the company still carries too much structural burden.

That produces a politically easier deal, but not necessarily a viable industrial one.

The opposite risk in closure-based scenarios is:
- higher immediate cost,
- industrial conflict,
- loss of know-how,
- slower stabilization,
- and possible damage to future product ramp-up.

So the trade-off is not “good vs bad.” It is:
- **speed and certainty of structural cost reduction** vs.
- **social and execution risk**.

## What I would require in an independent audit
To compare the scenarios properly, I would want plant-by-plant data on:

- current and forecast utilization
- direct labour cost
- fixed overhead
- logistics and supply-chain cost
- depreciation and maintenance burden
- capacity overlap by product family
- headcount by function
- one-time restructuring cost
- investment required to make each site viable
- 3–5 year EBITDA or margin impact
- and sensitivity to demand, wage, and volume assumptions

Without that, “equivalence” is just a slogan.

---

## Bottom line
A no-closure restructuring is **economically credible only if it can demonstrably remove enough permanent cost and capacity** to match the effect of closures over the relevant planning horizon.

If it cannot, then the no-closure route is not equivalent — it is a compromise that preserves more jobs in the short term but leaves the company with a weaker cost structure.
