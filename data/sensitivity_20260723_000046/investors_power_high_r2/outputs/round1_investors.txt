From the perspective of dispersed institutional shareholders, the test is straightforward: **does the plan credibly restore competitive margins, free investment capacity, and reduce execution risk?** That is what we will back. Anything short of that is not a restructuring; it is delay.

## Where we agree
We agree with management on the core diagnosis:
- the German cost base is too high,
- structural overcapacity is a real problem,
- the company needs durable rather than symbolic savings,
- and near-term margin repair matters because the market will not fund a slow-motion decline.

We also agree with the state government on one important point: a purely closure-driven strategy is not automatically the best answer if there is a credible alternative that achieves the same economics with less disruption.

And we agree with the workforce representatives on one thing as well: **a deal is better than prolonged conflict**. A prolonged strike or open-ended confrontation destroys value quickly and makes an already difficult turnaround worse.

## Where we disagree
That said, I need to be explicit: **“No closures, no compulsory redundancies, no meaningful break with existing guarantees” is not, by itself, an investable solution**.

If those red lines mean the company cannot materially reduce fixed costs, then they are incompatible with the financial reality of the business. We would not support an outcome that:
- leaves the cost base structurally uncompetitive,
- preserves excess capacity,
- or produces only temporary savings that fade after the headlines.

In plain terms: **a cosmetic settlement is unacceptable**. If the company merely buys peace without fixing the economics, shareholders lose twice — once through continued margin erosion and again through loss of credibility with the market.

## On plant closures
We are not ideologically attached to closures. But we are also not prepared to rule them out in advance.

If management can demonstrate that comparable savings can be delivered through capacity reduction, working-time measures, redeployment, and employee contributions — and if those measures are binding, durable, and enforceable — then that is preferable to a destructive plant closure.

But if the alternatives are not mathematically sufficient, then closures must remain on the table. We will not support a settlement that preserves every site simply because those sites are politically difficult to touch.

## On compulsory redundancies
We prefer minimizing compulsory redundancies because they are disruptive, costly, and can prolong conflict. But again, our support is conditional:
- if voluntary measures and redeployment are enough, good;
- if they are not enough, then the company cannot be forced into an economically insufficient compromise.

We would rather see fewer forced layoffs, but not at the price of an inadequate restructuring.

## On labour-cost concessions
This is where we are most focused. Any employee contribution must be:
- **substantial enough to matter**,
- **durable enough to move the cost base**,
- and **credible enough that investors believe it will stick**.

Temporary gestures, one-off sacrifices, or vague promises are not sufficient. We need measurable savings that show up in margins and in the return-on-sales target. If labour wants to avoid deeper structural cuts, the economic equivalent must be real, not symbolic.

## On guarantees and co-determination
We understand the desire for security and governance involvement. But guarantees cannot be so rigid that they prevent adaptation to market conditions. A company in structural decline cannot be managed by preserving every historic constraint.

We support transparency, proper board process, and serious negotiation. We do **not** support governance arrangements being used to block necessary restructuring indefinitely.

## What would move us
We would support a settlement if it credibly includes:
1. **A durable, quantified cost reduction**
   - large enough to restore competitive margins.

2. **Capacity reduction that actually changes the economics**
   - whether through closures, consolidated production, or other binding reductions.

3. **A meaningful employee contribution**
   - wage moderation, working-time flexibility, bonus restraint, or similar measures,
   - with a clear financial effect.

4. **Limited disruption and deal certainty**
   - because prolonged industrial action would destroy value.

5. **A credible investment plan**
   - savings must translate into future competitiveness, not just short-term accounting relief.

## Our bottom line
We are not here to choose between management’s preferred closure package and labor’s preferred preservation package. We are here to evaluate whether the final outcome restores value.

So our position is:
- **we will back serious restructuring**, including closures if necessary;
- **we will also back a credible no-closure alternative**, but only if it delivers equivalent savings and margin improvement;
- **we will not back a deal that leaves the company structurally uncompetitive**;
- and **we will not tolerate a prolonged conflict that destroys value while producing no durable savings**.

Management should bring us a package that proves the economics. If the employee-side proposal can truly deliver the same cost outcome without closures, then it deserves support. If not, then sentiment cannot substitute for financial reality.
