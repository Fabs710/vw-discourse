Yes. Within my remit, the key question is straightforward:

**Can a no-closure package credibly remove enough permanent fixed cost and capacity to be economically equivalent to closures on a 3–5 year horizon?**

## Short answer
**Only if it is unusually deep, highly specific, and contractually enforceable.**  
In most large automotive networks, a no-closure path can reduce labor cost and some variable overhead, but it often struggles to eliminate enough **structural fixed cost** unless it includes:
- major shift eliminations,
- plant specialization,
- product concentration,
- substantial voluntary exits,
- durable wage/flexibility concessions,
- and binding fallbacks if targets are missed.

If those are not present, it is usually **not equivalent** to closure-based restructuring.

---

## What would need to be true for a no-closure package to work

A credible no-closure solution would need all of the following:

### 1) Real capacity removal
Not just “less output.” It must remove:
- one or more shifts,
- line capacity,
- duplicate product allocations,
- and associated indirect/support costs.

If plants remain open but still carry near-original footprint, the fixed-cost base stays too high.

### 2) Durable labor-cost reduction
A credible package needs a **multi-year** reduction in labor expense, not a one-off concession.

That typically means some combination of:
- wage moderation or temporary pay restraint,
- bonus/variable compensation restraint,
- longer working time per unit of output,
- lower paid absence burdens,
- and relaxation of legacy guarantees.

One-time payments or short-term freezes do not solve a structural competitiveness problem.

### 3) Headcount reduction large enough to matter
Voluntary exits can help, but the scale has to be material.

What matters is not just gross headcount reduction, but:
- direct labor removed,
- indirect/support labor removed,
- and whether the remaining workforce can support the simplified network.

If headcount falls but the same plant overhead remains, the economic gain is limited.

### 4) Network simplification
A no-closure package must simplify the network in practice:
- fewer product variants per site,
- fewer duplicate processes,
- more specialization,
- lower logistics complexity.

If every site keeps broadly similar roles, the network remains expensive even if employment is trimmed.

### 5) Binding governance and fallback triggers
This is critical.

A no-closure settlement only works if there is:
- measurable milestone tracking,
- an agreed audit mechanism,
- automatic escalation if savings underdeliver,
- and a predefined fallback if the package misses targets.

Without that, the company risks ending up in a “half-restructuring”: not cheap enough to satisfy management/investors, not secure enough to satisfy labor.

---

## What closure does that no-closure often cannot

A closure removes:
- plant-level fixed overhead,
- local support functions,
- building and utility costs,
- duplicated logistics and internal transport,
- and the structural drag of underutilized capacity.

That is why closure is usually the more direct instrument for permanent savings.

By contrast, no-closure measures tend to cut:
- labor spend,
- some indirect costs,
- and some complexity,

but often leave the **plant shell** and a large part of the **fixed footprint** intact.

That is the core economic problem.

---

## Plant-by-plant comparison: what must be measured

If you want a serious comparison, each plant needs the same template:

### For each site:
- annual output capacity
- current utilization
- fixed cost base
- labor cost per vehicle
- indirect/support cost allocation
- logistics cost
- product overlap with other sites
- required capex to remain competitive
- transition cost if closed or downsized
- 3–5 year margin contribution under each scenario

### Then compare:
#### Closure scenario
- which fixed costs disappear
- what volume moves elsewhere
- what one-time costs are incurred
- when savings become recurring

#### No-closure scenario
- what shifts disappear
- what labor cost falls
- what overhead is actually removed
- what productivity gains are assumed
- how much capacity truly comes out
- what remains structurally unchanged

If the no-closure scenario does not show a comparable reduction in **recurring fixed cost per vehicle**, it is not equivalent.

## Risk profile

### Closure-based path
Pros:
- faster fixed-cost removal
- clearer capacity reset
- more certainty in steady-state economics

Cons:
- high transition cost
- industrial conflict risk
- output disruption
- political and governance resistance

### No-closure path
Pros:
- lower immediate disruption
- better retention of know-how and morale
- potentially faster agreement if concessions are meaningful

Cons:
- savings can be slower and weaker
- savings may depend on ongoing compliance
- may preserve too much structural overhead
- higher risk of “temporary” measures being rolled back later

---

## My judgment on credibility

A no-closure package is credible **only if** it is:
- quantitatively close to the closure case,
- durable through 2030,
- and enforceable if performance misses.

If it relies mainly on:
- wage restraint,
- voluntary departures,
- and promises of future efficiency,

then it is usually **not enough** for a structurally overbuilt automotive network.

---

## What I would look for before calling it economically equivalent

At minimum, I would want evidence that the no-closure package achieves:

1. **Comparable annual recurring savings**
2. **Comparable capacity reduction**
3. **Comparable fixed-cost reduction**
4. **A clear headcount adjustment path**
5. **A lower or at least manageable execution risk**
6. **Binding fallback mechanisms**

Absent that, the package is a negotiated compromise, not an economically equivalent alternative.

---

## Bottom line
**No-closure can work, but only if it acts like a real network reset, not a soft landing.**  
In a large German automotive footprint with structural overcapacity, that usually means the package must be deep enough to remove fixed cost almost as decisively as a closure would.
