## Core assessment

At a high level, the discussion has converged on the right test:

- **A closure-based restructuring** is the more direct way to remove structural overcapacity, fixed cost, and complexity.
- **A no-closure restructuring** can be viable only if it achieves **economically comparable** permanent savings, capacity reduction, and simplification.
- The real issue is not the label, but whether the final network is **durably cost-competitive through 2030**.

That said, a no-closure route is usually harder to execute to full economic equivalence because it must replace the cost removal that closures deliver automatically with a bundle of other measures that are slower, more conditional, and often less complete.

## Equal economic terms: what must be compared

To compare closure and no-closure scenarios on equal terms, the comparison must include:

1. **Annual recurring savings**
   - labour cost
   - overhead
   - maintenance and utilities
   - logistics
   - indirect site support

2. **Capacity removed**
   - units per year
   - number of lines/shifts removed
   - surplus fixed assets eliminated or stranded

3. **Headcount impact**
   - direct jobs
   - indirect jobs
   - redeployment versus departure
   - compulsory versus voluntary adjustment

4. **Transition costs**
   - severance
   - transfer/disruption costs
   - one-time write-downs
   - relocation and start-up expenses

5. **Investment needs**
   - automation/productivity upgrades
   - tooling
   - model reallocation
   - IT/process standardisation

6. **Timing**
   - when savings start
   - when transition costs hit
   - how long until the new steady state is reached

7. **Risk**
   - execution risk
   - strike risk
   - delivery risk
   - underachievement risk if voluntary tools fall short

## Economic reality of the two paths

### 1) Closure-based scenario
A closure-based restructuring generally has these characteristics:

**Advantages**
- Removes fixed cost more decisively
- Simplifies the network faster
- Gives clearer capacity alignment
- Usually produces stronger permanent savings
- Reduces the chance of “half-measures” that preserve excess cost

**Disadvantages**
- High one-time cash cost
- High industrial-relations and political risk
- Risk of disruption to production continuity
- Loss of know-how and local flexibility
- May trigger longer conflict and weaker short-term output

In economic terms, closures are often **faster and more certain** for cost removal, but expensive up front.

### 2) No-closure scenario
A no-closure scenario can include:
- capacity reduction through shift cuts or line simplification
- working-time changes
- wage moderation or temporary pay concessions
- removal of legacy guarantees
- voluntary exits
- redeployment
- qualification and productivity measures
- product specialization by site

**Advantages**
- Lower immediate social destruction
- Lower political and legal friction
- Better preservation of skills and local morale
- Can preserve option value if the market recovers
- Often easier to maintain industrial peace if negotiated well

**Disadvantages**
- Savings are usually slower and less complete
- Fixed-cost reduction is harder without site exit
- Requires strong compliance and governance
- Higher risk of underdelivery if measures are voluntary
- Can preserve too much structural complexity

In economic terms, no-closure only works if it achieves a **hard reset of cost structure** without a plant exit. That is possible in some cases, but it requires unusually strong concessions and disciplined execution.

## My judgment on feasibility

### Can no-closure equal closures economically?
**Sometimes, but not by default.**

A no-closure package can match a closure-based package only if all of the following are true:

- the plant network can be simplified without site exit;
- enough capacity is removed through shifts, model concentration, and specialization;
- labour cost relief is durable, not temporary;
- voluntary exits are sufficiently large and timely;
- productivity gains are real and funded;
- management commits to not re-adding cost later;
- the company accepts a sufficiently deep change in operating model.

If those conditions are not met, the no-closure route is usually **inferior on a permanent-cost basis**.

## The key economic constraint
The biggest problem with no-closure solutions is that they often reduce **variable labour expense** more than **structural fixed cost**. But the current problem described here is fundamentally a **fixed-cost and capacity problem**. That means:

- wage concessions help, but they are rarely enough alone;
- voluntary exits help, but they do not remove a plant’s fixed footprint;
- working-time adjustments help, but only partially and often temporarily;
- productivity investments help, but take time and capital.

A closure, by contrast, directly removes plant-level fixed cost and network duplication.

## Transition timing

### Closure path
- **Year 0–1:** heavy one-time costs, negotiations, production transfer planning
- **Year 1–3:** savings begin to materialize as output shifts
- **Year 3–5:** clearer steady-state margin improvement if execution succeeds

### No-closure path
- **Year 0–1:** negotiations and concessions
- **Year 1–2:** implementation of shift/capacity changes and voluntary exits
- **Year 2–5:** savings depend on compliance, productivity, and whether concessions hold

The no-closure path usually reaches full benefit **later** and with more uncertainty.

## Risk comparison

### Closure-based risks
- strike escalation
- litigation and political intervention
- transfer failure
- brand and reputational damage
- talent loss
- underutilized new network if volume falls further

### No-closure risks
- savings underdelivery
- partial compliance
- lingering overcapacity
- “temporary” concessions becoming contested later
- management losing credibility if promised targets are missed
- hidden cost reaccumulation

From a pure restructuring perspective, the biggest no-closure risk is **not that it fails immediately**, but that it leaves the company stuck in an intermediate state that is still too expensive.

## What would make a no-closure scenario credible

If stakeholders want a serious no-closure alternative, it must include:

- site specialization by product or technology
- meaningful shift/line reduction
- a quantified headcount reduction path, mostly voluntary but sufficiently large
- durable labour-cost relief
- binding productivity investment
- closure-equivalent fixed cost reduction from overhead elimination
- explicit fallback triggers if targets are missed

Without those elements, the package is not economically equivalent; it is a delay mechanism.

## Bottom line

My professional conclusion is:

- **Closure-based restructuring** is generally the more reliable way to achieve structural cost reset.
- **No-closure restructuring** can be viable only if it delivers a truly comparable reduction in permanent cost, capacity, and complexity.
- If the comparison shows that the no-closure package leaves too much fixed cost in place, then it is not a real alternative, only a softer form of adjustment.
