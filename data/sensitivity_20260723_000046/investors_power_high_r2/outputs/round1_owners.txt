I agree with the diagnosis that this company cannot drift on with a structurally too high cost base, excess capacity, and weak margins. From an owner’s perspective, that would erode value, constrain investment, and ultimately threaten the industrial core we are trying to protect.

I also agree that a prolonged conflict would be destructive. A strike, a board stalemate, or a public escalation would harm the company, the workforce, and the brand. We need a durable settlement, not theatre.

That said, I do **not** accept the idea that plant closures in Germany should simply be treated as the default or first-choice instrument. If there is a credible alternative that achieves the same durable economic effect — real capacity reduction, meaningful cost relief, and restored competitiveness — then that is preferable from a long-term ownership perspective. Once a site is closed, the damage is often irreversible: skills are lost, supply chains are disrupted, and trust in governance is damaged.

So my position is this:

## What I support
- A restructuring that **restores margins and competitiveness**
- A **durable** solution, not a temporary bridge
- A **substantial employee contribution**, provided it is matched by real commitments elsewhere
- **Capacity reduction** and productivity improvements
- **Investment in future products and technology**
- A settlement that preserves **labour peace** and avoids open-ended escalation

## What I do not support
- A cosmetic agreement that preserves too much capacity while pretending the economics are fixed
- A one-sided burden on employees without corresponding commitment from management and owners
- A prolonged conflict that destroys value
- Any approach that permanently weakens the enterprise’s value or undermines governance stability

## On the works council and union position
Your insistence on no compulsory redundancies and serious negotiation is understandable, and I hear the concern about social stability. But I cannot support an absolute “no closures under any circumstances” position if it means the company remains structurally uncompetitive. If the alternatives do not close the economic gap, then they are not a solution.

At the same time, I am not asking for closures for their own sake. I am asking for a plan that actually works. If management can prove that a no-closure path delivers equivalent savings with binding measures, then that deserves serious consideration. If it cannot, then sentiment alone is not enough to block structural change.

## On management’s position
Management is right that the problem is not superficial. But if you want support from governance, labour, and the state, you need to show that the proposed restructuring is not just aggressive, but also economically necessary and proportionate. “Closures are on the table” is not yet a plan; it is a pressure tactic unless you can demonstrate the numbers and the durability of the savings.

## On the state government and institutional investors
I share the state’s caution about irreversible damage to the industrial base. I also share the investors’ insistence that the outcome must be financially credible. Those are not contradictory if the economics are genuinely solved. They are contradictory only if each side demands to preserve its preferred structure without delivering the required savings.

## Where I would land
The family holding would support a settlement only if it clearly does all of the following:
1. **Restores competitiveness in a durable way**
2. **Produces measurable, long-term cost relief**
3. **Protects the company’s future investment capacity**
4. **Avoids unnecessary instability**
5. **Preserves governance control and long-term enterprise value**

If the final package meets those conditions through alternative measures rather than closures, that is acceptable. If it does not, then I will not support a settlement that merely delays the problem.

So I am open to a negotiated outcome — but only a serious one. I am not prepared to endorse a deal that sacrifices competitiveness, or one that destroys value through conflict, or one that treats structural reality as negotiable.
