I will be direct.

## To management

I agree with you on the diagnosis:
- the company has a real structural cost problem;
- the turnaround must be durable through 2030;
- a cosmetic compromise would fail;
- and employee contributions are part of any serious settlement.

Where I disagree is on your insistence that closures and compulsory redundancies must remain the default instruments unless labour proves an alternative at your preferred level of pain. That is not a neutral economic claim; it is a bargaining strategy.

You still have not shown, in a convincing plant-by-plant way, that a no-closure package cannot achieve the needed savings. Until that comparison is on the table, I will not treat closures as the benchmark of seriousness.

## To the Works Council and Union

I agree with your core red lines:
- no compulsory redundancies;
- no closure-first logic;
- no dismantling of job-security protections;
- no erosion of co-determination.

That said, I also want to be honest: simply saying “every German plant stays open” is not enough by itself. If we are to defend that line, we need a package that is economically credible, not just morally right.

So I expect you to bring concrete measures that can actually reduce cost and capacity:
- working-time tools;
- shift reductions;
- redeployment;
- qualification;
- voluntary exits;
- site specialization;
- and a fair employee contribution.

That is the burden of a serious alternative. But I repeat: it does **not** have to include closures or forced layoffs to be serious.

## To the state government

I agree with your basic stance much more than I agree with management’s. You are right that this company must remain a regional economic anchor, and you are right not to accept closure-first logic as the starting point.

But caution alone is not enough. Use the state’s leverage to insist on a binding industrial settlement for the German sites:
- no closures as the starting framework;
- no compulsory redundancies;
- binding investment commitments;
- and a real path to competitiveness that protects the regional base.

What I will not accept is a “maybe later, if unavoidable” logic that silently prepares the ground for the first irreversible step.

## To the controlling family holding

I agree that a cosmetic deal would destroy value. I also agree that the company cannot carry too much cost, too much duplication, and too much complexity.

Where I disagree is your assumption that closures are simply the cleanest expression of economic realism. They may be one instrument, but they are not automatically the right instrument if they gut the regional anchor and social partnership that made this company strong.

If you want my support, then the question is not whether labour can be made to feel enough pain. The question is whether the company can be restructured while preserving the industrial base in Germany and the governance role of the state and co-determination stakeholders.

## To institutional investors

You are right to insist on economic sufficiency. I share that standard.

But I do **not** accept a definition of “equivalence” that only counts pain imposed on workers. Equivalence means economic effect: recurring savings, capacity reduction, and long-term competitiveness. If a no-closure package achieves that, then it is a serious alternative. If it does not, then it is not.

I am open to hard numbers. I am not open to using those numbers as a pretext to force closures before less destructive options are fully tested.

## My position, clearly stated

I will support a serious restructuring if it:
- protects jobs and plants in Germany as far as possible;
- avoids compulsory redundancies;
- preserves co-determination and the state’s veto role;
- delivers real and durable cost reduction;
- and keeps the company a strong regional economic anchor.

I will **not** support:
- a closure-first restructuring;
- mass layoffs as the default adjustment tool;
- a deal that hollows out the German industrial base;
- or any settlement that sidelines the state’s role.

If management, owners, and investors want my support, they need to prove that a no-closure package can meet the economic test. If they cannot, then they are asking us to trade away regional jobs and governance rights for an unproven promise of competitiveness. I will not do that.
