## To the Works Council

You keep repeating the same red lines:
- no plant closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- no change to legacy guarantees,
- no closure trigger.

I understand the political and industrial logic of that position. I do **not** accept it as a sufficient restructuring answer.

The problem is not that management “prefers” closures. The problem is that the company has a **structural cost and capacity gap**. If you rule out the tools that remove fixed cost at the plant-network level, then you are asking us to solve a structural problem with only marginal instruments.

### Where I agree
I agree that:
- a cosmetic deal is worthless;
- the solution must be durable through 2030;
- co-determination matters;
- employee contributions should be part of any package;
- the comparison must be plant-by-plant and quantified.

### Where I disagree
I disagree with your claim that a no-closure package does **not** need to be economically equivalent to closures. It absolutely does.

If the company keeps every site open, then the package must still deliver:
- meaningful capacity reduction,
- substantial fixed-cost reduction,
- durable labor-cost relief,
- simplified site roles,
- and a credible path to margin improvement.

Anything less is not a restructuring. It is postponement.

And I’ll be blunt: saying “the burden is on management to prove necessity” is not enough when the numbers are already clear enough to show the German cost base is uncompetitive. You are not being asked to surrender for no reason. You are being asked to engage with the scale of the problem.

## To the Union

You are more constructive than the works council in one respect: you at least acknowledge that the savings target matters. That is important.

But you are still too close to a defensive position that assumes the company can be stabilized without any hard instrument on capacity and workforce.

### Where I agree
I agree with:
- no open-ended conflict,
- a serious comparison of scenarios,
- employee contributions as part of the solution,
- and the need for transparency on targets and timelines.

### Where I disagree
I do **not** accept the idea that the company can be forced into a durable turnaround purely through:
- working-time tools,
- voluntary exits,
- redeployment,
- and wage moderation,

if the underlying network remains oversized.

Those tools can help. They do not automatically fix a structurally too-large footprint.

You also keep treating closure as if it were merely a bargaining threat. It is more than that. In a network with structural overcapacity, closure is one of the few instruments that can permanently remove fixed cost. That is not intimidation; that is industrial arithmetic.

## To the State Government

I appreciate that you are resisting a closure-first reflex. That caution is understandable.

But your position still does not answer the central question: **What package do you support that actually gets the cost base down enough?**

### Where I agree
I agree that:
- the company must remain a regional industrial anchor;
- irreversible damage should not be taken lightly;
- and a cosmetic compromise would destroy value.

### Where I disagree
I disagree with any implication that the state can insist on no closures and no forced redundancies while leaving the economic burden unchanged.

If you want to block closures, then you need to support a package that is demonstrably strong enough to replace what closures would have achieved. Not rhetorically — financially.

That means:
- lower fixed cost,
- lower capacity,
- lower labor cost,
- and a network simplification that is actually durable.

If you cannot support that level of adjustment, then you are not solving the problem; you are just shifting it into the future.

## To the Controlling Family Holding

You are right about one thing: a cosmetic compromise would destroy value.

But I think you still understate the risk of leaving the network structurally overextended. Value is destroyed not only by conflict, but by preserving too much cost, too much complexity, and too much duplicated capacity.

### Where I agree
I agree that:
- the final package must be economically sufficient;
- a temporary peace that avoids hard choices is not acceptable;
- and governance stability matters.

### Where I disagree
I do **not** accept the idea that every legacy structure is automatically entitled to protection unless management can prove otherwise site by site in the narrowest possible way.

The burden is on all sides to build a network that can earn its way through 2030. If a no-closure package can truly do that, fine. But if it cannot, then insisting on no closures is simply insisting on a weaker company.

## To Institutional Investors

You are correct to demand economic equivalence. That is the right standard.

But I want to be very clear: economic equivalence does **not** mean equivalent social pain. It means equivalent economic effect.

So the right question is not whether a no-closure package hurts workers enough to “feel serious.” The question is whether it removes enough:
- recurring cost,
- capacity,
- and complexity.

### Where I agree
I agree that:
- prolonged strikes destroy value;
- the final package must improve margins durably;
- and transition costs matter.

### Where I disagree
I disagree with any tendency to treat closure as automatically superior without a full comparison.

But I also disagree with labour’s implication that every non-closure measure can be counted as equivalent simply because it is politically easier. It cannot.

Wage moderation, voluntary exits, and specialization are useful only if they reach the required scale. If they do not, they are not economically equivalent.

## My position, plainly stated

I will not support:
- a deal that leaves the company structurally uncompetitive;
- a cosmetic no-closure compromise;
- a settlement that preserves the current cost base and calls it a turnaround;
- or a package that pushes the problem beyond 2030.

I **will** support a serious negotiated solution if it achieves:
- substantial and durable cost reduction,
- real capacity reduction,
- meaningful labor-cost contribution,
- network simplification,
- and a credible return to competitiveness and investment capacity.

That is the standard. Not slogans, not political comfort, not open-ended resistance.

## Bottom line

If the no-closure route can prove it matches the economic effect of a deeper restructuring, then I’m prepared to consider it.

If it cannot, then plant closures and deeper structural measures remain on the table because the company’s competitiveness comes first.

That is not escalation for its own sake. It is the reality of the situation.
