## Where I agree

## Where I disagree
I disagree with the repeated attempt to frame closures and compulsory redundancies as “available if needed” as if that were a neutral technical stance. It is not neutral. It is a one-way lever against the workforce.

The works council’s position is simple and unchanged:

- **Every German plant stays open.**
- **No compulsory redundancies.**
- **No dismantling of job-security guarantees.**
- **No fait accompli.**

If a restructuring proposal cannot work within those boundaries, then the burden is on management and the owners to explain why they failed to design a credible alternative — not on workers to surrender the only protections that still exist.

## To management
You say closures must remain on the table because the cost gap may be too large. Fine — then show a **plant-by-plant, scenario-by-scenario comparison** that proves no other path can work.

But be honest about what that means: you are asking us to accept irreversible damage before proving necessity. That is not acceptable.

And let me also say this: if your “credible” solution still depends on plant closure threats to discipline the workforce, then it is not a sustainable industrial strategy. It is bargaining by intimidation.

## To investors and the family holding
You keep saying you will support a no-closure path only if it is economically equivalent. I understand that standard. What I do **not** accept is the implicit assumption that equivalence can only be measured by how much pain is imposed on employees.

There are other ways to create equivalence:
- capacity rationalization through working-time and shift tools,
- internal redeployment,
- specialization,
- voluntary exits,
- qualification,
- investment in productivity,
- and management/owner contributions.

If you refuse to put meaningful commitments on your own side of the ledger, then of course every worker contribution will look “insufficient.” That is a rigged comparison.

## To the state government
Your position is closer to reality than management’s. You are right not to accept closure-first logic. But I need to be blunt: caution is not enough if it becomes a waiting strategy while management keeps the closure gun on the table.

Use your blocking power where it matters:
- no closures as the starting point,
- no forced layoffs,
- and a real industrial plan for the German sites.

Do not allow “maybe later, if needed” to become the back door through which the first irreversible step is taken.

## The expert comparison I want
If we are really being serious, then the expert/auditor must not just produce abstract theory. I want a **concrete network comparison** with the following minimum outputs:

   - overhead,
   - logistics burden,
   - product overlap.

3. **Scenario A: closure-based**

   - what transition cost,

   - what production transfer.

4. **Scenario B: no-closure**
   - what specific measures,
   - how many shifts removed,
   - how many jobs reduced voluntarily,
   - what wage/flexibility contribution,
   - what investment in productivity,
   - what durable savings.

5. **Fallback logic**
   - if the no-closure package underdelivers, who bears the cost?
   - because the answer cannot simply be “then workers take the hit later.”
