## My position remains unchanged
The family holding will support a restructuring that **restores durable competitiveness and margins**. We will **not** support:
- a cosmetic compromise,
- a deal that leaves structural overcapacity and excess fixed cost in place,
- or a prolonged conflict that destroys value.

We are not interested in symbolic peace. We are interested in a solution that is **economically sufficient and governable**.

## Where I agree

### With management
Management is right that:
- the cost base is too high;
- the company needs real, durable savings;
- capacity and complexity must come down;
- and temporary measures alone will not fix a structural problem.

That is correct. If the package does not materially improve the economics, then it is not a turnaround.

### With investors
Investors are right that the final package must be **economically equivalent**, not merely socially easier. I support that standard.

### With the state government
The state is right that closures should not be the reflex first answer. A site closure is irreversible in practice, and we should not treat irreversible damage as a bargaining convenience.

### With labour on one point
The works council and union are right that prolonged industrial conflict would damage value. That is not in dispute.

---

## Where I disagree

### With the works council and union
I do **not** accept the position that:
- no closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- no meaningful change to legacy guarantees,
- can be declared in advance as absolute red lines,
while still expecting the company to solve a serious structural cost problem.

That is not a restructuring framework. It is a refusal to let economics touch the existing structure.

I also disagree with the idea that closure threats are simply coercion. If the network is structurally oversized, then closure is not a political gesture; it is one of the possible mechanisms for restoring competitiveness. Nobody likes it. That does not make it illegitimate.

### With management
At the same time, I do **not** accept management treating closures as the default bargaining weapon without a quantified comparison. “Closures remain on the table” is not enough. It has to be backed by a serious scenario analysis.

### With investors
I agree with the investors’ economic standard, but not with any temptation to treat closure as automatically preferable. Closure is only justified if the alternative cannot remove enough permanent cost and capacity.

## What I think the expert has established
The expert’s analysis is useful and, importantly, balanced:

- A **closure-based** restructuring is usually the faster and more direct way to remove structural overcapacity.
- A **no-closure** restructuring can work only if it delivers **comparable permanent savings, capacity reduction, and network simplification**.
- If it cannot, then it is not economically equivalent.

That is the correct test.

So the real issue is not ideology. It is whether the no-closure path is deep enough to be credible on a 3–5 year horizon.

## My view on the current bargaining positions

### Works Council / Union
You are right to insist that workers should not carry the burden alone. But you are not yet answering the core economic question sufficiently:

- How exactly does a no-closure package remove enough fixed cost?
- How much capacity is actually taken out?
- How much labor-cost relief is durable?
- What happens if the voluntary instruments underdeliver?

Simply saying “keep all plants open” does not solve that. It may be a legitimate political demand, but it is not yet a restructuring answer.

### Management
You are right that the numbers matter. But you still owe the table a full comparison. If you want governance support for keeping closures available, bring the plant-by-plant case and the time horizon. Do not substitute urgency for proof.

### State government
You are right to resist closure-first logic. But the state should also not pre-commit to preserving every site regardless of economics. A serious comparative audit is the right next step.

### Investors
You are right that value destruction comes from both bad restructuring and no restructuring. But you should be careful not to define “economically equivalent” so narrowly that only the most painful option counts. Equivalent means equivalent in economic effect, not equivalent in optics.

## What would move me
I am prepared to support a no-closure path **if and only if** it proves, with hard numbers, that it achieves:

1. **Durable cost reduction**
   - not temporary relief, but a lasting improvement in margins.

2. **Real capacity reduction**
   - not just shifting volume around or compressing the workforce without fixing the network.

3. **Meaningful labor-cost contribution**
   - one that actually moves the economics.

4. **Network simplification**
   - site specialization, lower duplication, and fewer structural inefficiencies.

5. **Execution certainty**
   - milestones, monitoring, and fallback mechanisms if savings are missed.

6. **Labour peace**
   - because conflict that burns value is not acceptable.

If the no-closure package cannot meet those conditions, then it is not serious enough.
