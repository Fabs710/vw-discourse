I will respond directly.

## To the Works Council
I agree with your core aim: **no compulsory redundancies, no one-sided burden, no dismantling of collective protections**. That is a legitimate and necessary line.

But I need to be equally clear: if we want to defend jobs and the collective agreement in a durable way, we must not reduce this to a purely defensive “no closures” slogan. The company is under structural pressure. If we do not offer a credible, measurable alternative, management will keep using the closure threat as leverage.

So I support your red lines, but I will not support a posture that refuses to engage with the required savings scale. We need a package that is strong enough to hold the line in practice — not just in principle.

## To Management
Your position is still too close to an ultimatum:
- accept deep cuts and keep closures in play, or
- the restructuring is supposedly not serious.

I reject that framing.

Yes, the company has a real competitiveness problem. Yes, the cost base must come down. But you still have not shown, in a transparent and plant-by-plant way, why a negotiated no-closure package cannot achieve the necessary effect.

What I still want from management is not rhetoric, but numbers:
- What exact annual savings are required?
- What capacity reduction is needed?
- What labor-cost reduction is being demanded?
- What share is management/ownership prepared to bear?
- What is the 3–5 year comparison between closure and non-closure scenarios?

Until that is on the table, “closures remain on the table” is pressure, not strategy.

## To the State Government
Your position is broadly aligned with ours on the key point: **closures should not be the starting answer**. I welcome that.

But the state also needs to be careful not to drift into a role where it merely asks for restraint while management keeps the closure option as a disciplinary threat. If the state is serious about defending industrial capacity, it should insist on a binding package that:
- protects sites,
- avoids compulsory redundancies,
- commits to investment,
- and delivers real capacity and cost adjustment through other means.

## To the Controlling Family Holding
You are right about one thing: a cosmetic compromise would destroy value. We agree on that.

Where we disagree is on the implication that workers must prove their willingness to accept closures or else be blamed for failure. That is not a fair comparison. The workforce has already signaled willingness to contribute. The unresolved question is whether management and owners are prepared to contribute visibly as well.

If ownership wants credibility, it must back a settlement that is not based on breaking the workforce’s security model. That means:
- investment commitment,
- product allocation,
- and support for a negotiated restructuring that protects sites and jobs.

## To Investors
You keep saying the package must be economically equivalent. Fine. That is the right test.

But you cannot define “equivalent” in a way that only counts pain inflicted on employees. A serious no-closure solution can create equivalence through:
- capacity reduction,
- specialization,
- voluntary exits,
- working-time changes,
- wage moderation,
- bonus restraint,
- and productivity improvements.

The question is not whether the workers can be made to sacrifice. The question is whether the company can be stabilized **without** irreversibly damaging its industrial base.

## On the expert/auditor
The expert has already made the key point: a no-closure solution is only credible if it achieves **comparable permanent savings and capacity reduction**. That is exactly why we need a proper plant-by-plant comparison.

So I support the call for a detailed audit, but with a clear purpose:
- not to create a pretext for closures,
- but to test whether a serious negotiated alternative can work.

## My bargaining position
The union remains prepared to support a serious restructuring if it includes all of the following:

- **No compulsory redundancies**
- **No plant closures as the default or starting solution**
- **A fair employee contribution**
- **Management and owner contributions**
- **Binding investment and future product commitments**
- **Transparent economic targets and monitoring**
- **Preservation of the collective bargaining framework**

What we will not accept:
- a restructuring that pushes all pain onto workers;
- forced layoffs;
- a closure-first logic dressed up as realism;
- or a temporary deal that becomes a back door to dismantling jobs and standards later.

## Bottom line
I agree that the company needs a durable turnaround. I disagree that this justifies making closures and forced redundancies the central benchmark of seriousness.

If management, owners, and investors want a real settlement, they need to stop treating the workforce as the adjustment variable of last resort and start treating shared sacrifice as the only credible basis for a durable deal.

If they refuse that, then the union will use every lever available — collective bargaining, industrial action, and co-determination — to defend jobs, sites, and the collective wage framework.
