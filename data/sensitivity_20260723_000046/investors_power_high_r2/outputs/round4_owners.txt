I will be direct.

## To management
I agree with your diagnosis that the company has a structural cost and capacity problem, and that a cosmetic deal would destroy value. I also agree that the package must be durable through 2030.

Where I **do not** agree is with treating closures and forced redundancies as the default proof of seriousness. That is too blunt for a company with co-determination, political constraints, and a controlling owner that must think in decades, not quarters. A solution that triggers prolonged conflict or damages the industrial base would also destroy value.

What I need from management is not more insistence that the problem is serious — that is already clear — but a **quantified comparison**:
- required annual recurring savings,
- required capacity reduction,
- plant-by-plant fixed costs,
- transition costs,
- and 3–5 year margin impact.

Without that, “closures remain on the table” is bargaining language, not a credible restructuring case.

## To the works council
I agree with you on several points:
- no cosmetic compromise,
- no prolonged conflict,
- employee contributions are part of any settlement,
- and the comparison must be plant-by-plant and quantified.

But I do **not** agree with your attempt to make “every German plant stays open” an absolute principle in advance of the numbers. That may be your red line, but it is not yet an economically sufficient answer. If a no-closure package truly can remove enough permanent cost and capacity, I would support it. If it cannot, then insisting on that red line simply postpones the problem.

I also do not accept the idea that closures are merely intimidation. If the network is structurally oversized, closure is a legitimate instrument of industrial restructuring. Unpleasant, yes. But not illegitimate.

## To the union
You are closer to the practical center of gravity than the works council, because you at least acknowledge the savings target and the need for a serious alternative.

I agree with you that:
- worker contributions are legitimate only if shared fairly,
- management and owners must also contribute visibly,
- and a temporary peace deal that kicks the problem into the next cycle is unacceptable.

Where I disagree is with any solution that assumes voluntary tools, working-time measures, and wage moderation will automatically be enough. They may help, but they may not remove enough structural fixed cost. If they do not, then we are just buying time.

## To the state government
I agree with your caution and with your resistance to closure-first logic. A site closure is irreversible in practice, and the state should not treat irreversible damage as a bargaining convenience.

But I do **not** agree with any position that says closures should simply be excluded while leaving the economic question unresolved. If the no-closure route is truly equivalent, fine — prove it. If it is not, then the state must not support a settlement that preserves political comfort while weakening the company’s long-term competitiveness.

## To investors
I agree with your insistence on economic equivalence. That is exactly the right standard.

Where I disagree is if “equivalence” becomes code for “whatever hurts workers enough to feel serious.” That would be a poor standard and bad governance. Equivalence means comparable permanent economic effect: cost reduction, capacity reduction, and simplification.

You are right to demand:
- recurring savings,
- capacity removal,
- lower fixed cost,
- and execution certainty.

You are also right that prolonged conflict destroys value. That is a real constraint, not a side issue.

## My position, plainly stated
The family holding will support a restructuring only if it:
- restores durable competitiveness,
- materially improves margins,
- reduces structural overcapacity,
- and avoids value-destructive instability.

That means:

- I will support a **serious no-closure package** if it proves economically equivalent.
- I will **not** support a cosmetic compromise.
- I will **not** support a prolonged conflict that burns value.
- I will **not** pre-approve closures as ideology, but I will not rule them out if the numbers show they are necessary.

## Bottom line
The discussion must now stop circling around slogans.

If the no-closure route can demonstrably deliver the required permanent cost and capacity reset, then I am prepared to support it.

If it cannot, then the company must retain the ability to use deeper structural measures.

That is the standard I will apply, and it is the only one that protects the long-term value of the family holding’s core asset.
