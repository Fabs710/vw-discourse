## Expert assessment: closure-based vs. no-closure restructuring on equal economic terms

The correct way to compare these options is **not** by asking whether closures are socially preferable. The economic question is narrower:

> Can a no-closure package deliver the same durable reduction in fixed cost, capacity, and complexity as a closure-based restructuring, after accounting for transition costs, productivity effects, investment needs, and execution risk?

My assessment is:

### 1) Closure-based restructuring is usually the fastest route to permanent cost reduction
If a manufacturer has **structural overcapacity**, multiple underutilized plants, and a cost base materially above peers, then plant closure or major site consolidation is typically the **most direct** way to remove fixed cost. It reduces:
- overhead,
- maintenance and utilities,
- support functions,
- internal logistics,
- line-change complexity,
- and often a meaningful amount of labor cost as well.

It also tends to improve:
- capacity utilization at the remaining plants,
- production concentration,
- planning simplicity,
- and productivity measured per plant or per employee.

But closures come with major economic burdens:
- severance and social plan costs,
- write-downs on buildings and equipment,
- decommissioning and remediation,
- relocation of tooling and product launch costs,
- disruption to supply chains,
- legal and industrial-relations delays,
- and possible loss of tacit know-how.

So closure is not “free efficiency.” It is often a **high upfront cost, lower recurring cost** strategy.

### 2) A no-closure restructuring can work only if it removes enough capacity and enough labor cost elsewhere
A no-closure path is economically credible only if it still achieves the same core objectives:
- enough **capacity reduction**,
- enough **labor-cost reduction**,
- enough **productivity improvement**,
- and enough **investment discipline**.

In practice, a no-closure package usually relies on some combination of:
- working-time reduction or more flexible schedules,
- voluntary exits,
- natural attrition,
- redeployment across the group,
- outsourcing of non-core activities,
- temporary concessions on pay or bonuses,
- site specialization,
- and deferred investment tied to volume commitments.

This can be effective, but only if the measures are:
- **large enough in scale**,
- **durable enough in duration**,
- and **credible enough in enforcement**.

The main risk is that a no-closure approach often produces **partial savings now and full costs later**. If it preserves too much excess capacity, then the company gets:
- lower payroll cost, but
- the same fixed plant burden,
- the same complexity,
- and continued weak utilization.

That is not equivalent to a closure-based solution.

### 3) On equal economic terms, “no closure” is usually more expensive unless labor accepts very substantial concessions
If one compares both options honestly over a 3- to 5-year horizon, the no-closure variant often has one of two outcomes:

#### Case A: It is too small
Savings are insufficient because the company keeps too much physical capacity and too many structural costs. This is common when the no-closure package depends mainly on:
- wage restraint,
- temporary freezes,
- or voluntary exits only.

That rarely fixes a serious overcapacity problem.

#### Case B: It becomes closure by another name
To match the savings from closures, the no-closure route has to become very deep:
- large headcount reductions,
- major line or shift eliminations,
- sustained labor concessions,
- and hard constraints on future cost escalation.

At that point, the package may avoid the legal label of “closure,” but economically it is often doing much of the same work through other means.

So the real question is not “closure or no closure?” but:
> Can the alternative package remove enough permanent cost from the system?

### 4) The biggest economic difference is transition timing
A closure-based plan is often better for **speed**. It creates a clear decision, forces the network to reconfigure, and can deliver faster improvement in utilization and earnings.

A no-closure plan is often better for **social continuity**, but it is usually slower because it requires:
- negotiations over every instrument,
- sequencing of voluntary measures,
- ongoing governance oversight,
- and continuous adherence to the agreed contribution path.

That slower pace matters in a competitive market. If the company is under pressure from Chinese entrants, EV transition costs, and European demand weakness, delay itself has economic cost.

### 5) The investment requirement may actually be higher under a no-closure solution
This is often overlooked.

If all plants stay open, the company may still need to fund:
- site specialization,
- product allocation changes,
- new tooling,
- digital manufacturing systems,
- energy-efficiency upgrades,
- and qualification/retraining.

That means a no-closure solution can require **more capex upfront**, even if it avoids severance and closure costs. The company must then carry a larger ongoing fixed base unless labor savings are substantial.

So from a purely economic standpoint, “keeping sites open” is not automatically cheaper. It can be cheaper in one-off social costs, but more expensive in recurring industrial cost if underutilization remains.

### 6) Risk profile differs sharply
#### Closure-based scenario: principal risks
- industrial conflict,
- legal challenge,
- political intervention,
- loss of skills,
- implementation delays,
- and reputational damage.

#### No-closure scenario: principal risks
- insufficient savings,
- ambiguous commitments,
- gradual erosion of discipline,
- failure of voluntary exits to reach target scale,
- and a later, larger crisis when the savings prove inadequate.

In restructuring terms, the no-closure plan has a common failure mode: it **defers the hard decision** and buys time without restoring competitiveness.

### 7) What would make a no-closure package economically credible?
A credible no-closure alternative would need, at minimum:
- quantified capacity reduction equivalent to what closures would have achieved,
- a verified headcount reduction path,
- durable labor-cost relief,
- plant specialization and product concentration,
- explicit investment tied to productivity gains,
- and enforcement mechanisms if savings targets are missed.

Without these elements, the alternative is not economically equivalent.

### 8) My bottom-line judgment
On equal economic terms:

- **Closure-based restructuring** is usually the more direct and reliable way to eliminate structural overcapacity and restore cost competitiveness.
- **No-closure restructuring** can be viable, but only if it delivers **comparable permanent savings** through other instruments; otherwise it becomes a compromise that preserves too much cost base.
- If labor and governance constraints make closures impossible, the company must be prepared for a **deeper alternative package** than is often politically comfortable.

## Practical conclusion
If management’s numbers show a large, durable gap between current German cost structure and peer benchmarks, then a no-closure solution must be tested against three hard questions:

1. **Does it reduce fixed capacity enough?**
2. **Does it reduce labor and overhead cost enough for long enough?**
3. **Does it do so without simply postponing the same restructuring problem?**

If the answer to any of those is no, then the no-closure path is not economically equivalent.
