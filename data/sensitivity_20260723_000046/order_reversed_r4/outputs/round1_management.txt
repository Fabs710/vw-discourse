I appreciate that the capital-markets side and the controlling family are focusing on the central issue: **this company needs durable competitiveness, not symbolism**. On that point, there is no serious disagreement. If we do not bring the German cost base down structurally, we will not hit the return-on-sales target, and we will not have the free cash flow to invest in the next product cycle and technology through 2030.

Where I do disagree is with any implication that we can solve this without hard structural choices.

To the regional state government: I hear the concern about industrial capacity, skilled jobs, and the regional ecosystem. Those are legitimate interests. But they do not change the commercial reality. **Keeping every site open regardless of economics is not a strategy**. If German plants remain well above target cost and underutilized in a shrinking European market, then the burden simply shifts onto the rest of the company and onto future investment. That is not sustainable, and it would ultimately damage the very industrial base you want to protect.

Let me be blunt:  
- **A cosmetic compromise is not acceptable.**  
- **A deal that preserves excess capacity is not acceptable.**  
- **A package built mainly around voluntary measures, soft promises, and delayed pain is not acceptable.**

I am open on the instruments, but not on the objective. The objective must be a **meaningful, permanent reduction in capacity and labour cost**. Plant closures are one option. Capacity consolidation, working-time changes, site realignment, and a material workforce contribution are others — but only if they produce savings that are economically comparable, enforceable, and timely. If they do not, then they are not alternatives; they are delays.

On labour peace: prolonged strikes would damage output, customer confidence, and investor trust. That is a serious concern. But the answer is not to retreat from restructuring. The answer is to reach a settlement that is **hard enough to matter**. If the workforce side wants to avoid closures, then it must bring something material to the table:
- lower labour cost,
- greater flexibility,
- reduced capacity,
- and a credible path to sustainable competitiveness.

What I will not do is trade away the company’s future for short-term peace.

So my position is:

1. **We need deep structural change now.**  
2. **The package must materially reduce overcapacity and labour cost.**  
3. **Any alternative to closures must be economically equivalent.**  
4. **Future investment capacity must be protected through real savings, not accounting tricks.**  
5. **If no credible deal emerges, management will continue to pursue the necessary restructuring.**

I am prepared to negotiate on how the savings are delivered. I am not prepared to negotiate away the savings themselves.
