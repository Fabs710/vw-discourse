I will be very clear.

### Where I agree
I agree with everyone on one point: **the company needs a real restructuring, not a cosmetic one**. If the cost base is structurally wrong, then we cannot pretend it is not a problem. I also agree that a prolonged conflict would hurt the company, the workforce, and the regions.

### Where I sharply disagree
I do **not** accept the premise that this can only be solved through plant closures or forced redundancies.

That is exactly the line management keeps trying to normalize, and it is exactly what we reject.

You keep saying “economic equivalence” as if that were a neutral technical standard. It is not neutral if it is used to predetermine the outcome. If the benchmark is defined so narrowly that only closures count as “real,” then the discussion is not a negotiation — it is a pressure tactic.

### To management
You say the operating footprint must change. Fine. But the burden of proof is on you to show why that requires **closing German plants** and destroying job security.

You have not answered the central question:
- Why are **capacity adjustments, shift changes, working-time measures, product allocation, redeployment, and voluntary exits** not enough when combined in a serious package?
- Why is your default still a measure that is **most destructive and least reversible**?

We do not accept “because it is the clearest tool” as a justification for closures. The clearest tool is not automatically the right tool.

And I want to be equally clear:  
**no compulsory redundancies, no plant closures, no dismantling of the historic job-security framework.**  
That is our line. Anything that crosses it is not a settlement we can accept.

### To the institutional investors
You keep demanding durable savings and timing. I understand that. But you are speaking as if value only exists in the spreadsheet. It does not.

A package that preserves skills, avoids social rupture, and keeps industrial capacity intact is not “cosmetic” just because it does not satisfy the harshest possible cost-cutting model. If you push for the cheapest short-term solution, you may get a worse long-term outcome: destroyed trust, industrial conflict, and a company that cannot rely on its workforce.

Execution certainty does not come from threatening closures. It comes from a settlement people can live with.

### To the controlling family
You say you prefer alternatives if they are truly equivalent. Then stop treating plant closures as the reference point and prove that you are genuinely open to a negotiated alternative.

What you still have not accepted is that the workforce is not a line item to be erased. If you want stability, then stability must include:
- binding site guarantees,
- protection from forced layoffs,
- serious investment commitments,
- and respect for co-determination.

Without that, you are asking employees to finance your “stability” by surrendering their future.

### To the regional state government
I appreciate that you reject closures as a first resort. But I need to be frank: if the state is serious about preserving the industrial base, then it must use its position accordingly.

That means:
- no support for any plan that closes German plants,
- no support for forced redundancies,
- no support for hollowing out sites under another name,
- and real backing for a negotiated alternative built on working-time, redeployment, retraining, and capacity planning.

We will judge the state by what it blocks, not just what it says.

### To the union
We are aligned on the core issue: **no compulsory redundancies, no closures, no one-sided burden shifting**. But I want to stress that plant-level co-determination is not a decorative layer. We must have a real role in shaping any restructuring, not just being informed after the fact.

### What we are prepared to discuss
We are prepared to discuss:
- working-time measures,
- voluntary exits,
- redeployment,
- retraining,
- productivity improvements,
- bonus restraint,
- temporary sacrifices,
- and capacity reduction **without closures**.

But all of that is contingent on one thing:
**binding guarantees that the German plants stay open and the workforce is not broken up by force.**

### What is not negotiable
- **No plant closures**
- **No compulsory redundancies**
- **No unilateral breaking of job guarantees**
- **No fait accompli**
- **No pretending that social destruction is the only path to competitiveness**

If management wants cooperation, it must come back with a package that protects sites, protects jobs, and respects co-determination. If it does not, then it should not be surprised that the workforce resists with everything it has.
