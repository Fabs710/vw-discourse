We are ready to continue the numbers-based discussion. But let me be equally clear: **“economic equivalence” cannot become a code word for plant closures and forced redundancies being treated as the only serious option.** That is the point where we fundamentally disagree.

## Where we agree
- The company needs a **real, durable restructuring**.
- Cosmetic measures are useless.
- The cost base must come down.
- Capacity has to be adjusted to demand.
- A deal must be **enforceable** and not just a press release.
- Prolonged conflict would damage the company and the workforce.

## Where we disagree
Management, investors, and the family keep presenting the issue as if the choice is:
1. closures and layoffs, or  
2. an unserious compromise.

That is false.

The union has already put real alternatives on the table:
- working-time adjustments,
- productivity measures,
- capacity reduction **without closures**,
- natural attrition,
- voluntary exits,
- bonus restraint,
- temporary pay moderation,
- and a material employee contribution.

That is not avoidance. That is a **shared-sacrifice restructuring**.

What we reject is a model where:
- workers carry the decisive burden,
- jobs and sites are made the first adjustment variable,
- and management, owners, and capital present themselves as merely observing the process.

## To management
You say closures are the cleanest and most enforceable tool. That may be technically true in isolation. But the real question is whether they are **necessary** here.

You have **not yet shown** that the only way to reach the required savings is to close plants or break the collective wage framework. You keep saying the burden of proof is on us. No — if you want to cross our red lines, the burden of proof is on **you**.

So bring the numbers:
- what annual run-rate savings are required,
- how much comes from labor cost,
- how much from capacity adjustment,
- how much from management and owner contribution,
- and what exactly would be permanent without closures.

If the answer is just “more pain for workers,” then we are not negotiating a restructuring. We are negotiating surrender, and that will not happen.

## To the institutional investors
You keep insisting that anything short of closure-level economics is cosmetic. That is too narrow.

A company does not need to destroy plant structures to become competitive if it can achieve:
- real capacity reduction,
- durable labour-cost relief,
- operational simplification,
- and binding commitments from management and owners.

If you want to claim a non-closure package is inferior, then quantify **why**:
- what savings shortfall remains,
- what timing gap remains,
- what enforceability gap remains.

Otherwise your position amounts to a preference for the harshest instrument, not a demonstrated necessity.

## To the controlling family
You say you are not pro-closure as a principle, only pro-economic reality. Fine. Then stop treating closures as the benchmark against which every labour proposal is judged.

If you truly want stability and long-term stewardship, then support a settlement that:
- keeps sites open,
- avoids forced redundancies,
- includes real employee contributions,
- and binds management and owners to investment and restraint.

A “durable” solution that destroys trust in the workforce is not durable. It is brittle.

## To the regional state government
You are right that regional industrial capacity matters. But if that is true, then you must be prepared to use your position to defend a **real alternative to closures**, not just say you prefer one.

That means backing:
- working-time measures,
- redeployment,
- retraining,
- product and line allocation,
- voluntary exits,
- and capacity planning that preserves the industrial base.

But let me be equally direct: if the state sides with a model that treats closures as the default or inevitable outcome, then it is not protecting the region’s industrial future — it is managing decline.

## On the expert’s remarks
The expert said something important: a non-closure package can work if it is **deep, permanent, and tightly enforced**. We agree.

But the expert also said closures are only clearly superior if the savings need is very large. That is exactly why we need the actual numbers before anyone declares closures necessary.

So here is the key issue:
- If management can show that the gap can only be closed by shutting plants, then we will contest that claim hard.
- If the package is designed properly, with real capacity reduction and permanent labor contributions, then closures are not automatically justified.

## What we are prepared to discuss
We are prepared to discuss a package that includes:
- **material employee contributions**
- **working-time flexibility**
- **temporary wage moderation**
- **bonus restraint**
- **voluntary exits**
- **redeployment and retraining**
- **productivity measures**
- **capacity reduction without closures**
- **binding investment commitments**
- **site security**
- **no compulsory redundancies**

That is a serious offer. It is not a free lunch.

## What is not negotiable
- **No compulsory redundancies**
- **No plant closures**
- **No dismantling of the collective wage framework**
- **No one-sided burden shifting onto workers**

If management says those boundaries make restructuring impossible, then say that openly — but do not pretend that the only “real” restructuring is one that makes employees carry all the pain.

## My position now
We are ready for hard bargaining. We are not ready to sign off on closures or forced layoffs disguised as restructuring.

If you want a durable settlement, then it must be:
- economically credible,
- socially balanced,
- and built on shared sacrifice.

If that is refused, then the conflict remains real, and the union will use its full leverage.
