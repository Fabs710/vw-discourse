We are broadly aligned with the capital-markets view on one essential point: **the company needs a credible, durable improvement in competitiveness**. If the German cost base stays structurally too high, the business will keep bleeding margin and strategic flexibility. That is not acceptable for long-term owners.

At the same time, I want to be explicit about the boundary: **we will not support value-destructive instability**. A prolonged conflict, uncontrolled strikes, or a breakdown in governance that damages the brand and customer confidence would destroy the very value the restructuring is supposed to protect.

So our position is:

- **Yes** to a restructuring that restores margins and capacity discipline.
- **Yes** to material labour contributions if they are necessary and durable.
- **No** to cosmetic measures that simply postpone the problem.
- **No** to a drawn-out confrontation that burns value without delivering a workable settlement.
- **No** to any outcome that undermines ownership control or leaves the enterprise permanently impaired.

On the plant question: we are not attached to preserving every site in its current form. What matters is whether the final package is **economically equivalent** to the required restructuring. If closures are truly necessary, that must be judged on the numbers and the long-term strategic logic, not sentiment. But if a negotiated package can achieve comparable savings, capacity reduction, and labour-cost adjustment with greater stability, that is preferable.

What we would need to see before endorsing any alternative to closures:

1. **Structural savings**, not one-off fixes.  
2. **Clear timing and enforceability**.  
3. **A credible path back to target margins**.  
4. **Protection of future investment capacity**.  
5. **Labour peace and governance stability**.

If those conditions are not met, we will not back a weak compromise. The company needs real correction, not delay.
