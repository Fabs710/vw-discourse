I will be equally direct.

## Where I agree
I agree with everyone on one point: **the company needs a real restructuring**. Cosmetic measures, procrastination, and endless consultations are not enough.

I also agree that:
- the cost base is too high,
- capacity must be adjusted to demand,
- the package must be enforceable,
- and prolonged conflict would damage the company and the workforce.

So yes, the diagnosis is clear.

## Where I disagree
I do **not** accept the way management, investors, and the family keep framing this as if **closures and forced redundancies are the natural benchmark of seriousness**.

That is exactly the line I will not let become normalized.

### To management
You say closures are the cleanest instrument. Technically, perhaps. But “cleanest” is not the same as **necessary**. And it is certainly not the same as **acceptable** when it means breaking job security, destroying sites, and using workers as the main adjustment variable.

You have still **not proven** that the required savings can only be achieved through closures or compulsory layoffs. You keep demanding that labour prove equivalence first. No — if you want to cross our red lines, the burden is on **you** to prove necessity.

### To the investors
You keep insisting on “closure-level economics.” That sounds neutral, but in practice it becomes a moving standard designed to make any non-closure solution fail by definition.

A serious restructuring is not automatically identical to shutting plants. A package with:
- working-time changes,
- productivity gains,
- voluntary exits,
- natural attrition,
- shift consolidation,
- product reallocation,
- bonus restraint,
- and binding investment commitments

can be real restructuring if it is **deep, durable, and enforceable**.

What I reject is your attempt to treat the harshest instrument as the only credible one.

### To the controlling family
You say you are not pro-closure in principle, only pro-economic reality. Fine. Then act accordingly.

Do not hide behind “reality” while adopting closures as the benchmark every alternative must beat. That is not neutral ownership; that is pressure politics with a financial vocabulary.

A durable solution must preserve trust, governance stability, and the industrial base. A deal that tears up job guarantees and co-determination may look strong on paper, but it is brittle in the real world.

### To the regional state government
You are right that the region’s industrial base matters. But that means the state must do more than express concern. It must clearly oppose:
- plant closures,
- compulsory redundancies,
- and the dismantling of long-standing job protections.

If the state only says it “prefers” alternatives, that is too weak. We need active defense of the industrial base, not polite commentary while jobs disappear.

## What we are prepared to discuss
We are prepared to discuss a package that includes:
- material employee contributions,
- temporary wage moderation,
- bonus restraint,
- voluntary exits,
- natural attrition,
- redeployment,
- retraining,
- productivity measures,
- and capacity reduction **without closures**.

That is not a giveaway. That is a serious contribution from labour.

But let me be equally clear: **no compulsory redundancies, no plant closures, no dismantling of the collective wage framework, and no one-sided burden shifting onto workers**.

Those are not bargaining chips. Those are our red lines.

## On the expert’s view
The expert confirmed something important: a non-closure package can work if it is **deep, permanent, and tightly enforced**.

That is exactly the route we should pursue.

What the expert did **not** say is that closures are automatically justified. They are stronger on permanence, yes — but that does not make them legitimate if there are credible alternatives that preserve sites and jobs.

## What I want next
Before anyone starts speaking as if closures are inevitable, management must present a **fully quantified comparison**:
1. what closures would save,
2. what a non-closure package would save,
3. how quickly each takes effect,
4. which measures are permanent,
5. what enforcement mechanism exists,
6. and what the employment and site consequences are.

If the comparison shows that a non-closure package can deliver the needed savings, then closures are not justified. If management refuses to provide that comparison, then I will assume they are avoiding scrutiny because they know the non-closure path is stronger than they want to admit.

## Bottom line
We are ready for hard bargaining. We are **not** ready to sign off on closures or forced layoffs disguised as restructuring.

If you want a durable settlement, it must be:
- economically credible,
- socially balanced,
- and built on shared sacrifice.

If that is refused, then the conflict remains real, and the works council will use its full co-determination leverage to defend the German plants and the job-security framework.
