We are ready to talk numbers, but let’s be clear: **“numbers” cannot be used to smuggle in plant closures and forced redundancies as the only legitimate outcome**. That is where we disagree most sharply.

### Where we agree
- The company needs a **real, durable restructuring**.
- A cosmetic compromise would be irresponsible.
- The cost base must come down.
- Capacity must be adjusted to demand.
- A settlement must be **enforceable** and not just a press release.
- Prolonged conflict would hurt the company and the workforce.

### Where we disagree
Management keeps framing the issue as if the only serious choices are:
1. closures and layoffs, or  
2. a weak compromise.

That is false.

The union has already said we are prepared to discuss:
- working-time adjustments,
- productivity measures,
- capacity reduction **without closures**,
- natural attrition,
- voluntary exits,
- bonus restraint and other employee contributions,
- and temporary or reversible sacrifices.

That is not “protections first and savings second.” That is a **serious shared-sacrifice package**. What we reject is the idea that workers must hand over jobs, plants, and guarantees before anyone else contributes.

### To management
You say the burden cannot fall mainly on management discipline alone. Fine — we are not asking you to do that alone. But you also cannot demand that the workforce carry the decisive burden through closures and forced redundancies while calling that “competitiveness.”

If you want our contribution, then show us a package where:
- the employee side contributes materially,
- management accepts real restraint,
- owners accept limits on short-term extraction,
- and sites remain open.

Without that, your “economic equivalence” test becomes a one-way test: labor must prove equivalence, while closure is treated as the default. We do not accept that standard.

### To the investors and owners
You keep saying you want durable savings and execution certainty. So do we. But execution certainty is not created by threatening the workforce into submission. It is created by a fair, binding settlement.

And let me be blunt: if you insist on treating plant closures as the clearest tool, you will trigger exactly the instability you claim to fear. The workforce will not accept being made the sole adjustment variable.

### To the regional state government
You are right to insist that this is a regional-industrial question, not just a quarterly-margin question. But that means you cannot simply stand aside while management uses “competitiveness” to justify tearing apart plant structures and employment security.

If the state wants to preserve industrial capacity, it should support a package that:
- keeps plants open,
- avoids compulsory redundancies,
- uses transfers, retraining, working-time measures, and capacity planning,
- and includes investment commitments.

That is not sentimental. It is how you preserve skills, suppliers, and long-term industrial strength.

### The key point
We are **not** refusing restructuring. We are refusing a restructuring where:
- employees pay first and most,
- sites are closed to create leverage,
- and the burden is made socially one-sided.

That is not a durable settlement; it is a conflict waiting to happen again.

### What we would accept as a serious path
We can negotiate a package if it includes all of the following:
- **No compulsory redundancies**
- **No plant closures**
- **Material employee contribution** through pay restraint, time, flexibility, or bonus sacrifices
- **Management and owner contribution** as well
- **Binding site and investment commitments**
- **A quantified savings target** that is real, permanent, and jointly verified
- **Capacity reduction through negotiated means**, not through closure threats

If management wants us to accept painful adjustment, then it must be **shared pain**.

### On your three hard questions
1. **What annual savings are being delivered?**  
   We are prepared to discuss a quantified target, but it must be derived from a balanced package, not from closures being assumed as the benchmark.

2. **Which capacity is being removed, and when?**  
   Capacity can be reduced through shifts, product allocation, and working-time redesign — not only by shutting sites.

3. **What exact labour-cost contribution is permanent and enforceable?**  
   We can discuss wage restraint, bonuses, and flexibility — but only if the deal also includes job and site protection.

So my answer is simple:  
**We are ready for hard bargaining. We are not ready to sign off on plant closures or forced layoffs disguised as restructuring.** If that is still the management line, then the conflict remains real, and the union will use its full leverage.
