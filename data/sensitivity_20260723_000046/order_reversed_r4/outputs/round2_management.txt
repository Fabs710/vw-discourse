I’ll respond plainly.

We all agree on the diagnosis: **the company’s German cost base is structurally too high** and the current footprint is not aligned with a shrinking European market. We also agree that a cosmetic deal is worthless. On that, there is no dispute.

Where I disagree is on the idea that this can be solved while **preserving all sites, all guarantees, and avoiding hard structural consequences**.

### To the institutional investors
You are right to demand **durable, enforceable savings** and a credible path back to margin target. That is exactly the standard management is working to. I also agree that execution certainty matters. But let me be clear: if the workforce package does not deliver the needed structural cost reduction, it is not a solution. It is a delay.

### To the controlling family
I agree that value destruction from prolonged conflict would be unacceptable. But stability without competitiveness is also value destruction. We are not seeking confrontation for its own sake; we are trying to prevent a slow-burn decline that would damage the company far more than decisive restructuring now.

### To the regional state government
I understand the political and regional concern. But I need to push back hard on the idea that plant closures must be ruled out as a first resort. That is not a commercial answer; it is a political preference. If a plant is structurally overcostly and underutilized, preserving it at any price simply transfers the burden onto the rest of the business and onto future investment.

You keep saying “show me the numbers.” Fair enough. The numbers are exactly why we are here: **we need a material, permanent reduction in capacity and labor cost**. If the alternative package can genuinely match the economics of closures — not in slogans, but in annual run-rate savings, timing, and permanence — then management will consider it. But I have not yet heard anything from the labour side that credibly demonstrates equivalence at the required scale.

### To the industrial union and works council
This is where I have to be most direct.

You are asking for:
- no plant closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- no broken guarantees,
- no structural pain that is visible enough to matter,

while also expecting the company to restore competitiveness. That combination does **not** add up.

I am not dismissing worker contributions. I welcome them — if they are real. But “working-time measures,” “natural attrition,” “voluntary exits,” and “productivity improvements” are only helpful if they create a **substantial and permanent** P&L effect. So far, your position sounds like a package of protections first and savings second. That will not restore margins.

And let me say this clearly: **the burden cannot fall mainly on management discipline while the operational cost base stays broken**. Management cannot cut its way out of this alone. Owners cannot “think long term” their way out of this alone. The operating footprint has to change.

### On what management needs
We need a settlement that delivers:
- **permanent cost reduction**
- **real capacity removal**
- **material labour-cost relief**
- **timely impact on the 2030 competitiveness path**
- **enforceability if market conditions worsen**

If the proposal does not hit those requirements, it does not protect future investment capacity — it destroys it.

### My current position
Management is prepared to negotiate on the instruments. We are **not** prepared to negotiate away the objective.

So here is the boundary:

- **Yes** to a negotiated package if it is economically equivalent.
- **Yes** to alternatives if they are truly durable and quantified.
- **No** to a deal that preserves excess capacity under another name.
- **No** to a settlement that leaves German operations uncompetitive.
- **No** to postponing the hard part until after the strike risk has passed.

If the labour side wants to avoid closures, then it must bring forward a package that is not just politically easier, but financially real. Until then, plant closures remain on the table because they remain the clearest tool for removing structural overcapacity.

If useful in the next round, I want the discussion narrowed to three hard questions:
1. What annual savings are being delivered?
2. Which capacity is being removed, and when?
3. What exact labour-cost contribution is permanent and enforceable?

Without those answers, we are still talking around the problem.
