I will respond directly.

I agree with the others on one essential point: **the company needs a real, durable restructuring**. Delay, symbolism, and accounting tricks are not an option. If the German cost base stays structurally uncompetitive, the company will lose margin, investment capacity, and strategic room to maneuver.

But I do **not** accept the conclusion that this necessarily means plant closures or a one-sided burden on the workforce.

### Where I agree
- **Competitiveness must be restored.**
- **Savings must be real, permanent, and enforceable.**
- **Capacity must be aligned with demand.**
- **A deal that merely postpones the problem is unacceptable.**
- **Prolonged strikes would damage the company and the regional economy.**

### Where I disagree
I must be very clear: I will not support a package that:
- closes plants in the state as the default answer,
- creates mass layoffs or forced redundancies,
- hollows out the regional industrial base,
- or sidelines the state’s veto and co-determination role.

The regional state is not here to rubber-stamp a cost-cutting exercise that treats German sites as disposable. This company is a regional anchor employer, with suppliers, apprenticeships, innovation networks, and long-term public value attached to its footprint. That matters.

### On the numbers
The current discussion still lacks the key piece: **a quantified, balanced package**. Before anyone claims that closures are “necessary,” I want to see the actual comparison:

- What annual savings are required?
- How much comes from productivity, working-time changes, redeployment, natural attrition, and voluntary exits?
- How much from labor-cost measures?
- How much from capacity reduction?
- What is the timing?
- What is permanent, and what is only temporary?

If management says alternatives are not equivalent, then it must prove that with transparent numbers, not just assert it.

### On plant closures
I do **not** accept closures as a first resort. They are the most destructive and least reversible tool. If there is a package that can achieve comparable savings and capacity reduction **without** closures, then that must be pursued first.

That said, I am not here to defend every legacy structure regardless of economics. I am open to **real capacity reduction**, site specialization, shift adjustments, working-time changes, and negotiated workforce contributions — but only if they are socially balanced and protect the company’s role as an industrial anchor.

### On burden-sharing
I share the union’s core point here: if employees are expected to contribute, then management, owners, and capital must also contribute. It is not acceptable to ask workers for wage restraint, flexibility, and guarantees while others simply preserve their returns and call it a restructuring.

A viable package must include:
- material employee contributions,
- management discipline,
- owner restraint where needed,
- and binding investment commitments for future products and sites.

### On governance
I also need to push back on the idea that governance constraints are somehow an obstacle to be worked around. The state’s role under the special statute, the supervisory-board balance, and co-determination are part of the company’s legitimacy and stability. They are not incidental. Any durable solution must respect that structure.

### My position, plainly stated
I am prepared to support a negotiated restructuring if it:
- preserves core sites where possible,
- minimizes job losses,
- avoids compulsory redundancies,
- delivers genuine cost reduction,
- protects investment in the future,
- and keeps the company anchored in the region.

I am **not** prepared to support a plan whose practical meaning is “jobs and plants in the state are the price of competitiveness.”

If management wants my support, it needs to come back with a serious, quantified alternative to closures — one that is economically credible **and** socially responsible. If that cannot be done, then the proposal is not mature enough to carry.

If useful, I suggest we now move to a structured package discussion:
1. required savings,
2. capacity measures,
3. labor-cost contribution,
4. site guarantees,
5. investment commitments,
6. governance safeguards.

But I will not give away the principle that regional jobs and sites are to be protected unless there is a compelling, proven necessity.
