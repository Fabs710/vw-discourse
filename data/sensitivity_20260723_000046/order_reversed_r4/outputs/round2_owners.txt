We are aligned on the central economic point: **this company needs a durable step-change in competitiveness**, not another layer of postponement. The capital-markets side is right to insist on quantified savings, enforceability, and timing. If the package does not move the P&L materially and permanently, it is not good enough.

Where I part company with the labour-side positions is just as clear: **a solution that simply preserves every site and every historic guarantee, while leaving the cost base structurally uncompetitive, is not acceptable**. I do not care whether that failure is dressed up as “stability” or “social balance” — if margins remain weak, the company loses strategic freedom, investment capacity, and ultimately jobs.

That said, I also want to push back on a simplistic “closures or nothing” mindset. **We do not need plant closures as a principle; we need the economics.** If an alternative package can achieve the same permanent savings, capacity reduction, and labor-cost adjustment with better stability, then that is preferable from an owner’s perspective. But it has to be truly equivalent, not just politically easier.

### What I agree with
- Management is right that the current German cost base is too high.
- Investors are right that delay destroys value.
- A real restructuring must include **capacity reduction** and **material labor contributions**.
- Prolonged conflict and strikes would be value-destructive.

### What I reject
- Any package that is mostly symbolic.
- Any arrangement that leaves excess capacity intact and merely shifts costs around.
- Any outcome that creates long-term governance instability or weakens ownership control.
- Any attempt to define “success” primarily by preserving every site regardless of economics.

### What would move us
If management wants governance support for a non-closure route, we need to see:
1. **A quantified annual run-rate savings target**
2. **A clear timeline for realization**
3. **Permanent, enforceable labor-cost relief**
4. **Actual capacity reduction, not just promises of flexibility**
5. **A credible link to return-on-sales improvement and future investment capacity**
6. **A settlement that secures labour peace rather than inviting another confrontation in twelve months**

### On the union and works council position
I understand the insistence on co-determination and social responsibility. But “no closures, no compulsory redundancies, no site loss” cannot be the starting point if the commercial reality says the present footprint is too large. That position leaves no room for the restructuring the company plainly needs. If the workforce wants to avoid the harshest tools, it must offer something materially equivalent in savings and permanence.

### On the regional state
Your insistence on avoiding hollowing-out is legitimate, but it cannot become a veto over all meaningful adjustment. The company is not a regional subsidy instrument; it is a competitive industrial business. If the state wants this enterprise to remain strong in Germany, it should support a settlement that restores competitiveness, even if that means painful changes.

### On management
Management should not confuse hard restructuring with reckless confrontation. The goal is not conflict for its own sake; the goal is a durable solution that improves the business. If there is a negotiated package that achieves the numbers, it should be preferred. But if the package is weak, the company cannot be asked to pretend otherwise.

So my position is simple: **we support a serious restructuring that delivers real, durable savings and capacity discipline.** We are open on the instruments. We are not open on weakening the economics or the company’s long-term value.
