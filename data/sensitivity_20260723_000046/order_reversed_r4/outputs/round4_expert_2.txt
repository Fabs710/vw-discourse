## Short answer
A non-closure package is only credible as a closure substitute if it delivers:

1. **large annual run-rate savings**
2. **real capacity reduction**
3. **multi-year labor cost relief**
4. **fast implementation**
5. **automatic fallback if targets are missed**
6. **independent verification**
7. **enforceability despite co-determination and bargaining constraints**

Without those elements, it is not economically equivalent; it is a delay package.

---

## Minimum structure of a credible non-closure package

### 1) Labor-cost relief: must be material and durable
To substitute for closures, labor relief cannot be symbolic.

A credible package would normally need some combination of:
- temporary wage moderation or direct pay cuts
- bonus and allowance reductions
- working-time flexibility with lower effective hourly cost
- staffing norm tightening
- overtime reduction
- longer-duration concessions, typically several years
- voluntary exits and natural attrition at scale

**Rule of thumb:** if labor-cost relief is less than roughly **mid-single-digit percent of relevant German payroll cost**, it is usually too small to stand in for closure economics in a high-cost auto site. In a large plant context, that often means **hundreds of millions annually across the site network**, not tens of millions.

---

### 2) Capacity reduction: must be real, not just “efficiency”
A non-closure package must remove enough operating capacity to avoid simply preserving excess fixed cost.

That usually means:
- shift elimination
- line consolidation
- product or model reallocation
- reduced planned output
- fewer indirect staff per unit
- closure of specific functions even if not entire plants

If the package preserves the same footprint, the savings usually erode over time.

**Minimum standard:** there should be a measurable reduction in installed or usable capacity, not just a promise of higher productivity.

---

### 3) Timing: savings must arrive quickly enough
For management and capital to view it as equivalent, the package needs:
- **first visible savings within the current year**, and
- **near-full run-rate within 12–24 months**

Longer than that, and the package often fails the comparison because closures create a cleaner, faster structural reset once executed.

This is especially important where the company is under margin pressure and needs near-term credibility.

---

### 4) Permanence: concessions must not be easily reversible
A non-closure package must avoid “temporary relief that rebounds.”

That means:
- concessions lasting multiple years
- changes embedded in collective agreements or side agreements
- altered staffing rules and flexibility arrangements
- no automatic reversion after a short honeymoon period

If the company can drift back to the old cost base after 1 year, capital will not regard it as closure-equivalent.

---

### 5) Enforceability: automatic fallback is essential
This is the key difference from a soft compromise.

A credible package needs:
- **hard savings targets**
- **deadline dates**
- **independent auditing**
- **automatic trigger clauses**

For example:
- if annual run-rate savings are not achieved by a given date, then additional shift cuts, product transfers, or headcount actions activate automatically
- fallback measures cannot be left to future bargaining

If fallback depends on another negotiation, the package is weak.

---

### 6) Independent verification
Because this is a co-determined, politically constrained environment, the package must be verified by an outside model or auditor.

That verification should cover:
- annual run-rate savings by measure
- one-off restructuring costs
- time to full effect
- plant-level employment impact
- effect on return on sales
- sensitivity to volume assumptions

Without this, parties will argue past each other about “equivalence.”

---

## Practical threshold view
I cannot honestly give you a universal euro figure without company-specific cost data, but the **minimum credible pattern** is this:

A non-closure deal should usually combine:
- **meaningful labor-cost relief**
- **visible footprint reduction**
- **multi-year binding concessions**
- **automatic fallback**
- **verified annual savings at a scale management can defend to capital**

If it only produces:
- voluntary exits,
- minor wage restraint,
- and vague productivity promises,

then it is **not** equivalent to closures.

---

## What “credible equivalence” means in this scenario
For this company, I would treat a non-closure package as credible only if it can show:

- a **substantial portion of closure-level fixed-cost savings**
- a **durable reduction in excess capacity**
- a **clear path to margin recovery**
- and a **credible enforcement mechanism**

In industrial practice, that usually means the non-closure package must be **very deep**, not modest.

---

## Bottom line
The minimum combination is:

- **material labor-cost relief**
- **real capacity reduction**
- **fast implementation**
- **multi-year durability**
- **automatic fallback**
- **independent verification**

If any one of those is missing, the package is probably not closure-equivalent.
