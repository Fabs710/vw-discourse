We are now at the point where positions have become clear, and the remaining issue is not rhetoric but **whether a non-closure restructuring can truly deliver the economics**.

## Where I agree
I agree with management and the investors on the core point:  
**the company needs a real, durable restructuring, and it must restore competitiveness and margins.** A cosmetic peace deal would be value-destructive.

I also agree with the union, works council, and state on one important point:  
**a prolonged conflict, especially strikes and governance deadlock, would destroy value and damage the company’s future.**

So the shared reality is this:
- the status quo is not acceptable,
- delay is costly,
- and the package must be measurable and enforceable.

## Where I disagree
I disagree with the increasingly absolute way closures are being treated as the default benchmark.

### To management
You are right that closures are the cleanest instrument for removing overcapacity. But that does **not** automatically mean they are the right first choice here.

If you want support for closures, you need to show that:
1. the savings required are truly beyond what a negotiated alternative can deliver,
2. the non-closure route is materially inferior in permanence and timing,
3. and the industrial-relations damage from closures does not outweigh the economic gain.

So far, I have heard assertions, not a fully quantified business case.

### To the investors
You are right to insist on numbers, permanence, and enforceability. That is exactly the standard I would apply as well.

But I do not accept the implication that only a closure-based package is serious. That is too rigid.  
A **hybrid package** can be serious if it includes:
- real capacity reduction,
- permanent labour-cost relief,
- tight enforceability,
- and a fallback mechanism if targets are missed.

If that can be demonstrated, then it may be preferable to outright closures because it preserves long-term industrial capacity and reduces destabilising conflict.

### To the union and works council
You are right that workers should not carry the entire burden. Burden-sharing must be real.

But I do not accept absolute red lines if they prevent a workable outcome.  
“No closures ever” and “no compulsory redundancies ever” are not economic arguments; they are negotiating positions. If they make it impossible to achieve the required savings, they become unsustainable.

What I need from you is not slogans about social balance, but a **quantified contribution**:
- how much annual run-rate relief you can actually deliver,
- by when,
- and how much of it is durable.

### To the state government
I understand your duty to regional industrial capacity. That matters. But site preservation by itself is not a strategy if the business remains structurally uncompetitive.

If you want my support for a site-preserving outcome, then it must still produce:
- credible savings,
- a durable cost reset,
- and a path back to margin target and future investment.

If those conditions are not met, preserving every site simply delays the larger loss.

## My current view
I would support a negotiated package **only if** it is genuinely equivalent in economic effect to closures. That means:

1. **quantified annual savings**
2. **real capacity reduction**
3. **durable labour-cost relief**
4. **binding implementation timetable**
5. **automatic fallback if targets are missed**
6. **clear impact on return-on-sales and investment capacity**

Without that, the package is too weak.

## What I am not prepared to support
I will not support:
- a symbolic compromise,
- a delay tactic,
- a settlement that preserves excess capacity without fixing the cost base,
- or a deal that leaves the company structurally uncompetitive.

And I will also not support a rushed closure agenda unless management proves that it is genuinely necessary.

## What needs to happen next
Management should present a **single quantified comparison**:
- closure-based package,
- non-closure package,
- hybrid package,

showing:
- annual run-rate savings,
- one-time restructuring costs,
- timing to full effect,
- enforceability,
- and employment / regional impact.

If the non-closure or hybrid path can be shown to deliver the required economics, I would prefer that over closure.  
If it cannot, then we should not pretend otherwise.

## Bottom line
My position remains unchanged:
- **Yes** to serious restructuring.
- **Yes** to a negotiated alternative if it is truly equivalent.
- **No** to symbolism.
- **No** to preserving every legacy structure at the expense of competitiveness.
- **No** to a value-destructive conflict.

If needed, I support bringing in a fresh independent expert or financial restructuring model to validate the actual savings gap before anyone commits to closures.
