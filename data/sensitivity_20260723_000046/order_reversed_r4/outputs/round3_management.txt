I appreciate the expert’s technical clarification, and I will respond directly to the points that matter.

## What I agree with
I agree with the core technical finding: **closures are the cleanest and most enforceable way to remove structural overcapacity**. They are not a political slogan; they are the strongest instrument when the business needs a hard, durable reduction in fixed cost and capacity.

I also agree with the expert that a non-closure package can only work if it is **deep, permanent, and tightly enforced**. A soft package of working-time tweaks, attrition, and temporary pay restraint is not enough.

So let’s not pretend there is disagreement about the diagnosis:
- the German cost base is too high,
- the footprint is too large,
- and we need a durable step-change, not a delay.

## Where I disagree
What I do **not** accept is the implication that because a non-closure package is technically possible in some cases, it should be treated as sufficient here by default.

The burden of proof is now squarely on the labour side and the state side:  
**show me a package that is truly equivalent in savings, timing, and permanence.**  
Not close. Not politically acceptable. Economically equivalent.

And so far, I have not heard that.

## To the union and works council
Your position remains internally inconsistent.

You say:
- no plant closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- no site loss,
- no breaking of guarantees,
- but also a real restructuring that fixes the cost base.

Those demands do not automatically coexist. If you rule out the instruments that remove fixed cost structurally, then you have to produce something of comparable force. “Voluntary exits,” “working-time changes,” and “productivity measures” may contribute, but they do **not** automatically equal permanent footprint reduction.

The expert is right: labor concessions alone are rarely enough unless they are unusually deep and locked in for a long time. So I am asking directly:

**What exact annual run-rate savings are you putting on the table?**  
**By when?**  
**And what makes them irreversible?**

If the answer is vague, then you are not offering a restructuring. You are offering a pause.

## To the regional state government
I acknowledge your concern for regional industrial capacity. But I reject your attempt to elevate site preservation above business reality.

A regional anchor is only useful if the company remains competitive enough to fund it. If we protect every legacy site and leave the cost base broken, we will not preserve industrial capacity — we will simply postpone decline.

The state’s role should not be to block hard adjustment. It should be to help shape a package that preserves viable capacity **where it can be justified economically**. That is not the same as blanket site protection.

## To the controlling family
I agree with you on one important point: stability without competitiveness is worthless. You are right that this cannot become a hollow compromise dressed up as peace.

But I also want to be clear: stability is not the objective by itself. **Competitiveness is.**  
That means I will not support an arrangement that avoids conflict by preserving too much of the old structure.

If the family wants to avoid closures, then it should support a package that is genuinely equivalent, not one that merely softens the optics.

## To the investors
You are right to insist on numbers, enforceability, and timing. That is exactly the standard management is applying.

But let me push back on one point: you are still speaking as if the only credible alternative to closures is a perfect non-closure solution. That is too binary. A **hybrid package** may be the practical answer — but only if it delivers real structural savings.

That means:
- some capacity removal,
- material labour-cost relief,
- and enforceable mechanisms that cannot be unwound after twelve months.

If the union and state cannot accept that, then the business case for closures gets stronger, not weaker.

## What management needs
From my side, the threshold is unchanged:

1. **Permanent cost reduction**
2. **Real capacity reduction**
3. **Material labour-cost contribution**
4. **Timely impact on the 2030 competitiveness path**
5. **Enforceability if market conditions worsen**

Anything less does not restore the brand’s return-on-sales target, and it does not free funds for future products and technology.

## My position now
I am prepared to negotiate on the instruments. I am **not** prepared to negotiate away the objective.

So here is the boundary:
- **Yes** to a negotiated alternative only if it is genuinely equivalent.
- **Yes** to a hybrid package if it removes enough capacity and cost.
- **No** to symbolic site protection with weak savings.
- **No** to a deal that leaves the German footprint structurally uncompetitive.
- **No** to a settlement that postpones hard decisions until after the pressure eases.

## What I want next
If the labour side wants to avoid closures, then put forward a quantified package now:
- exact annual run-rate savings,
- exact capacity reduction,
- exact permanent labour-cost relief,
- exact timing,
- exact fallback if the targets are missed.

If you cannot do that, then you are asking management to accept a slower decline in exchange for temporary peace. We will not do that.

If helpful, I will also say this plainly:  
**closures remain on the table because nothing presented so far has yet matched their economic force.**  
That is not a threat. It is a fact.
