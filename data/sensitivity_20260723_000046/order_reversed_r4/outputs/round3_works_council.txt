I will be direct.

## Where I agree
I agree with everyone on one point: **this company needs a real restructuring**. No one here can credibly argue for cosmetic measures or delay. I also agree that a prolonged conflict would damage the business and the workforce.

But agreement stops there.

## Where I fundamentally disagree
I do **not** accept the idea that the only serious way to restructure is through **plant closures** or **compulsory redundancies**.

That is the line management, investors, and the family keep trying to normalize. I reject it.

You keep saying “economic equivalence” as if it were a neutral standard. It is not neutral if it is used to force through closures by another name. If the benchmark is defined so narrowly that only shutdowns count as “real,” then you are not negotiating — you are imposing an outcome.

## To management
You say closures are the cleanest tool. Maybe technically. But that does **not** mean they are justified here.

You have still not shown why a serious package of:
- working-time changes,
- product and line reallocation,
- shift consolidation,
- voluntary exits,
- natural attrition,
- productivity measures,
- bonus restraint,
- and targeted capacity reduction

cannot deliver the required savings **without** destroying sites and job security.

So I ask again:  
**Show the numbers.**  
Not slogans. Not pressure. Not “the market won’t wait.”

If you want to cross our red lines, the burden of proof is on **you**.

And let me be equally clear: if your answer is still “closures remain on the table,” then we will treat that as what it is — an attempt to use fear to break resistance. That will not work.

## To the institutional investors
You keep demanding durability, timing, and enforceability. Fine. That is legitimate.

What is **not** legitimate is treating the harshest possible instrument as the only credible one. That is an ideological preference for destruction, not a technical conclusion.

A non-closure package is not “cosmetic” just because it does not satisfy your appetite for maximal cutback. If the package includes real capacity reduction, permanent labour-cost contributions, and binding rules, then it is a serious restructuring.

What I will not accept is your attempt to make workers prove equivalence while closures are treated as the default reference point. That is backwards. If you want closures, **prove they are necessary**.

## To the controlling family
You say you are not pro-closure as a principle, only pro-economic reality. Then act like it.

Stop speaking as if plant closures are the benchmark for seriousness. They are not. The benchmark should be:
- preserving viable industrial capacity,
- protecting long-term stewardship,
- avoiding irreversible damage,
- and maintaining trust in the governance system.

A “durable” solution that tears up job security and triggers a permanent conflict with the workforce is not durable. It is brittle.

And no, I do not accept that preserving jobs means refusing restructuring. We are ready for restructuring. We are **not** ready for plant closures or forced redundancies.

## To the regional state government
I appreciate that you reject closures as a first resort. But words are cheap.

If the state truly wants to protect the region’s industrial base, then it must oppose:
- plant closures,
- compulsory redundancies,
- and the dismantling of established job protections.

I agree with you that regional industrial capacity matters. That is exactly why closures are the wrong answer. Once a plant is gone, the skills, the supplier network, and the social fabric do not come back quickly — if ever.

But I also want to be clear: if the state is only prepared to “prefer” alternatives while leaving the door open to closures, then that is not protection. That is hesitation.

## To the union
We are aligned on the core red lines:
- **no compulsory redundancies**
- **no plant closures**
- **no one-sided burden shifting**
- **no dismantling of the collective wage framework**
- **no fait accompli**

That is the correct position. We should not weaken it.

I also want to stress that plant-level co-determination is not decorative. We must have a real role in shaping the restructuring, not simply be informed after the fact. That means binding involvement in how capacity is adjusted, how workloads are redistributed, how retraining is organized, and how site security is guaranteed.

## On the expert view
The expert confirmed something important: a non-closure package can work if it is **deep, permanent, and tightly enforced**.

That is exactly the route we should pursue — not because it is easy, but because it is the only route that does **not** violate our core interests.

The expert also noted that closures are strongest on permanence. That may be true technically. It does **not** make them acceptable here. The fact that a tool is strong does not mean it should be used when it destroys the workforce and industrial base.

## What I am prepared to discuss
I am prepared to discuss a serious package with:
- working-time adjustments,
- temporary wage moderation,
- bonus restraint,
- voluntary exits,
- natural attrition,
- redeployment,
- retraining,
- productivity measures,
- and capacity reduction **without closures**.

But I am only prepared to discuss that package if it includes:
- **binding site guarantees**
- **no compulsory redundancies**
- **full respect for co-determination**
- **a real job-security framework**
- **investment commitments**
- **and a quantified savings plan that does not rely on shutdowns**

## What is not negotiable
Let me be unambiguous:

- **No plant closures**
- **No compulsory redundancies**
- **No breaking of historic job guarantees**
- **No bypassing co-determination**
- **No settlement that shifts the burden mainly onto workers**

If management says that makes restructuring impossible, then say that openly. But do not pretend that shutting plants is the only serious answer.

## What I want next
If this discussion is to move forward, then management must bring a **full quantified comparison**:
1. what closures would save,
2. what a non-closure package would save,
3. how quickly each takes effect,
4. which measures are permanent,
5. and what the employment and site consequences are.

If that comparison shows a non-closure package can deliver the needed savings, then closures are not justified. If management refuses to provide the comparison, then I will assume it is avoiding scrutiny because the non-closure alternative is stronger than it wants to admit.
