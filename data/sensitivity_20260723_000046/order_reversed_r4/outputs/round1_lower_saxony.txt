I agree with one central point from both the institutional investors and the controlling family: **the company needs a credible, durable restructuring that restores competitiveness**. A purely symbolic compromise would be irresponsible, because it would leave the cost problem unresolved and weaken the group’s future investment capacity.

But I need to be equally clear about where I do **not** agree:

- I will **not** support plant closures in the state as a first resort.
- I will **not** support a restructuring that turns into mass layoffs and a permanent hollowing-out of regional industrial capacity.
- I will **not** accept a process in which the regional state, works councils, and co-determination are treated as obstacles to be bypassed rather than as legitimate governance actors.

From the perspective of the state and the regional economy, the issue is not just the next quarterly margin. It is whether this company remains a strong industrial anchor with skilled jobs, supplier networks, apprenticeships, and innovation capacity in Germany for the long term.

That said, I am not rejecting adjustment. I am prepared to support a **negotiated restructuring** if it meets real tests:

1. **Sites are preserved where at all possible.**  
   Capacity reduction can be part of the package, but closure is the most destructive and least reversible instrument.

2. **Job losses are minimized and primarily handled through natural attrition, transfers, working-time measures, retraining, and agreed voluntary exits.**  
   Forced redundancies would be a serious escalation.

3. **Any labour contribution must be balanced and socially fair.**  
   If the workforce gives on pay, flexibility, or guarantees, management and owners must also give on investment commitments, product allocation, and long-term site security.

4. **The restructuring must be economically real, not cosmetic.**  
   I do not support a deal that keeps excess capacity while merely shifting costs onto employees or the public side.

5. **The company’s governance balance must remain intact.**  
   The state’s co-determination and veto role is not negotiable.

So my position is this: I am open to **deep restructuring through negotiated means**, but not to a strategy that treats German plants as disposable. If management wants the state’s support, it must come forward with a package that protects core sites, preserves the company’s regional role, and delivers genuine competitiveness without social rupture.

If the choice is presented as “closures or nothing,” I will challenge that framing. There are other routes, but they must be serious and quantified.
