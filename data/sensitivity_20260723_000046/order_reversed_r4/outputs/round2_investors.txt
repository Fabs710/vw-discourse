We agree with the moderator’s refocus: the discussion now has to move from rhetoric to **quantified, enforceable economics**.

Our position remains unchanged in substance:

### What we agree with
- The company needs a **real restructuring**, not a symbolic one.
- **Delay is value-destructive**.
- A package that combines **capacity reduction, labour-cost relief, and operational discipline** can be preferable to outright closures **if** it is economically equivalent.
- Prolonged strikes or open-ended conflict would damage margins, execution, and valuation.

### Where we disagree
We do **not** accept the current labour-side position that effectively demands:
- no closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- no real reduction in excess capacity,
- while still expecting the market to reward the outcome.

That is not a restructuring; it is a re-labelling exercise.

We also do **not** accept a standard for “acceptability” defined mainly by site preservation or job preservation if the result leaves the cost base structurally uncompetitive. If the company comes out of this still above peer cost levels and still missing its ROS target, shareholders will simply keep discounting the stock and capital will remain constrained. That outcome is not acceptable to us.

### What we need to see
If management and labour want support for a non-closure solution, then bring a package with numbers:

1. **Target savings amount**
   - What annual run-rate savings are being delivered?
   - By when are they realized?
   - How much is permanent versus temporary?

2. **Capacity reduction**
   - How much capacity is taken out?
   - Which lines, shifts, or sites are underutilized?
   - What is the impact on fixed-cost absorption?

3. **Labour-cost contribution**
   - Wage restraint, concessions, working-time changes, bonus reductions, or loss of guarantees must be **material**, not symbolic.
   - If employees are “contributing,” we need to see that contribution in the P&L.

4. **Enforceability**
   - What makes the deal durable if demand weakens further?
   - What prevents a staged return of the old cost structure?

5. **Timing**
   - Savings delayed beyond the near term are less valuable.
   - We need a path that improves margins quickly enough to matter for capital allocation and investment.

### On closures
We are not ideologically attached to plant closures. But they remain a legitimate tool if they are the only way to achieve the required economics. If alternatives cannot match the savings, timing, and durability of closures, then rejecting closures on principle is simply refusing the problem.

### On labour peace
The union and works council are right that execution matters. But execution certainty is not served by pretending there is a cost-free alternative. A deal that avoids strikes by avoiding hard decisions will just create a slower margin problem and more conflict later.

### On burden-sharing
We accept that management, owners, and labour all have roles to play. But burden-sharing is only credible if it produces **real structural savings**. It is not enough for capital to “restrain expectations” if the operating cost base remains broken.

### Our bottom line
We are open to a negotiated package **if and only if** it can demonstrate:
- durable cost reduction,
- capacity alignment,
- timely delivery,
- and a credible return to competitive margins.

Absent that, we will view the outcome as a cosmetic compromise and treat it accordingly in our capital allocation and market commentary.
