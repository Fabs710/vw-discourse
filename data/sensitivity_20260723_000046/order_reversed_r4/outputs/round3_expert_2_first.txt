My assessment is limited to **industrial restructuring, plant economics, and German industrial relations**.

## Executive conclusion
A **closure-based package** is usually the **stronger and more certain instrument** for achieving permanent cost and capacity reduction in German automotive manufacturing.

A **non-closure package** can be viable, but only if it is unusually deep:
- real capacity reduction,
- durable labor-cost relief,
- enforceable contractual mechanisms,
- and a long enough duration to prevent cost creep.

If the required savings are **large and fast**, non-closure measures often fall short unless they include **hard capacity removal** that is economically close to closure. If the required savings are **moderate**, a negotiated alternative may be sufficient.

## What closures typically do better
Closures are stronger on:
- **permanence**: capacity disappears;
- **verification**: easier to measure;
- **enforceability**: less dependent on future cooperation;
- **run-rate savings**: clearer and usually larger.

That is why management tends to treat closures as the benchmark.

## What non-closure packages typically do better
Non-closure solutions are better on:
- **speed of agreement** if all sides want to avoid conflict;
- **lower immediate social damage**;
- **preserving regional industrial presence**;
- **political and co-determination acceptability**.

But they are often weaker on:
- **durability**;
- **reversibility risk** after peace periods expire;
- **partial savings** if capacity remains too high;
- **implementation complexity** across multiple plants.

## The key technical issue: equivalence
For a non-closure package to be genuinely “equivalent” to closures, it must deliver most of the following:
1. **permanent annual run-rate savings** on a closure-like scale,
2. **real capacity reduction**, not just labor concessions,
3. **binding timing**, with benefits arriving quickly enough,
4. **automatic consequences** if targets are missed,
5. **low reversal risk** when agreements expire.

If these conditions are missing, the non-closure package is usually **inferior in economic force**, even if it is socially preferable.

## Site-level employment impact
### Closure-based
- direct job loss is highest at the site;
- supplier, logistics, and local service losses follow;
- regional shock is concentrated and immediate;
- but the company’s footprint becomes smaller and clearer.

### Non-closure
- direct job losses can be lower upfront;
- more employment is preserved in the short term;
- but if the package does not fix the cost base, later restructuring risk remains;
- the region may get delayed rather than avoided job losses.

So the employment trade-off is not simply “jobs vs no jobs”; it is **immediate loss vs deferred loss** and **certainty vs risk**.

## German governance constraint
In Germany, major plant closure decisions are not just operational choices; they are heavily constrained by:
- co-determination,
- supervisory-board mechanics,
- collective bargaining,
- works-council rights,
- and likely political scrutiny in a large industrial group.

That means:
- closures may be economically strong,
- but their **execution risk and timing risk** can be very high;
- non-closure packages may be easier to implement if they can be agreed within the bargaining framework.

## My technical judgment on this scenario
Based on the facts as presented:
- management appears to need a **structural** reduction in cost and capacity;
- the labor side is rejecting closures and forced redundancies;
- therefore the real question is whether a **hybrid package** can deliver enough savings without crossing red lines.

My view:
- If the savings gap is **material but not extreme**, a hybrid non-closure package with real capacity rationalization, labor concessions, and binding fallback clauses may work.
- If the savings gap is **very large**, then a package without closures or closure-equivalent hard capacity removal is unlikely to be durable enough.

## What management should quantify next
To make a valid comparison, management should present:
1. **annual run-rate savings by measure**,
2. **one-time restructuring costs**,
3. **time to realize savings**,
4. **reversal risk after agreement expiry**,
5. **employment impact by site**,
6. **supplier and regional knock-on effects**.

Without that, nobody can honestly say whether the non-closure path is economically equivalent.

## Bottom line
- **Closures**: strongest on permanence and certainty, highest social cost.
- **Non-closure restructuring**: better socially and politically, but only credible if it includes **real capacity removal and durable cost cuts**.
- **Most likely outcome in practice**: a **hybrid deal** if the numbers permit it; otherwise, closures remain the stronger economic instrument, even if they are harder to execute.
