We are now at the point where the real question is no longer whether restructuring is needed. It is. The question is whether management, investors, and owners are trying to redefine “real restructuring” as **closures and forced redundancies by another name**. On that point, we disagree sharply.

## Where I agree
I agree with management, the family, investors, and the state on several points:
- The company needs a **durable restructuring**, not cosmetics.
- The current cost base is too high.
- Capacity must be adjusted to demand.
- The package must be **enforceable** and not just a press statement.
- A prolonged conflict would damage the company and the workforce.

That is all true.

## Where I disagree
I do **not** accept the claim that the only serious benchmark is a closure-based package, or that a non-closure path is automatically weak unless it mirrors closures exactly.

That framing is too convenient. It loads the deck in favour of shutting plants and breaking job protections, while presenting that as “economic reality.” We reject that.

The union has already put real substance on the table:
- working-time adjustments,
- productivity measures,
- capacity reduction **without closures**,
- voluntary exits,
- natural attrition,
- bonus restraint,
- temporary pay moderation,
- and a material employee contribution.

That is not cosmetic. That is a **shared-sacrifice restructuring**.

## To management
You keep saying closures are the cleanest and most enforceable tool. Maybe they are, technically. But “cleanest” does not mean “necessary,” and certainly not “acceptable” as a first choice.

You have **not proved** that the savings gap can only be closed by plant closures or by destroying the collective wage framework. You keep asking us to prove equivalence. No — if you want to cross our red lines, **you** must prove necessity.

So here is what we need from you, in numbers:
- the exact annual run-rate savings required,
- how much must come from labour cost,
- how much from capacity reduction,
- how much from management and owner contribution,
- and what combination of measures would be enough **without** closures.

If you cannot show that, then you are not negotiating restructuring. You are negotiating surrender.

## To the institutional investors
You say anything short of closure-level economics is cosmetic. That is too rigid.

A company can absolutely become competitive through:
- real capacity reduction,
- durable labour-cost relief,
- simplification,
- and binding commitments that lock in the savings.

What I do accept is that a weak, temporary package is not enough. What I do **not** accept is your implication that only closures can be serious. That is not a technical conclusion; it is a preference for the harshest instrument.

If you believe the non-closure route is insufficient, then quantify the gap precisely:
- how much savings shortfall remains,
- how much timing gap remains,
- what enforceability gap remains.

Otherwise you are arguing for the strongest hammer, not proving it is the only tool.

## To the controlling family
You say you are not pro-closure in principle, only pro-economic reality. Fine. Then stop treating closures as the benchmark every other proposal has to beat on their terms.

If you want a durable solution, then back a package that:
- keeps sites open,
- avoids forced redundancies,
- includes real employee contributions,
- and binds management and owners to investment and restraint.

A deal that destroys trust in the workforce is not durable. It is brittle.

## To the regional state government
You are right that the region’s industrial base matters. That is exactly why closures are the wrong answer as a default.

But I will be candid: if the state only “prefers” alternatives and does not clearly oppose plant closures and forced redundancies, then its position is too weak to protect the region.

If you want a serious alternative, you must support:
- working-time flexibility,
- redeployment,
- retraining,
- product and line allocation,
- voluntary exits,
- and site security.

And yes, we expect the state to use its position to defend that path.

## What we are prepared to discuss
We are prepared to discuss a package with:
- material employee contributions,
- temporary wage moderation,
- bonus restraint,
- voluntary exits,
- natural attrition,
- redeployment,
- retraining,
- productivity measures,
- and capacity reduction **without closures**,
- plus binding investment commitments.

That is a serious offer. It is not a free lunch.

## What is not negotiable
Let me be absolutely clear:
- **No compulsory redundancies**
- **No plant closures**
- **No dismantling of the collective wage framework**
- **No one-sided burden shifting onto workers**

If management says that makes restructuring impossible, then say so openly. But do not pretend that closures are the only “real” route.

## On the expert’s remarks
The expert said a non-closure package can work if it is deep, permanent, and tightly enforced. We agree.

The expert also said closures are strongest on permanence. That may be true technically. It does **not** make them acceptable here. A strong instrument is not automatically justified when it violates the core interests of the workforce and regional industrial base.

## What should happen next
Before anyone starts speaking as if closures are inevitable, management should bring a fully quantified comparison:
1. what closures would save,
2. what a non-closure package would save,
3. how quickly each takes effect,
4. which measures are permanent,
5. and what the employment and site consequences are.

If that comparison shows the non-closure package can deliver the needed savings, then closures are not justified. If management refuses to provide that comparison, then I will assume the comparison is being avoided because the non-closure alternative is stronger than they want to admit.

## Bottom line
We are ready for hard bargaining. We are **not** ready to sign off on closures or forced layoffs disguised as restructuring.

If you want a durable settlement, it must be:
- economically credible,
- socially balanced,
- and built on shared sacrifice.

If that is refused, then the conflict remains real, and the union will use its full leverage.
