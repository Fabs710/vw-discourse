We are at the point where slogans have exhausted themselves. I will be equally direct.

## Where I agree
- **To investors:** you are right that this must be judged by **durable economics**, not by optics.
- **To management:** you are right that the current cost base is too high and the footprint must change.
- **To the union and works council:** you are right that a prolonged conflict would destroy value and weaken the company’s future.
- **To the state:** you are right that this is not just a quarterly margin exercise; regional industrial capacity matters.

So yes, there is shared ground on the diagnosis: **the company needs a real restructuring, and delay is destructive**.

## Where I disagree
What I do **not** accept is the idea that the only credible route is plant closures and/or forced redundancies, with labour expected to absorb the decisive pain while everyone else calls that “competitiveness.”

That is not a long-term stewardship solution. It may be fast, but speed alone is not value. If the package produces social fracture, governance confrontation, and a brittle operating model, then it can still destroy value even if it improves this year’s numbers.

## My position as controlling shareholder
My test is simple:

1. **Does the package restore competitiveness and margins?**
2. **Is it durable and enforceable?**
3. **Does it avoid unnecessary value destruction and instability?**
4. **Does it preserve the company’s strategic freedom and governance control?**

If the answer to any of those is no, then I do not support the package.

## On closures
I am **not** pro-closure as a matter of principle.  
I am pro-**economic reality**.

That means:
- If a non-closure solution is genuinely equivalent in savings, timing, and permanence, I will prefer it.
- If it is not, then refusing closures merely to avoid political discomfort is not acceptable.

So I reject both extremes:
- **No closures ever** is not credible.
- **Closures as the default answer** is also not necessarily the best owner solution.

The burden is on management and labour to produce a package that is **actually equivalent**, not merely rhetorically equivalent.

## On the labour-side red lines
I understand the desire for no compulsory redundancies and no loss of sites. But if those red lines make it impossible to achieve the required savings, then they are not sustainable red lines — they are an invitation to drift.

I will be blunt:  
**I cannot support a settlement that preserves everything while leaving the business structurally uncompetitive.**  
That would be a slower decline dressed up as peace.

## On burden-sharing
I agree with the investors that the labour contribution must be **material and visible in the P&L**.  
I also agree with the state and works council that burden-sharing cannot fall on workers alone.

So the question is not “who sacrifices nothing?”  
The question is “what combination of sacrifices is sufficient, enforceable, and durable?”

That means I would look for a package with:
- real capacity reduction,
- meaningful labour-cost relief,
- productivity improvements,
- voluntary exits and attrition,
- working-time flexibility,
- management discipline,
- and, where justified, owner restraint on capital expectations.

## What would move me
A package could gain my support if it shows:
- a **quantified annual run-rate savings target**,
- **credible timing** for realization,
- **permanent** cost reduction rather than temporary deferment,
- **enforceable mechanisms** if the deal is missed,
- and a clear connection to improved margins and investment capacity.

If management comes back with numbers that show the required savings can be achieved **without closures**, I would regard that as preferable. But if that proof is not there, then we should not pretend otherwise.

## Where I am now
So my position is:

- **Yes** to a serious restructuring.
- **Yes** to a negotiated alternative if it is genuinely equivalent.
- **No** to symbolic deals.
- **No** to preserving every legacy structure regardless of economics.
- **No** to a package that weakens the company’s long-term value in order to avoid hard decisions.

If helpful, I would now ask management to put forward a **single quantified package** with:
1. required savings,
2. source of savings,
3. permanence of savings,
4. timetable,
5. fallback if targets are missed.

Without that, we are still debating ideology rather than the enterprise.
