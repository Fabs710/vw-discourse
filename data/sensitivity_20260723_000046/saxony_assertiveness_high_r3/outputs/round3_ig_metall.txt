We are still not moving on the core issue, and I want to be equally direct: **the workforce will not accept a restructuring that shifts the crisis onto employees while management, owners, and capital keep full optionality**.

## Where I agree
I agree with everyone on the diagnosis that the company needs:
- a durable industrial future,
- investment in EVs and future products,
- a lower cost base,
- and a settlement that is not merely cosmetic.

I also agree with the Works Council and the state that **a deal built on panic and permanent insecurity will fail**. If workers do not trust the process, there will be no durable peace.

## Where I disagree

### 1) Closures are not a “normal” restructuring tool we must eventually accept
Management, the family holding, and investors keep treating plant closures as the credible backstop. I do **not** accept that as the baseline.

A closure is not just a cost-saving line item. It destroys jobs, know-how, supplier networks, and the trust needed for any future bargaining. Once that step is taken, it cannot be undone.

If management believes a site is unviable, then it must prove that with a **fully quantified alternatives package**, not simply insist that closures stay available because that preserves bargaining pressure.

### 2) Compulsory redundancies remain unacceptable
I want to be explicit here because there is too much drift in the room: **forced layoffs are not an acceptable instrument**.

If there has to be workforce reduction, it must come through:
- voluntary departures,
- retirement pathways,
- internal redeployment,
- working-time flexibility,
- qualification and transition measures,
- and negotiated capacity reduction.

That is the line. If management cannot achieve restructuring without compulsory redundancies, then the restructuring model itself is wrong.

### 3) A “time-limited” guarantee that only postpones closure is not security
The Works Council is right to reject a sword hanging over the workforce.

But I also want to say this clearly: **I am not interested in a fake compromise where you call it “phased” or “deferred” and leave closure lurking behind a review trigger.**  
If that is the offer, it is not a security package — it is a delayed threat.

The workforce needs real protection, not a promise that the same battle returns in 12 or 18 months under worse conditions.

### 4) Shared sacrifice must actually be shared
I will not accept a package where workers give up wages, flexibility, and certainty while management and owners contribute only rhetoric.

If the company wants a serious labour contribution, then I expect:
- management restraint,
- overhead reduction,
- owner-side financial support,
- product and investment commitments,
- and binding protection for viable sites.

That is not radical. That is fairness.

## What could move us
I remain open to a **negotiated labour contribution** if — and only if — it is matched by real counter-contributions and does not break the collective wage framework.

Possible components:
- temporary moderation in wage growth,
- a time-limited contribution to a restructuring fund,
- flexible working-time arrangements,
- voluntary reduction instruments,
- internal capacity adjustments,
- and use of non-wage measures before any discussion of headcount reduction.

But I repeat: this is only conceivable in a package with:
1. **no compulsory redundancies**
2. **no plant closures**
3. **binding site and employment security**
4. **management and owner contributions**
5. **investment and product allocation for the German sites**

Without those, any “contribution” becomes one-sided pain.

## To management
You keep saying alternatives must be “economically equivalent.” Fine. Then show us the numbers.

Bring a fully quantified comparison that sets out:
- the cost gap,
- the savings from management and overhead cuts,
- the savings from capital-side contributions,
- the savings from working-time and capacity measures,
- the savings from voluntary departures,
- and what remains after those measures.

If you want us to consider any tougher step, you need to prove that every non-destructive route has been exhausted. So far, I do not see that proof.

## To the Works Council
We are aligned on the red lines:
- no compulsory redundancies,
- no plant closures,
- real job and site security.

I support that line. What I will not support is a process that quietly turns “temporary” concessions into permanent erosion of wages and protections. If labour contributes, it must be limited, fair, and reversible.

## To the state
Your position is important, and your blocking role matters. But I want to be blunt: **the state should not settle for a pause before the same conflict returns**.

If you want stability, then back a settlement that actually secures:
- jobs,
- sites,
- investment,
- and the collective bargaining framework.

A phased deal with hidden closure triggers does not create stability. It creates delay.

## To the family holding and investors
You are right about one thing: a cosmetic compromise would be bad for everyone.  
But you are wrong if you think “credibility” requires keeping closures and forced layoffs available as threats.

Credibility comes from a package that is:
- economically sound,
- socially viable,
- implementable,
- and durable.

A plan that detonates labour relations and then hopes to win trust later is not credible. It is reckless.

## Bottom line
Our position remains clear:

- **No compulsory redundancies**
- **No plant closures**
- **A binding employment and site framework**
- **A fair, limited employee contribution only in exchange for management and owner contributions**
- **Investment and product commitments for the German sites**
- **Protection of the collective wage agreement**

If management wants a settlement, bring a real burden-sharing package with quantified savings and binding commitments.  
If not, then the union will continue to mobilise. We are not here to ratify a restructuring that makes employees carry all the pain.
