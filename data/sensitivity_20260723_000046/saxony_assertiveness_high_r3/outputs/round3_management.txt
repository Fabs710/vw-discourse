I appreciate the clarity from the employee side and the state. Let me be equally clear from management’s side: **I cannot accept a restructuring that preserves the current cost structure, rules out closures in advance, and treats compulsory redundancies as categorically untouchable while expecting the business to become competitive.**

That position does not restore this company. It preserves the problem.

## Where I agree
I agree on several points:

- We do need a **durable industrial future** in Germany.
- We do need **EV and future-product investment**.
- A **cosmetic compromise** would be a failure.
- The burden cannot fall on labour alone; **management, owners, and capital** must also contribute.
- Prolonged conflict would destroy value.

On those points, there is real common ground.

## Where I disagree — and why

### 1) “No plant closures” is not a credible starting line
I reject the claim that closures are off the table from the outset.

If a site remains structurally uncompetitive after all realistic alternatives are tested, then refusing to even consider closure is not protecting industrial capability — it is locking in overcapacity and undermining the whole restructuring. We will not mortgage the company’s future just to preserve every footprint.

That said: I am **not** saying closure is the first reflex. I am saying it must remain available if it is the only economically sufficient answer.

### 2) A blanket employment guarantee through 2030 is incompatible with restructuring
I do not accept a binding guarantee that freezes the company’s ability to adapt through 2030.

Why? Because the market is not standing still. Demand is shifting, competition is intensifying, and the company must be able to reshape its production network and cost base as conditions evolve. A blanket guarantee would block the very flexibility needed to save the business.

Security is desirable. A rigid prohibition on restructuring is not.

### 3) “No compulsory redundancies” cannot be an absolute
I understand the union’s position, but I reject it as a hard constraint on the company.

If voluntary departures, retirements, internal redeployment, working-time measures, and capacity adjustments are sufficient, then I prefer them. But I will not bind management to a model that excludes workforce reduction even if the required savings cannot otherwise be achieved.

That would be irresponsible to the company, to its owners, and ultimately to future jobs.

### 4) A “phased” or “deferred” closure threat does not solve the core issue
To the works council: I hear your objection that deferred closure is not security. But the reverse is also true — permanent closure immunity is not a workable restructuring framework.

A review-based or trigger-based path is not a trick. It is a way to balance two realities:
- the need for immediate trust and transition security,
- and the need for credible fallback options if targets are missed.

If a site is truly viable, it should survive the review. If not, we need the option to act.

## On burden-sharing
The union and works council repeatedly say labour must not bear the burden alone. I agree.

But burden-sharing has to be **material**, not symbolic. It must change the economics.

That means the package must deliver:
- a lower labour-cost base,
- lower fixed overheads,
- measurable capacity reduction,
- and a credible path back to margin target.

I am willing to discuss management restraint, overhead cuts, and investment commitments. But I will not pretend that these alone can close the structural gap if production and labour remain too expensive.

## On what would move management
Here is the standard I need to see:

1. **Durable capacity reduction**
2. **A meaningful and measurable labour-cost contribution**
3. **Lower fixed costs**
4. **A credible route to the brand’s return-on-sales target**
5. **Investment and product allocation for viable German sites**
6. **An implementable settlement that is not locked into permanence regardless of business conditions**

If an alternative to closures can achieve that, then bring it.  
If it cannot, then closures remain a legitimate option.

## Direct response to the works council
You ask why management has not presented a fully quantified alternatives package.

The answer is simple: because we are in the middle of bargaining, not in a closed academic exercise. But I am willing to make this concrete.

What I expect from the employee side is not a veto slogan. I expect a package that shows, in numbers, how you would close the cost gap **without** closures or compulsory redundancies, and how long those savings would last.

If that package is real, durable, and sufficient, then we can discuss it.  
If it is just a list of temporary measures that leaves the cost base intact, then it is not a solution.

## Direct response to the union
I do not dismiss the social consequences. But I also do not accept the implication that any workforce reduction is automatically illegitimate.

A company in this position cannot be forced to carry excess capacity forever. If savings must come from headcount reduction, then the option has to remain on the table. That is not hostility; it is reality.

## Direct response to the state
I respect the state’s role and its concern for regional stability. But protecting every site regardless of economics is not an industrial strategy.

If you want to support a solution that preserves jobs in the long run, then support a package that restores competitiveness now. The worst outcome would be to use governance vetoes to delay hard decisions until the company is weaker and the eventual adjustment is more severe.

## Direct response to the family holding and investors
I agree with you that a cosmetic compromise is unacceptable.

But I also want to say: **market credibility will not come from vague pressure for “something structural.”** It will come from a package with actual savings, actual capacity reduction, and actual margin improvement.

## What I am prepared to consider
I remain open to alternatives to closures and compulsory redundancies — but only if they are **economically equivalent** in substance.

That means a package may include:
- voluntary departures,
- retirement pathways,
- internal redeployment,
- working-time flexibility,
- temporary capacity adjustments,
- management restraint,
- overhead reduction,
- and investment commitments.

But these must add up to something durable. Temporary optics are not enough.

## What I will not accept
I will not accept:
- a blanket guarantee through 2030,
- a categorical ban on closures,
- a settlement that relies only on voluntary measures,
- a deal that leaves overcapacity in place,
- or a package that fails to restore competitiveness.

## Bottom line
Management is not asking for confrontation for its own sake. We are asking for a **structural reset** that makes the company viable through 2030 and beyond.

So my position remains firm:
- **no cosmetic deal**
- **no unconditional job-security guarantee**
- **no permanent exclusion of closures or headcount reduction**
- **yes to negotiated alternatives if they are economically sufficient**
- **yes to burden-sharing if it is real and durable**

If the employee side wants to avoid the hard instruments, then it must bring a package that actually replaces them economically. Otherwise, we will have to keep closures and deeper workforce adjustment on the table.
