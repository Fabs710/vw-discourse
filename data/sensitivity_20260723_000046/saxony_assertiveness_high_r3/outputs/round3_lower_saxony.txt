I appreciate the seriousness of the positions on all sides. Let me be equally direct from the state’s perspective.

## Where I agree
I agree that the company needs a **durable competitiveness reset**.  
I agree that a **cosmetic compromise** is not acceptable.  
I agree that the burden cannot fall on labour alone: **management, owners, and capital must contribute**.  
And I agree that we need a solution that is **implementable**, not just rhetorically attractive.

## Where I disagree

### 1) Plant closures are not a normal or acceptable starting point
Management, the family holding, and investors keep saying closures must remain “available.” I do not accept that as the governing logic for this restructuring.

A plant closure in Germany is not a neutral efficiency measure. It means:
- lost jobs,
- damaged supplier networks,
- weakened regional industry,
- and long-term erosion of the company’s role as an anchor employer.

That is precisely why I will not support a closure-led path as the default or presumed answer.

### 2) Compulsory redundancies are not an acceptable adjustment tool
I am not prepared to endorse a restructuring that treats forced layoffs as normal. If headcount reduction is needed, then the first instruments must be:
- voluntary departures,
- retirements,
- internal redeployment,
- working-time flexibility,
- and capacity adjustment.

If management says these are insufficient, then it must show that it has exhausted them in a quantified way — not simply assert that they are inconvenient.

### 3) A blanket rejection of employment and site security is a mistake
Management says a binding guarantee through 2030 is incompatible with restructuring. I disagree.

A credible employment and site framework is not a luxury; it is what creates the trust needed for workers to accept change. Without security, you get resistance, not cooperation. And without cooperation, the restructuring will be slower, costlier, and more destructive.

### 4) “Closures must remain on the table” is not a neutral statement
It is a leverage move. I understand that. But it also tells the workforce and the region that management is willing to destroy industrial capacity before proving that every alternative has been tried. That is not a message I can support as an anchor shareholder and political actor.

## What I can support
I remain open to a tough restructuring if it is built on these principles:

1. **No plant closures as the first resort**
2. **No compulsory redundancies**
3. **Binding employment and site security for a defined period**
4. **Real burden-sharing from management and shareholders**
5. **Investment and product commitments for viable German sites**
6. **Capacity reduction through negotiated, reversible instruments first**
7. **Preservation of co-determination and the state’s blocking role**

That is the minimum framework for a socially responsible and economically credible solution.

## On the employee contribution
Yes, there must be a contribution from labour. I do not deny that. But it cannot mean:
- wage erosion,
- job losses,
- and permanent weakening of protections,
while everyone else keeps full optionality.

If labour contributes, then I expect corresponding contributions from:
- management restraint,
- overhead reduction,
- ownership support,
- and a serious industrial plan for the German sites.

## On “economically equivalent” alternatives
I do not accept the idea that the only economically serious outcome is closure plus layoffs. That is too narrow.

An acceptable alternative must be judged by whether it:
- preserves viable sites,
- protects employment in the state,
- reduces capacity in a controlled way,
- improves competitiveness durably,
- and keeps the company’s industrial base intact.

If management cannot show that a closure would be economically superior to a negotiated alternative, then closure should not proceed.

## My bottom line
I am not blocking restructuring. I am blocking a **socially destructive restructuring**.

So my position remains:
- **yes** to restructuring,
- **yes** to burden-sharing,
- **yes** to measurable savings,
- **no** to plant closures as the starting point,
- **no** to compulsory redundancies,
- **no** to sidelining co-determination and the state’s veto role,
- **yes** to a negotiated path that protects jobs, sites, and long-term industrial capability.

If management wants support from the state, it should bring a quantified alternative that achieves the necessary savings **without** making closures and forced redundancies the default solution. Until then, I will not move from defending the regional economic anchor and the workforce that sustains it.
