I will be very clear from the state shareholder side.

I agree with everyone on one point: **this company needs a real restructuring, not a cosmetic fix**. The expert confirms that. I also agree that **management, owners, and capital must contribute**, and that a prolonged conflict would be damaging.

But I do **not** accept the conclusion that the only credible route is to keep **plant closures and compulsory redundancies** live as normal fallback tools.

## Where I agree with management, owners, and investors
- The German cost base must come down.
- The company needs a durable path back to competitiveness.
- Any deal must be economically credible, not symbolic.
- Employee contributions can be part of the solution.

That is all true.

## Where I sharply disagree
### 1) Closures are not an acceptable starting logic
Management keeps saying closures must remain “available.” The family holding and investors echo that. I understand the bargaining logic, but I reject it as the governing principle.

For a regional anchor employer like this, plant closure is not just a line item. It means:
- jobs lost in the state,
- supplier damage,
- erosion of industrial capability,
- and lasting political and social harm.

If a site is truly viable, it should be protected. If it is not, then management must prove that with a **fully quantified alternatives package**, not simply preserve closure as a threat.

### 2) Compulsory redundancies are not an acceptable normal tool
I do not accept forced layoffs as the default backstop. If headcount reduction is needed, then the first instruments must be:
- voluntary departures,
- retirement pathways,
- internal redeployment,
- working-time flexibility,
- temporary capacity reduction,
- and management restraint.

The expert explicitly said a negotiated package can be plausible if the gap is moderate and if there is room for these measures. That is the route I want tested properly.

### 3) A blanket refusal to secure jobs and sites is the wrong message
Management says a binding framework through 2030 is too rigid. I disagree with that framing.

A state shareholder cannot support a restructuring that leaves the workforce and the region under permanent threat. If workers are asked to contribute, they need **real security**, not a timed pause before the same conflict returns.

### 4) Governance rights are not a nuisance
The state’s blocking minority and the co-determination structure are not obstacles to be worked around. They are part of the legitimate governance architecture of this company. I will not accept a solution that sidelines those rights in the name of “flexibility.”

## What I take from the expert
The expert was helpful in one important respect: a negotiated non-destructive package is **not impossible**.

That matters. It means we are not forced into a closure-first mindset from the outset.

The expert’s own assessment suggests:
- if the cost gap is **moderate**, a negotiated package may work;
- if the gap is large and fixed-cost driven, closure becomes more attractive economically.

But that is exactly why management must now show the numbers. Right now, we still do not have a convincing, quantified comparison of:
- management and overhead cuts,
- owner-side contributions,
- labor-side flexibility,
- working-time and capacity measures,
- and the residual gap.

Until that exists, I will not accept closures as “necessary.”

## What I could support
I remain open to a tough restructuring if it contains:
1. **No plant closures as the first resort**
2. **No compulsory redundancies as the first instrument**
3. **A binding employment and site framework for viable German sites**
4. **Real burden-sharing from management and shareholders**
5. **Investment and product commitments for the German plants**
6. **Capacity reduction through negotiated, reversible instruments**
7. **Preservation of co-determination and the state’s veto role**

That is not softness. That is a serious industrial policy position.

## On the employee side
I agree with the works council and the union that employees must not carry the burden alone. They are right to insist on:
- management restraint,
- owner contributions,
- and a fair distribution of pain.

But I also want to be precise: if labour contributes, that contribution must be **strictly limited, temporary, and matched**. I will not support permanent erosion of protections in exchange for vague promises.

## My challenge to management
You say the expert supports you. It only supports you if you can prove that the gap is so large that non-destructive alternatives are insufficient.

So I am asking for the same thing the works council and union have asked for:
- a quantified savings gap,
- a quantified alternatives package,
- a clear breakdown of management, owner, and labor contributions,
- and a demonstration of why closures or forced layoffs are unavoidable.

Until then, your call for “flexibility” is too open-ended to justify social destruction.

## My challenge to the family holding and investors
You are right that a cosmetic compromise would destroy value. But do not confuse **real restructuring** with **closure-led restructuring**.

If a negotiated package can deliver the needed savings and capacity adjustment, then that is the better long-term outcome:
- less conflict,
- less regional damage,
- more legitimacy,
- and a more stable industrial base.

That is not sentimental. It is good governance.

## Bottom line
My position remains firm:

- **Yes** to restructuring
- **Yes** to burden-sharing
- **Yes** to measurable cost improvement
- **No** to plant closures as the starting point
- **No** to compulsory redundancies as a normal solution
- **No** to sidelining the state’s co-determination and veto role
- **Yes** to a negotiated package that protects viable sites and the regional industrial anchor

If management wants my support, bring a **fully quantified, non-destructive alternatives package** showing how the company reaches competitiveness **without** making closures and forced layoffs the default answer.

Until then, I will defend jobs, sites, and the company’s role in the region.
