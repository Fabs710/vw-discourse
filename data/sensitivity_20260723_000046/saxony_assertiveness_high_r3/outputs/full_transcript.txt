[Works Council (Betriebsrat) (Plant-level employee representation under co-determination) — Round 1]

- **No plant closures in Germany**
- **No compulsory redundancies / forced layoffs**
- **No dismantling of co-determination and no breaking of the historic job-security framework**

We recognise that the company faces serious competitive pressure and that restructuring may be necessary. But we will not accept a solution that shifts the burden onto the workforce by destroying jobs, closing sites, or turning a structural management problem into a social one.

What we will argue for instead is:

1. **A binding site and employment guarantee through 2030**
2. **Alternative savings measures that protect jobs**, such as:
   - capacity adjustments
   - shorter working time / flex measures
   - voluntary instruments only
   - negotiated contribution from management and capital, not one-sided cuts to labour
3. **A genuine industrial strategy for the German sites**
   - clear future product allocation
   - investment commitments for EV and core technologies
   - real participation of employee representatives in shaping the restructuring

If management wants cooperation, it must come with a credible plan that preserves the future of the German plants and the workforce. If it comes instead with closures, forced layoffs, or unilateral fait accompli decisions, then we will resist with all available means, including industrial action and use of our governance rights.

We are ready to negotiate a solution that secures the company’s future — but not one built on sacrificing workers and shutting plants.

[Expert: Independent restructuring/industrial-fin — Round 4]
From a restructuring and industrial-finance perspective, the key question is not whether a compromise is desirable, but whether a **non-destructive package can credibly close the required cost gap** versus a closure-led restructuring.

## Bottom line
A negotiated package **can** sometimes substitute for closures, but only if:
1. the savings gap is **modest to medium** relative to the company’s German cost base,
2. there is substantial scope for **working-time, wage, capacity, and overhead measures**,
3. management is prepared to contribute through **investment prioritization and overhead reduction**, and
4. the package is **durable and enforceable** enough to convince markets and the board.

If the required adjustment is large and structural — meaning a need for **permanent fixed-cost removal**, not just a one-off cash save — then a no-closure/no-layoff package becomes much harder to make economically equivalent. In that case, non-destructive measures may reduce pain, but they usually do **not** fully replicate the economics of plant closure.

---

## 1) What a closure-led restructuring usually buys you
A plant closure or equivalent hard capacity exit typically delivers savings in four buckets:

- **Direct labor cost elimination**
- **Overhead and plant fixed-cost removal**
- **Depreciation / maintenance / utilities / logistics simplification**
- **Capacity discipline**, which can lift utilization at remaining sites

The largest and most durable benefit is usually the **structural reduction in fixed cost**. That matters because in automotive manufacturing, fixed costs remain heavy even when output falls.

A rough rule of thumb:
- If a site has 3,000–5,000 workers, a closure can remove a very large share of annual cash operating cost.
- The actual EBITDA effect depends on how much production is truly removable and whether that production can be absorbed elsewhere without major inefficiency.

Closure also tends to create **one-time charges**:
- severance / social plan costs
- asset write-downs
- decommissioning and remediation
- transfer/disruption costs

So closure is rarely “cheap” in year 1, but it can be very effective over years 2–5.

---

## 2) What a non-destructive package can realistically achieve
Non-destructive measures usually include:

- voluntary departures
- retirements
- hiring freezes
- working-time reductions or flexibility
- temporary wage moderation
- internal redeployment
- plant-specific output reallocation
- management overhead cuts
- supplier and procurement savings
- investment reprioritization

These can be effective, but they differ in durability.

### Typical economic characteristics
- **Voluntary departures / retirements:** can reduce headcount meaningfully, but often at a slower pace and with leakage in key skills.
- **Working-time reduction / flexibility:** useful for short- to medium-term cost relief; less permanent than a closure.
- **Wage moderation:** directly improves unit labor cost, but usually has a bounded duration and political/industrial limits.
- **Overhead cuts:** often the easiest “clean” savings, but the absolute amount is usually not enough on its own.
- **Capacity reduction without closure:** can save variable cost and some shift-related fixed cost, but if the plant remains open, much overhead remains.

### Practical limit
In large manufacturing restructurings, a negotiated non-closure package often reaches only part of the needed gap unless it includes:
- deep headcount reduction,
- meaningful compensation concessions,
- and a substantial reduction in underutilized capacity.

## 4) Economic equivalence to closures: what would be required
For non-destructive alternatives to be economically equivalent to a closure-led path, they must match three things:

### A. Annual cash savings
Not just in year 1, but on a normalized basis.

### B. Fixed-cost reduction durability
The savings must persist after temporary agreements expire.

### C. Capacity discipline
If excess capacity remains, the company may not get the margin recovery it needs, even if labor cost falls.

A negotiated package can be close to equivalent if it includes:
- long-duration labor-cost relief,
- substantial voluntary headcount reduction,
- explicit elimination of underutilized shifts or lines,
- and credible product allocation to higher-utilization sites.

Without that, it is usually not equivalent; it is only partial relief.

### Closure-led package

Cons:

[Expert: Independent restructuring/industrial-fin — Round 4]
## Expert assessment: can a non-closure package credibly close the gap?

### Short answer
**Possibly, but only under a fairly demanding set of conditions.**  
A non-closure package can replace plant closures **if** the required adjustment is mainly a **moderate fixed-cost and labor-cost reset** rather than a **deep structural capacity exit**. If the company truly needs a large, permanent removal of fixed cost, then a no-closure package is **unlikely to be economically equivalent** unless it is unusually large, durable, and enforceable.

In restructuring terms: **non-closure can work as a bridge or partial substitute; it is harder to make it a full substitute for closure-led rationalization.**

---

## What the closure-led package usually accomplishes
A closure-led restructuring normally delivers four things:

1. **Headcount removal**
2. **Fixed-cost elimination**
3. **Capacity reduction and utilization improvement at remaining sites**
4. **A clean reset of the cost base**

That is why management favors it when the issue is not just wages, but **structural overcapacity**.

The financial advantage is not only labor savings. It is also:
- site overhead removal
- plant maintenance and utilities reduction
- logistics simplification
- management span reduction
- and better utilization of the remaining footprint

The trade-off is that closure produces:
- severance and social-plan cost
- asset write-downs
- decommissioning costs
- transition risk
- execution and industrial-relations risk

So closure is expensive upfront, but powerful over time.

---

## What a non-closure package can realistically deliver
A no-closure package typically uses a mix of:

- voluntary departures
- retirements
- internal redeployment
- working-time reduction
- wage moderation or temporary concessions
- management overhead cuts
- procurement and indirect-cost reduction
- output reallocation across plants
- capacity trimming without outright closure

These measures are real and can be substantial. But they are not equally durable.

### Typical strengths
- **Voluntary exits and retirements** can reduce headcount materially.
- **Working-time flexibility** can reduce cost quickly.
- **Overhead cuts** are often achievable without industrial shutdown.
- **Product reallocation** can improve utilization without closure.

### Typical weaknesses
- They often **preserve most site fixed cost**.
- They can be **time-limited** or reversible.
- They may not remove enough capacity if the network is genuinely oversized.
- They can leave the company with **too much overhead per unit of output**.

That is the core issue: a company can lower labor expense without fully solving a capacity problem.

---

## Quantified view: what scale of savings is plausible?
I would frame it as a savings-gap question.

### Illustrative annual savings ranges
These are not company-specific verified figures; they are practical restructuring ranges:

- **Voluntary departures / retirements:**  
  roughly **€100m–€250m per year**
- **Wage moderation / temporary labor concessions:**  
  roughly **€50m–€150m per year**
- **Working-time flexibility / capacity adjustment:**  
  roughly **€25m–€100m per year**
- **Management and overhead cuts:**  
  roughly **€25m–€100m per year**
- **Procurement / indirect savings / footprint optimization:**  
  roughly **€50m–€150m per year**

### Combined plausible range
A serious negotiated package might therefore generate roughly:

**€250m–€750m per year**

That is a meaningful amount. It may be enough if the company’s required gap is moderate.  
But if the company needs to remove **very large fixed costs**, this may still fall short of a closure-led reset.

---

## The decisive test: how large is the required gap?
The answer depends on the size of the restructuring need.

### If the annual gap is around €300m
A negotiated package is **plausible**.  
With voluntary exits, labor moderation, and overhead cuts, a no-closure deal can likely do the job.

### If the gap is around €500m–€700m
It becomes **borderline**.  
It may still work, but only if the package is deep, durable, and paired with real capacity rationalization.

### If the gap is €1bn or more
A non-closure package becomes **much harder to justify as economically equivalent**.  
At that point, you usually need one or more of:
- plant closure
- major product transfer
- significant network consolidation
- or a very large, durable labor reset

That is where the debate tends to move from “can we save money?” to “can we actually reset the fixed-cost base?”

## What would make a non-closure package credible
For non-closure to be credible, it needs to satisfy four conditions:

### 1) Durability
Savings must persist beyond a short agreement horizon.  
If the concessions expire in two or three years, markets will treat them as temporary relief, not a structural reset.

### 2) Scale
The package must close most of the required gap, not just part of it.

### 3) Capacity discipline
The company must genuinely reduce underutilized output, not just lower wages while leaving excess capacity in place.

### 4) Enforceability
There must be a mechanism that makes the savings real:
- measurable targets
- clear implementation milestones
- review gates
- and consequences if targets are missed

Without that, it is more a political settlement than a restructuring solution.

## My judgment on the proposals in the room
### Works Council / union position
Their position is socially coherent, but **economically incomplete unless they can show the numbers**.

A no-closure/no-forced-layoff framework can work only if the alternatives package is large enough to replace the economics of closure. That has not yet been demonstrated.

### Management / investor position
Their position is economically plausible because it recognizes the need for **fixed-cost removal**.  
However, they still need to prove that closures are necessary rather than merely preferable. In restructuring terms, “necessary” means the alternatives package fails the quantified gap test.

### State / family holding
Their positions are understandable as governance and regional-anchor actors, but they cannot replace industrial math.  
If they want a non-closure outcome, they need to back a package that is financially equivalent, not just politically preferable.

## Practical conclusion
### A non-closure package is credible if:
- the gap is moderate,
- voluntary and negotiated measures are deep enough,
- labor-cost contribution is material but time-limited,
- management and owners also contribute,
- and capacity is truly reduced enough to improve utilization.

### It is not likely credible if:
- the company needs a large permanent fixed-cost exit,
- underutilization is severe,
- savings are mostly temporary,
- or the package leaves excess capacity structurally intact.

### My overall assessment
**A hybrid negotiated package is the most realistic path if the company wants to avoid immediate closures.**  
But a pure “no closures, no compulsory redundancies, full job guarantee through 2030” package is **unlikely to be economically equivalent** unless the savings target is modest relative to the group’s German cost base.

## Core impasse

The plateau is centered on **one unresolved question**:

> Is the restructuring gap large enough that closures and compulsory redundancies must remain available, or can it be closed through a fully negotiated package without those instruments?

That splits the room into two camps:

- **Works Council / Union / State**: want a binding no-closure, no-forced-layoff outcome with security through 2030.
- **Management / Family Holding / Investors**: want closures and workforce reduction to remain available if needed to achieve the required cost and capacity reset.

The expert’s analysis confirms the technical source of the impasse:
- a **non-destructive package may be plausible** if the gap is moderate,
- but if the gap is **large and fixed-cost driven**, then a no-closure package may not be economically equivalent.

So the deadlock is not about whether restructuring is needed. It is about **which instruments are allowed to remain on the table** and **what evidence is sufficient to rule them in or out**.

## Facilitation proposals to bridge the conflict

### Proposal 1: Quantified “equivalence test” with a non-closure first package
A structured compromise could be:

- all parties accept a **first-stage negotiated package** built on:
  - voluntary departures,
  - retirements,
  - internal redeployment,
  - working-time flexibility,
  - temporary wage moderation,
  - management overhead cuts,
  - owner-side financial support,
  - and product/investment commitments for German sites.
- management commits to a **full quantified alternatives package** and an agreed savings target.
- closure and compulsory redundancies are **not removed forever**, but are **deferred unless the quantified package fails to close the gap**.

This gives employee side a serious non-destructive route first, while giving management a formal mechanism to preserve structural options if the numbers do not work.

---

### Proposal 2: Binding “no closure for viable sites” plus review triggers for non-viable sites only
A second bridge could separate the plants into categories:

- **Viable sites**: receive binding site and employment guarantees.
- **Non-viable or borderline sites**: remain subject to a jointly audited review process.

Elements:
- no plant closure for sites that pass an agreed viability test,
- no compulsory redundancies at viable sites,
- if a site fails the viability test, management must present a quantified rescue plan before any closure pathway is activated,
- all reviews are jointly supervised with co-determination and state participation.

This addresses the works council’s concern about indiscriminate closure threats, while leaving management room to address genuinely weak sites.

---

### Proposal 3: A time-bound burden-sharing pact with automatic escalation
A third compromise is a staged settlement:

- **Phase 1**: 12–24 months of negotiated labor contribution, management cuts, and owner support.
- **Phase 2**: automatic review against defined financial and operational triggers.
- If targets are missed, the parties reopen talks on deeper measures.
- If targets are met, the stronger job and site framework continues.

This can bridge distrust by making concessions **temporary but conditional**, rather than permanently open-ended or permanently rigid.

## 1. Decision context
The company faces a genuine restructuring crisis: shrinking European demand, high German cost levels, intensifying competition, and missed profitability targets. The practical decision is therefore not “whether to restructure,” but whether the company can secure a cost and capacity reset through negotiated measures that avoid closures and forced redundancies, or whether closure-led restructuring remains necessary.

## 2. Stakeholder assembly
Weighted by standing and decision power, the key actors are:

- **Group & Brand Management**: initiator of the restructuring and operational diagnosis.
- **Controlling Family Holding**: highly powerful governance actor; prioritizes long-term value and structural competitiveness.
- **Regional State Government**: strong legitimacy and blocking capacity; prioritizes regional employment and industrial base.
- **Works Council**: very high legitimacy and formal veto power through co-determination; defends site and job security.
- **Industrial Union**: high bargaining power over labor peace and strikes; can mobilize industrial action.
- **Institutional Investors**: financially relevant but weaker governance standing; support value-preserving restructuring.

## 3. Final positions

### Group & Brand Management
Management’s position is that the German footprint has structural overcapacity and an uncompetitive cost base, so durable capacity and cost reduction is required. It is open to alternatives only if they are economically equivalent to closure-led restructuring; otherwise plant closures and workforce reduction must remain available.

### Controlling Family Holding
The controlling shareholder agrees the problem is structural and insists that flexibility must remain on the table. It is open to negotiated alternatives, but rejects categorical bans on closures, compulsory redundancies, or binding guarantees that would freeze restructuring through 2030.

### Regional State Government
The state wants a real restructuring but insists it should not be built around closures or forced layoffs as the default logic. It supports binding site and employment protection for viable plants, provided management can produce a quantified alternatives package showing that closures are not necessary.

### Works Council
The works council takes the strongest protective line: no plant closures, no compulsory redundancies, and binding job and site guarantees through 2030. It accepts that the company needs restructuring and contributions from management, owners, and capital, but rejects any staged or deferred closure logic as merely postponing the threat.

### Industrial Union
The union shares the works council’s red lines: no closures and no compulsory redundancies. It is prepared to discuss temporary labor-side contributions, but only if matched by management and owner contributions and only within a framework that preserves collective bargaining and job security.

### Institutional Investors
Investors support restructuring, cost reduction, and stronger competitiveness, and they reject cosmetic compromise. They are open to negotiated alternatives, but only if the package is economically sufficient and does not leave the company with a structurally uncompetitive cost base.

## 4. Genuine conflicts (structurally incompatible positions)
1. **Closure as fallback vs. closure as taboo**  
   Management, the controlling family, and investors want closures to remain available; the works council and union reject closures as an acceptable fallback, and the state wants them de-emphasized.

2. **Compulsory redundancies possible vs. categorically excluded**  
   Management, the family, and investors see forced reductions as a necessary backstop if savings fall short; labor and the state refuse that logic.

3. **Binding security through 2030 vs. restructuring flexibility**  
   Employee and state actors want durable guarantees; management and owners argue this would prevent the very adjustment the company needs.

4. **What counts as an adequate alternative**  
   Labor demands a fully quantified alternatives package before any destructive measures are contemplated; management and investors argue alternatives must be economically equivalent and durable, not merely preferable.

## 5. Areas of convergence
- Everyone agrees the company needs restructuring, not a cosmetic fix.
- All sides accept the need for EV/future-tech investment and viable product allocation.
- Everyone rejects a prolonged industrial conflict.
- There is shared openness to voluntary departures, redeployment, working-time flexibility, management overhead cuts, and some form of owner/shareholder contribution.
- The expert intervention created cross-cutting agreement that a negotiated package can work only if the savings gap is moderate and the measures are durable.

## 6. Key trade-offs for the decision-maker
- **Security vs. flexibility**: binding site and job guarantees build trust but can constrain restructuring.
- **Social peace vs. economic sufficiency**: avoiding closures reduces conflict, but only if the resulting package actually closes the gap.
- **Short-term legitimacy vs. long-term competitiveness**: preserving jobs and sites now may preserve legitimacy, but failing to reset fixed costs can weaken the company later.
- **Burden-sharing vs. implementation certainty**: a fair package requires contributions from labor, management, and owners, but more actors and conditions make implementation harder.
- **Regional stability vs. footprint rationalization**: the state’s industrial-policy goals favor protection, but the company may need footprint consolidation to restore margins.

## 7. Integrative pathways
1. **Quantified non-closure first package**
   - Start with voluntary exits, retirement pathways, redeployment, working-time flexibility, wage moderation, management cuts, and owner-side support.
   - Require a fully quantified savings target and independent verification.
   - Keep closures and forced redundancies as formally deferred options only if the quantified package fails.

2. **Viability-based site differentiation**
   - Distinguish viable sites from borderline or non-viable sites using agreed criteria.
   - Grant binding protection to viable sites; subject weaker sites to an audited rescue/review process.
   - This preserves the employee-side red lines where economically defensible while not pretending all sites are equally viable.

3. **Time-bound burden-sharing pact**
   - Negotiate a temporary labor contribution matched by management and owner contributions.
   - Pair it with investment commitments, product allocation, and a joint implementation body.
   - Add automatic review triggers tied to utilization, margins, and cost-base progress.
