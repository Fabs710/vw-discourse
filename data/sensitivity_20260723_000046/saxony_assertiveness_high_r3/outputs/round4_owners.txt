I’ll be direct.

## Where I agree
I agree with the management team, the investors, and the expert on one core point: **this is a structural competitiveness problem, not a cosmetic one**. The German cost base is too high for the market reality, and if we fail to fix that, we destroy value over time.

I also agree with the employee side and the state on another point: **a prolonged open conflict would be deeply value-destructive**. It would hit earnings, execution, reputation, and future investment capacity. That is not acceptable from an ownership perspective.

And yes, burden-sharing cannot sit on labour alone. Management restraint, overhead reduction, and owner responsibility have to be part of the package.

## Where I disagree
### 1) “No closures” cannot be an absolute
I do **not** accept the claim that German plant closures are categorically off the table.

If a site is structurally uncompetitive and alternatives do not close the gap, then refusing to consider closure is not stewardship. It is denial. As controlling shareholder, I cannot support preserving an uncompetitive footprint indefinitely just because it is historically sensitive or politically difficult.

That said, closure is **not** my preferred outcome. It is a last-resort instrument, not the starting point.

### 2) A blanket guarantee through 2030 is too rigid
I understand why employees want security. But a binding guarantee that freezes the company’s ability to adapt through 2030 would be incompatible with the restructuring challenge we are discussing.

Security that blocks necessary change does not protect the enterprise; it delays the adjustment and risks a worse outcome later. I cannot support a governance framework that removes flexibility entirely.

### 3) “No compulsory redundancies” as an absolute is also too rigid
I prefer voluntary departures, retirement pathways, redeployment, and working-time flexibility. But I will not accept a framework that rules out headcount reduction entirely if the required savings cannot otherwise be achieved.

If the numbers can be reached without forced reductions, good. If not, then the option must remain available. Otherwise we are merely preserving overcapacity and handing the problem to the next year.

### 4) Co-determination is a right, not a veto against economics
I respect the state’s role and the special governance structure. But those rights cannot be used to entrench an uncompetitive structure forever.

If governance prevents any hard decision, then governance becomes part of the problem rather than the solution.

## What the expert’s analysis means
The expert’s point is important: a negotiated non-destructive package **can** work if the cost gap is moderate and if the measures are deep enough and durable enough.

That is the test. Not slogans. Not moral preferences. **Economic sufficiency**.

The expert also made clear that if the gap is large and fixed-cost driven, then a pure no-closure/no-layoff package becomes much harder to justify economically. That supports my view that closures and workforce adjustment must remain available if needed.

## What would move me
I remain open to alternatives to immediate closures and immediate compulsory redundancies — but only if they are economically equivalent in substance.

That means any acceptable package must deliver:
- durable capacity reduction,
- a material and lasting cost reduction,
- a credible path back to margin improvement,
- clear investment and product allocation for viable sites,
- implementation certainty,
- and no prolonged conflict.

I am prepared to consider:
- voluntary departures,
- retirement pathways,
- internal redeployment,
- working-time flexibility,
- temporary capacity reductions,
- management overhead cuts,
- and targeted investment commitments.

But I will **not** support:
- a blanket ban on closures,
- a categorical ban on workforce reduction,
- a binding guarantee that blocks restructuring through 2030,
- or a package that leaves the cost base structurally uncompetitive.

## To management
You need to do more than say restructuring is necessary. You need to show the **quantified path**.

I still want a clear breakdown of:
- the cost gap,
- management and overhead savings,
- owner-side contributions,
- labour-side measures,
- capacity reductions,
- and the residual gap after all non-destructive measures.

If you cannot show that the alternatives are sufficient, then the market will not believe the compromise and the owners should not accept it.

## To the works council and union
I respect your red lines, but I do not accept them as governance absolutes.

If you want to avoid closures and forced redundancies, then bring a package that closes the gap in numbers. A principled refusal is not enough. It has to be operationally credible and durable.

I do not want conflict. But I also will not support a settlement that protects every existing structure while leaving the business uncompetitive.

## To the state
I hear your concern for regional stability. I share it. But stability that comes from postponing hard decisions is temporary and can become more damaging later.

If you want to preserve industrial capacity, support a package that genuinely restores competitiveness. That is the best protection for the region in the long run.

## To investors
You are right that a cosmetic compromise would be unacceptable. But market credibility will not come from vague pressure for “something structural.” It will come from real savings, real capacity discipline, and real implementation certainty.

## Bottom line
My position remains firm:

- **Yes** to tough restructuring
- **Yes** to negotiated alternatives if they are economically sufficient
- **Yes** to burden-sharing from management, labour, and owners
- **No** to a cosmetic deal
- **No** to a package that preserves the current cost problem
- **No** to permanently excluding closures or headcount reduction as tools

If the employee side wants to avoid the hard instruments, it must bring an economically equivalent solution. If it cannot, then the hard instruments remain on the table. That is the only responsible position for a controlling shareholder safeguarding long-term enterprise value.
