## 1. Decision context
The company faces a genuine restructuring crisis: shrinking European demand, high German cost levels, intensifying competition, and missed profitability targets. The governance setting is unusually constraining: co-determination gives employee representatives an effective veto over major site decisions, the controlling family can shape outcomes through the supervisory board, and the regional state holds blocking power as an anchor shareholder. The practical decision is therefore not “whether to restructure,” but whether the company can secure a cost and capacity reset through negotiated measures that avoid closures and forced redundancies, or whether closure-led restructuring remains necessary.

## 2. Stakeholder assembly
Weighted by standing and decision power, the key actors are:

- **Group & Brand Management**: initiator of the restructuring and operational diagnosis.
- **Controlling Family Holding**: highly powerful governance actor; prioritizes long-term value and structural competitiveness.
- **Regional State Government**: strong legitimacy and blocking capacity; prioritizes regional employment and industrial base.
- **Works Council**: very high legitimacy and formal veto power through co-determination; defends site and job security.
- **Industrial Union**: high bargaining power over labor peace and strikes; can mobilize industrial action.
- **Institutional Investors**: financially relevant but weaker governance standing; support value-preserving restructuring.

## 3. Final positions

### Group & Brand Management
Management’s position is that the German footprint has structural overcapacity and an uncompetitive cost base, so durable capacity and cost reduction is required. It is open to alternatives only if they are economically equivalent to closure-led restructuring; otherwise plant closures and workforce reduction must remain available.

### Controlling Family Holding
The controlling shareholder agrees the problem is structural and insists that flexibility must remain on the table. It is open to negotiated alternatives, but rejects categorical bans on closures, compulsory redundancies, or binding guarantees that would freeze restructuring through 2030.

### Regional State Government
The state wants a real restructuring but insists it should not be built around closures or forced layoffs as the default logic. It supports binding site and employment protection for viable plants, provided management can produce a quantified alternatives package showing that closures are not necessary.

### Works Council
The works council takes the strongest protective line: no plant closures, no compulsory redundancies, and binding job and site guarantees through 2030. It accepts that the company needs restructuring and contributions from management, owners, and capital, but rejects any staged or deferred closure logic as merely postponing the threat.

### Industrial Union
The union shares the works council’s red lines: no closures and no compulsory redundancies. It is prepared to discuss temporary labor-side contributions, but only if matched by management and owner contributions and only within a framework that preserves collective bargaining and job security.

### Institutional Investors
Investors support restructuring, cost reduction, and stronger competitiveness, and they reject cosmetic compromise. They are open to negotiated alternatives, but only if the package is economically sufficient and does not leave the company with a structurally uncompetitive cost base.

## 4. Genuine conflicts (structurally incompatible positions)
1. **Closure as fallback vs. closure as taboo**  
   Management, the controlling family, and investors want closures to remain available; the works council and union reject closures as an acceptable fallback, and the state wants them de-emphasized.

2. **Compulsory redundancies possible vs. categorically excluded**  
   Management, the family, and investors see forced reductions as a necessary backstop if savings fall short; labor and the state refuse that logic.

3. **Binding security through 2030 vs. restructuring flexibility**  
   Employee and state actors want durable guarantees; management and owners argue this would prevent the very adjustment the company needs.

4. **What counts as an adequate alternative**  
   Labor demands a fully quantified alternatives package before any destructive measures are contemplated; management and investors argue alternatives must be economically equivalent and durable, not merely preferable.

## 5. Areas of convergence
- Everyone agrees the company needs restructuring, not a cosmetic fix.
- All sides accept the need for EV/future-tech investment and viable product allocation.
- Everyone rejects a prolonged industrial conflict.
- There is shared openness to voluntary departures, redeployment, working-time flexibility, management overhead cuts, and some form of owner/shareholder contribution.
- The expert intervention created cross-cutting agreement that a negotiated package can work only if the savings gap is moderate and the measures are durable.

## 6. Key trade-offs for the decision-maker
- **Security vs. flexibility**: binding site and job guarantees build trust but can constrain restructuring.
- **Social peace vs. economic sufficiency**: avoiding closures reduces conflict, but only if the resulting package actually closes the gap.
- **Short-term legitimacy vs. long-term competitiveness**: preserving jobs and sites now may preserve legitimacy, but failing to reset fixed costs can weaken the company later.
- **Burden-sharing vs. implementation certainty**: a fair package requires contributions from labor, management, and owners, but more actors and conditions make implementation harder.
- **Regional stability vs. footprint rationalization**: the state’s industrial-policy goals favor protection, but the company may need footprint consolidation to restore margins.

## 7. Integrative pathways
1. **Quantified non-closure first package**
   - Start with voluntary exits, retirement pathways, redeployment, working-time flexibility, wage moderation, management cuts, and owner-side support.
   - Require a fully quantified savings target and independent verification.
   - Keep closures and forced redundancies as formally deferred options only if the quantified package fails.

2. **Viability-based site differentiation**
   - Distinguish viable sites from borderline or non-viable sites using agreed criteria.
   - Grant binding protection to viable sites; subject weaker sites to an audited rescue/review process.
   - This preserves the employee-side red lines where economically defensible while not pretending all sites are equally viable.

3. **Time-bound burden-sharing pact**
   - Negotiate a temporary labor contribution matched by management and owner contributions.
   - Pair it with investment commitments, product allocation, and a joint implementation body.
   - Add automatic review triggers tied to utilization, margins, and cost-base progress.

## 8. Open questions
- What is the actual quantified savings gap?
- How much of the cost problem is labor-related versus fixed-capacity and overhead related?
- Which plants are truly viable under agreed criteria?
- What savings from voluntary and reversible measures are realistically achievable?
- What level of owner and management contribution is material enough to change the economics?
- What independent verification mechanism will all sides accept?
- What happens if the agreed package underdelivers after 12–18 months?

## 9. Recommended next step
Require each stakeholder bloc to submit a written, quantified position on:
1. the savings gap they assume,
2. the measures they are willing to fund or accept,
3. the measures they consider non-negotiable,
4. the verification standard they will accept,
5. and the trigger at which they would reopen discussion on closures or workforce reductions.

In parallel, commission the independent expert to produce a side-by-side model of three cases: closure-led, hybrid, and non-closure restructuring.

## 10. Emergent insights that only appeared through multi-turn interaction
- The core deadlock is not simple labor resistance; it is a **battle over whether economic equivalence can be proved without destructive instruments**.
- The expert’s intervention shifted the debate from ideology to **threshold logic**: a negotiated package is plausible if the gap is moderate, but weak if the gap is large and fixed-cost driven.
- The works council and union moved from general opposition to a sharper claim: management has not yet presented a **fully quantified alternatives package**, so closure is not yet proven necessary.
- Management, the family, and investors converged on a more explicit position: **economic sufficiency, not symbolic compromise**, is the decisive test.
- The state’s role emerged as more than symbolic legitimacy: it is willing to back a non-closure solution, but only if it can be defended as economically real and regionally stabilizing.
- The interaction revealed that “moratorium” language is politically insufficient for labor unless it ends in a binding outcome; from the employee side, delayed closure logic is viewed as postponed conflict rather than compromise.
