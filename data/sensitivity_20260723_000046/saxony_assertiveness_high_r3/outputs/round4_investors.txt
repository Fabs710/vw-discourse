From the perspective of dispersed capital-market shareholders, the debate has become clearer, not softer.

## Where I agree
I agree with management, the family holding, and the expert on the central diagnosis:

- the German cost base is too high,
- the brand is missing its return-on-sales target,
- the market is shrinking,
- and a cosmetic settlement would destroy value.

I also agree with the employee side and the state on one important point: **a drawn-out conflict is value-destructive**. A prolonged strike, governance deadlock, or endless review process would hurt margins, execution, and investor confidence. That is not acceptable.

So yes: restructuring is necessary, and yes, the final package must be durable enough to actually change the economics.

## Where I disagree
### 1) “No closures” cannot be a hard line
I do **not** support the works council’s and union’s categorical rejection of plant closures.

Historic importance is not a financial argument. If a site is structurally uncompetitive and alternatives do not close the gap, then closure must remain an available option. We are not investing capital to preserve every footprint for symbolic reasons.

That said, I do **not** want closures as a reflex. They should be a last resort after serious alternatives are tested. But removing them entirely from the table would make it harder, not easier, to restore competitive margins.

### 2) A blanket guarantee through 2030 is too rigid
I understand the need for trust. But a binding employment and site guarantee that freezes restructuring for years would be a problem if it blocks the company from fixing its cost base.

Security without flexibility can become deferred value destruction. From an investor perspective, that is not a solution; it is a delay.

### 3) “No compulsory redundancies” as an absolute is not workable
I prefer voluntary exits, retirement pathways, redeployment, and working-time measures first. But if those are insufficient to achieve the savings target, then headcount reduction must remain possible.

If the company cannot reduce costs enough through voluntary means, it should not be locked into preserving excess capacity indefinitely.

### 4) Burden-sharing must be material, not rhetorical
I agree that labour should not carry the burden alone. But management restraint and owner contributions matter only if they materially change the economics.

A gesture is not a restructuring. The package has to produce real, measurable savings.

## What the expert’s analysis means
The expert’s view is the key one here: a negotiated non-destructive package can work **if the savings gap is moderate** and if the measures are deep and durable enough. But if the gap is large and fixed-cost driven, then a pure no-closure/no-layoff package is unlikely to be economically equivalent.

That is the standard I care about: **economic sufficiency**, not moral preference.

## What I would support
I can support a compromise only if it delivers:

1. **Durable capacity reduction**
2. **A material and lasting reduction in fixed and labour costs**
3. **A credible path back to margin improvement**
4. **Clear investment and product allocation for viable sites**
5. **Implementation certainty**
6. **No prolonged strike or governance deadlock**

That means I am open to:
- voluntary departures,
- retirement pathways,
- internal redeployment,
- working-time flexibility,
- temporary capacity reductions,
- management overhead cuts,
- investment prioritization,
- and targeted owner support if it is real.

But I will **not** support:
- a cosmetic compromise,
- a package that preserves the current cost problem,
- a binding guarantee that blocks restructuring,
- or a solution that rules out closures and deeper workforce adjustment under all circumstances.

## My challenge to the employee side
If you want to avoid closures and forced redundancies, stop arguing in principles and show the numbers.

Bring a quantified alternatives package showing:
- annual savings from voluntary exits,
- savings from working-time changes,
- savings from wage moderation,
- savings from overhead cuts,
- savings from capacity reductions,
- and the residual gap after all that.

If you cannot close the gap, then your position is not an alternative. It is a delay mechanism.

## My challenge to management and owners
You are right to insist on competitiveness, but do not confuse leverage with proof. If you want investors to back a hard package, you also need to demonstrate the cost gap clearly and show why the chosen mix is the least destructive route to closing it.

## Bottom line
Dispersed capital-market shareholders will support the outcome that best restores risk-adjusted value:

- **yes** to tough restructuring,
- **yes** to negotiated alternatives if they are economically sufficient,
- **yes** to material burden-sharing,
- **no** to a cosmetic deal,
- **no** to preserving an uncompetitive cost base,
- **no** to a settlement that blocks necessary change.

If the employee side can produce a package that is economically equivalent in substance, we can back it.  
If not, then management and the controlling owners should not be pressured into a compromise that protects comfort today and destroys value tomorrow.
