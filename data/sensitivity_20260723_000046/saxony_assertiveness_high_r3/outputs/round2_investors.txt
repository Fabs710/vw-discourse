From the perspective of dispersed capital-market shareholders, I can support only one kind of outcome: **a credible, durable restructuring that materially restores margins and reduces the cost base**. Everything else is noise.

## Where I agree

I agree with management, the family holding, and the state on the basic diagnosis:

- the current German cost base is uncompetitive,
- the market is not going to rescue the company,
- the brand is missing its return-on-sales target,
- and a prolonged conflict would destroy value.

I also agree with the union and works council on one important point: **a cosmetic settlement is unacceptable**. If the deal simply defers the problem, preserves excess capacity, and leaves the P&L structurally weak, that would be worse than no deal at all for investors.

## Where I disagree

### 1) I do not support “no closures” as an absolute
The works council and union keep treating plant closures as categorically unacceptable. From an investor standpoint, that is not credible if closures are needed to achieve durable savings.

I do **not** want closures for their own sake. But if they are required to remove excess capacity and permanently lower fixed costs, then they must remain available. “Historic site” is not a financial argument.

### 2) I do not support a blanket employment guarantee through 2030
I understand the need for trust and transition. But a binding employment guarantee that freezes restructuring through 2030 is not something I can back if it prevents the company from fixing its cost base.

That would protect today’s structure, not tomorrow’s earnings. Investors need flexibility to reset the business, not a promise that hard decisions will be postponed.

### 3) I do not support relying mainly on voluntary or temporary measures
Voluntary departures, working-time flexibility, and temporary contributions can help, but they are not enough unless they produce a **measurable, permanent** change in the economics.

If the final package is mostly short-term pain-sharing with no enduring reduction in cost and capacity, then it is not investable.

## What I require for support

Any package I could support must include, in measurable terms:

1. **Durable capacity reduction**
2. **A material and lasting reduction in labour and fixed costs**
3. **A credible route back to competitive margins**
4. **Clear implementation certainty**
5. **No prolonged strike or governance deadlock**
6. **Investment and product commitments that match the restructuring**

That is the standard. Not slogans. Not intentions.

## To management

You are right to insist that a cosmetic settlement is not enough. But investors will judge you on execution, not rhetoric. If you want capital market support, you need to show the concrete numbers:

- how much cost comes out,
- where it comes out,
- when it hits the P&L,
- and why it is durable.

If you cannot quantify the savings, then the market will assume the deal is weak.

## To the works council and union

I hear the insistence on no closures and no compulsory redundancies. I respect the political and social logic, but I do **not** accept it as a financial constraint that the company can afford.

If you want to avoid closures, then you need to present an alternative that is economically equivalent, not just socially preferable. That means real capacity reduction, real cost reduction, and real savings that stay in place.

## To the state

I agree that a reckless closure-led shock would be harmful. But the state should not confuse social responsibility with preserving every site regardless of economics.

A structure that cannot earn its cost of capital is not a sustainable industrial policy. If the state wants to protect jobs, it has to support a package that actually makes the company competitive again.

## To the family holding

I agree that a prolonged conflict would be value-destructive and that structural change is necessary. But governance certainty matters only if the resulting deal is economically real. A board-approved compromise that lacks operational bite is still a weak outcome.

## My bottom line

I will support a deal only if it is **economically real, durable, and implementable**.

I will **not** support:

- a cosmetic compromise,
- an outcome that leaves the cost base uncompetitive,
- a deal that blocks necessary restructuring,
- or a prolonged conflict that destroys value.

If the employee side wants to avoid closures and forced redundancies, then it must bring forward a package that produces equivalent savings in practice, not in theory. Otherwise, management and the controlling owners should not be asked to accept a settlement that preserves structural underperformance.

If useful, I can next comment on the **minimum financial thresholds** that would make a restructuring package credible to the market.
