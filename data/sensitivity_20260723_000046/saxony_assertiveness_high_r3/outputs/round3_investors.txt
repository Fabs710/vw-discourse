From the perspective of dispersed capital-market shareholders, the only question that matters is whether the final package will **restore competitive margins, reduce the cost base durably, and be implementable without endless conflict**.

## Where I agree
I agree with management, the family holding, and to a degree the state on the core diagnosis:

- the German cost base is too high,
- the brand is missing its return-on-sales target,
- the market is shrinking,
- and a cosmetic compromise would destroy value.

I also agree with the employee side on one point: **a deal that only postpones the problem is unacceptable**. If the final outcome leaves the company structurally uncompetitive, that is worse than an ugly but effective restructuring.

## Where I disagree
### 1) “No closures” cannot be a hard line
I do **not** support the works council’s and union’s attempt to rule out closures categorically.

I do not want plant closures as a reflex. But if a site is structurally uncompetitive and alternatives do not close the gap, then closure must remain available. Historic importance is not a sufficient financial argument. The value of our holdings depends on hard economics, not symbolism.

### 2) A blanket guarantee through 2030 is too rigid
I understand the desire for trust, but from an investor point of view a binding guarantee that freezes restructuring for years is not acceptable if it blocks the company from fixing its cost base.

Security without flexibility does not protect value. It delays the adjustment and risks a bigger destruction later.

### 3) “No compulsory redundancies” as an absolute is not workable
I prefer voluntary departures, redeployment, retirement pathways, and working-time adjustments first. But if those measures are insufficient, then headcount reduction must remain available. Otherwise we are just preserving overcapacity and hoping the market becomes kinder.

### 4) Temporary or symbolic burden-sharing is not enough
I agree with the union that labour should not bear the burden alone. But management restraint or owner contributions are only relevant if they are **material enough to change the economics**. If they are just gestures, the package will still fail.

## What I would support
I can support a compromise only if it is **economically equivalent** to closure-led restructuring in substance, not just in language. That means:

1. **Durable capacity reduction**
2. **A measurable and lasting reduction in fixed and labour costs**
3. **A credible path back to margin improvement**
4. **Clear investment and product allocation to viable sites**
5. **Implementation certainty**
6. **No prolonged strike or governance deadlock**

## My view on the bridge ideas
### Phased restructuring with review triggers
Potentially acceptable **only if** the triggers are objective and the fallback is real.  
Not acceptable if it becomes a disguised permanent guarantee.

### “No closure first” sequence
Reasonable as a process, but only if it leads to actual savings.  
If the sequence is just a delay tactic, it is worthless.

### Security-for-contribution swap
This is the most promising route, but only if the contribution is durable and meaningful, and only if it materially improves the economics. Otherwise it is just a softer way of preserving the same underperformance.

## Bottom line
We will support a deal only if it produces:

- a credible return to competitive margins,
- a durable cost and capacity reset,
- real implementation certainty,
- and a settlement that does not trap the company in structural weakness.

We will **not** support:
- a cosmetic compromise,
- an outcome that leaves the cost base uncompetitive,
- or a deal that blocks necessary restructuring.

If the employee side wants to avoid closures and forced redundancies, it must put forward a package that delivers the same financial result in practice. If it cannot, then management and the controlling owners should not accept a settlement that protects today’s structure at the expense of tomorrow’s value.
