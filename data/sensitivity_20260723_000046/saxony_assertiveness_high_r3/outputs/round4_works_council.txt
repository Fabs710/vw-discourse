I will be equally direct in round 4.

The “compromise matrix” still starts from the wrong place if it treats **closures and forced redundancies as normal fallback options**. For the works council, that is not a technical detail. It is the core issue. We are not bargaining over how to administer layoffs; we are defending whether German plants and German jobs remain intact.

## Where I agree
I agree with everyone on three things:

- The company needs a **serious restructuring**, not a cosmetic one.
- The company needs a **durable industrial future**, with EV investment and viable product allocation.
- The burden cannot be placed on workers alone; **management, owners, and capital must contribute**.

On that, there is real common ground.

## Where I disagree — and I need to be blunt

### 1) A closure “moratorium” is not a solution if closures remain the endgame
To management, owners, and investors: a time-limited moratorium on closures is not what the workforce needs. It just postpones the threat.

We have lived through enough “temporary” security arrangements to know what they are worth once the deadline arrives. If a plant is viable, it should be secured now. If it is not, then management must prove that with hard numbers under transparent conditions — not keep the workforce under a timed sword.

So I do **not** accept a structure that says: “no closures for now, but we reserve the right to close later.” That is not trust-building. It is delay.

### 2) “No compulsory redundancies first” is too weak
I also do not accept the framing that forced layoffs can remain on the table after a sequence of failed measures.

My position is unchanged and clear:
- **no plant closures**
- **no compulsory redundancies**
- **binding employment and site guarantees through 2030**
- **real co-determination in implementation**

If management is serious about restructuring, it must do so **without** destroying livelihoods by force.

### 3) The burden cannot be “labour contribution plus maybe later cuts”
The union is right to insist that workers must not pay the bill alone. But let me sharpen this: a “labor contribution” is only legitimate if it is **strictly limited, temporary, reversible, and matched by management/owner contributions**.

That means:
- management restraint,
- overhead cuts,
- owner-side financial support,
- investment commitments,
- product allocation for German sites,
- and no erosion of the job-security framework.

If those are missing, then the so-called contribution is simply a disguised wage cut and a transfer of risk to the workforce.

## To management
You keep asking us to prove an “economically equivalent” alternative. I reject that burden-shifting.

The burden is on **management** to prove that closures and forced layoffs are necessary. Not assumed. Not convenient. Necessary.

So answer the question properly:

- What exact cost gap must be closed?
- What savings come from top management and overhead reductions?
- What savings come from the owners?
- What savings come from product allocation and investment reprioritization?
- What savings come from working-time measures and internal redeployment?
- What savings come from voluntary instruments?
- And why have you still not presented a full quantified alternatives package?

If management cannot show that all non-destructive measures are insufficient, then closure is not a necessity; it is a choice. And we will not accept it.

## To the controlling family holding and investors
You keep talking about “economic equivalence.” Fine. Then let’s be honest: you are demanding that the workforce accept the same economic outcome as a closure-led path, but with less visible pain.

That is not a neutral technical demand. It means you want the same cost reduction while preserving optionality for capital and shifting uncertainty onto labour.

I do not accept that logic.

Historic German plants are not just cost centers. They are the industrial base of this company, built by generations of skilled workers. A governance model that sacrifices them whenever short-term targets are missed is not stewardship.

## To the state
I appreciate that you reject closures and forced redundancies. But a “review mechanism” or “governance condition” that leaves closures possible later is not enough.

If you want stability in the region, then use your blocking position for a **binding no-closure and no-forced-layoff settlement**, not a pause before the same conflict returns.

The state should not validate a framework that keeps the workforce in permanent uncertainty.

## To the union
We are aligned on the red lines. But I want to be precise: any labour-side contribution must not become the back door for permanent insecurity.

I can discuss:
- voluntary departure packages,
- retirement pathways,
- redeployment,
- working-time flexibility,
- temporary capacity adjustments,
- training and qualification,
- management restraint.

I cannot accept:
- closure-led restructuring,
- compulsory redundancies,
- or a deal that weakens the 2030 job and site framework.

## On the proposed “conditional moratorium”
Let me say this clearly: if the proposal is merely to pause closures while keeping them legally and politically alive, then it is not acceptable.

A moratorium is only useful if it leads to a **binding outcome**:
- no plant closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- guaranteed site viability,
- and a genuine restructuring of costs through non-destructive measures.

Anything less just shifts the deadline and worsens trust.

## What a serious package would have to contain
Not a “review trigger” package, but a truly defensible settlement:

1. **No German plant closures**
2. **No compulsory redundancies**
3. **Binding employment guarantee through 2030**
4. **Binding site guarantees for viable plants**
5. **Capacity reduction through voluntary and reversible instruments**
6. **Management and owner contributions**
7. **Investment and product allocation for German sites**
8. **Preservation of co-determination and the existing job-security framework**
9. **A quantified alternatives package from management**
10. **Transparent implementation with workforce participation**

That is the minimum I can defend.

## Bottom line
I am not signing up to a staged pathway to closure.
I am not signing up to forced layoffs.
I am not signing up to “temporary” security that merely postpones the same threat.

If management, owners, and investors want a deal, they must bring a package that **protects every German plant and every job guarantee**, and that demonstrates, in numbers, how the company becomes competitive **without** destruction of sites or compulsory redundancies.

Until then, my answer is no.
