## Expert assessment: can a non-closure package credibly close the gap?

### Short answer
**Possibly, but only under a fairly demanding set of conditions.**  
A non-closure package can replace plant closures **if** the required adjustment is mainly a **moderate fixed-cost and labor-cost reset** rather than a **deep structural capacity exit**. If the company truly needs a large, permanent removal of fixed cost, then a no-closure package is **unlikely to be economically equivalent** unless it is unusually large, durable, and enforceable.

In restructuring terms: **non-closure can work as a bridge or partial substitute; it is harder to make it a full substitute for closure-led rationalization.**

---

## What the closure-led package usually accomplishes
A closure-led restructuring normally delivers four things:

1. **Headcount removal**
2. **Fixed-cost elimination**
3. **Capacity reduction and utilization improvement at remaining sites**
4. **A clean reset of the cost base**

That is why management favors it when the issue is not just wages, but **structural overcapacity**.

The financial advantage is not only labor savings. It is also:
- site overhead removal
- plant maintenance and utilities reduction
- logistics simplification
- management span reduction
- and better utilization of the remaining footprint

The trade-off is that closure produces:
- severance and social-plan cost
- asset write-downs
- decommissioning costs
- transition risk
- execution and industrial-relations risk

So closure is expensive upfront, but powerful over time.

---

## What a non-closure package can realistically deliver
A no-closure package typically uses a mix of:

- voluntary departures
- retirements
- internal redeployment
- working-time reduction
- wage moderation or temporary concessions
- management overhead cuts
- procurement and indirect-cost reduction
- output reallocation across plants
- capacity trimming without outright closure

These measures are real and can be substantial. But they are not equally durable.

### Typical strengths
- **Voluntary exits and retirements** can reduce headcount materially.
- **Working-time flexibility** can reduce cost quickly.
- **Overhead cuts** are often achievable without industrial shutdown.
- **Product reallocation** can improve utilization without closure.

### Typical weaknesses
- They often **preserve most site fixed cost**.
- They can be **time-limited** or reversible.
- They may not remove enough capacity if the network is genuinely oversized.
- They can leave the company with **too much overhead per unit of output**.

That is the core issue: a company can lower labor expense without fully solving a capacity problem.

---

## Quantified view: what scale of savings is plausible?
I would frame it as a savings-gap question.

### Illustrative annual savings ranges
These are not company-specific verified figures; they are practical restructuring ranges:

- **Voluntary departures / retirements:**  
  roughly **€100m–€250m per year**
- **Wage moderation / temporary labor concessions:**  
  roughly **€50m–€150m per year**
- **Working-time flexibility / capacity adjustment:**  
  roughly **€25m–€100m per year**
- **Management and overhead cuts:**  
  roughly **€25m–€100m per year**
- **Procurement / indirect savings / footprint optimization:**  
  roughly **€50m–€150m per year**

### Combined plausible range
A serious negotiated package might therefore generate roughly:

**€250m–€750m per year**

That is a meaningful amount. It may be enough if the company’s required gap is moderate.  
But if the company needs to remove **very large fixed costs**, this may still fall short of a closure-led reset.

---

## The decisive test: how large is the required gap?
The answer depends on the size of the restructuring need.

### If the annual gap is around €300m
A negotiated package is **plausible**.  
With voluntary exits, labor moderation, and overhead cuts, a no-closure deal can likely do the job.

### If the gap is around €500m–€700m
It becomes **borderline**.  
It may still work, but only if the package is deep, durable, and paired with real capacity rationalization.

### If the gap is €1bn or more
A non-closure package becomes **much harder to justify as economically equivalent**.  
At that point, you usually need one or more of:
- plant closure
- major product transfer
- significant network consolidation
- or a very large, durable labor reset

That is where the debate tends to move from “can we save money?” to “can we actually reset the fixed-cost base?”

---

## What would make a non-closure package credible
For non-closure to be credible, it needs to satisfy four conditions:

### 1) Durability
Savings must persist beyond a short agreement horizon.  
If the concessions expire in two or three years, markets will treat them as temporary relief, not a structural reset.

### 2) Scale
The package must close most of the required gap, not just part of it.

### 3) Capacity discipline
The company must genuinely reduce underutilized output, not just lower wages while leaving excess capacity in place.

### 4) Enforceability
There must be a mechanism that makes the savings real:
- measurable targets
- clear implementation milestones
- review gates
- and consequences if targets are missed

Without that, it is more a political settlement than a restructuring solution.

## My judgment on the proposals in the room
### Works Council / union position
Their position is socially coherent, but **economically incomplete unless they can show the numbers**.

A no-closure/no-forced-layoff framework can work only if the alternatives package is large enough to replace the economics of closure. That has not yet been demonstrated.

### Management / investor position
Their position is economically plausible because it recognizes the need for **fixed-cost removal**.  
However, they still need to prove that closures are necessary rather than merely preferable. In restructuring terms, “necessary” means the alternatives package fails the quantified gap test.

### State / family holding
Their positions are understandable as governance and regional-anchor actors, but they cannot replace industrial math.  
If they want a non-closure outcome, they need to back a package that is financially equivalent, not just politically preferable.

---

## Practical conclusion
### A non-closure package is credible if:
- the gap is moderate,
- voluntary and negotiated measures are deep enough,
- labor-cost contribution is material but time-limited,
- management and owners also contribute,
- and capacity is truly reduced enough to improve utilization.

### It is not likely credible if:
- the company needs a large permanent fixed-cost exit,
- underutilization is severe,
- savings are mostly temporary,
- or the package leaves excess capacity structurally intact.

### My overall assessment
**A hybrid negotiated package is the most realistic path if the company wants to avoid immediate closures.**  
But a pure “no closures, no compulsory redundancies, full job guarantee through 2030” package is **unlikely to be economically equivalent** unless the savings target is modest relative to the group’s German cost base.

---

## Key blockers
From a restructuring perspective, the main blockers are:

1. **Unclear savings gap**
2. **No agreed definition of economic equivalence**
3. **Potentially too much excess capacity**
4. **Durability of labor-side concessions**
5. **Whether owners/managers are prepared to contribute materially**
6. **Whether the remaining footprint can be utilized efficiently enough**

Until those are resolved with numbers, the debate is ideological rather than financial.
