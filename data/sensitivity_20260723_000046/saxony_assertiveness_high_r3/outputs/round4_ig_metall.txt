I will be direct as well.

## Where I agree
I agree with the Works Council, the state, and even management on one point: **this company needs a real restructuring, not a cosmetic deal**.  
We also agree that:
- the industrial future must be secured,
- EV and future-product investment are necessary,
- the cost base has to come down,
- and a prolonged conflict would damage everyone.

I also agree that **employees should contribute**. The union has already signaled openness to:
- temporary labor-cost moderation,
- working-time flexibility,
- voluntary instruments,
- internal redeployment,
- and a restructuring contribution if it is fair and time-limited.

So I am not blocking change. I am blocking a **one-sided** restructuring.

## Where I disagree — and why

### 1) No plant closures, not as a “fallback”
To management, owners, and investors: I reject the idea that closures remain the normal backstop.  
That is not a neutral economic argument; it is leverage. It says to the workforce: “accept our terms or we will shut sites.”

I do **not** accept a restructuring model that keeps German plant closures as an active option while demanding concessions from labour first. That is exactly how you destroy trust and trigger resistance.

### 2) No compulsory redundancies
I want to be absolutely clear: **forced layoffs are not acceptable**.

If headcount has to be reduced, then only through:
- voluntary departures,
- retirement pathways,
- internal redeployment,
- working-time reduction,
- qualification and transition measures,
- and capacity adjustment.

If management says this is not enough, then management must prove it with numbers. Not with pressure. Not with threats.

### 3) A “moratorium” or “review trigger” is not security
To the Works Council and state: I understand the instinct behind a time-limited moratorium. But I share your concern that a delayed threat is still a threat.

So let me be equally blunt: I am **not** interested in a fake deal where the company gets labour concessions today and simply reserves the right to close plants or impose layoffs later. That would be a postponement of the same conflict, not a solution.

If there is to be a restructuring package, it must create **real stability**, not a temporary truce.

### 4) Burden-sharing must actually be shared
This is the key point. The burden cannot land on workers while management, owners, and capital keep full flexibility.

If labour contributes, then I expect binding contributions from the other side too:
- management restraint,
- overhead reduction,
- owner-side financial support,
- investment commitments,
- product allocation for viable sites,
- and a serious willingness to protect the collective bargaining framework.

If those are missing, then “contribution” just means employees paying for a crisis they did not create.

## What could move the union
I remain open to a negotiated labour contribution if — and only if — it is tied to a package that protects jobs and sites.

That means:
- no compulsory redundancies,
- no plant closures,
- binding employment and site security for viable sites,
- and a clear end date for any employee-side contribution.

That contribution could include:
- temporary moderation in wage growth,
- a restructuring fund,
- working-time flexibility,
- and capacity adjustments.

But it must be matched by:
- management cuts,
- owner contributions,
- and investment commitments.

And it must not erode the collective wage framework permanently.

## To management
You keep saying “economic equivalence.” Fine. Then show it.

Bring a fully quantified package that answers:
- what savings come from management and overhead cuts,
- what savings come from owners,
- what savings come from working-time and capacity measures,
- what savings come from voluntary departures,
- and what remains after that.

If you cannot show that non-destructive measures are insufficient, then closures and forced redundancies are not necessary — they are a choice. And we will oppose that choice.

## To the Works Council
We are aligned on the red lines:
- no plant closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- binding job and site security,
- real co-determination.

I support that line. But I also want us to be disciplined: if we demand protection, we must present a serious alternative that is operationally credible, not just morally correct. That means quantified measures, not only principles.

## To the state
Your blocking role matters. I appreciate that you reject a socially destructive restructuring. But I do **not** want the state to settle for a pause before the same fight returns.

If you want stability, then back a settlement that actually secures:
- jobs,
- sites,
- investment,
- and the collective bargaining framework.

A timed pause with closure options still alive is not sufficient.

## To the family holding and investors
I’ll be frank: your insistence on “flexibility” reads, in practice, like asking labour to absorb risk while capital preserves options.

That is not a fair restructuring. If you want a durable solution, then capital must also contribute to the adjustment, not only demand it.

## Where I stand
The union’s position remains:

- **No compulsory redundancies**
- **No plant closures**
- **A binding employment and site framework**
- **A fair, temporary employee contribution only in exchange for management and owner contributions**
- **Investment and product commitments for German sites**
- **Protection of the collective wage agreement**

That is not maximalism. That is the minimum for a settlement workers can accept and defend.

If management wants a deal, bring a **quantified burden-sharing package** that protects viable sites and distributes pain fairly.  
If not, we will use the instruments we have. The peace obligation has ended, and the workforce is ready to act.
