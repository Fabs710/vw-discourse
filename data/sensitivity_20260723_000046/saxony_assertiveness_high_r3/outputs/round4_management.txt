## Where I agree
I agree with the expert on one essential point: **this is not a debate about whether restructuring is needed; it is about whether the package is economically sufficient**.

I also agree that:
- a purely cosmetic deal is worthless,
- burden-sharing must not fall on labour alone,
- and any settlement must be durable enough to convince the market and the board.

That said, the expert’s conclusion actually supports my core position more than the employee side’s. If the gap is large and structurally driven, then **a serious footprint and cost reset is required**. That is exactly why I keep plant closures and workforce reduction on the table.

## Where I disagree with the works council
You keep repeating “no closures, no compulsory redundancies, no closure triggers, no review path.” I understand the political logic, but economically it is not enough.

You are asking us to accept a binding industrial future while ruling out the instruments that would make it credible. That is not a restructuring framework; it is a demand to preserve the current structure and hope a few voluntary measures somehow fix a major cost problem.

The expert was clear: if the gap is meaningful, then **non-destructive measures may help, but they often do not fully replicate the economics of capacity exit**. That is the reality you are refusing to confront.

You also argue that the burden of proof lies entirely with management. Fine — and we are proving it with the facts of the market:
- German factory costs are above target,
- European demand is shrinking,
- competition is intensifying,
- and the brand is missing its return-on-sales target.

That is proof of the problem. What I have not seen from you is proof that a no-closure package can close it.

## Where I disagree with the union
You say compulsory redundancies are unacceptable in any circumstances. I reject that as a hard red line.

I prefer voluntary departures, retirement pathways, redeployment, working-time flexibility, and negotiated reductions. But if the required savings cannot otherwise be achieved, then **headcount reduction must remain possible**. Otherwise you are simply locking in overcapacity and shifting the adjustment to the future, when it will be harsher.

You also say closures are just leverage. Of course they are leverage — because the economics require leverage. If a site is structurally uncompetitive, preserving it by political assertion alone is not responsible bargaining; it is denial.

## Where I disagree with the state
I respect the state’s role and its concern for regional stability. But your position is too close to “protect every site first, solve the economics later.”

That is backward.

A state can help enable restructuring, but it cannot substitute for it. If you use your blocking position to prevent structural adjustment altogether, you may preserve jobs for a while, but you weaken the company’s ability to invest, innovate, and remain a major industrial employer at all.

That is not industrial policy. That is delay.

## Where I agree with the controlling family and investors
Here I am closer to you than some others in the room.

You are right that:
- a cosmetic compromise is unacceptable,
- flexibility matters,
- and a settlement must produce durable cost and capacity improvement.

I also agree that closures cannot be ruled out in advance if they are the only economically sufficient option.

Where I would push you further is this: it is not enough to say “do something structural.” We need a package that actually hits the numbers, and that means you must support management in insisting on real burden-sharing from labour.

## On the expert’s analysis
The expert’s framework is useful because it confirms what management has been saying:

- If the cost gap is **moderate**, then negotiated measures may be enough.
- If the cost gap is **large and fixed-cost driven**, then a no-closure/no-layoff package is unlikely to be sufficient.
- If you want to avoid closures entirely, then the package must be exceptionally large, durable, and multi-levered.

That is the key point. The employee side keeps talking as if refusing closures is enough. It is not. You need an **economically equivalent package**, not a morally preferable one.

And the expert also noted that the likely path, if we want to avoid the most severe disruption, is a **hybrid negotiated package** with:
- meaningful voluntary headcount reduction,
- labor-cost moderation,
- overhead cuts,
- capacity rationalization,
- and binding product/investment commitments.

That is the kind of package I can work with. But it still requires real structural change.

## What I am prepared to accept
I am still open to alternatives to immediate closures and immediate compulsory redundancies — but only if they deliver the following:

1. **Durable capacity reduction**
2. **A material and lasting labor-cost contribution**
3. **Lower fixed costs**
4. **A credible path to the brand’s return-on-sales target**
5. **Investment and product allocation for viable German sites**
6. **Implementation certainty**
7. **No open-ended delay disguised as protection**

That means I can discuss:
- voluntary departures,
- retirement pathways,
- internal redeployment,
- working-time flexibility,
- temporary capacity adjustments,
- management overhead reduction,
- and targeted investment commitments.

But I will not accept:
- a blanket ban on closures,
- a categorical ban on headcount reduction,
- a binding guarantee that freezes restructuring through 2030,
- or a deal that leaves the cost base structurally uncompetitive.

## What I need from the employee side
If you want to avoid closures and compulsory redundancies, then stop repeating red lines and bring a package that closes the gap in numbers.

I want a quantified alternatives proposal showing:
- the annual savings from voluntary exits,
- the savings from working-time changes,
- the savings from wage moderation,
- the savings from overhead cuts,
- the savings from internal reallocation and capacity reduction,
- and the residual gap after all that.

If you cannot close the gap, then you are not offering an alternative; you are offering a delay.

## My position on a possible path forward
I am not softening my position, but I will be precise:

- **Yes** to negotiated alternatives if they are economically sufficient.
- **Yes** to burden-sharing if it is real and durable.
- **Yes** to investment commitments for viable sites.
- **No** to a cosmetic deal.
- **No** to a package that preserves today’s cost base.
- **No** to ruling out closures or deeper workforce adjustment in advance.

## Bottom line
Management is not asking for conflict. We are asking for a credible structural reset.

The expert has reinforced that the central question is economic sufficiency. On that basis, I am not moving from the view that:
- closures must remain available if necessary,
- compulsory redundancies cannot be ruled out categorically,
- and the final package must produce a lasting cost and capacity reduction.

If the employee side wants a different outcome, it must bring a package that is not just socially preferable, but financially equivalent. Until then, the hard instruments remain on the table.
