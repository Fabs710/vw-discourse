The proposed “bridge options” still start from the wrong premise: that plant closure and forced job cuts are normal tools we must eventually accept if management cannot quickly prove otherwise. I reject that framing.

## First: what I agree with
I agree that the company needs a **durable industrial future**.  
I agree that we need **EV investment, product allocation, and a credible cost path**.  
I also agree that a **cosmetic deal would be irresponsible**.

So yes: there must be a real restructuring.

But a real restructuring is **not** the same as closing German plants or threatening compulsory redundancies to force acceptance.

## Where I disagree with the current “bridge” ideas

### 1) Phased restructuring with closure decisions “deferred, not canceled”
That is not security. It is a sword hanging over the workforce.

A 12–18 month review with closures still sitting in the background creates permanent uncertainty, destroys trust, and weakens any willingness to cooperate. Workers cannot plan their lives, families, mortgages, or skills development under a timed threat of closure.

And let me be explicit: **deferring closure is not an acceptable substitute for ruling it out.**  
If a site is viable, invest in it. If it is not, then management must show this with hard numbers today — not keep every plant hostage to later management discretion.

### 2) “No closure first” sequence
This sounds better, but I still see the problem: it leaves closure as a legitimate endpoint. For the works council, the line is not merely “closure last.”  
Our line is: **no German plant closures and no compulsory redundancies.**

I am willing to discuss:
- voluntary departures,
- retirement pathways,
- internal redeployment,
- working-time changes,
- temporary capacity reduction,
- training and qualification,
- management restraint,
- overhead reduction.

I am **not** willing to discuss a pathway whose intended logic is “try alternatives until we can justify closing a plant.”

### 3) Security-for-contribution swap
This is only acceptable if the “security” is real and binding.  
A time-limited contribution from labour while closures remain possible later is not balanced; it is one-sided risk transfer.

We have already said what we need for a serious settlement:
- **no plant closures**
- **no compulsory redundancies**
- **binding employment and site guarantees through 2030**
- **real co-determination in implementation**
- **investment commitments for all viable sites**

Without those points, “contribution” just means the workforce pays while capital keeps optionality.

## To management
You keep saying the burden of proof is on us to provide an “economically equivalent” alternative. No. The burden is on management to prove that closures and forced layoffs are necessary.

So I am asking again, directly:

- What exact cost gap must be closed?
- What savings come from management and overhead cuts?
- What savings come from owner-side contributions?
- What savings come from investment prioritization?
- What savings come from flexible working arrangements and capacity changes?
- Why have you not presented a full alternatives package with quantified effects?

If management cannot show that every non-destructive route has been exhausted, then closures are not “necessary”; they are a choice.

## To the family holding and investors
You say historic sites are not a financial argument. Fine. But neither is simply invoking “market credibility” while ignoring the company’s social responsibility, employee rights, and the legal structure of co-determination.

Your position reduces the company to a short-term financial model. That is not how a major German industrial company can or should be governed.

And let me be blunt: if you force a closure-led path through this system, you will trigger resistance that damages value much more than a negotiated, job-secure restructuring would.

## To the state
I appreciate that you reject closures and compulsory redundancies. On that, we stand together.

But I do not accept a “phased agreement” if it leaves the workforce exposed to later closure triggers. The state should use its blocking position to support a settlement with **real guarantees**, not a temporary pause before the same conflict returns.

If the state wants stability in the region, then it should back:
- binding site protection,
- binding job protection,
- investment commitments,
- and a restructuring based on capacity adjustment without plant closures.

## To the union
We are aligned on the core red lines.  
If management keeps insisting on closures and compulsory redundancies, then yes, industrial action remains on the table.

But I want to be very clear: any labour contribution must be **strictly limited, fair, and temporary**, and it must not become the back door for permanent insecurity. Workers cannot be asked to finance competitiveness by surrendering their future.

## On what a serious deal would have to contain
Not the compromise matrix as written, but a real worker-protective settlement:

1. **No plant closures in Germany**
2. **No compulsory redundancies**
3. **Binding employment guarantee through 2030**
4. **Binding site guarantees for all viable plants**
5. **Capacity reduction through working-time and internal reallocation**
6. **Voluntary instruments only**
7. **Management and owner contributions**
8. **Investment and product allocation for German sites**
9. **Effective co-determination in implementation**
10. **A plan to preserve skills, apprenticeships, and industrial capability**

That is a restructuring we could defend. Everything else is a dressed-up transfer of risk to the workforce.

## Bottom line
I reject the idea that closure must remain a live option to make management credible.  
I reject the idea that compulsory redundancies are an unavoidable tool.  
I reject time-limited “security” that merely postpones the same threat.

We are ready to negotiate a tough restructuring.  
We are **not** ready to negotiate away German plants, job security, or co-determination.

If management wants to keep talking, bring a quantified package that protects every German plant and every job security guarantee — not a staged pathway to acceptable layoffs.
