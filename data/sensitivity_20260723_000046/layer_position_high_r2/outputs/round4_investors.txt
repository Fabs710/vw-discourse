We are prepared to be very clear.

## Our position
We support a restructuring that **credibly restores competitive margins** through **disciplined, durable cost reduction**. We will not support:
- a cosmetic settlement,
- a package that leaves the cost base structurally uncompetitive,
- or a deal that preserves every legacy feature while equity value keeps eroding.

That is our line.

## To management
We agree with the diagnosis: the company has a structural competitiveness problem and the German cost base is too high.

Where we agree less is on the way you are presenting the choice. Repeating that closures and compulsory redundancies remain on the table is not, by itself, a strategy. It is a pressure mechanism.

What we need from you is the thing you have not yet given in a sufficiently decision-useful form:
- a **quantified comparison** of closure-led, hybrid, and no-closure scenarios,
- including **annual savings**, **one-time restructuring costs**, **capex**, **timing**, **headcount impact**, **utilization effects**, and especially **execution risk**.

We are not opposed to closures in principle. We are opposed to pretending they are automatically the best shareholder outcome. In this governance setting, they may create theoretical savings but still destroy value if they trigger:
- strikes,
- legal delay,
- political blockage,
- ramp-up losses,
- and prolonged output disruption.

So prove the **risk-adjusted** case. Don’t just assert the static one.

---

## To the works council
We understand your red lines, but your position is not economically sufficient as stated.

You are asking for:
- no plant closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- no weakening of guarantees,
- and preservation of every German site.

That is acceptable only if you can show, in hard numbers, that a no-closure package or hybrid package **actually closes the savings gap** and does so durably. So far, that proof has not been made.

We do agree with you on one important point: a strategy that simply starves plants of volume until “voluntary” exits become forced redundancies is not acceptable. That would be closure by attrition, and we would not regard it as a credible compromise.

But “all plants open” is not enough. What matters is whether the footprint becomes economically viable. If the answer is capacity shifts, specialization, productivity, redeployment, and time-limited labour contributions, fine — show that it is enough. If not, then your position is protecting a structure that keeps destroying value.

---

## To the union
We agree that symbolic concessions are worthless.

Where we disagree is with the implied assumption that closures and forced redundancies should be ruled out before the economics are tested. That is too rigid. If the business case for tough structural action is real, then it should not be hidden behind politics.

At the same time, we agree with your warning that a one-sided deal dumping all pain on workers is unacceptable. Any viable package must include:
- a material employee contribution,
- management restraint,
- and owner willingness to accept that value creation requires a real reset, not just labor-side concessions.

But temporary wage restraint and bonus cuts alone are not enough if the footprint remains oversized. A serious contribution must be **durable** and **structural**.

---

## To the state government
We respect the political and regional concerns, but they do not override economic reality.

If the current footprint is too expensive, then blocking closures by principle can leave shareholders with a permanently impaired business. That is not acceptable.

You are right that conflict costs must be counted. We agree. But the comparison must also include the cost of preserving too much capacity for too long. A non-closure package that is politically attractive but economically weak is not a solution.

If you want to insist on no closures, then help produce a package that is genuinely equivalent on a risk-adjusted basis. Otherwise your position is understandable, but not sufficient.

---

## To the family holding
We are closer to you than to labour on the central question: the company needs a real competitiveness reset, and a cosmetic compromise would be value destructive.

Where we remain cautious is on the assumption that closure is the cleanest answer merely because it is the toughest one. In this governance structure, the execution risk is unusually high. Closure may be superior on a spreadsheet and inferior in present-value terms once conflict, delay, and operational disruption are counted.

So we support your insistence on a quantified comparison. But that comparison has to be genuine, not a way to slide toward closures without proving they are better **net of risk**.

A serious hybrid may well be the best outcome if it materially reduces capacity, labor cost, and complexity. But “hybrid” must not be a euphemism for slow-motion postponement.

---

## Our bottom line
We are open on the **method**, not on the **result**.

The result must be:
- competitive margins restored,
- the cost base structurally reduced,
- and the restructuring durable enough to protect long-term equity value.

If a hybrid or no-closure package can do that on a credible, risk-adjusted basis, we will support it.

If not, then we will not support a deal that keeps the company uncompetitive and calls that a settlement.
