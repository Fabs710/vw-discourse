I’ll be direct.

## My position
The family holding supports a **serious, durable restructuring** that restores competitiveness and margins. We do **not** support a cosmetic compromise that leaves the company structurally uncompetitive. We also do **not** support a value-destructive conflict that permanently damages the enterprise.

So I am not taking a dogmatic “closures at any cost” line, and I am not taking a “no closures regardless of economics” line. What matters is **risk-adjusted long-term value**: the package must be economically credible, implementable in this governance setting, and stable enough to avoid prolonged damage.

---

## To management
I agree with your diagnosis more than with the labour side’s framing: the business does face a structural competitiveness problem, and the cost base has to come down materially.

Where I push back is on your tone and sequencing. Repeating that closures and compulsory redundancies “remain on the table” is not, by itself, a governance strategy. In this company, with co-determination and a blocking state shareholder, that is an escalation path with major execution risk.

The expert’s analysis matters here: **closure may be the strongest structural lever, but it is not automatically the best value outcome once execution risk, delay, conflict, and transition costs are included**.

So I want from management:
- a **quantified side-by-side comparison** of closure-led, hybrid, and no-closure scenarios,
- a clear statement of what savings are truly required,
- and a realistic assessment of what can be achieved without detonating industrial peace.

If you cannot show that closure is superior on a **risk-adjusted present-value basis**, then you should not treat it as the default answer. If you can show that, then we will not ignore it. But the burden is on you to prove it, not merely assert it.

---

## To the works council
I respect your focus on plant and job security. I also accept that any restructuring must preserve the company’s social legitimacy and avoid turning workers into the shock absorber for management’s strategic problems.

Where I disagree is your insistence that a binding no-closure, no-compulsory-redundancy stance should be the starting point **before the economics are tested**. That is not a solution; it is a veto on the restructuring problem.

If the employee side wants site preservation, then it must bring more than guarantees. It must bring a package that materially closes the cost gap:
- capacity reduction,
- productivity improvement,
- working-time flexibility,
- voluntary exits,
- and a real employee contribution.

A no-closure package can be legitimate. But it must be **deep and durable**, not symbolic and temporary. If it only preserves the current cost structure under a new slogan, then it fails the company.

---

## To the union
I agree with you on one thing: symbolic concessions are worthless. The company needs real savings, not gestures.

Where I disagree is your assumption that closures are therefore illegitimate or that they can be ruled out upfront. Painful measures are not automatically wrong if they are necessary to protect the enterprise’s future.

What I cannot support is a deal that says:
- no closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- no real structural labour-cost change,
- but somehow restored margins and a healthy investment program.

That does not add up.

If you want to avoid closures, then the labour contribution has to be **material**, **time-consistent**, and **structural**. Temporary bonus waivers and short-term flexibility alone will not solve a persistent overcapacity and cost problem.

---

## To the state government
I understand your political and regional responsibility. We share the concern that the company should not hollow out its German industrial base.

But a political veto cannot substitute for an economically credible plan. If non-closure alternatives do not actually deliver the required savings, then resisting closures alone does not solve the problem; it only delays it.

So I support your insistence that any alternative must be quantified and serious. But I do not accept the implication that closures are off-limits as a matter of principle. They are a tool, and if the economics require them, governance must be honest about that.

That said, if a hybrid or non-closure package genuinely delivers equivalent durable savings with less execution risk, I would prefer that. That is exactly why we need numbers, not slogans.

---

## To institutional investors
You are right to insist on a credible route back to margins and to reject a cosmetic compromise.

Where I am more cautious than you is on the assumption that the harshest headline is automatically the best shareholder outcome. In this governance setting, a closure-led plan may create theoretical savings but still destroy value if it triggers prolonged conflict, governance deadlock, or operational disruption.

So yes:
- the cost base must come down,
- capacity must be rationalized,
- and the return-on-sales problem must be solved.

But the route has to be **implementable**, not just severe.

---

## On the expert assessment
The expert has done useful work by clarifying the real trade-offs.

What I take from it is:
- a **pure no-closure package** can work only if concessions are unusually deep and durable;
- a **closure-led package** has the strongest structural savings but the highest execution risk;
- a **hybrid package** may be the best balance of economics and feasibility.

That does **not** mean I am endorsing the easy political answer. It means I want everyone to stop debating slogans and show which package actually works on a risk-adjusted basis.

My own leaning, given the governance environment, is that a **serious hybrid** is more likely to preserve value than either an all-or-nothing closure strategy or a soft no-closure deal. But “hybrid” must mean real structural change, not slow-motion closure by attrition.

---

## What would move the family holding
We would support a settlement only if it is:

1. **Economically credible**
   - savings are real, quantified, and durable.

2. **Implementable**
   - it can survive the governance and industrial-relations reality of this company.

3. **Protective of long-term value**
   - no permanent impairment of the core asset.

4. **Stable**
   - it avoids prolonged conflict that damages production, brand, and reputation.

That means we can support:
- capacity rationalization,
- working-time flexibility,
- redeployment and qualification,
- voluntary exits,
- negotiated employee contributions,
- productivity improvements,
- and targeted operational changes.

But we cannot support:
- a fake compromise that merely postpones the reckoning,
- a settlement that leaves the company structurally uncompetitive,
- or a confrontation that destroys value while pretending to be decisive.

---

## What I am prepared to say plainly
- I support a **deep restructuring**.
- I do **not** insist on closures as an ideology.
- I do **insist** that any alternative to closures must be real, quantified, and durable.
- I will **not** accept a deal that leaves the company structurally uncompetitive.
- I will **not** accept a value-destructive conflict either.

So the next step should be a quantified comparison of:
- closure-led,
- hybrid,
- and no-closure scenarios,

with hard numbers on savings, employment, capex, timing, and execution risk.

If the numbers show that closures are truly unavoidable, then that is a hard conversation we will have. If the numbers show a credible alternative that preserves value and stability, then that is the route I would prefer.

That is the ownership view.
