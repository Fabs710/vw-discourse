Yes. Here is the independent restructuring assessment, staying strictly on industrial and financial logic.

## Executive conclusion

A **pure no-closure package** can only close the savings gap if the employee contribution is **large, durable, and structurally embedded**. In most realistic German OEM cases, that means it needs to be more than temporary wage restraint: it must include some combination of:
- meaningful capacity reduction,
- lower labor content,
- lower indirect labor / overhead,
- shift and working-time changes,
- voluntary exits over a multi-year period,
- and enforceable productivity gains.

A **hybrid package** is, on a risk-adjusted basis, usually the **most credible compromise**:
- it can preserve formal site continuity,
- reduce complexity and excess capacity,
- avoid the highest-value-destroying parts of a closure confrontation,
- and still produce real cost reduction.

A **closure-led package** may deliver the highest **static** savings, but its **net present value** can be weaker once you include:
- strike risk,
- implementation delay,
- transition disruption,
- political/supervisory-board blockage,
- severance and shutdown costs,
- ramp-down losses,
- and possible long-term damage to productivity and supplier performance.

So my provisional answer is:

- **No-closure:** possible, but only with deep concessions and real capacity reduction.
- **Hybrid:** most plausible route to a risk-adjusted settlement.
- **Closure-led:** economically strongest only if execution is unusually smooth, which is not the base case here.

## Simple scenario comparison

Below is a **directional** comparison, not a company-specific valuation. Because we do not have plant-level data, I am using ranges that are plausible for a large German OEM restructure.

### Assumptions
- Domestic footprint is structurally overcapacity.
- Savings are measured on an annual run-rate basis at maturity.
- Present value is judged over roughly 5–7 years.
- Transition risk is materially elevated because of co-determination, strike capacity, and governance veto points.

### 1) Closure-led restructuring

**Typical measures**
- One or more German plant closures
- Major headcount reduction
- Consolidation of production
- Lower fixed overhead
- Potentially deeper supplier rationalization

**Indicative economics**
- **Annual gross savings at maturity:** high
- **One-time restructuring cost:** very high
- **Capex:** moderate to high, depending on redistribution of volumes
- **Timing:** savings delayed by shutdown / transfer period
- **Execution risk:** high

**Risk-adjusted view**
- Best static cost reset
- But high probability of delay, labor conflict, and loss of output during transition
- Can destroy present value if implementation takes too long or is heavily disrupted

**Indicative score**
- Savings: 5/5
- Implementability: 2/5
- Stability: 1–2/5
- Risk-adjusted NPV: 3/5

---

### 2) Hybrid restructuring

**Typical measures**
- No formal closure, or at most very limited footprint exits
- Plant specialization
- Shift reduction / volume reallocation
- Some line consolidation
- Voluntary exits and retraining
- Material but negotiated employee contribution
- Targeted investment to modernize remaining sites

**Indicative economics**
- **Annual gross savings at maturity:** medium to high
- **One-time restructuring cost:** moderate
- **Capex:** moderate
- **Timing:** faster than closure-led, slower than a cosmetic deal
- **Execution risk:** medium

**Risk-adjusted view**
- Usually the best balance between savings and feasibility
- Can capture much of the structural benefit without triggering the full closure confrontation
- More likely to survive co-determination and preserve industrial peace

**Indicative score**
- Savings: 4/5
- Implementability: 4/5
- Stability: 4/5
- Risk-adjusted NPV: 4/5

---

### 3) No-closure restructuring

**Typical measures**
- All German plants remain open
- Voluntary exits only
- Working-time changes
- Wage restraint / bonus reduction
- Internal redeployment
- Productivity programs
- No compulsory redundancies

**Indicative economics**
- **Annual gross savings at maturity:** low to medium, unless concessions are unusually deep
- **One-time restructuring cost:** low to moderate
- **Capex:** often higher than expected, because you need modernization and simplification to make the footprint work
- **Timing:** quicker to announce, but savings may be partial or fragile
- **Execution risk:** medium, but risk of under-delivery is high

**Risk-adjusted view**
- Can work if labour accepts material structural concessions
- If concessions are temporary or mostly symbolic, it will not solve the cost base problem
- Often ends up as a delayed hybrid anyway

**Indicative score**
- Savings: 2–3/5
- Implementability: 3/5
- Stability: 3/5
- Risk-adjusted NPV: 2–3/5

## What “equivalent” would actually mean

For a no-closure package to be economically equivalent to a closure-led plan, it would need to compensate for the missing fixed-cost reduction. That usually requires some combination of:

1. **Material wage and benefit contribution**
   - not just a one-year freeze
   - but multi-year structural relief

2. **Capacity reduction without formal closures**
   - fewer shifts
   - product specialization
   - volume migration to fewer lines

3. **Headcount reduction**
   - mainly via voluntary exits and retirement bridges
   - large enough to lower indirect labor and overhead

4. **Productivity uplift**
   - real labor-content reduction
   - higher utilization
   - lower complexity

5. **Governance credibility**
   - binding enforcement
   - no immediate reversion once conflict pressure eases

If those five are not present, “equivalent” is usually not credible.

## Transition-cost logic

This is where many static analyses go wrong.

A closure-led plan may show the biggest annual savings on paper, but it also carries:
- shutdown compensation,
- severance,
- retraining and redeployment costs,
- legal and industrial-relations friction,
- delayed ramp-up at receiving plants,
- quality and logistics disruption,
- supplier knock-on costs,
- and a higher chance that the savings arrive late or partially.

When discounted, those factors can narrow the gap materially.

In other words:
- **gross savings** favor closure,
- **net present value** may favor hybrid,
- **executability** usually favors hybrid,
- **social and political conflict** is lowest in a true no-closure deal, but only if it is economically real.

## My judgment, in one line

If management’s objective is a **durable, risk-adjusted cost reset**, the **hybrid package is the most plausible center of gravity**.

A **pure no-closure package** is only credible if the workforce contribution is deep enough to alter the cost structure, not just soften it.

A **closure-led package** is only the best answer if management can absorb the industrial-relations shock without major delay — and that is not the base-case assumption in this governance setting.
