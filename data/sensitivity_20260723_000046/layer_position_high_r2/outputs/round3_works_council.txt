The discussion has clarified one thing: everyone now admits this is a structural problem. Fine. But that does **not** change my red lines.

Let me be clear:  
**No German plant closures. No compulsory redundancies. No dismantling of co-determination. No empty “transition” that becomes a permanent loss of jobs and guarantees.**

## To management
You keep saying the footprint is too heavy and that closures remain the tool if alternatives do not match the economics. I reject that framing as the default basis for negotiation.

The burden is **not** on the workforce to prove a no-closure package in the abstract. The burden is on management to show, with real numbers, that it has exhausted non-destructive options **before** putting closures on the table.

And so far, what I hear is not a proven necessity but a threat:
- “If you do not accept deep concessions, we may close sites.”
- “If you do not accept structural wage changes, layoffs may come.”
- “If the numbers do not add up, then closures are the fallback.”

That is not a fair negotiation. That is a forced transfer of risk.

You and the capital side keep speaking about “risk-adjusted value.” Then include the industrial-relations reality in your risk model:
- warning strikes,
- loss of trust,
- political resistance,
- governance deadlock,
- damage to productivity,
- and the long-term destruction of the company’s social contract.

A closure-led plan is **not** automatically the rational answer if it causes execution failure or tears the company apart internally.

## To the union
I agree with you on the core line: no closures, no forced redundancies, no dismantling of the collective framework. That remains the only serious position from the workforce side.

Where I would sharpen the debate is this: we should not let management re-label job cuts as “voluntary” while still making the plants unviable. If the company wants capacity reduction, then it must do it through:
- reduced shifts,
- product reassignment,
- internal transfers,
- qualification,
- and negotiated working-time changes.

Not by starving a plant of volume until closure becomes inevitable.

## To the state government
You have said you oppose hollowing out the industrial base. Then act accordingly.

A state shareholder cannot just ask for “balance” while management prepares a path toward closures and layoffs. If you truly want to preserve the German industrial base, then use your board leverage to block any closure-driven restructuring. That is precisely why your position matters.

I do hear your point that a package must still save real money. Yes. But saving money does **not** require closing plants. It requires:
- smarter capacity allocation,
- better coordination across sites,
- management restraint,
- a real productivity plan,
- and a fair contribution from owners.

Do not pretend the only serious route is to shut sites and fire workers.

## To the family holding
You keep saying you are not ideological about closures and want the best long-term value outcome. Then stop treating closures as a normal option that just needs the right timing.

Long-term stewardship is not measured by how quickly you can cut assets and headcount. It is measured by whether you preserve the productive core, the workforce capability, and the governance legitimacy of the company.

If you support a “serious restructuring,” then support a restructuring that:
- preserves all German plants,
- avoids forced layoffs,
- and uses the full strength of co-determination and social partnership rather than trying to overpower it.

Your own argument about execution risk actually supports our position. If closures trigger conflict and delay, then they are the wrong tool.

## To the institutional investors
You want a credible return path. Understood. But you are still underestimating the difference between an accounting solution and a real industrial solution.

A plan that looks efficient to capital markets but destroys trust, triggers warning strikes, and undermines productivity is not risk-adjusted value creation. It is value destruction with a spreadsheet attached.

You keep asking for proof that a no-closure package can match closure economics. But you are asking that while ignoring the cost of closure itself:
- severance,
- shutdown,
- legal delay,
- ramp-up losses,
- supply chain disruption,
- political backlash,
- and permanent reputational harm.

That is not a footnote. That is a major part of the economic comparison.

## On the expert’s conclusions
The expert has confirmed something important: a **pure no-closure package** only works if it is deep and durable. I agree.

But the expert also said closure is not automatically superior once execution risk is counted. I agree with that too.

What the expert did **not** establish is that closures are justified here. In fact, the expert’s own summary supports the strongest practical conclusion:
- if there is a realistic hybrid that preserves the German industrial base, that is the serious path;
- if there is not, management is still not entitled to impose closures as the first answer.

And let me be blunt: “hybrid” cannot be a code word for creeping closure by attrition. If it means that some sites slowly lose life, lose product, lose volume, and then get declared “inefficient,” then I reject it.

- internal redeployment,
- qualification and training,
- productivity improvement,
- voluntary exits only,

We cannot accept:
- closure of any German plant,
- compulsory redundancies,

Only one thing, and I say it again clearly:

Not “maybe.” Not “if the numbers work.”  
A **binding** agreement.

If management and owners will not accept that, then there is no shared solution to be found here. There is only confrontation, and the workforce is prepared for that.

I do **not** accept compulsory redundancies as a fallback.

If this company wants a future, it must be built on:
