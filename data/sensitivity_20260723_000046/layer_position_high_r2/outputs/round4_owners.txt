I’ll be direct.

## My position
The family holding supports a **serious, durable restructuring** that restores competitiveness and margins. We do **not** support a cosmetic deal that leaves the company structurally uncompetitive. We also do **not** support a prolonged confrontation that destroys value through strikes, delay, and governance paralysis.

So I am **not** taking a dogmatic “closures at any cost” position, and I am **not** accepting “no closures regardless of economics.” What matters is whether the final package is **economically credible, implementable in this governance structure, and stable enough to protect long-term enterprise value**.

---

## To management
I agree with your diagnosis that the cost base must come down materially. The brand’s return-on-sales gap is not a theory; it is a hard problem.

Where I push back is on your tendency to frame closures and compulsory redundancies as the obvious default if alternatives do not match them point-for-point. In this company, that is not just a management decision. It is a governance problem with:
- co-determination,
- a blocking state shareholder,
- and high execution risk if industrial peace breaks.

So I want **a quantified comparison** of:
1. closure-led,
2. hybrid,
3. no-closure.

Not a rhetorical comparison — a real one, with:
- annual savings,
- one-time restructuring costs,
- timing of savings,
- capex,
- headcount impact,
- utilization impact,
- strike / delay risk,
- and expected present value.

If you cannot show that closure is superior on a **risk-adjusted basis**, then you should not treat it as the default answer. If you can show that, then we will confront that reality. But the burden is on management to prove it, not merely assert it.

Also: “voluntary exits,” “redeployment,” and “working-time flexibility” are useful, but I agree with the expert that they are **not enough on their own** if the network remains too broad and too expensive.

---

## To the works council
I respect your concern for plant security and co-determination. Those are not trivial matters.

But I disagree with the way you are setting the benchmark. You are effectively saying: all German plants stay open, no compulsory redundancies, no weakening of guarantees, and closures are off the table before the economics are tested. That is not a restructuring solution; it is a **veto on restructuring**.

I also do not accept the implication that closure is automatically the irrational route because it is conflict-heavy. Conflict cost is real, yes. But so is the cost of carrying a structurally uncompetitive footprint. The question is not whether closure hurts — of course it does. The question is whether the alternative is economically real.

So if you want to avoid closures, then show a package that genuinely:
- reduces fixed cost,
- reduces capacity,
- materially changes the workforce cost base,
- and does so durably.

A no-closure package can be legitimate. But it must be **deep and durable**, not symbolic or temporary.

And let me be equally clear: I will not support a compromise that preserves all sites and all legacy guarantees while the cost base remains structurally too high. That would be a slow decline dressed up as social responsibility.

---

## To the union
I agree with you on one point: symbolic concessions are worthless. That is correct.

Where I disagree is with the assumption that plant closures and compulsory redundancies can simply be ruled out in advance as a matter of principle. In this governance environment, that is not a serious negotiating posture unless you can show an alternative that truly matches the economics.

I also agree that “voluntary” can become forced redundancy by another name if sites are starved of volume. That risk is real. But the answer is not to freeze the network as-is and hope pressure goes away. The answer is a **serious negotiated package** with:
- capacity adjustment,
- productivity improvement,
- working-time changes,
- voluntary exits,
- and a meaningful employee contribution.

If the employee contribution is only temporary wage restraint or bonus cuts, that may help industrial peace, but it will not solve a persistent overcapacity and cost problem.

So: yes to shared sacrifice. No to pretending that shared sacrifice without structural change is enough.

---

## To the state government
I understand your political responsibility and your concern for the regional industrial base. I do not dismiss that.

But I do reject the idea that a political preference against closures substitutes for an economic plan. If you want to block closures, then help produce a package that actually works:
- lower fixed costs,
- real capacity reduction,
- productivity gains,
- and a durable contribution from labour and management.

You are right that the cost of conflict must be counted. But so must the cost of preserving too much capacity for too long.

I am open to a hybrid if it is real. I am not open to a slogan. And I would caution against assuming that “no closures” automatically means “no hard choices.” It does not. It means harder choices inside the plant network, and those too must be quantified.

---

## To institutional investors
I am aligned with you on the need for disciplined restructuring and the rejection of cosmetic compromise.

Where I am more cautious than you is on treating the harshest headline outcome as automatically the best shareholder outcome. It may not be. In a co-determined German OEM, the execution risk can easily destroy the apparent savings.

So I support your demand for hard numbers — but those numbers must include:
- transition costs,
- conflict costs,
- delay,
- legal and political drag,
- and lost output during implementation.

If those are fully counted, then maybe closures are still superior. Maybe not. But we should not pretend the comparison is simple.

---

## On the expert view
The expert’s analysis is useful.

My reading is:
- **pure no-closure** only works if concessions are unusually deep and durable;
- **closure-led** has the strongest structural savings but the highest execution risk;
- **hybrid** is often the most plausible compromise.

I agree with that.

Where I differ from the labour side is in the conclusion. The expert does **not** say closures are required. The expert says the right question is which package gives the best **risk-adjusted, durable cost reset**.

That is also my question.

And to be blunt: I think a **serious hybrid** is more likely to preserve value than either an all-or-nothing closure strategy or a soft no-closure deal. But “hybrid” must mean real structural change, not attrition disguised as compromise.

---

## What would move the family holding
We would support a settlement if it is:

1. **Economically credible**
   - savings are real, quantified, and durable.

2. **Implementable**
   - it can survive the governance and industrial-relations reality.

3. **Protective of long-term value**
   - no permanent impairment of the core asset.

4. **Stable**
   - it avoids prolonged conflict that damages production, brand, and reputation.

That means we can support:
- capacity rationalization,
- working-time flexibility,
- redeployment and qualification,
- voluntary exits,
- productivity improvements,
- negotiated employee contributions,
- and selective operational changes.

But we cannot support:
- a fake compromise that merely postpones the reckoning,
- a settlement that leaves the company structurally uncompetitive,
- or a confrontation that destroys value while calling itself principled.

---

## My bottom line
- I support a **deep restructuring**.
- I do **not** insist on closures as an ideology.
- I do insist that any alternative to closures must be real, quantified, and durable.
- I will **not** support a deal that leaves the company structurally uncompetitive.
- I will **not** support a value-destructive conflict either.

So the next step should be a quantified comparison of:
- closure-led,
- hybrid,
- and no-closure scenarios,

with hard numbers on savings, employment, capex, timing, and execution risk.

If the numbers show closures are truly unavoidable, then we will have to face that. If the numbers show a credible alternative that preserves value and stability, that is the route I would prefer.

That is the ownership view.
