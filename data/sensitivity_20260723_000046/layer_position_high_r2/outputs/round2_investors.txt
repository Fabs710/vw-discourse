We are aligned with management, the family holding, and the expert on the core diagnosis: **this is a structural competitiveness problem, not a temporary dip**. If the German cost base stays where it is, margins remain impaired and the equity case weakens further. We will not support a settlement that merely preserves the status quo under a nicer label.

## Where we agree
- **A durable cost reset is necessary.**
- **Symbolic concessions are worthless.**
- **Capacity has to come down somehow.**
- **The solution must be implementable, not just politically palatable.**
- **Industrial conflict has value costs too.**

The expert’s point is especially important: a closure-led solution is not automatically superior if it is delayed, contested, and operationally messy. We care about **risk-adjusted value**, not headline toughness.

## Where we disagree
### To the works council and union
Your insistence on **no closures, no compulsory redundancies, no meaningful erosion of guarantees** is not enough unless you can show a package that actually matches the savings problem in hard numbers. So far, I have not seen that.

A promise to preserve all sites and all jobs through 2030, while asking for only limited and time-bound concessions, sounds stable — but if the economics do not add up, it is just a postponement of the problem. That is unacceptable to us.

We are not persuaded by arguments that basically say: “trust us, we can improve enough without real structural pain.” Maybe, but show it. Right now the burden of proof is on the no-closure camp.

### To the state government
We respect the political and regional constraints, but they cannot substitute for economic reality. If the company’s current footprint is too expensive, then preserving every site by fiat risks leaving shareholders with a structurally weaker business. That is not a sustainable anchor strategy.

### To management
We support a hard restructuring, but we want **proof of equivalence** before endorsing closures as the default. The expert’s assessment is useful here: a hybrid package may well be the best balance of economics and feasibility. But if management wants the capital markets to back a no-closure compromise, the numbers must show:
- permanent savings,
- reduced overcapacity,
- measurable productivity gains,
- and a clear path back to competitive returns.

## What would move us
We would back whichever path delivers the strongest **risk-adjusted** outcome, but only if it is concrete. That means a quantified comparison of:

1. **Closure-led scenario**
   - highest theoretical savings,
   - but also highest execution and conflict risk.

2. **Hybrid scenario**
   - likely the most plausible balance,
   - if it removes enough fixed cost and capacity to restore margins.

3. **No-closure scenario**
   - acceptable only if it produces equivalent durable savings, not temporary relief.

Right now, the most convincing route appears to be a **hybrid restructuring** if and only if it is tough enough: real capacity reduction, material labor-cost moderation, voluntary exits, productivity gains, and enough simplification to reset margins.

## Our red line
We will not support:
- a cosmetic compromise,
- an outcome that leaves the cost base uncompetitive,
- or a deal that preserves legacy structures while margins stay structurally impaired.

## Bottom line
We are open on the method, not on the result.  
If a no-closure package can truly match the economics of closure on a durable basis, fine. If not, then management and owners should not be expected to carry an underpowered compromise just to avoid confrontation.

So our position is:
- **competitiveness first,**
- **durability second,**
- **method third.**

If helpful, our next ask would be a **side-by-side quantified scenario table** from management showing expected savings, capex, timing, headcount impact, and execution risk for:
- closure-led,
- hybrid,
- and no-closure options.
