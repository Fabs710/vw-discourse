I will respond plainly.

## My position
I support a **serious restructuring** because the competitiveness problem is real.  
I do **not** support a restructuring that solves the problem by **closing German plants or imposing compulsory redundancies**. That would violate the regional and public-interest responsibilities attached to our shareholding and would damage the company’s role as an economic anchor.

I am open to **hard, negotiated measures** if they:
- protect German sites,
- avoid forced layoffs,
- preserve co-determination and our veto role,
- and deliver real, durable cost improvement.

Anything short of that is not acceptable to me.

---

## To management
You are right about one thing: this cannot be cosmetic.  
But you are wrong if you think the only serious options are closures and possible forced redundancies.

You have not shown that every non-closure route has been exhausted. You keep treating plant closures as a normal instrument. In this company’s governance structure, and given the regional consequences, that is not a neutral technical choice. It is an escalation with major political, industrial-relations, and long-term economic costs.

What I need from management is not more insistence, but **quantified proof**:
- What exact savings are required?
- What fixed-cost reduction is needed?
- What can be achieved through capacity reduction, working-time flexibility, voluntary exits, internal redeployment, and productivity measures?
- What employee contribution would be considered fair and durable?
- What investment commitments would accompany it?

If you cannot show that a non-closure package is insufficient, then I will not accept closures as the default answer.

---

## To the works council
I share your core red lines:
- no closures in Germany,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- no weakening of co-determination,
- no dismantling of long-standing protections.

I also agree that employees cannot be asked to carry the entire burden. Any contribution must be **limited, negotiated, and matched** by management and owners.

Where I would be more precise is this: a no-closure package must still be **serious enough to restore competitiveness**. I cannot support a settlement that simply preserves everything as it is and hopes the market becomes kinder. That would not protect jobs in the long run.

So yes to:
- internal redeployment,
- qualification,
- shift and working-time adjustments,
- voluntary exits,
- and a time-limited employee contribution.

But only if the package is genuinely durable and comes with credible investment and product commitments.

---

## To the union
Your insistence that symbolic concessions are worthless is correct.  
Your insistence that the workforce should not be made the shock absorber for management error is also correct.

Where I disagree is with any implication that a binding no-closure stance is merely political rhetoric. For the state, it is a legitimate governance position because the company is not just a financial asset; it is part of the region’s industrial backbone.

That said, a credible alternative to closure must still have real economic substance. If the employee side wants site security, then it must be prepared to support a package that materially improves productivity and lowers costs. I will back that if it is fair and time-bounded.

---

## To the family holding
You are right to insist on long-term value and to reject a cosmetic compromise.  
You are also right that a prolonged conflict would destroy value.

But I disagree with the way closure is being normalized as a “tool.” In this governance setting, closures are not clean managerial optimization. They carry high execution risk and can permanently weaken the company’s social legitimacy and regional embeddedness.

If there is a hybrid solution that preserves the industrial base and achieves the required savings, that is preferable. But it must be real, not a disguised path to attrition and shutdown by another name.

---

## To institutional investors
I understand your focus on risk-adjusted value and margin recovery.  
That is legitimate.

But I do not accept the implicit assumption that the harshest structural cut is automatically the best shareholder outcome. In this company, closure risk is not just a line item; it can trigger delay, strikes, legal conflict, political resistance, and loss of trust that damage value in a very real way.

So yes, the cost base must come down.  
No, the answer is not automatically closures and layoffs.

---

## On the expert assessment
The expert has helped clarify the real trade-off:
- a **pure no-closure package** works only if concessions are deep and durable;
- a **closure-led package** may be strong on paper but risky in execution;
- a **hybrid package** may be the most plausible compromise.

That is useful, but it does **not** change my core line.

For me, the question is not which option is toughest.  
The question is which option:
- protects regional employment,
- preserves the industrial base,
- sustains co-determination,
- and still gives the company a viable future.

On that basis, I am prepared to support a **hard negotiated hybrid** only if it is built around:
- preservation of German sites,
- no forced layoffs,
- meaningful but limited employee contribution,
- management and owner contributions,
- and binding investment commitments.

I will not support a hybrid if it is simply a slow-motion closure strategy.

---

## What would move me
I could support a settlement if it contains all of the following:
1. **No closure of German plants**
2. **No compulsory redundancies**
3. **A quantified savings package**
4. **Fair burden-sharing**
5. **Investment and product commitments through 2030**
6. **Protection of co-determination and the state’s veto role**

That is the line. It has not changed.

---

## What I reject
I reject:
- treating closures as the default solution,
- using compulsory redundancies as a bargaining instrument,
- a deal that weakens the workforce’s rights,
- or any outcome that hollows out the company’s regional function.

---

## My conclusion
We are not here to preserve the status quo for its own sake.  
We are here to preserve the company as a **strong, modern, regional industrial anchor**.

That requires restructuring.  
It does **not** require plant closures or forced redundancies.

If management wants our support, it must bring a package that proves the opposite with numbers — not assumptions. Until then, my position remains firm.
