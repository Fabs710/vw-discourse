I’ll be direct.

## To the works council
We are aligned on the core red lines:
- **no compulsory redundancies**,
- **no dismantling of the collective wage framework**,
- and **no outcome where the workforce bears all the pain**.

I also agree that any “transition” must not be a disguised permanent downgrade of jobs, pay, or co-determination. If management tries to smuggle closures or forced layoffs in under softer language, we will oppose that just as firmly.

Where I add a point is this: we should not let the company turn the debate into a false choice between “everything stays as it is” and “closures tomorrow.” If the company needs a restructuring, then it must be negotiated as a **shared-sacrifice package** with real contributions from management and owners. That is the only route that can still preserve jobs and industrial peace.

## To management
Your position is still too close to a pressure tactic.

You keep saying:
- closures remain on the table,
- compulsory redundancies remain possible,
- and labour concessions must be material.

That is not a negotiated starting point; it is a threat architecture. It may be legally permissible in your view, but it is not a basis for durable settlement.

I do agree with one part of your argument: **symbolic concessions are not enough**. The company does need real savings. But you have not shown that the only way to get them is by pushing plant closures and layoffs into the foreground. The burden is on management to demonstrate a genuinely balanced package, not just to repeat that the gap is serious.

And I want to be clear on this: a package that lowers wages, worsens conditions, and still leaves the company free to close sites later is not a compromise. It is a one-way transfer of risk onto employees.

## To the state government
I appreciate that you are trying to balance industrial competitiveness with regional responsibility. On substance, we share a lot:
- no hollowing out of the industrial base,
- no forced layoffs as the default,
- and no value-destructive confrontation.

But “balance” cannot mean that workers are asked to contribute while management and owners keep all strategic options open. If the state wants employee restraint, it must help create **binding security** in return.

That means:
- support for **site security**,
- support for **no compulsory redundancies**,
- and a role in ensuring that any employee contribution is matched by management and ownership contributions.

If you are serious about the German industrial base, then your leverage should be used to prevent the burden from being dumped on labour alone.

## To the family holding
You say you are focused on long-term value and not ideological about closures. Good. Then long-term value must include:
- stable workforce relations,
- preserved collective agreements,
- and an implementation path that does not trigger lasting industrial conflict.

Where I disagree with you is your suggestion that if the numbers justify it, closures may simply be the rational tool. That may be true in a spreadsheet. It is not enough in a co-determined industrial company if the social and governance costs are catastrophic.

You are right to insist on a quantified comparison. I support that. But the comparison must include:
- strike risk,
- loss of trust,
- political resistance,
- and the damage from tearing up the collective framework.

If those costs are fully counted, a closure-led path may look far less attractive than you imply.

## To institutional investors
You want risk-adjusted value, not slogans. Fine. Then include the risk of confrontation in your calculation.

I agree with you that the company cannot be held in a status quo that is structurally uncompetitive. I do **not** agree that the right answer is to use the workers as the shock absorber for the entire restructuring.

If you want a credible path back to margins, then support a package where:
- management contributes through restraint and operational discipline,
- owners contribute through stewardship rather than just pressure,
- and employees contribute in a limited, time-bound, fair way.

But I will not accept a bargain that says: “accept wage pain now, and trust us on jobs later.” That is exactly the kind of open-ended insecurity that members will not accept.

## What we can support
We remain open to a **serious negotiated package** that includes:
- capacity reduction,
- productivity gains,
- working-time flexibility,
- internal redeployment,
- voluntary exits,
- and a **material but limited employee contribution**.

That contribution could include, for example:
- temporary wage restraint,
- reduced bonuses,
- flexible working-time arrangements,
- and other negotiated cost measures.

But only if there are **binding protections** in return:
- no compulsory redundancies,
- no plant closures in Germany,
- and a durable settlement that protects the collective wage framework.

## What we will not accept
Let me be equally clear:
- no compulsory redundancies,
- no plant closures in Germany,
- no permanent wage dumping,
- no erosion of collective bargaining standards,
- and no one-sided restructuring where workers pay and everyone else waits.

## On the expert view
The expert basically confirms what we already know:
- a pure no-closure package only works if the concessions are deep and durable,
- closures may look cleaner on paper but carry major execution risk,
- and a hybrid arrangement may be the most realistic industrial compromise.

That does **not** mean we should accept closures as the “serious” option. It means management must put real numbers on the table for a non-closure path and stop assuming that plant shutdown is the default answer.

If the hybrid path is truly the best balance, then it must be built around:
- site security,
- job security,
- and negotiated burden-sharing.

Not around creeping redundancies or future shutdown rights disguised as flexibility.

## My position, unchanged
The union’s position remains:
- **no plant closures in Germany**,
- **no compulsory redundancies**,
- **protect the collective wage agreement**,
- **yes to a fair, limited, negotiated employee contribution**,
- **but only if management, owners, and — where relevant — the state also contribute materially**.

If management wants an agreement, bring a package that meets those conditions.  
If not, then this is not a settlement discussion. It is a confrontation over who pays for the crisis.
