## Round 4 — Industrial Union Position

I will be equally clear.

### To the works council
We are still aligned on the essentials:
- **no compulsory redundancies**
- **no plant closures**
- **no dismantling of the collective wage framework**
- **no creeping attrition disguised as “transition”**

I agree with your warning that “voluntary” can become forced redundancy by another name if management starves sites of volume. That is exactly why the union will not sign off on a package that leaves plants alive only on paper while jobs are quietly destroyed.

Where I would sharpen the point is this: we should not let management define the benchmark as “closure or nothing.” That framing is a pressure tactic, not a fair negotiation basis. If there is a restructuring path, it must be one that preserves jobs and sites and shares the burden fairly.

### To management
Your position is still too one-sided.

You say the facts justify closures and possible forced redundancies. I say: you have **not** shown that those are necessary once you count the full costs:
- shutdown and severance costs,
- legal delay,
- strikes and warning strikes,
- trust loss,
- supply-chain disruption,
- ramp-up losses,
- and the long-term damage to labor relations.

You keep asking labour to prove a no-closure alternative in the abstract. That is backwards. The burden is on management to demonstrate that it has exhausted non-destructive options before threatening plant closures.

And I want to be blunt: a deal that lowers wages, weakens guarantees, or cuts benefits while keeping the option of future closures open is **not** a compromise. It is a one-way transfer of risk onto the workforce.

### To the state government
I appreciate that you are more openly aligned with site and job security than management is. But “balance” cannot mean asking workers for restraint while keeping closure threats alive.

If you want the workforce to contribute, then you must also help create **binding security**. That means:
- no forced redundancies,
- no closures,
- and a negotiated package that cannot later be used as a bridge to plant shutdowns.

The state’s leverage matters precisely because this is not a normal shareholder situation. Use it to protect the industrial base, not to legitimize a threat architecture.

### To the family holding
You say you are focused on long-term value and are open to a hybrid if it is real. Fine. But I reject the way closure keeps being normalized as the “serious” option.

Long-term value is not just a spreadsheet result. It depends on:
- workforce trust,
- stable production,
- co-determination legitimacy,
- and avoiding industrial war.

So if you are genuinely comparing scenarios on a risk-adjusted basis, then you must accept that closure-led restructuring carries very high execution risk and can destroy value even if the static savings look better. That risk is not secondary. It is central.

### To institutional investors
You are right that symbolic measures are not enough. I agree.

But you are not right if you think the only meaningful savings come from closures or forced layoffs. Durable savings can also come from:
- capacity reduction through shifts and product allocation,
- internal redeployment,
- qualification,
- voluntary exits,
- temporary wage restraint,
- bonus reductions,
- working-time flexibility,
- and productivity improvement.

What you still have not answered is why a fair, negotiated contribution from workers, combined with management restraint and owner contribution, cannot close enough of the gap to avoid destroying sites and jobs.

### On the expert view
The expert confirmed something important: a **pure no-closure package** can work only if it is deep and durable.

That is correct. And that is exactly why we are not offering a symbolic package. We are open to a **serious shared-sacrifice settlement**.

But the expert also did **not** say closures are the right answer here. The expert said the best question is which package gives the best **risk-adjusted, durable cost reset**. On that basis, I do not see a closure-led path as superior in this governance and industrial-relations environment.

### What we are prepared to discuss
We remain open to:
- capacity reduction without plant closures,
- internal transfers,
- qualification and retraining,
- working-time flexibility,
- productivity measures,
- voluntary exits,
- and a **material but limited employee contribution**.

That contribution could include:
- temporary wage restraint,
- reduced bonuses,
- flexible working-time arrangements,
- and other negotiated measures.

But only if there are **binding protections** in return:
- **no compulsory redundancies**
- **no plant closures in Germany**
- **protection of the collective wage framework**
- **a durable settlement that does not convert concessions into future job cuts**

### What we reject
We will not accept:
- plant closures,
- compulsory redundancies,
- permanent wage dumping,
- erosion of collective bargaining standards,
- or a deal where employees pay now and management keeps all strategic weapons for later.

### My bottom line
The union’s position remains unchanged:
- **no plant closures in Germany**
- **no compulsory redundancies**
- **protect the collective wage agreement**
- **yes to a fair, limited, negotiated employee contribution**
- **but only if management, owners, and the state also contribute materially**

If management wants an agreement, it needs to bring a package that meets those conditions. If it won’t, then we are not in a settlement process; we are in a confrontation over who carries the cost of the crisis.
