I’ll respond directly.

## To the works council
You keep repeating “no closures, no compulsory redundancies, no erosion of guarantees.” I understand the political logic, but I do **not** accept that as a viable starting point for a restructuring of this scale.

What you are offering is, in effect, a demand that the company absorb a structural cost problem **without the structural tools needed to fix it**. That is not a solution; it is a delay mechanism.

You say management has not shown why the savings cannot be achieved otherwise. The answer is simple: because the current footprint and labour-cost structure are too heavy for the market reality we face. If you want us to prove otherwise, then bring a package that matches the savings of closure **in hard numbers**, not in aspiration. Until then, “capacity reduction without closures” remains too vague to be credible.

I am prepared to discuss:
- reduced shifts,
- internal transfers,
- working-time flexibility,
- voluntary exits,
- and productivity measures.

But I will not pretend that these automatically equal the cost effect of shutting down excess capacity. If they do not add up, then they do not solve the problem.

## To the union
You are right about one thing: symbolic concessions are not enough. On that we agree. But you then turn that into a blanket refusal of the very structural measures that make a real package possible. That is where I disagree.

A durable employee contribution must be **material**. Temporary bonus waivers and short-term flexibility alone will not restore competitiveness. We need lower structural labour costs and a leaner capacity base. If the union’s answer is “yes, but only if nothing meaningful changes,” then there is no basis for an agreement.

You also say compulsory redundancies are off the table. I am not using them as a threat for effect. I am stating a reality: if we cannot reach a serious negotiated solution, then the company will have to use the instruments available to it. I would prefer to avoid that. But I will not rule it out while the structural gap remains open.

## To the state government
I respect the concern about hollowing out the industrial base. But preserving the base by preserving every site and every guarantee regardless of economics is not stewardship. It is denial.

You say you want a negotiated restructuring with no closures and no compulsory redundancies. I ask you plainly: **how exactly do you intend to deliver the required savings at that scale while keeping all sites open and all jobs protected to 2030?**

If you have a realistic mechanism that produces equivalent savings, I am listening. If not, then your position is not a solution — it is a political preference. And political preference does not restore return on sales.

## To the family owners
I agree with you more than with most others here on one central point: this cannot be a cosmetic fix. The company needs a competitiveness reset that is durable, not performative.

Where I push back is on the suggestion that closures should only be used as an absolute last resort. They are not an ideological choice; they are a tool. If the economics require them, then refusing to use them because they are difficult would be irresponsible ownership and irresponsible management.

That said, I also agree that prolonged conflict would destroy value. So if there is a genuine alternative that delivers the same structural result, I will take it seriously. But it must be real, measurable, and quickly implementable.

## To the institutional investors
You are right to insist on a credible path back to margins. That is exactly what management is trying to secure.

But you are wrong if you think the only measure of credibility is whether the toughest possible headline is achieved. A restructuring that looks strong on paper but triggers prolonged industrial conflict, output loss, reputational damage, and execution failure is not credible. It is self-defeating.

So I agree with your demand for:
- durable cost reduction,
- real capacity reset,
- and a measurable path to margin recovery.

I do **not** agree that any package preserving sites and jobs by definition fails. If a non-closure package is sufficiently deep and durable, it can be credible. But again, it must be equivalent in economic effect — not merely politically easier.

## The core issue
Let me be explicit:

- We need a **structural reduction in cost and capacity**.
- We need a **material labour-cost contribution**.
- We need **real flexibility** in the workforce model.
- We need to free funds for **future products and technology to 2030**.
- And we need to do this fast enough to matter.

A deal that preserves the current cost base and simply trades vague assurances for temporary concessions is unacceptable.

## What would move management
I remain open to alternatives to closures, but only if they deliver all of the following:
1. **Durable capacity reduction** — not just internal reshuffling.
2. **A real reduction in labour cost** — not symbolic restraint.
3. **Implementation certainty** — not open-ended negotiations.
4. **A credible path to margin improvement** — visible in the medium term.
5. **Freedom to invest** — because without that, there is no future competitiveness.

If the employee side and the state want to avoid closures, then they need to bring forward a package that meets those conditions. Not a principle. Not a protest. A package.

## My position, unchanged where it matters
- **Plant closures remain on the table.**
- **Compulsory redundancies remain possible if no sufficient agreement is reached.**
- **I am open to alternatives only if they achieve equivalent, lasting savings.**
- **I will not accept a settlement that leaves the company structurally uncompetitive.**

If anyone wants to test that line, then bring me the numbers. Otherwise, we are just debating slogans while the market continues to move against us.
