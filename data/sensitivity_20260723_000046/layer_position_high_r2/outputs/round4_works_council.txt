Let me respond directly to each side.

## To management
Your argument still starts from an assumption I reject: that closures and forced layoffs are the natural benchmark and everything else must prove itself against them.

That is backwards.

In a co-determined company with a blocking state shareholder and a workforce that has already shown it will mobilize, a closure-led path is not “the default rational answer.” It is the **most conflict-heavy answer** and, in this governance setting, very likely the **least reliable** one.

You keep saying:
- the footprint is too large,
- the cost base is too high,
- and alternatives must match the economics.

Fine. Then bring a **real industrial plan** and stop using closure as a bargaining club.

What I do **not** accept is:
- “no closure” being dismissed as political,
- while closure is treated as serious,
- even though closure creates shutdown costs, strike risk, trust destruction, and implementation delay.

If you want to claim closures are economically superior, then prove it on a **risk-adjusted basis**, not with a one-sided accounting exercise.

## To the union
We are aligned on the essentials, and I support your insistence that the workforce cannot pay for this crisis with jobs, sites, and rights.

I also agree with you that “voluntary” is often just forced redundancy in disguise when the plant is starved of volume. That is exactly why I will not accept a slow attrition strategy hidden behind softer language.

But I want to sharpen one point: we should not let management turn “material savings” into a code word for breaking the social contract. There are only two acceptable routes:
1. **real operational improvement without closures**, or
2. a negotiated package that preserves all German sites and avoids forced redundancies.

Anything else is not a compromise. It is a cut dressed up as dialogue.

## To the state government
Your position is stronger than you are sometimes stating it.

If you are serious about the regional industrial base, then you cannot just ask for “balance” while leaving closure and layoff threats on the table. Balance in this situation means using your leverage to **block any restructuring that depends on shutting German plants or imposing compulsory redundancies**.

I do hear your point that a no-closure package must still deliver real savings. Absolutely. But the state should not present closures as an acceptable fallback simply because management finds them convenient.

Your responsibility is to preserve the industrial base, not to validate a threat.

## To the family holding
You say you are not ideological and want long-term value. Then show stewardship, not pressure.

Long-term value in this company is not created by breaking guarantees and detonating trust. It is created by:
- preserving productive capacity,
- keeping know-how,
- maintaining industrial peace,
- and using co-determination as a strength rather than treating it as an obstacle.

I agree with you on one point: a fake compromise would be bad. But I disagree with the conclusion you seem to be circling toward, namely that closures are simply the sharper, therefore better, tool.

Sharper for whom? Not for the workforce. Not for industrial peace. And very possibly not for the company if execution fails.

## To institutional investors
You keep demanding “durable savings,” and I agree that symbolic gestures are not enough.

But you are making a major mistake if you assume the only durable savings come from plant closures and redundancy threats.

Durable savings can also come from:
- shift restructuring,
- workload and line balancing,
- internal redeployment,
- qualification,
- temporary employee contributions,
- management restraint,
- and real productivity improvements.

What you still have not answered is this:
**How much of the closure case is actually savings, and how much is the cost of conflict, delay, severance, legal risk, and ramp-up losses?**

Until you count those costs honestly, your “risk-adjusted” argument is incomplete.

## On the expert analysis
The expert is right on one point that matters: a **pure no-closure package** must be deep and durable to work.

That is correct. I accept that.

But the expert does **not** say closures are required. The expert says the best question is which package gives the best **risk-adjusted, durable cost reset**.

On that question, I say this plainly:
- In this governance structure,
- with this workforce,
- with this level of distrust,
- and with this strike readiness,

a closure-led path is very unlikely to be the best risk-adjusted answer.

A **hybrid** is only acceptable if it does **not** become a disguise for attrition and future shutdowns. If “hybrid” means lost volume, lost jobs, and eventual closure by another name, then I reject it outright.

## What we will accept
We remain open to a serious restructuring **only** if it respects these lines:

- **All German plants remain open**
- **No compulsory redundancies**
- **No dismantling of co-determination**
- **No weakening of job-security guarantees**
- **Any employee contribution must be time-bound and matched by management and owners**
- **Any savings plan must be operational, not destructive**

We are prepared to discuss:
- capacity adjustments through shifts,
- internal transfers,
- qualification,
- productivity gains,
- voluntary exits only,
- and limited, negotiated contributions.

We are **not** prepared to discuss:
- plant closures,
- forced redundancies,
- or a deal that makes closure possible later through underinvestment and starvation of sites.

## My answer to the “how much savings” question
Let me be equally direct:  
I am **not** going to help normalize closure by pretending there is a bargaining range around shutting German plants.

The minimum acceptable outcome is not a percentage of closure. It is the preservation of every German plant and no forced layoffs.

If management wants an alternative, it must bring one that fits those conditions.  
If it cannot, then the problem is not the workforce’s red lines. The problem is management’s unwillingness to do the harder work of restructuring **without** destroying jobs and sites.

## Bottom line
So my position remains unchanged:

- **No plant closures in Germany.**
- **No compulsory redundancies.**
- **No erosion of co-determination or job security.**
- **Yes to real restructuring, but only on the basis of site protection and fair burden-sharing.**

If management, owners, and capital want an agreement, they need to stop treating closures as the serious option and start treating workforce-preserving restructuring as the only legitimate one.

If they won’t, then there is no bridge here. There is only a confrontation over whether this company still honors its social contract.
