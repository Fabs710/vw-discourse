# 1) How to think about the economics

A German OEM with excess footprint typically has four cost buckets to attack:

1. **Fixed plant overhead**
   - site management
   - maintenance
   - utilities
   - indirect labor
   - local logistics
   - plant support functions

2. **Direct labor**
   - wages
   - supplements
   - bonuses
   - working-time inefficiencies

3. **Underutilization cost**
   - too many plants / shifts for the volume
   - poor line loading
   - duplicated complexity

4. **Transition / restructuring cost**
   - severance
   - tooling moves
   - ramp-up losses
   - inventory buffers
   - decommissioning / remediation

A closure removes a meaningful share of buckets 1 and 3.  
A no-closure package mostly attacks buckets 2 and 3, but keeps much of bucket 1 intact.

That is why closure is usually the stronger **structural** lever when the problem is genuine overcapacity.

# 2) Scenario comparison

I will define three stylized scenarios. These are not company-specific forecasts; they are industrial logic ranges based on typical German automotive restructuring patterns.

## Scenario A: No-closure negotiated package
Assumptions:
- all German plants remain open
- no compulsory redundancies
- headcount reduction via attrition, voluntary exits, transfers, and working-time reduction
- moderate-to-material wage / bonus / benefit concessions
- some capacity reduction through shifts, product allocation, and line consolidation
- additional productivity program

### Likely effects
- **Savings:** moderate, but often insufficient unless concessions are deep
- **Timing:** slower; benefits accrue over 2–4 years
- **Execution risk:** lower than closures, but only if labor cooperation is real
- **Employment:** fewer forced losses, but still a meaningful headcount reduction likely needed
- **Capex:** moderate to high, because modernization substitutes for footprint cuts

### Industrial assessment
This scenario is only credible if employee contributions are **structural**, not symbolic. Temporary bonus waivers or short-term wage restraint will not usually close a large gap. If the company truly has a high German cost base relative to competitors, this scenario may preserve too much fixed cost.

### Rough qualitative rating
- Cost improvement: **medium**
- Durability: **medium**
- Execution risk: **medium**
- Political / social feasibility: **high**
- Ability to fix severe overcapacity: **low to medium**

---

## Scenario B: Hybrid package
Assumptions:
- selective consolidation of production and functions
- one or more sites may lose a model, shift, or activity
- some capacity is removed, but not necessarily full closure of every weak site
- voluntary exits and internal redeployment
- moderate labor concessions
- investment commitments for remaining sites

### Likely effects
- **Savings:** materially better than no-closure
- **Timing:** medium; some benefits sooner than full closure, less disruption than a hard shutdown
- **Execution risk:** still meaningful, but lower than full closure if staged well
- **Employment:** larger reductions, but more spread out
- **Capex:** often elevated initially, because surviving sites need modernization and transfer capacity

### Industrial assessment
This is often the most realistic compromise in a German co-determined environment. It can remove enough structural cost to matter while avoiding the full political and operational blast radius of closure. If the business is not in a terminal situation, hybrid is frequently the best value-for-risk trade.

### Rough qualitative rating
- Cost improvement: **medium to high**
- Durability: **high if structural**
- Execution risk: **medium**
- Political / social feasibility: **medium**
- Ability to fix severe overcapacity: **medium to high**

---

## Scenario C: Closure-led restructuring
Assumptions:
- one or more German plants closed
- output concentrated in fewer sites
- compulsory redundancies possible if needed
- stronger fixed-cost reduction
- labor concessions still likely but less central than footprint reduction

### Likely effects
- **Savings:** highest structural savings potential
- **Timing:** potentially faster on paper, but often delayed in reality
- **Execution risk:** highest in this governance setting
- **Employment:** concentrated and severe job losses
- **Capex:** significant at surviving plants; shutdown costs substantial

### Industrial assessment
If the footprint is truly too large and certain sites are structurally underloaded, closure is the cleanest way to remove persistent cost. But in this specific German governance setting, closure may be the **most expensive way to attempt the cheapest restructuring**, because delay and conflict can erode the value of the theoretical savings.

### Rough qualitative rating
- Cost improvement: **high**
- Durability: **high**
- Execution risk: **high**
- Political / social feasibility: **low**
- Ability to fix severe overcapacity: **high**

# 3) What matters most: not headline savings, but net present value

A proper comparison needs to include:

- direct restructuring cost
- loss of output during transition
- savings ramp-up timing
- labor conflict / strike risk
- capex to consolidate production
- supply chain reconfiguration
- ramp-up losses at receiving plants
- probability of delay or dilution

A closure can look superior in operating margin terms but underperform in NPV terms if:
- it takes 2–3 years to execute,
- gets delayed by governance or industrial action,
- and forces expensive dual-running.

Conversely, a no-closure deal can look softer but produce better NPV if:
- it is signed quickly,
- avoids disruption,
- and secures durable concessions.

So the real question is: **Can a no-closure deal generate enough permanent savings to offset the remaining footprint cost?**

# 4) Likely employment outcomes

## No-closure
- fewer forced exits
- more attrition and voluntary separation
- possible transfer of workers to growth areas
- likely suppression of hiring
- lower disruption to local communities

But: if the savings target is large, job reduction still has to be real.  
“No closure” does **not** mean “no headcount reduction.”

## Hybrid
- targeted job losses
- some sites shrunk, some specialized
- redeployment more feasible than in outright closure
- better chance of preserving critical capabilities

## Closure-led
- largest and fastest absolute job reduction
- sharp regional concentration of losses
- higher risk of losing plant-specific know-how
- higher political and governance friction

---

# 5) Productivity implications

## No-closure
Potentially better for knowledge retention and labor peace, but it can preserve too much complexity. To work, it needs:
- tighter staffing norms
- line balancing
- reduced variants
- automation
- stronger indirect labor cuts

Without that, it just freezes legacy inefficiency.

## Hybrid
Usually the best balance: removes some complexity, allows specialization, and preserves enough footprint to keep the network functional.

## Closure-led
Often improves utilization of remaining sites, but only if receiving plants are not overloaded. Otherwise, productivity can initially worsen due to ramp-up strain.

---

# 6) Capex and implementation burden

A crucial point: **substituting for closure with internal restructuring usually costs capex**.

If you do not close sites, you often need:
- automation
- digitalization
- tooling changes
- process redesign
- flexibility investments

That is not necessarily bad, but it means the company must fund both:
- near-term restructuring savings, and
- future competitiveness investment.

If balance sheet capacity is limited, this becomes a constraint.
