Here is the industrial-restructuring view, based on the facts and the positions stated so far.

## Core assessment

A **pure no-closure / no-forced-redundancy package** can work only if it produces **durable, quantified, structural savings** that are close to what closures would deliver. If it relies mainly on temporary wage restraint, bonus reductions, or generic “flexibility,” it will likely fall short.

A **closure-led package** usually delivers the clearest structural cost reset on paper, but in this governance setting it carries unusually high **execution risk**:
- co-determination veto risk,
- union conflict and strikes,
- political resistance,
- delay,
- lost output,
- supply-chain disruption,
- ramp-up losses after restructuring,
- and possible lasting damage to productivity and trust.

That means the best answer is not “closures are always right” or “closures are always wrong.” The right question is: **which package gives the best risk-adjusted, durable cost reset?**

## What the economics usually look like

### 1) Closure-led restructuring
**Strengths**
- Largest and most durable fixed-cost reduction.
- Cuts excess capacity directly.
- Usually gives the clearest headcount reduction.
- Can simplify the plant network and improve utilization at remaining sites.

**Costs / risks**
- One-time transition costs are high:
  - severance and social plan costs,
  - shutdown and write-off costs,
  - logistics and retooling costs,
  - transfer costs for production and tooling.
- Output disruption is likely during transition.
- Political and labor conflict can be prolonged.
- The savings can be partly offset if remaining plants run below planned utilization or if product migration is delayed.
- If the closure triggers major industrial action, the present value of savings falls sharply.

**Bottom line**
- Strongest structural lever.
- Not automatically the best value outcome once execution risk is counted.

### 2) Hybrid restructuring
This is usually the most plausible compromise in a German co-determined setting.

A hybrid can combine:
- capacity reduction without full site closure,
- product reassignment,
- line consolidation,
- shift reductions,
- voluntary exits,
- internal redeployment,
- targeted working-time changes,
- some employee cost contribution,
- and selective investment.

**Strengths**
- Lower conflict risk than outright closures.
- Better chance of preserving industrial peace.
- May avoid the worst political and governance blockages.
- Can still deliver meaningful savings if designed tightly.

**Weaknesses**
- Savings may be less certain than closure.
- If the footprint remains too broad, fixed costs may not fall enough.
- There is a real risk of “slow-motion attrition” if the hybrid is not sharply designed.
- Can require more capex and management attention to keep the network productive.

**Bottom line**
- Often the best balance of feasibility and value.
- But it only works if it is genuinely tough, not a disguised status quo.

### 3) No-closure restructuring
This can work only if the company gets **real structural concessions** without closing plants.

Possible tools:
- reduced shifts and line utilization,
- internal redeployment,
- voluntary exits,
- productivity programs,
- working-time flexibility,
- temporary or partial wage restraint,
- bonus reductions,
- changes to premium pay, allowances, or other labor-cost items.

**Strengths**
- Lowest immediate political and social disruption.
- Less risk of shutdown-related execution failures.
- Better odds of preserving trust and reducing strike intensity.
- Protects local production continuity.

**Weaknesses**
- Hardest to make durable.
- Often insufficient unless concessions are deep.
- May require management to accept a more gradual footprint adjustment.
- Can leave residual overcapacity in place.
- Often needs stronger capex or process redesign to produce comparable savings.

**Bottom line**
- Viable only if the labor contribution is substantial and enduring.
- Temporary measures alone are not enough.

---

## Employment impact

- **Closure-led**: highest job loss, with the sharpest direct headcount reduction.
- **Hybrid**: moderate job loss, usually through a mix of voluntary exits, redeployment, and targeted reductions.
- **No-closure**: lowest direct job loss, but may still require meaningful workforce reduction via natural attrition, voluntary exits, and reduced hiring.

Important point: preserving all sites does **not** mean preserving all jobs if the company is serious about competitiveness. A no-closure route still usually needs a lower labor base, just achieved more gradually and less disruptively.

---

## Capex and working-capital consequences

### Closure-led
- Capex may rise in the short term because production has to be transferred, rebalanced, or retooled.
- Working capital can improve if inventory and network complexity fall.
- But there are transition costs and possible stranded assets.

### Hybrid
- Often requires **selective capex** to improve productivity at retained sites.
- May need investment in automation, logistics, and model allocation to offset the missing savings from closures.
- Working-capital gains can be moderate if the network is simplified.

### No-closure
- Usually requires the **most operational investment discipline** to make the remaining footprint work harder.
- May need more capex per unit of savings if the company is trying to modernize rather than shrink.
- Working-capital benefits may be limited unless the network is meaningfully simplified.

---

## Transition costs and operational risks

### Transition costs
Closure packages have the highest visible transition costs:
- severance,
- closure execution,
- site wind-down,
- asset impairment,
- transfer of tooling,
- supplier changes,
- retraining and relocation.

Hybrid and no-closure packages avoid some of that, but they are not free:
- retraining,
- redeployment,
- process redesign,
- IT and planning changes,
- labor negotiations,
- productivity investments.

### Operational risks
The major risks are:
- industrial action,
- loss of trust,
- implementation delay,
- quality slippage,
- productivity drop,
- management distraction,
- and deterioration of labor relations.

These risks are especially important here because the workforce side has explicitly signaled willingness to escalate.

---

## What is most likely in this case

Given the governance structure described, a **forced closure-led solution is difficult to execute cleanly**. Even if management believes closures are economically superior in the abstract, the combination of:
- co-determination veto power,
- state blocking stake,
- labor resistance,
- and likely industrial action

means the company may not be able to realize the theoretical savings without major delay and value leakage.

That makes a **hybrid deal** the most plausible economically and politically, if one can be made sufficiently hard. But it must include:
- genuine capacity reduction,
- real headcount reduction,
- measurable labor-cost savings,
- binding productivity measures,
- and investment commitments tied to performance.

If the hybrid is too soft, it becomes simply a deferred crisis.

---

## My judgment, in one line

- **Closures**: strongest structural savings, highest execution risk.
- **No-closure**: lowest disruption, but only viable if concessions are deep and durable.
- **Hybrid**: most likely to be implementable and value-preserving, but only if it is materially tougher than a compromise on paper.
