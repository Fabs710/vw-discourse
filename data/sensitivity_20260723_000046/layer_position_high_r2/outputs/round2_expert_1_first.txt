From an industrial restructuring standpoint, the key question is not whether the company “can” cut costs without closures in the abstract; it is whether a **non-closure package can generate savings of sufficient scale, durability, and speed** to restore competitiveness **without simply deferring the same problem**.

## Bottom line

A **credible no-closure scenario** is possible only if it combines several levers at once:

- meaningful **capacity reduction**,
- **productivity gains** and tighter staffing norms,
- **working-time flexibility**,
- **labour-cost moderation**,
- **voluntary exits / natural attrition**,
- and some mix of **investment reallocation and process simplification**.

But if the structural gap is large, a no-closure package usually has three weaknesses versus closure:

1. **It saves less, or saves slower**  
   because fixed costs remain spread across too much footprint and too many layers.

2. **It tends to be less durable**  
   if it relies heavily on temporary concessions, voluntary programs, or one-off deferrals.

3. **It can preserve industrial capability but at a higher ongoing unit cost**  
   unless there is very deep operational redesign.

That said, closures are not “free” either. They typically bring:

- **large one-off transition costs**,
- significant **industrial-relations disruption**,
- loss of **installed capacity and option value**,
- potential **supply-chain diseconomies**,
- and, in a co-determined German context, high **execution risk** that can delay savings.

So the economically correct answer is:  
**closure is usually the stronger solution for structural overcapacity; non-closure can work only if the company is willing to accept deeper internal cuts and real labour-cost changes than labour-side rhetoric usually implies.**

## 1) What a no-closure savings package would have to contain

If all German plants stay open and compulsory redundancies are excluded, the savings must come from somewhere else. The realistic sources are:

### A. Capacity rationalization without closure
This can mean:
- shifting output across plants,
- reducing shift lines,
- eliminating underutilized segments,
- consolidating model allocation,
- shortening operating windows.

This helps, but if you keep all sites open, you still retain:
- site overhead,
- local management,
- maintenance,
- logistics interfaces,
- utilities,
- and a chunk of labour and indirect cost.

So this is **partial** capacity reduction, not equivalent to closure.

### B. Productivity gains
Possible levers:
- line balancing,
- reduced rework and scrap,
- automation,
- fewer indirect layers,
- better planning and scheduling,
- simplified variants/options.

These can be material, but productivity improvement is usually **gradual** and depends on:
- capex,
- process stability,
- labour cooperation,
- and management execution.

It is rarely enough on its own to close a large structural cost gap quickly.

### C. Working-time adjustments
For example:
- shorter hours,
- banked time,
- shift redesign,
- temporary flexibility in peaks and troughs.

This can reduce wage cost and improve utilization, but it is often **cyclical** or **temporary**. It does not fully solve a structural fixed-cost problem unless permanently embedded.

### D. Labour-cost concessions
This is where much of the arithmetic usually sits:
- wage restraint or reductions,
- suspension of supplements,
- lower entry pay for new cohorts,
- longer time to progression,
- reduced bonuses,
- altered guarantees/pensions.

These can generate meaningful savings, but they are often the hardest to sustain unless tied to a strong reciprocal deal.

### E. Employment adjustment without compulsory redundancies
This includes:
- voluntary severance,
- retirement bridging,
- attrition management,
- transfers to growth areas.

This reduces headcount, but it is slower and often more expensive per exit than compulsory redundancy. It also may not align the remaining workforce mix as well as a targeted restructuring.

## 2) How no-closure compares to closure economically

### Closure scenario: strengths
A plant closure can produce:
- a **step-change reduction in fixed costs**,
- removal of excess capacity,
- better network utilization at the remaining sites,
- clearer product allocation,
- fewer duplicated overheads.

If a plant is structurally underutilized, closure can be the cleanest way to eliminate persistent losses.

### Closure scenario: weaknesses
But closure also brings:
- redundancy and severance costs,
- decommissioning and remediation,
- contract exit costs,
- logistics reconfiguration,
- sunk-capital write-downs,
- transfer and ramp-up costs at receiving plants,
- productivity risk at the surviving sites if they become overloaded,
- and legal/industrial delay risk.

The bigger the co-determination and political veto environment, the more likely closure becomes:
- **delayed**,
- **contested**,
- and therefore **more expensive in present value terms**.

## 3) Employment impact

### No-closure package
Likely outcome:
- lower workforce through attrition, voluntary exits, and redeployment,
- but most sites remain intact,
- morale damage is less severe than with closure,
- local employment base is preserved.

However, if savings targets are aggressive, the company may still need:
- significant headcount reduction,
- suppression of hiring,
- and a reshaping of skill profiles.

So “no closure” does **not** mean “no job losses.” It usually means:
- fewer forced separations,
- more time,
- and more negotiated exits.

### Closure package
Likely outcome:
- concentrated job losses at the closed site(s),
- ripple effects in suppliers and local services,
- higher regional political cost,
- possible retention of jobs elsewhere if capacity is consolidated efficiently.

From a pure labour-count perspective, closure often yields a **larger and faster** reduction.

---

## 4) Productivity implications

### Non-closure
Pros:
- preserves organizational knowledge and plant-specific capability,
- avoids disruption from shutdown and transfer,
- maintains workforce buy-in if negotiated.

Cons:
- may preserve too much legacy complexity,
- can leave marginal sites and duplicated functions in place,
- can dilute managerial focus across too many plants.

A no-closure deal can still improve productivity substantially if it is paired with:
- line simplification,
- standardization,
- automation,
- and stricter labour deployment.

But productivity gains may be capped by the need to keep all sites politically viable.

### Closure
Pros:
- forces network simplification,
- can raise utilization at remaining sites,
- reduces complexity and often improves scheduling efficiency.

Cons:
- transition shocks,
- loss of local tacit knowledge,
- risk that surviving plants inherit too much volume too fast,
- potential labour resentment affecting output quality.

---

## 5) Capex and working-capital consequences

### Capex
A non-closure plan often requires **more capex** than closure, not less, if it is to substitute for footprint reduction.

Why?
- To get equivalent savings without shutting plants, the company may need:
  - automation,
  - digitalization,
  - tooling changes,
  - flexible manufacturing upgrades,
  - and energy/process efficiency investments.

Closure, by contrast, can sometimes reduce capex needs by eliminating the need to modernize a weak site. But it may also require **more** capex at the remaining plants to absorb volume and product launches.

So the capex answer depends on network state:
- **non-closure**: more distributed modernization capex;
- **closure**: more consolidation capex plus shutdown costs.

### Working capital
Closure can reduce working capital if it:
- simplifies inventory,
- reduces WIP and logistics complexity,
- and improves plant loading.

But during transition, working capital can actually rise due to:
- dual-running,
- transfer inventory,
- de-risking stock,
- and supply-chain buffers.

Non-closure scenarios may avoid some of that disruption, but if they preserve low utilization, they can keep working capital inefficient for longer.

---

## 6) Transition costs and risk

This is often underestimated.

### No-closure transition costs
These include:
- negotiated compensation for employee concessions,
- training and redeployment costs,
- process redesign,
- management time,
- potentially higher near-term labour cost if job guarantees are purchased with wage concessions that are only partial.

### Closure transition costs
These include:
- severance,
- decommissioning,
- re-sourcing,
- ramp-up losses,
- supplier reshuffle,
- litigation or dispute costs,
- political costs that can affect permitting, subsidies, or future investment relations.

In a German co-determination setting, closure is often not just a cost decision; it is an **execution-risk decision**.

- **one or more site role changes**,
- **voluntary exits**,
- **multi-year wage or benefit concessions**,
- and **investment commitments** tied to performance milestones.

That is the industrially realistic middle ground if governance constraints make outright closure difficult.

## 9) Practical conclusion

A non-closure scenario is credible only if it delivers **near-closure-level economics** through other means. That typically requires:

- substantial and permanent labour-cost concessions,
- aggressive capacity rationalization,
- meaningful productivity improvement,
- and credible headcount reduction without forced layoffs.

If those cannot be achieved, then the company is likely to face a choice between:
- accepting a weaker long-term margin structure, or
- pursuing closure despite high conflict and transition risk.

So from an industrial restructuring perspective:  
**closures are the cleaner cost solution; non-closure packages are the more governable solution, but only if they are materially tough and not just symbolic.**
