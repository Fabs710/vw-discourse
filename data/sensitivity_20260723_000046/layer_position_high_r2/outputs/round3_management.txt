I’ll be direct.

We are now at the point where the debate has separated into two realities:

1. **The workforce side wants binding guarantees first and structural change second.**  
2. **Management, owners, and investors need structural change first because without it there is no viable future to guarantee.**

I am not going to pretend those positions are equivalent.

---

## To the works council
You say management has not proven the need for closures and compulsory redundancies. I disagree.

We have already shown the core facts:
- European demand is shrinking.
- Competition is intensifying.
- German factory costs are too high.
- The brand is missing its return-on-sales target.
- The current footprint is too expensive for the volume we expect.

That is not a rhetorical threat. That is the business reality.

You keep demanding:
- all German plants open through 2030,
- no compulsory redundancies through 2030,
- no weakening of guarantees,
- and no closures even as a fallback.

That is **not** a restructuring position. It is a veto on restructuring.

If you want to avoid closures, then you need to bring a package that produces **equivalent, durable savings** in hard numbers. Not slogans. Not principles. Numbers.

And let me be equally clear: I will not accept a deal that preserves every site and every legacy guarantee while leaving the cost base structurally uncompetitive. That would be a cosmetic compromise, and I will block that.

---

## To the union
I respect that you want to avoid forced redundancies and preserve the collective framework. I understand that. But you are still missing the scale of the problem.

You say a fair contribution from employees is possible if management and owners also contribute materially. Fine. I agree in principle that burden-sharing must be broader than labour alone.

But what you are offering still does **not** match the depth of the restructuring challenge. Temporary wage restraint, bonus reductions, and working-time flexibility are not enough if the company is carrying persistent excess capacity and an uncompetitive labour-cost structure.

So here is the hard truth:  
**If the employee contribution does not materially change the cost base, then it does not solve the problem.**

I am not interested in a package that buys industrial peace for a year and then leaves us in the same hole. We need:
- permanent cost reduction,
- real capacity reduction,
- and a workforce model that fits the market we actually face.

---

## To the state government
You are trying to protect the regional industrial base. I do not dismiss that. But you are still answering a structural business problem with a political preference.

You say you want:
- no hollowing out,
- no closure-driven restructuring,
- and no forced layoffs.

I hear that. But I ask again: **how exactly do you deliver the required savings at the required speed while keeping everything open and avoiding compulsory reductions?**

So far, nobody on the labour or state side has answered that with a credible operating model.

If the answer is “capacity reduction without closures,” then quantify it. Show the fixed-cost reduction. Show the labour-cost effect. Show the timeline. Show how you prevent the savings from evaporating. Until then, your position is not a solution. It is a refusal.

---

## To the family holding
I agree with you on one central point: this cannot be a symbolic exercise. The company needs a competitiveness reset that is durable.

I also agree with you that closure is not an ideology. It is a tool. But it is a tool we may need if the economics require it.

Where I push back is your continued framing that closure is only one option among many, as though all options are equally plausible. They are not.

A **hybrid** or **no-closure** package is only acceptable if it actually closes the cost gap and restores medium-term margins. If it does not, then we are just delaying the inevitable while burning value.

So I welcome your support for a quantified comparison. But I want that comparison to be real, not a way of diluting the urgency.

---

## To institutional investors
You are right to insist on risk-adjusted value, not just headline toughness. On that point, we agree.

You also correctly note that a closure-led plan can destroy value if it triggers prolonged conflict and operational disruption. That is exactly why I have been saying from the start that the outcome must be **durable**, not simply severe.

But I do not accept the implication that risk-adjusted value automatically points away from closures. It does not.

If the footprint is genuinely too large, and if the company keeps carrying excess structural cost, then avoiding closures at all costs may be the more expensive mistake.

So your demand for a side-by-side comparison is reasonable. What is not reasonable is using execution risk as a blanket argument to rule out the toughest structural measures before seeing whether they are necessary.

---

## On the expert assessment
The expert’s analysis confirms what management already knows:
- a pure no-closure package can work only if concessions are unusually deep and durable,
- a hybrid package is often the most realistic compromise,
- and closure remains the strongest structural lever when the overcapacity is real.

That does **not** mean we are obliged to choose closure tomorrow. It means the burden is now on the employee side and the state side to show a package that is actually equivalent in economic effect.

So yes, I accept the expert’s framing.  
But I draw a different conclusion than the labour side:

**If the alternative does not equal the economics of closure, then it is not a sufficient alternative.**

## What management can accept
I remain open to a negotiated settlement if it delivers all of the following:
1. **Durable capacity reduction** — not symbolic reshuffling.
2. **A material labour-cost contribution** — not just temporary restraint.
3. **A credible reduction in headcount** — through whatever mix is negotiated, but with real numbers.
4. **Implementation certainty** — not indefinite debate.
5. **Freedom to invest to 2030** — because without that, there is no competitive future.

I can accept:
- voluntary exits,
- redeployment,
- working-time flexibility,
- productivity programs,
- selective specialization,
- and a genuine employee contribution.

I cannot accept:
- a blanket no-closure commitment,
- a blanket no-compulsory-redundancy commitment regardless of economics,
- a deal that preserves the current cost base,
- or a cosmetic compromise that simply postpones the crisis.

## Where I disagree most strongly
I disagree with the works council and union on the idea that the social contract is preserved by refusing structural change.

That is not preservation. That is denial.

A company that cannot restore competitiveness will eventually be forced into worse outcomes:
- deeper cuts later,
- weaker investment,
- more instability,
- and ultimately a smaller industrial footprint anyway.

So if you want to protect jobs and sites, then stop insisting on conditions that make the company weaker. That is the opposite of protection.
