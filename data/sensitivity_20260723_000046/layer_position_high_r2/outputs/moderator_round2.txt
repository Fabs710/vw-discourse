## Neutral Moderator Summary — Round 2

### 1) What has been agreed
- All parties accept that the company faces a **serious structural competitiveness problem** and that some form of **cost reset and productivity improvement** is necessary.
- There is broad agreement that **symbolic or temporary measures alone are insufficient**.
- Most parties accept that any viable package would likely need some combination of:
  - capacity reduction,
  - productivity gains,
  - working-time flexibility,
  - voluntary exits / redeployment,
  - some employee contribution,
  - and investment commitments.
- There is shared recognition that **industrial conflict and execution risk matter**, not just headline savings.
- Several parties, and the expert, agree that a **quantified comparison of scenarios** is needed rather than debate at the level of principle.

### 2) Where the core disagreements remain
- The main fault line is still **plant closures and compulsory redundancies**:
  - Works council, union, and state government reject closures as the preferred or acceptable basis for negotiation.
  - Management, family holding, and investors keep closures on the table as a possible structural tool.
- There is no agreement on whether a **no-closure / no-forced-redundancy package** can achieve savings equivalent to closure.
- There is also no agreement on the **extent and durability of employee concessions**:
  - labor-side actors want them tightly limited and conditional on binding guarantees,
  - management and capital-side actors want them to be materially deeper and more structural.
- The role of the state shareholder remains contested:
  - labor-side actors want the state to use its leverage to block closures,
  - management and investors see political resistance as insufficient if the economics do not work.

### 3) What key trade-offs are still unaddressed
- **Savings versus stability**: closure may deliver stronger structural savings, but no-closure may be more governable and less disruptive.
- **Speed versus durability**: temporary or negotiated measures may be faster to implement, but may not be durable enough to solve the structural gap.
- **Capex versus footprint reduction**: a non-closure solution may require more investment to modernize and simplify operations.
- **Employment protection versus competitiveness**: preserving sites and jobs may reduce social conflict, but could leave residual cost pressure if the footprint remains too large.
- **Risk-adjusted value versus headline toughness**: parties differ on whether the “cleanest” accounting solution is actually the best outcome once execution risk is considered.

### 4) Brief refocus for the next round
The next round should move from positions to evidence. Management should present a **side-by-side quantified scenario comparison** for:
1. closure-led restructuring,
2. hybrid restructuring,
3. no-closure restructuring.

Each scenario should include, at minimum:
- expected savings,
- employment impact,
- capex requirements,
- timing of benefits,
- execution risk,
- and likely industrial-relations/political risk.

The discussion should then focus on which package is **economically credible, implementable, and durable** rather than on abstract red lines alone.
