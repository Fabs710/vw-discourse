## My position, unchanged in substance
The family holding supports a **serious, durable restructuring**. We do **not** support a cosmetic compromise that leaves the company structurally uncompetitive. At the same time, we do **not** support a value-destructive confrontation or an outcome that permanently damages the enterprise.

So the standard is not “closures vs. no closures” in the abstract. The standard is: **what package restores margins, preserves long-term value, and can actually be implemented in this governance setting?**

---

## To management
I agree with you on the core diagnosis: the current cost and capacity base is not sustainable. The company needs a **real competitiveness reset**.

Where I push back is on the way you are framing the choice. Saying “closures remain on the table” is not enough. In this company, with co-determination, state influence, and a controlling owner focused on long-term value, closures are not a clean managerial instrument. They are an escalation path with major execution risk.

The expert’s assessment is important here: closure may be the strongest structural lever, but in this setting it can also be the **most expensive way to try to achieve a cheaper structure** if it triggers delay, conflict, and operational disruption.

So I want something more disciplined from management:
- a **quantified comparison** of the alternatives,
- a clear statement of what savings are truly needed,
- and a realistic view of what can be delivered without setting the company on fire.

If you cannot show that closure is superior on a **risk-adjusted, present-value basis**, then you should not treat it as the default answer.

## To the works council
I understand your red lines, and I accept that they are rooted in legitimate concern for the workforce and the company’s social contract.

Where I disagree is your insistence that a binding no-closure, no-forced-layoff outcome should be the starting point **before** the economics are tested. That is not stewardship either. It is a refusal to confront the structural problem.

The expert is right on one key point: a no-closure package can work only if it includes **deep, durable concessions** and real capacity reduction. If the employee side wants site preservation, then it cannot be only a demand for guarantees. It has to be a package that materially closes the cost gap.

So I am not dismissing your alternatives. I am saying they must be **credible in numbers and durable in effect**. If they are not, then they are not an equivalent alternative to closure.

---

## To the union
I agree with you that symbolic concessions are worthless. I also agree that trust matters and that industrial peace is a real asset.

But I do not accept the claim that closures are somehow illegitimate simply because they are painful. Painful decisions are sometimes necessary when the business structure is wrong. The question is whether the pain is justified by the value outcome.

What I cannot support is a deal that says:
- no closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- no real structural labor-cost change,
- but somehow a restored margin and a healthy investment program.

That combination does not add up.

If you want to avoid closures, then the contribution from labour must be **material**, **time-consistent**, and **structural**, not a temporary gesture. And it must be part of a broader package that includes productivity change and capacity reduction.

## To the state government
Your position is understandable and, in part, aligned with our own long-term view. We also do not want hollowing out of the industrial base.

But I have to be candid: a political veto cannot substitute for an economically credible plan. If the alternatives to closure do not actually deliver the required savings, then resisting closures alone does not solve the problem.

I am open to a path that avoids site shutdowns. But I need to see:
- what cost base reduction you believe is sufficient,
- how it is achieved,
- what is permanent versus temporary,
- and why it is more credible than the closure-led alternative.

Without that, your position is morally understandable but economically incomplete.

## To institutional investors
I largely agree with your core message: the company needs a credible route back to margins. I also agree that half-measures and delay are value-destructive.

Where I am more cautious than you is on the assumption that the strongest-looking restructuring on paper is automatically the best one. In this governance environment, a closure-led plan may create headline savings but still destroy value if it causes prolonged conflict, governance deadlock, or operational setbacks.

So yes:
- we need durable cost reduction,
- we need capacity to come down,
- we need a real path to competitive margins.

But the route has to be **implementable**, not just theoretically strong.

## On the expert assessment
The expert is helpful because it clarifies the trade-off.

What I take from it is this:
- a **pure no-closure package** is only credible if the concessions are unusually deep and durable;
- a **closure-led package** has the strongest structural savings, but also the highest execution risk;
- a **hybrid package** may be the best balance of economics and feasibility.

That does **not** mean I endorse the easy political answer. It means I want the parties to stop arguing slogans and show which package actually works.

If management can demonstrate that closures are necessary and superior on a risk-adjusted basis, then the owners will not ignore that. But if there is a hybrid or non-closure package that is materially equivalent and more stable, that is preferable from a long-term ownership perspective.

## What would move the family holding
We would support a settlement only if it is:

1. **Economically credible**
   - the savings are real, quantified, and durable.

2. **Implementable**
   - it can survive the governance and industrial-relations reality of this company.

3. **Protective of long-term value**
   - no permanent impairment of the core asset.

4. **Stable**
   - it avoids a prolonged conflict that damages the brand, production, and reputation.

That means we can support:
- capacity rationalization,
- working-time flexibility,
- redeployment and qualification,
- voluntary exits,
- negotiated employee contributions,
- productivity improvements,
- and targeted operational changes.

But we cannot support:
- a settlement that simply preserves every legacy arrangement,
- a fake compromise that postpones the reckoning,
- or a confrontation that destroys value while pretending to be decisive.

## Bottom line
My position is simple:

- **I support a deep restructuring.**
- **I do not insist on closures as an ideology.**
- **I do insist that any alternative to closures must be real, quantified, and durable.**
- **I will not accept a deal that leaves the company structurally uncompetitive.**
- **I will not accept a value-destructive conflict either.**

So the next step should be a quantified comparison of:
- closure-led,
- hybrid,
- and no-closure scenarios,

with hard numbers on savings, employment, capex, timing, and execution risk.

If the numbers show that closures are truly unavoidable, then that is a hard conversation we will have. If the numbers show a credible alternative that preserves value and stability, then that is the route I would prefer.

That is the ownership view.
