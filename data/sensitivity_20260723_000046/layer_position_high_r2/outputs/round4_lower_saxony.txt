## My position
I support a **serious restructuring** because the competitiveness problem is real.  
I do **not** support solving it by **closing German plants or imposing compulsory redundancies**. That would damage the regional economy, weaken the company’s social legitimacy, and undermine the co-determination balance that is part of this company’s governance.

If the restructuring is serious, then it must be serious **without destroying sites and jobs in our state**.

---

## To management
I agree with you on one point: this cannot be cosmetic.

You are right that the company needs:
- real capacity adjustment,
- productivity improvement,
- lower cost,
- and a durable path back to competitiveness.

Where I disagree is your repeated assumption that closures and forced redundancies are the benchmark that others must simply accept as the “hard reality.”

That is not acceptable here. In this governance structure, with a blocking state shareholder and a workforce that can veto plant closures, a closure-led plan is not a clean managerial choice. It is a high-risk confrontation with major execution and political costs.

You have still not shown that every non-closure route has been exhausted. You have not shown that a hybrid package cannot work. You have not shown that closures would be superior once strike risk, delay, legal conflict, output loss, and reputational damage are fully counted.

So yes, I want hard numbers. But I also insist that the numbers include the **cost of conflict and disruption**, not just the static cost savings from shutting a site.

---

## To the works council and union
I agree with your central line:  
- no compulsory redundancies,  
- no dismantling of co-determination,  
- no destruction of sites under the guise of restructuring.

I also agree that “voluntary” can become forced redundancy in disguise if a plant is starved of volume. That is a legitimate concern.

Where I would caution is this: a no-closure package must still be **economically real**, not just politically comfortable. If we want to preserve all German plants, then the package has to deliver genuine productivity and cost improvements, not symbolic gestures.

So I support:
- capacity reduction through shifts and line adjustments,
- internal redeployment,
- qualification and retraining,
- productivity measures,
- voluntary exits,
- and a **limited, time-bound, negotiated employee contribution**.

But I will not accept:
- closures,
- forced layoffs,
- or a deal that only postpones the problem by weakening the plants over time.

---

## To the family holding
I agree with you that we need a durable solution and not a fake compromise.

I also agree that long-term value matters. But long-term value in this company is not created by aggressively shrinking the footprint at the expense of industrial legitimacy and regional stability. If execution fails, the supposed savings become very expensive very quickly.

So I support your call for a quantified comparison. But that comparison must be genuinely risk-adjusted. It must include:
- shutdown costs,
- legal and industrial conflict,
- strike risk,
- transition losses,
- and the effect on trust and future productivity.

If those are fully counted, then the case for closures may look much weaker than a simple spreadsheet suggests.

---

## To institutional investors
You are right to insist on durable savings and to reject a cosmetic compromise.

Where I disagree is the suggestion that the harshest headline outcome is automatically the best shareholder outcome. It is not. In this company, a closure-led plan can destroy value if it triggers prolonged conflict and delays the reset.

So yes: the cost base must come down.  
No: that does not automatically mean closures or compulsory redundancies.

If a hybrid or no-closure package can deliver:
- real capacity reduction,
- lower cost,
- better utilization,
- and a credible path to margin improvement,

then that is the better outcome for long-term stability and value.

---

## My answer to the expert view
The expert has helped clarify the key trade-off:
- a pure no-closure package only works if it is deep and durable;
- closures have stronger static savings but much higher execution risk;
- hybrid is often the most plausible compromise.

I accept that assessment.

My conclusion is this:
- **closure-led restructuring is not acceptable** because of the regional and governance damage it would cause,
- **pure no-closure is only acceptable if it is real and durable**,
- **hybrid is the only credible path if it preserves all German plants and avoids forced redundancies**.

But “hybrid” must not be a code word for attrition and shutdown later. If it is, I reject it.

I reject:
- treating closures as the default solution,
- using layoffs as a bargaining instrument,

- and any outcome that weakens the social partnership this company depends on.

## My conclusion
We are not here to preserve the status quo for its own sake.  
We are here to preserve the company as a **strong, modern, regional industrial anchor**.

That requires restructuring.  
It does **not** require plant closures or forced redundancies.

If management wants support, it must bring a package that proves that with numbers — and those numbers must include the cost of conflict, not just the cost of labor. Until then, my position remains firm.
