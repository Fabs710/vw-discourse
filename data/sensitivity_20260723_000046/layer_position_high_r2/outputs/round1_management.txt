I hear the message from the works council and the union, but I need to be equally clear: **the company cannot remain competitive on its current cost and capacity base.** We are facing a structural crisis, not a temporary dip.

Let me respond directly.

### Where I agree
I agree that we need a **durable** solution, not a cosmetic one. I also agree that **internal redeployment, qualification, working-time flexibility, and voluntary instruments** should be part of the package where they genuinely deliver savings.

I also agree that trust has been damaged. That is real. But trust alone does not close the gap between our cost base and the market reality.

### Where I disagree
I do **not** accept the proposition that:
- all German sites must be guaranteed regardless of economics,
- no compulsory redundancies can occur under any circumstances,
- and no structural capacity adjustment can be on the table.

If we lock ourselves into those red lines now, then we are not solving the crisis — we are postponing it until it becomes much worse.

The facts are simple:
- European demand is shrinking,
- competition is intensifying,
- and our German factory costs are far above target.

If we do not reduce capacity and labour cost structurally, then we will not restore the brand’s return-on-sales target, and we will not free the funds needed for product and technology investment through 2030.

### On plant closures
I am not making closures a slogan. I am saying they are **a legitimate option** if that is what it takes to achieve a sustainable cost base. The board and the owners will not accept a deal that leaves the company overcapacity and uncompetitive.

If there are alternative measures that produce **comparable, durable savings** — not theoretical savings, not temporary deferrals — then I am open to them. But they must add up to the same structural effect as a closure would.

### On jobs and labour contribution
I am prepared to discuss a negotiated employee contribution, but it cannot be symbolic. It has to be material enough to change the economics of the business.

That means we need to discuss:
- lower labour costs,
- more flexible working arrangements,
- productivity improvements,
- and a real reduction in structural overheads.

A package based only on voluntary measures and temporary concessions will not be enough.

### On guarantees
I cannot commit to blanket job and site guarantees through 2030 while the business remains structurally uncompetitive. That would remove the very leverage needed to fix the cost base.

What I can discuss is this:
- if the employee side brings forward a credible package that delivers durable savings and capacity reduction,
- and if it is implementable quickly,
- then management can consider investment commitments and a more orderly transition.

But those commitments must follow the restructuring logic — not replace it.

### Bottom line
So my position is:

1. **A deep structural reduction in cost and capacity is non-negotiable.**
2. **Plant closures remain on the table.**
3. **Compulsory redundancies may become unavoidable if no sufficient negotiated solution is reached.**
4. **I am open to alternatives only if they deliver equivalent, lasting savings.**

We can negotiate the path. We cannot negotiate away the need for a fundamentally lower cost base.

If the other side is prepared to discuss a serious package that meets those conditions, then I will engage immediately. If not, then we are headed for confrontation — but I will not accept a deal that leaves the company in the same strategic trap.
