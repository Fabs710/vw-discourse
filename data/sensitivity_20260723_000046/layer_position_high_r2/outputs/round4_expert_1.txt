Yes — and the short answer is:

**A no-closure or hybrid package can plausibly close part of the gap, but whether it closes the gap on a risk-adjusted present-value basis depends on how deep the labor-cost contribution, capacity rationalization, and productivity reset actually are.**  
In this governance setting, the main issue is not just static savings; it is **execution risk, delay, and whether savings survive 2028–2030**.

## My independent assessment

### 1) What closures usually buy you
A closure-led plan typically delivers:
- the **largest fixed-cost reduction**
- the **cleanest headcount reduction**
- lower complexity in the network
- less long-run overcapacity

But it also brings:
- high one-time restructuring costs
- strike risk and delay
- political and supervisory-board blockage risk
- ramp-down and ramp-up inefficiencies
- possible loss of know-how and supplier disruption

So the “headline savings” overstate the real value unless implementation is smooth.

### 2) What a no-closure package can realistically do
If all German plants stay open, the savings must come from:
- shift reduction / capacity utilization cuts
- voluntary exits and retirement bridges
- internal redeployment
- wage restraint / bonus cuts / working-time concessions
- productivity gains on labor content, line balancing, logistics, and indirect labor
- management and overhead reductions
- possibly product specialization to remove duplication

That can produce meaningful savings, but it is usually **hard to get all of the structural savings of closure without some combination of**:
- lower headcount,
- lower labor cost,
- lower output capacity,
- and lower complexity.

A pure no-closure deal is most likely to work only if:
- the company is willing to accept **substantial labor concessions**
- and/or **material volume rationalization** across sites

If the package is mostly temporary or symbolic, it will not close the gap.

### 3) What a hybrid can do
A hybrid is the most plausible compromise because it can:
- preserve formal site continuity
- reduce complexity through specialization
- cut one or two production lines or shifts
- lower indirect labor and overhead
- allow voluntary exits rather than forced layoffs
- preserve enough industrial peace to avoid major PV destruction

In practice, hybrid often gives **most of the savings benefit of closure** without the same degree of execution shock, but only if it includes:
- real capacity reduction,
- binding volume reallocations,
- and a credible labor-cost reset.

## Risk-adjusted present value: the key point
When you discount future savings by:
- strike probability,
- delay probability,
- transition execution losses,
- and the probability that a fragile compromise erodes before 2030,

the gap between closure and non-closure narrows materially.

In plain terms:
- **Closure** may have higher gross savings
- **Hybrid** may have better net present value if closure triggers severe disruption
- **No-closure** can be PV-competitive only if the concessions are deep, durable, and enforceable

## My estimate, qualitatively
Without seeing management’s actual numbers, I would frame it this way:

- **Closure-led:** highest theoretical savings, highest execution risk
- **Hybrid:** likely best balance of savings and implementability
- **No-closure:** viable only if concessions are large enough to materially change the cost base

## What I would want to see in the model
To decide this properly, the comparison must include:
- annual savings by measure
- one-time severance / shutdown / retraining costs
- capex required to rationalize remaining plants
- lost output during transition
- strike and delay probabilities
- present value of savings through 2030 and beyond
- sensitivity cases for lower volume in Europe

## Bottom line
**My provisional view is that a hybrid package is the most credible candidate to close most of the savings gap on a risk-adjusted basis.**  
A pure no-closure deal can work only if the employee contribution is **material, durable, and structurally large** — not temporary or symbolic.  
A closure-led solution may still be economically superior on paper, but it carries a much higher risk of destroying value during implementation.
