I will be direct.

## My position
I support a **durable restructuring that restores competitiveness and margins**. I do **not** support a settlement that turns “no closures, no redundancies” into an absolute rule regardless of economics. That would be value-destructive if the footprint is structurally too large.

At the same time, I do **not** want a reckless confrontation or a strike spiral. That would also destroy value. So my standard is simple:

- **real savings**
- **durable competitiveness**
- **labour peace**
- **no permanent impairment of the enterprise**
- **no weakening of ownership governance**

---

## Where I agree

### With management
You are right that the status quo is not viable.  
You are also right that the company needs a quantified reset, not slogans.

### With the union and works council
You are right that the expert did **not** prove closures are necessary.  
You are also right that burden-sharing must be real and measurable, not just rhetorical pressure on labour.

### With the state
You are right to insist on social responsibility and industrial stability.  
A chaotic plant destruction would be bad industrial policy and bad governance.

### With investors
You are right that the company needs durable margin improvement, not a temporary pause that leaves structural overcapacity untouched.

---

## Where I disagree

### 1) To the union and works council: “No closures, no compulsory redundancies” cannot be the starting law of the land
I understand why you say it. But from an ownership and governance perspective, it is not a credible restructuring principle.

If the German footprint is structurally uncompetitive and overbuilt, then:
- some capacity must come out,
- some workforce adjustment may be necessary,
- and some legacy protections may have to change.

If you want to rule that out entirely, then you must show a **quantified alternative** that closes the gap at scale and durably. So far, I have not seen that.

### 2) To management: you still have not completed the burden-sharing case
You keep saying the non-closure package is “likely insufficient.” That may be true, but it is not yet a decision-grade proof.

Before any irreversible step, I need:
- the exact savings target,
- the savings from each measure,
- the residual gap,
- and why that gap cannot be closed through social instruments, redeployment, attrition, and working-time measures.

Without that, closure is a preference, not a proven necessity.

### 3) To the state: “avoid closures as far as possible” is too weak if it becomes a permanent veto
You are right to protect regional industry. But protecting industry means protecting a company that can compete in 2030, not preserving every site regardless of economics.

If the state uses its position to freeze an overcapacity structure, it will end up protecting decline.

### 4) To investors: the market will not accept a compromise that preserves every site while leaving margins structurally weak
I agree with your insistence on durability. But you also need to be careful not to treat closures as the only serious tool from the outset.

The correct approach is not ideology. It is:
1. quantify the gap,
2. test the labour package,
3. define the residual gap,
4. then decide what structural action remains necessary.

---

## On the expert assessment
The expert’s point is the one we should anchor on:

- a non-closure package can contribute meaningfully,
- but it may still be insufficient if the cost gap is large and structural,
- especially if overcapacity is embedded in the footprint.

That means two things simultaneously:
1. **We should test the non-closure route seriously**
2. **We should not pretend it automatically solves the problem**

So I agree with the union on the first point, and with management/investors on the second.

---

## What I can support
I can support a package with:
- temporary wage restraint,
- bonus moderation,
- working-time flexibility,
- early retirement,
- attrition,
- internal redeployment,
- qualification and transfer measures,
- and binding investment commitments.

I can also support a **staged process** with review points and clear triggers.

But I cannot support:
- a permanent no-closure rule,
- a permanent no-redundancy rule,
- or a deal that leaves the company structurally uncompetitive.

---

## What I need next
If we want a serious decision, we need a side-by-side model with three options:

1. **Non-closure package**
2. **Hybrid package**
3. **Partial closure / workforce reduction package**

For each, I want:
- annual savings
- one-time costs
- capacity impact
- employment impact
- implementation risk
- and the residual margin gap

And I want the model to include:
- employee contribution,
- management contribution,
- owner contribution,
- and investment commitments.

Without that, we are still debating principles instead of solvency and competitiveness.

## My line, clearly stated
- I will support **durable competitiveness**.
- I will support **real employee contributions**.
- I will support **visible management and owner contributions**.
- I will support **binding investment commitments**.
- I will support a **non-closure test first**.

But I will **not** accept an outcome that permanently locks in value erosion by ruling out structural action in advance.

If the non-closure route proves sufficient, excellent.  
If it does not, then the company must retain the tools needed to restore competitiveness.
