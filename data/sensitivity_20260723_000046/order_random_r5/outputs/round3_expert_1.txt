## Core conclusion

A **non-closure package can plausibly close a meaningful part of the cost gap**, but **it is unlikely to fully solve a large structural overcapacity problem on its own** unless the required savings are relatively modest.

So, from a restructuring perspective:

- **Non-closure only** = credible as a first step, but often **insufficient** for a deep and durable reset.
- **Hybrid package** = usually the **most plausible** route if the gap is large.
- **Partial footprint rationalization / closures** = becomes more likely when fixed-cost overhang and underutilization remain significant after labor concessions.

## Restructuring mechanics: why non-closure measures help, but may not be enough

A non-closure package can reduce:
- direct labor expense,
- bonus and special-payment outlays,
- some shift and overtime inefficiency,
- and near-term cash outflows via attrition/retirement.

But it does **not fully remove**:
- plant overhead,
- building and utility costs,
- supervisory and indirect labor,
- complexity costs,
- and underutilized fixed manufacturing capacity.

That is the key distinction.

If the core problem is mainly **labor rate**, labor concessions can do a lot.  
If the core problem is **excess footprint**, labor concessions usually only partly solve it.

## Likely scenario logic

### 1) Non-closure package only
This is most plausible if:
- the required savings bridge is **moderate**,
- utilization is weak but not structurally broken,
- and the company can absorb a slower transition.

Expected outcome:
- meaningful annual savings,
- lower one-time cash cost,
- better labor peace,
- but a risk that the company still carries too much fixed cost.

### 2) Hybrid package
This is usually the most realistic industrial compromise when the gap is large.

It would combine:
- temporary employee concessions,
- working-time flexibility,
- early retirement / attrition,
- internal redeployment,
- plus some form of footprint reduction, line consolidation, or product reallocation.

This is often the **best balance** between:
- recurring savings,
- implementation feasibility,
- and avoiding a full confrontation.

### 3) Direct closure / workforce reduction
This is the strongest fix for overcapacity in pure economic terms, because it removes:
- recurring fixed costs,
- indirect overhead,
- and complexity.

But it also creates:
- higher one-time restructuring costs,
- higher labor conflict risk,
- execution risk in transferring production,
- and significant stakeholder resistance.

## Order-of-magnitude assessment

Because there are no hard numbers, I can only give ranges:

- A **moderate non-closure package** often yields savings in the **low hundreds of millions of euros annually** for a very large industrial footprint.
- An **aggressive non-closure package** may reach the **mid-hundreds of millions to around €1 billion annually**, depending on scale and durability.
- A **footprint rationalization** can add materially more recurring savings if the company is truly overbuilt, but at the cost of much higher disruption.

So if the structural gap is:
- **small to moderate**: non-closure may be enough.
- **large and persistent**: non-closure alone is usually not enough.
- **very large**: some capacity reduction beyond labor concessions becomes economically likely.

## What would make a non-closure deal credible?

For a non-closure package to stand on its own, it would need to show:

1. **Quantified annual savings**
2. **Durability** over several years, not just temporary deferral
3. **High participation rates** in concessions and attrition
4. **Material reduction in indirect costs**
5. **A clear path to lower utilization costs**
6. **Binding investment/product commitments** that improve future plant loading

If those are not present, the residual gap probably remains too large.
