I will be equally direct.

## My position
I support a **durable restructuring** because the status quo is not viable. But I do **not** support a restructuring that uses “competitiveness” as a code word for closing plants, hollowing out regional employment, or weakening the state’s co-determination and veto role.

For me, the core requirements remain:
- **protect jobs and sites in the state**
- **preserve the company as a regional economic anchor**
- **avoid plant closures as long as viable alternatives exist**
- **avoid compulsory redundancies**
- **maintain the state’s co-determination and veto role**

That is not sentimental. It is what makes the company durable in the long run.

## Where I agree
### With management
I agree that the status quo is not sustainable and that a serious reset is needed. I also agree that the company cannot be rescued by slogans or symbolic concessions.

### With the union and works council
I agree that irreversible steps should not be taken before the non-closure route is tested properly and in numbers.

### With the family holding and investors
I agree that burden-sharing must be real, visible, and quantified. If labor contributes, owners and management must contribute too.

### With the expert
I agree with the basic industrial logic: a non-closure package can help meaningfully, but it may not solve a large structural gap on its own. That is exactly why the test must be done properly.

## Where I disagree
### 1) To management: you are still trying to make closures a default conclusion
You keep saying that if the non-closure package is “likely insufficient,” then structural action must remain available. That may be true in principle, but it is not yet a decision-grade case for closures.

The expert did **not** prove that closures are necessary. The expert said:
- the non-closure package may materially narrow the gap,
- but sufficiency is not yet proven,
- and a residual gap may remain.

That means the correct next step is not “therefore close plants.”  
The correct next step is:
1. quantify the savings target,
2. quantify the labor contribution,
3. quantify management and owner contributions,
4. then test whether any residual gap can still be closed without plant destruction.

You are trying to jump directly to the hardest instrument before proving that the softer instruments fail.

### 2) To the family holding and investors: your version of “shared sacrifice” still looks asymmetrical
You say owners should not subsidize an unchanged structure indefinitely. Fair enough.

But I still do not see a real ownership contribution on the table that matches the pressure being placed on labor. If capital wants credibility, it must show:
- what cash or investment it is putting in,
- what returns it is willing to forgo in the transition,
- what German product and volume commitments it is making,
- and which jobs and sites it is committing to secure.

If the only concrete contribution from capital is pressure for cuts, then that is not shared responsibility.

### 3) To the union and works council: your red lines are legitimate, but they must be backed by a quantified package
I support your refusal of closures and forced redundancies as a starting position. I also support preserving collective bargaining and job security.

But it is not enough to say “no closures, no compulsory redundancies” and leave it there. That position becomes credible only if you bring a **quantified alternative** that can actually close the gap at scale.

So I need from the labor side:
- annualized savings from the package,
- duration of the concessions,
- participation rate,
- expected attrition and redeployment volumes,
- and a credible explanation of how overcapacity is addressed without hollowing out the industrial base.

If that alternative is strong enough, I will back it. If it is not, then we have not solved the problem.

## On the expert assessment
The expert’s assessment is useful, but it should not be abused.

What it says is:
- a non-closure package can contribute materially,
- it often will not fully solve a large structural cost gap,
- and hybrid solutions are usually the most plausible when overcapacity is real.

That is **not** a mandate for closures.  
It is a mandate for a genuine scenario test.

So I support the call for a side-by-side model, but it must test the non-closure route first and rigorously, with the residual gap clearly shown before anyone talks about plant destruction.

## What I can support
I can support a package with:
- temporary wage restraint
- bonus moderation
- working-time flexibility
- early retirement
- attrition
- internal redeployment
- qualification and transfer measures
- temporary solidarity contributions
- binding investment and product commitments

I can also support a **defined review period** with clear, jointly verified triggers.

## What I cannot support
I cannot support:
- an absolute no-closure rule only as a bargaining slogan, without quantified counterproposal
- a restructuring that makes the workforce the sole adjustment buffer
- any outcome that guts regional employment
- any outcome that removes or weakens the state’s veto and co-determination role
- any “deal” that preserves ownership discretion while leaving the region to absorb the damage

## My line, clearly
I will back a tough but socially responsible restructuring **only if** it protects:
- sites in the state,
- core employment,
- co-determination,
- and a credible industrial future.

If that cannot be achieved, I will oppose the package.

## What I want next
I support the call for a decision-grade scenario model, but it must be real and balanced.
