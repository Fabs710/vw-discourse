[Industrial Union (Trade union and collective-bargaining counterpart) — Round 1]

Our priorities are:

1. **Job security and site security**
   - No closure of German plants.
   - No forced layoffs.
   - Any workforce reduction must be handled through socially responsible instruments: natural turnover, retirement, internal transfers, training, and agreed working-time measures.

2. **A fair sharing of the burden**
   - Employees are prepared to make a contribution.
   - But the burden cannot fall only on the workforce.
   - Management, owners, and shareholders must also contribute visibly and materially.

3. **A durable collective agreement**
   - We want a settlement that gives the company cost relief, but also protects the collective wage structure and bargaining standards.
   - Temporary or targeted concessions may be discussable, but not a permanent weakening of labour standards.

4. **A credible industrial future**
   - If the company wants sacrifices, it must offer something in return:
     - binding investment commitments,
     - future product allocation,
     - protection of German sites,
     - and a medium-term plan that secures competitiveness without destroying the workforce.

What we can discuss:
- a temporary waiver or reduction of bonuses,
- working-time flexibility,
- capacity adjustments,
- a solidarity contribution from employees,
- and other negotiated cost measures.

What we cannot accept:
- closures,
- forced redundancies,
- a one-sided austerity plan imposed on workers,
- or open-ended insecurity dressed up as restructuring.

So our position is straightforward: **we are ready for a fair, shared-sacrifice deal, but not for a restructuring in which employees pay for all the pain while management and owners protect themselves.** If that is the direction, we will resist it with all available means.

## 1) Bottom line

**A non-closure package can usually generate meaningful savings, but by itself it is often insufficient when the gap is structural and large.**  
Whether it is enough depends on three things:

1. **Size of the annual savings target**
2. **Depth of the labor concessions actually achievable**
3. **How much overcapacity is embedded in the plant network**

If management’s diagnosis is that domestic manufacturing is **materially above peer cost** and the company has **persistent overcapacity**, then the likely outcome is:

- **Non-closure package:** can close part of the gap, often a large part in the near term
- **Residual gap:** commonly remains unless there is also some combination of
  - capacity reduction,
  - product allocation changes,
  - structural headcount reduction,
  - or a long-dated cost reset that is deeper than normal bargaining concessions

So the key question is not whether the labor package has value. It does. The question is whether it is **large and durable enough** relative to the required reset.

---

## 2) What a non-closure package can realistically contain

A serious non-closure package typically includes:

- temporary wage restraint or deferred pay
- bonus moderation
- reduction or suspension of special payments
- working-time flexibility
- short-time, shift, or schedule optimization
- attrition/retirement measures
- internal redeployment
- training and transfer programs
- temporary productivity commitments
- site/product guarantees in exchange

### Typical savings profile
These measures tend to save money in three buckets:

1. **Direct labor cost savings**
2. **Capacity utilization improvements**
3. **Avoided severance/closure cash outflows**

But only the first two are relevant to **durable annual operating savings**.  
Avoided closure costs improve cash, but they do **not** solve the recurring cost base.

## 3) Order-of-magnitude scenario ranges

Because no plant-by-plant financials were provided, I can only give **bounded estimates**.

### Scenario A: moderate non-closure package
- Wage/bonus moderation: **3–6% of relevant labor cost base**
- Attrition and early retirement: **small to moderate headcount reduction**
- Working-time flexibility and shift optimization: **modest savings**
- Net annualized cost reduction: often **low hundreds of millions of euros** for a very large German industrial footprint

### Scenario B: aggressive non-closure package
- Multi-year wage restraint
- Meaningful bonus removal
- larger attrition/early-retirement wave
- extensive working-time flexibility
- internal redeployment into growth programs
- durable productivity measures

This can sometimes produce **mid-hundreds of millions to around €1 billion annually**, depending on scale.

### Scenario C: structural reset requiring plant rationalization
If the company has **multiple underutilized plants** and a cost base significantly above competitors, then plant-level rationalization can add:
- labor cost removal
- overhead reduction
- depreciation/capital intensity improvement over time
- logistics and complexity savings

That can move the annual benefit into the **high hundreds of millions to low billions** range, but with higher implementation disruption.

---

## 4) Why non-closure packages often fall short

A non-closure package has limits:

### A. It cannot remove fixed costs fast enough
If plants are structurally underfilled, the company still carries:
- building overhead
- utilities
- maintenance
- local supervision
- plant logistics
- indirect labor
- complexity cost

Wage restraint does not eliminate these.

### B. It often fades over time
Temporary concessions can help for 2–4 years, but if the underlying product mix and utilization do not improve, the savings are not fully durable.

### C. Headcount reduction without closures is limited
If management refuses closures, the reduction must come from:
- attrition
- retirement
- redeployment
- voluntary severance

These methods are slower and often less complete than direct restructuring.

### D. Capacity reduction is the real issue
If the problem is **capacity surplus**, then the company needs fewer sites, fewer lines, or lower output.  
You can reduce labor cost without closing plants, but you cannot fully solve overcapacity without changing the footprint or product allocation.

## 5) Site-level implications of the options

## Option 1: Non-closure package only
### Operational effect
- All sites remain open
- Output is redistributed internally
- shifts/working time adjusted
- some lines may run less intensively
- investment concentrated in selected sites

### Employment effect
- job losses limited mainly to attrition/voluntary exits
- employment preserved better in the short term
- but indirect pressure may emerge later if utilization stays weak

### Financial effect
- lower annual recurring savings than closure-based restructuring
- lower one-time cash outflow
- lower disruption risk

### Risk
- may not close the competitiveness gap
- may only delay harder decisions

---

## Option 2: Partial closure / footprint rationalization
### Operational effect
- one or more plants reduced, repurposed, or phased out
- volume concentrated in fewer sites
- logistics and overhead rationalized

### Employment effect
- larger, more immediate workforce reduction
- site-specific dislocation
- higher redeployment needs

### Financial effect
- larger recurring savings
- possibly large one-time cash costs:
  - severance
  - redundancy
  - site remediation
  - transfer and ramp-down costs

### Risk
- higher labor conflict risk
- execution risk on volume transfer
- political and social resistance
- but more credible if the problem is true overcapacity

---

## Option 3: Hybrid settlement
This is often the most realistic industrial compromise:

- no immediate hard closure announcement
- but
  - one site functionally downsized,
  - one product line moved,
  - indirect functions reduced,
  - voluntary exits and early retirement accelerated,
  - workforce contribution locked in for a fixed period,
  - investment tied to the remaining footprint

This can produce **most of the savings of closure** while reducing the political and operational shock.

## 6) What size of gap matters?

The feasibility of a no-closure deal depends on the **residual gap after employee contribution**.

A practical decision rule:

- If the annual gap is **small to moderate** relative to labor cost base, a negotiated non-closure package may be enough.
- If the annual gap is **very large and structural**, non-closure alone is unlikely to be sufficient.

### Indicative test
If the company needs, for example:
- **€300–500 million annual improvement**: non-closure package may plausibly cover much of it
- **€700 million–€1.5 billion annual improvement**: non-closure package may help materially, but footprint action becomes more likely
- **well above €1.5 billion annual improvement**: non-closure alone is usually not credible unless concessions are unusually deep and durable

These are not company-specific facts; they are restructuring heuristics.

---

## 7) Cash impact vs recurring savings

This distinction matters a lot.

### Non-closure package
- Mostly improves **recurring EBIT/cash operating expense**
- Low one-time cash cost
- Easier to implement quickly
- Lower permanence if temporary

### Closure / rationalization package
- Higher one-time cash outflow
- Stronger long-run annual savings
- Better for fixing chronic overcapacity
- Higher execution and conflict risk

Management often prefers closures because they address fixed costs.  
Labor often prefers non-closure packages because they preserve sites and reduce immediate job destruction.  
From a pure restructuring standpoint, the best answer depends on whether the issue is **cost rate** or **capacity structure**.

Without company data, I can only describe the pattern:

Likely residual gap:
- **low** if the savings target is limited and the cost issue is mostly labor-rate driven
- **material** if the problem is overcapacity and indirect fixed cost

### Non-closure + aggressive redeployment/early retirement
Residual gap can shrink substantially, but usually not to zero if underutilization is severe.

### Non-closure + partial footprint rationalization
This is the most likely route to fully close a structural gap.

## 9) Implementation risks

### Non-closure package risks
- savings are temporary rather than permanent
- attrition too slow
- productivity commitments not enforced
- site guarantees constrain future flexibility
- underutilization persists

### Closure/partial closure risks
- industrial action
- transfer disruption
- supply-chain instability
- talent loss
- political escalation
- higher near-term cash burn

### Hybrid risks
- complex to negotiate
- can create ambiguity if closure triggers are vague
- may defer rather than solve the problem unless milestones are explicit
