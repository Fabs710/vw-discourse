## What is clearly supported by restructuring mechanics

### 1) The company likely needs a durable fixed-cost reset
If management says German costs are above target and the brand is missing its return-on-sales target, that usually means:
- overhead is too high,
- utilization is too low,
- labor cost per vehicle is too high,
- and the footprint is not matched to expected volume.

That is not a temporary issue; it is a classic **industrial restructuring** problem.

### 2) Employee contributions can help, but only to a point
Measures like:
- temporary wage restraint,
- bonus deferral,
- working-time flexibility,
- reduced overtime premiums,
- internal redeployment,
- early retirement,
- and attrition

can produce meaningful savings, especially if they are:
- broad-based,
- durably applied,
- and tied to headcount reduction without compulsory layoffs.

But these tools usually have limits:
- they are often **partial and time-limited**,
- they may not reduce fixed costs enough if overcapacity is large,
- and they cannot fully solve a footprint problem if plants are fundamentally underutilized.

### 3) Closures are not the only way to remove capacity
That said, management often overstates the binary choice. In industrial restructurings, capacity can be reduced by a mix of:
- product transfer,
- line consolidation,
- end-of-shift elimination,
- allocation of future models to fewer sites,
- natural attrition,
- retirement programs,
- and outsourcing of non-core functions.

Those are often more feasible than outright closure if governance blocks closures.

Expected effects:
- higher credibility,
- more durable savings,
- less destruction than full closure,
- but still painful employment reductions.

### Scenario C: Full closure-based restructuring

- strongest cost reduction,
- highest operational clarity,

- greatest implementation risk,
