Let me be equally direct.

## Where I agree
I agree with the union, the works council, the state government, the family holding, and investors on one point: **the status quo is not viable**. We do need a durable restructuring, and we do need quantified facts rather than slogans.

I also agree that a strike spiral would be damaging, and that employee contributions, owner discipline, and investment commitments all belong in the same package.

## Where I disagree
### 1) “Test the non-closure path first” cannot become “no structural action”
I am willing to test alternatives. I am **not** willing to pretend that a non-closure package is automatically sufficient just because it is politically easier.

The expert’s assessment was careful:
- non-closure measures can help,
- they may narrow the gap materially,
- but they are **not proven sufficient** to close a large structural cost gap.

That is the key point.  
It means the burden of proof is **not** satisfied by saying “let’s try flexibility, attrition, and bonuses.” Those tools may be part of the answer, but they do not yet solve the footprint problem.

### 2) “No closures” as an absolute is not credible
I understand why the workforce and the state reject closures. But I cannot accept a framework that rules them out categorically before we have even established the savings bridge.

If a site is structurally uncompetitive, preserving it indefinitely is not social responsibility. It is deferred decline.

I am not saying every site should close. I am saying **closures must remain available** where the economics require them. Otherwise we are not restructuring — we are freezing the problem in place.

### 3) “No compulsory redundancies” is not an absolute either
Again: I prefer social instruments first. I want attrition, retirement, redeployment, working-time changes, and voluntary programs used aggressively.

But I will not accept a permanent veto on workforce reduction if the quantified gap remains after every reasonable social measure has been tried. That is not a serious restructuring mandate.

## What I take from the expert
The expert’s conclusion is important because it clears away wishful thinking:

- A non-closure package can contribute meaningfully.
- It may even deliver a substantial share of the needed savings.
- But if the cost gap is truly structural and large, then **capacity reduction still has to happen**.
- And if capacity reduction cannot be achieved through social instruments alone, then **footprint rationalization remains on the table**.

That is exactly my position.

## My framework
We should test the options in this order:

1. **Employee contribution**
   - temporary wage restraint
   - bonus moderation
   - working-time flexibility
   - solidarity contribution
   - early retirement
   - internal redeployment

2. **Management and owner contribution**
   - visible discipline at top level
   - lower short-term returns where necessary
   - binding investment commitments
   - product allocation for the German footprint

3. **Capacity reduction through social instruments**
   - shift optimization
   - utilization reduction
   - line consolidation
   - voluntary exits

4. **If the residual gap remains: structural footprint action**
   - site rationalization
   - closures where unavoidable
   - workforce reduction where unavoidable

That sequence is disciplined, fair, and economically honest.  
What is not acceptable is stopping at step 1 and declaring victory.

## To the union and works council
You are asking me to accept:
- no closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- no permanent labor-cost change,
- and no erosion of job-security guarantees.

I cannot sign up to that as a principle.

You say the expert does not prove closures are necessary. True. But the expert also does **not** prove your package is sufficient. That means the correct conclusion is not “therefore no closures.” The correct conclusion is: **test the non-closure route in numbers, and be prepared for residual structural action if the gap remains**.

If you want to avoid harsher measures, then bring a package that closes the gap at scale, durably, and in hard numbers.

## To the family holding and investors
I agree with you that a cosmetic compromise is useless. We need a real structural reset. I also agree that the company cannot keep carrying an uncompetitive cost base forever.

But I want to be clear: the answer is not to turn closures into an ideological starting point. The answer is to secure a restructuring that is executable and durable.

That means:
- real savings,
- not symbolic cuts,
- real capacity reduction,
- not accounting gestures,
- and a labour-cost contribution that is visible and lasting.

If the non-closure path can deliver that, I will support it. If it cannot, then I will not hesitate to use the structural options available.

## To the state government
I respect the state’s role and political responsibility. But I cannot accept the idea that the state should effectively pre-empt economic necessity.

You want to preserve the industrial anchor in the region. So do I — but preserving it means preserving a company that can compete in 2030, not a footprint that looks stable only because decline has been postponed.

The state should support a tough settlement:
- socially managed,
- investment-backed,
- and legally executable.

It should not demand that management ignore the cost reality.

## What I am prepared to do
I am prepared to support a serious package with:
- temporary concessions,
- working-time flexibility,
- attrition and early retirement,
- internal redeployment,
- binding investment commitments,
- and a defined review period.

I am also prepared to accept a staged process that tests the non-closure route first.

## What I am **not** prepared to do
I am **not** prepared to accept:
- an absolute no-closure rule,
- an absolute no-redundancy rule,
- permanent labour-cost rigidity,
- or a deal that leaves the company still structurally uncompetitive.

## My challenge to the labor side
You keep asking management to prove that alternatives are insufficient. Fair. But then prove your own alternative is sufficient.

Specifically:
- What annualized savings do you deliver?
- Over what duration?
- With what participation rate?
- What happens if demand falls further?
- What fixed costs are actually removed?
- How do you address overcapacity if all sites remain open?

If those answers are not convincing, then your proposal is not a restructuring solution. It is a delay mechanism.

## My challenge to the owners and investors
If you want this to land, show me:
- what financial contribution you are making,
- how much near-term return you are willing to forgo,
- and what investment is locked in for German future products and technology.

If capital’s only contribution is to demand cuts from labor, then this process loses legitimacy.

## My position, plainly
I want a **durable competitiveness reset**.  
I want **material cost reduction**.  
I want **capacity adjustment**.  
I want **a real labour-cost contribution**.  
I want **binding investment for future products and technology to 2030**.

A site-preserving package is acceptable only if it actually delivers those outcomes.  
If it doesn’t, then deeper structural action remains necessary — including closures and workforce reductions where unavoidable.

If the other sides want to avoid that outcome, they need to stop speaking in principles and start putting numbers on the table.
