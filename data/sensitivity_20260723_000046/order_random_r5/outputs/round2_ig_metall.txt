We are at the point where principles are no longer enough. So let me respond directly to what has been said.

### To management
You keep saying the scale of the problem justifies closures and forced cuts. I do not accept that as a conclusion, only as a claim. If you want the workforce to carry a major burden, then you must prove that **alternative instruments cannot deliver the required savings**.

Right now, I have not seen evidence that the company has exhausted:
- working-time reductions,
- temporary wage and bonus restraint,
- internal redeployment across sites,
- natural attrition and early retirement,
- qualification and transfer measures,
- capacity reduction without closures,
- and a fair owner/management contribution.

You are asking for irreversible measures before demonstrating that reversible ones are insufficient. That is exactly backward.

### To the family holding and investors
You both talk about “durable competitiveness,” but you are treating workers as the first and largest adjustment variable. That is not shared sacrifice. If owners want sacrifices, then owners must contribute visibly:
- no dividend-first mentality while jobs are cut,
- no one-sided pressure for wage concessions,
- no permanent erosion of collective standards.

If the burden is truly shared, show us concrete commitments from the top: investment, product allocation, and acceptance of reduced returns during the adjustment period. Otherwise, your “reset” is just cost shifting.

### To the state government
I appreciate that you reject closures and mass layoffs. But support in principle is not enough. If you mean it, then use your position to insist on a package that keeps sites open and jobs secure, not just “socially mitigated” downsizing that slowly empties out plants over time.

### On the core issue: what is acceptable
We are prepared to discuss a serious employee contribution, but only in a package that protects the industrial base. That means we can discuss:
- temporary bonus restraint,
- temporary wage concessions,
- working-time flexibility,
- a solidarity contribution,
- internal redeployment,
- early retirement,
- training,
- and temporary capacity adjustments.

But we need clear boundaries:
- **no compulsory redundancies,**
- **no plant closures,**
- **no permanent dismantling of the collective wage framework,**
- **no open-ended insecurity.**

That is not stubbornness. That is the minimum required for legitimacy with the workforce.

### What a viable package would have to look like
If management wants real cooperation, then put forward a package with all of the following:
1. **Binding site guarantees**
2. **A defined investment program for German plants**
3. **A time-limited employee contribution**
4. **Owner and management contribution**
5. **No forced layoffs**
6. **No closures**
7. **A capacity plan that uses social instruments first**

If you can put real numbers on that, we will engage seriously. If your numbers only work by closing plants and pushing people out, then you are not negotiating a restructuring — you are demanding that workers pay for the entire crisis.

### Our counter-position
We are not rejecting savings. We are rejecting a one-sided solution.

A credible alternative would be:
- capacity reduction through shift optimization and reduced utilization,
- a temporary waiver or reduction of bonuses,
- a negotiated solidarity contribution from the workforce,
- moderate, time-limited adjustments to pay components,
- no forced redundancies,
- no closures,
- and binding commitments on products, investment, and job security.

That is the path to a durable settlement. It is also the only path that preserves industrial peace. If that path is refused, then you should not expect the workforce to absorb structural risk quietly. We are prepared to escalate if necessary.
