## Where I strongly disagree
### 1) “Non-closure may be insufficient” is **not** the same as “closures are necessary”
That is the central attempt here, and I reject it.

- it may narrow the gap,
- but sufficiency is not yet proven.

That means the burden is still on management and capital to show, in hard numbers, why the site-preserving route cannot work. Until they do, closures remain an unjustified leap.

### 2) Management is using “structural overcapacity” as a blanket excuse
You keep repeating that the footprint is too expensive and that capacity must be reduced. Fine — but **how**?

You have not demonstrated that capacity reduction must mean:
- plant closures,
- compulsory redundancies,
- or tearing up job-security guarantees.

Capacity can also be addressed through:
- shift and utilization changes,
- product reallocation,
- internal transfers,
- retirement pathways,
- short-time arrangements,
- qualification and redeployment,
- temporary concessions,
- and negotiated workload adjustments.

If you have evidence that these are insufficient, then put it on the table. But do not present closure as a foregone conclusion.

### 3) The family holding and investors are still shifting the burden onto labour
You talk about “shared sacrifice,” but your version of it is:
- workers take the cuts,
- sites take the risk,
- and capital keeps strategic discretion.

That is not burden-sharing. That is burden-shifting.

If owners want credibility, then they must commit to:
- visible financial contribution,
- reduced near-term returns,
- binding investment in German sites,
- and acceptance of a settlement that keeps plants open and jobs secure.

Anything less is asking the workforce to subsidize the restructuring while capital preserves its options.

### 4) The state must not let “test first” become “close later”
I appreciate the state government’s resistance to plant destruction. But I want to be direct: “avoid closures as far as possible” is too weak if it becomes the path to eventual closure under pressure.

The state must insist on:
- binding site guarantees,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- no erosion of co-determination,
- and a restructuring path that preserves the industrial base in the region.

If the state’s role is only to soften the blow after management has already chosen closure logic, then it is not protecting industry — it is managing decline.

What is missing is:
- the exact savings target,
- the exact contribution of the labor package,

- and why the remaining gap cannot be closed without destroying sites.

Until that is shown, you do **not** have a basis for closures or forced redundancies. You have a preference.
