We are aligned on the destination, but not on the credibility of the path.

## Where we agree
- The status quo is not viable.
- A cosmetic deal would destroy value.
- Employees should contribute.
- Management and owners should also contribute visibly.
- Any settlement must be quantified and durable.
- A strike spiral would be value-destructive.

## Where we disagree

### 1) To the union and works council
Your insistence on:
- no plant closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- and preservation of all existing protections

is understandable, but it is **not yet a sufficient solution** unless you can show, in hard numbers, that it closes the structural cost gap at scale.

Right now, what you are offering is a **possible bridge**, not a proven answer. The expert did **not** say your package is sufficient. The expert said it can contribute meaningfully, but may still leave a residual gap if the footprint is too large.

That matters. If the company remains structurally uncompetitive, then margins stay impaired and the share price stays under pressure. We will not support a settlement that simply delays the adjustment and preserves an uncompetitive cost base.

### 2) To the state government
We appreciate the political and regional constraints. But “avoid closures as far as possible” is only acceptable if the alternative is **decision-grade credible**.

If the non-closure route is not enough, then the state cannot block all structural action and still claim to protect industry. Protecting industry means preserving a company that can compete in 2030, not freezing a footprint that continues to underperform.

### 3) To the family holding
We agree that labor should not carry the whole burden. But capital’s contribution has to be more than rhetorical support for a turnaround.

We need to see:
- concrete financial support,
- visible acceptance of lower near-term returns,
- binding investment commitments,
- and discipline on overhead and capital allocation.

If the owners only ask for labor flexibility while keeping all strategic options and returns protected, that is not shared sacrifice.

### 4) To management
You are right that non-closure measures may not be enough. But you still have not completed the burden-sharing case in numbers.

Before pushing closures or compulsory redundancies, you need to show:
1. the required annual savings,
2. the savings from the labor package,
3. the contributions from management and owners,
4. the residual gap,
5. and why that gap cannot be closed through a serious combination of attrition, redeployment, working-time changes, and targeted capacity reduction.

Without that bridge, closure is a preference, not a proven necessity.

## Our position
From a capital-markets perspective, we are **not** here to defend closures for their own sake. We are here to protect risk-adjusted equity value.

That means:
- we support a **real non-closure test first**,
- but only if it is quantified and time-bound,
- and only if there is a clear trigger for moving to deeper structural action if the gap remains.

What we will **not** support is a settlement that:
- preserves every site indefinitely,
- avoids structural adjustment in the name of comfort,
- and leaves the company with structurally weak margins.

## What we can support
We can support:
- temporary wage restraint,
- bonus moderation,
- working-time flexibility,
- early retirement,
- attrition,
- redeployment,
- qualification measures,
- and a time-limited employee contribution,

if these are tied to:
- real management cost discipline,
- owner participation,
- and a credible path back to competitive margins.

We can also support a staged approach with triggers.

## What we cannot support
We cannot support:
- a cosmetic settlement,
- a permanent no-adjustment rule,
- or a package that leaves overcapacity and high cost unaddressed.

If a non-closure package truly restores competitiveness, fine.  
If it does not, then the company must retain the tools to fix the footprint.
