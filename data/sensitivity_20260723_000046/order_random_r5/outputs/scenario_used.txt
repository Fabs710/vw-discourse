1. Context: a restructuring confrontation at a large German carmaker

This section gives the background needed to understand the decision. It describes how such a decision works; individuals appear only at the level of stakeholder roles. This is presented as a live decision whose outcome is open.

1.1 The company. At the centre of this decision is one of the world's largest carmakers, the core company of a large, listed German automotive group and one of the country's biggest industrial employers, with a very large domestic workforce across several German plants. After years of heavy investment in the transition to electric vehicles, the company faces a shrinking European market, intense competition including from lower-cost and Chinese manufacturers, and German factory costs that management judges to be well above target and above those of competitors. Its core passenger-car brand is missing its return-on-sales target.

1.2 Ownership and governance. Three features shape any major decision. First, co-determination: the twenty-seat supervisory board is composed half of shareholder representatives and half of employee representatives, and any decision to close or relocate a plant requires a two-thirds board majority, giving the workforce an effective veto. Second, a controlling family holding company owns a majority of the voting rights and acts through the supervisory board. Third, a regional state government holds a blocking-minority stake under a special law, with board seats and a veto over major decisions, alongside other anchor shareholders. Ordinary institutional investors hold largely non-voting capital. The result is that several parties hold effective blocking positions.

1.3 The move. Management has concluded that the company's German operations carry structural overcapacity and an uncompetitive cost base, and has put forward a restructuring aimed at a large, durable reduction in cost and capacity. The proposals on the table include closing one or more German plants for the first time in the company's history, reducing production capacity, cutting a substantial number of jobs, and lowering labour costs through measures such as pay reductions and the removal of long-standing guarantees. Management frames this as necessary for the company's competitiveness and its future investment in technology. The employee side and the co-determination stakeholders regard core elements of the proposal as unacceptable, and the collective-bargaining peace obligation has ended, opening the way to industrial action.

2. The parties

The following stakeholders have a claim on the decision: the group and brand management driving the restructuring; the works council representing the workforce at plant level and holding half the supervisory-board seats; the trade union that negotiates the collective wage agreement and can call strikes; the regional state government acting as an anchor shareholder and political actor; the controlling family owners acting through governance; and the dispersed institutional investors in the capital markets. Their interests are only partially aligned, and several of them hold effective blocking positions.

3. The decision

The stakeholders must deliberate the company's path out of the crisis. Several broad directions are open: proceed with deep structural cuts including plant closures; achieve comparable savings through alternative measures that avoid closures and forced layoffs, such as capacity reductions, working-time changes, and a negotiated employee contribution; or reach some combination of these on agreed terms. The decision is not a simple yes or no: any path may carry conditions such as site and job guarantees, the scale and timing of job reductions, the size and sharing of any labour-cost contribution, and commitments on investment and future products. The outcome is open and is the subject of this discussion.
