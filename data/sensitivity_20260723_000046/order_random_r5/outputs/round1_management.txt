I’ll be direct.

I agree with one point from everyone here: **the status quo is not an option**. German factory costs are too high, our capacity is too large for a shrinking European market, and the brand is missing its return-on-sales target. If we do not make structural changes now, we will destroy value, starve future investment, and weaken the company further.

But I disagree with the attempt to draw a red line around **all closures, all compulsory job reductions, and any meaningful structural cost reset**. That position does not solve the crisis; it postpones it.

To the union:  
You say no closures, no forced redundancies, no dismantling of collective standards. I understand the political and social reasons behind that, but I have to say clearly: **that is not compatible with the scale of the problem**. We are not dealing with a temporary dip. We are dealing with a structural competitiveness gap. If we only use retirement, transfers, and working-time tweaks, we will not close that gap. Those tools may help at the margin, but they do not fix an uncompetitive footprint.

I am prepared to discuss socially responsible workforce reductions and negotiated instruments. I am also prepared to discuss binding investment commitments and a future industrial plan for Germany. But I will not pretend that a purely voluntary adjustment, without meaningful capacity reduction and labour-cost contribution, is enough.

To the family holding:  
I agree that a cosmetic deal would be unacceptable. We do need **durable savings and real capacity adjustment**. However, I would caution against framing closures as the default or only credible solution. Given the governance reality, a confrontation-first approach could lead to delay, strike damage, and a weaker outcome for everyone. We need a package that is economically sufficient and legally executable. If closures are part of that package, then so be it—but they are not the only lever. We should still pursue the full range of structural measures.

To the regional state government:  
I share the view that this must be a negotiated settlement and that the company’s industrial base matters. But I cannot accept the idea that plant closures are automatically unacceptable. If a site is structurally non-competitive, preserving it indefinitely helps no one. It would be a false promise to workers and a drag on the company’s future. The real question is not whether change happens, but whether it happens in an orderly, negotiated way with proper social mitigation.

So let me be equally clear about management’s position:

- We need **a substantial and durable reduction in cost base**.
- We need **capacity reduction** that matches market reality.
- We need **a material labour-cost contribution**.
- We need **flexibility on workforce size and site structure**.
- And we need **commitments that protect future investment in products and technology**.

What I am prepared to accept:
- working-time flexibility,
- temporary or targeted concessions,
- early retirement and internal redeployment where feasible,
- a negotiated employee contribution,
- and binding investment commitments in return for genuine savings.

What I am not prepared to accept:
- a deal that preserves all sites regardless of economics,
- a settlement that leaves the cost base structurally uncompetitive,
- or a cosmetic compromise that avoids hard decisions while pretending to be a restructuring.

If the employee side wants to avoid closures and harsher measures, then it must come forward with **quantifiable savings at the scale required**, not just principles. We need numbers, not slogans. If those numbers are not there, then management will continue to argue for deeper structural action, including closures where unavoidable.

This is not about punishing the workforce. It is about securing the company’s long-term viability. If we get the structural reset right now, we can protect the future of the brand and the majority of jobs. If we do not, we risk losing both.
