Let me answer directly.

The expert’s assessment does **not** change my core position. It confirms what we have been saying all along: the management side has **not proven** that its preferred route is the only route, and it has **not proven** that closures or forced redundancies are necessary.

## Where I agree
I agree with one important point from management, the family holding, and investors: **the company needs a durable solution, not a cosmetic one**. I also agree that the cost base must come down and that employee contributions can be part of a settlement.

And I agree with the expert on one procedural point: **we need real numbers, not slogans**.

## Where I disagree
But let’s be precise: the expert did **not** say that closures are required. It said the opposite of what management keeps implying:
- the non-closure package is **plausible**,
- it may **narrow the gap**,
- and the claim that closures are unavoidable is **not yet evidence-based**.

That is a crucial distinction.

Management and investors are trying to turn “not yet proven sufficient” into “therefore we must close plants.” That jump is not justified. If you want to impose irreversible measures, then you must first show:
1. the exact savings target,
2. the savings from each alternative measure,
3. why site-preserving instruments cannot close the gap,
4. and why compulsory redundancies would be unavoidable.

You have not done that.

## To management
You keep saying “quantify it.” Fine. Then quantify **your** case too.

Show us:
- the plant-by-plant cost problem,
- the exact capacity gap,
- the savings from closures,
- the transition costs,
- the social and industrial risk of forced cuts,
- and the long-term consequence for know-how, productivity, and future model allocation.

Right now, management is using “structural overcapacity” as a blanket justification for breaking the historic job-security framework. That is not acceptable.

And let me be very clear: **we will not accept closures as a default planning assumption**. If the company wants serious cooperation, then the starting point must be:
- keep every German plant open,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- capacity reduction through social instruments,
- internal redeployment,
- working-time flexibility,
- retirement pathways,
- training,
- and temporary, time-limited contributions from the workforce.

## To the family holding
You speak as if the only responsible stewardship is to force a hard reset on workers. That is too narrow.

Yes, the company needs competitiveness. But if your answer to every cost problem is to sacrifice sites and jobs, then you are treating the workforce as the adjustment buffer while preserving strategic freedom for capital. That is exactly the burden-shifting we reject.

You say “preserve value.” Fine — then preserve the industrial capability that creates value:
- the skilled workforce,
- the German production base,
- the co-determined governance structure,
- and the trust that makes this company governable.

Destroying that trust is not stewardship. It is a short-term financial tactic with long-term damage.

## To the state government
I appreciate that you are not giving management a free hand. But I need to be equally direct: “no closures as a first resort” is not enough if it becomes a back door to accepting closures later under pressure.

The state should use its position to insist on a package that truly preserves sites and jobs, not one that merely delays decline. That means:
- binding site guarantees through 2030,
- no forced redundancies,
- concrete future product commitments,
- and a real role for co-determination in shaping the implementation.

If the state wants to stand with the region, then stand firmly — not conditionally.

## On the expert’s sequence
The expert suggested a sequence:
1. concessions,
2. attrition/retirement/redeployment,
3. capacity reduction,
4. then closures or involuntary reductions if the gap remains.

We accept the first two steps immediately. We reject the fourth step as a predetermined outcome.

And we also challenge the third step as it is being framed here. Capacity reduction does **not** automatically mean plant destruction. Capacity can be adjusted by:
- shift optimization,
- temporary short-time work arrangements,
- product reallocation,
- voluntary programs,
- internal transfers,
- and other social instruments.

So the expert’s assessment actually supports our position: the non-closure path is **credible and should be tested first**. It is not honest to jump straight from “maybe insufficient” to “therefore closures are justified.”

## What we are prepared to discuss
We remain prepared to discuss a serious, temporary workforce contribution if — and only if — the package includes all of the following:
1. **Binding guarantees that every German plant stays open**
2. **No compulsory redundancies**
3. **A time-limited employee contribution**
4. **Management and owner contribution**
5. **Concrete investment and product commitments**
6. **A genuine co-determination role in implementation**
7. **A measurable path to competitiveness without breaking the workforce**

That is the minimum for a legitimate settlement.

## What is still not acceptable
Let me repeat the red lines:
- plant closures,
- compulsory redundancies,
- tearing up job-security guarantees,
- and a restructuring that treats the workforce as expendable.

If management insists on that path, then there will be resistance — in the board, in the plants, and publicly. We have already shown we can mobilise. That remains on the table.

## My challenge back to management and investors
If you claim the non-closure package is insufficient, then put the numbers on the table:
- how much savings it delivers,
- how much gap remains,
- and why that gap cannot be bridged without closing plants or forcing people out.

Until then, your position is not a restructuring plan. It is a pressure tactic.

We are not here to bless a fait accompli. We are here to defend jobs, sites, and co-determination — and we will do that.
