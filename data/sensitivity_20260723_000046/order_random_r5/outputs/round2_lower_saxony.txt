We are now at the point where the discussion has to become concrete, and I will be equally concrete.

### First: what I agree with
I agree with management, the family holding, and investors on one central point: **the company needs a real competitiveness reset, not cosmetic change**. The current cost base, capacity profile, and profitability are clearly not sustainable.

I also agree with the union and works council that **a prolonged confrontation would be damaging** and that **employees should not be the only ones making sacrifices**. A fair solution must include:
- management discipline,
- owner responsibility,
- and binding investment commitments for the German footprint.

### Second: what I do not accept
I do **not** accept a restructuring logic that treats **German plant closures, mass job losses, or the hollowing-out of co-determination** as normal instruments.

That is not just a political preference. It would damage the company’s legitimacy, the regional economy, and the long-term trust that has made this industrial model workable. If the price of “competitiveness” is the loss of the company’s role as a regional anchor, then the plan has failed strategically, even if it looks tidy on paper.

So I need to be blunt:
- **No plant closures as a first resort.**
- **No compulsory redundancies if workable alternatives exist.**
- **No permanent dismantling of collective protections as the price of a one-sided reset.**
- **No attempt to sideline the state’s veto and co-determination role.**

### On the union’s position
I hear the union’s argument that alternatives have not been exhausted, and that is a fair point. Before anyone demands irreversible steps, they must show why reversible measures are insufficient.

But I also have to say: if the union’s position is simply “no closures, no compulsory redundancies, no permanent labour-cost change,” then that is too rigid to be a complete answer. The company does need measurable savings and capacity correction. The real question is **how much can be achieved without crossing the lines that would break the social contract**.

So I am open to:
- temporary wage and bonus restraint,
- working-time flexibility,
- internal redeployment,
- early retirement,
- qualification and transfer measures,
- and time-limited solidarity contributions.

Those are serious instruments. They are not slogans, and they should be tested against real numbers.

### On management, the family holding, and investors
You all keep asking for “quantified” alternatives, and that is fair. But quantification cuts both ways.

If you want the state and workforce to accept burden-sharing, then you must quantify:
- how much owners will contribute,
- how much management will give up,
- what investment is guaranteed,
- how many jobs are protected,
- which sites remain secure,
- and over what time horizon.

I will not support a package where the only concrete number is how many jobs disappear.

### My benchmark for any acceptable package
A viable settlement must meet all of these conditions:

1. **Protect the German industrial footprint**
   - Sites in this state must remain viable.
   - If capacity must be reduced, it must be done without destroying entire plants.

2. **Avoid compulsory redundancies if at all possible**
   - Socially responsible instruments must come first.
   - If direct workforce reduction is unavoidable, it must be the last resort, tightly limited, and fully justified.

3. **Deliver real cost relief**
   - Temporary concessions from labour are possible.
   - But they must be matched by management and ownership contributions.

4. **Include binding investment commitments**
   - Not vague promises.
   - Concrete product allocation, technology investment, and site commitments.

5. **Preserve co-determination and the state’s role**
   - The governance structure is not an obstacle to be bypassed.
   - It is part of the legitimacy of any durable solution.

### Where I am drawing the line
If the proposal coming out of this round is still fundamentally:
- closures first,
- layoffs as the main adjustment tool,
- workforce as the primary shock absorber,
- and the state reduced to a spectator,

then I will oppose it.

But if the parties are prepared to build a package around:
- capacity adjustment without plant destruction,
- temporary and fair employee contributions,
- management and owner discipline,
- and binding investment for the German sites,

then I am prepared to use my position to support that route.

### My challenge to management and the controlling shareholders
Bring forward a **site-by-site plan** that shows:
- which savings are needed,
- what can be achieved by non-closure measures,
- what burden the owners and management will accept,
- and how German jobs and future investment will be secured.

If you cannot show that closures are truly unavoidable, then you should not insist on them. And if you do insist on them, you should expect that I will use the state’s rights and our board position accordingly.

### My challenge to the union
I need to see whether the union’s alternative is not just principled, but numerically credible. If you want to avoid harsher measures, then bring a **quantified** proposal that:
- materially reduces costs,
- preserves sites,
- protects as many jobs as possible,
- and remains durable beyond the next bargaining round.

### Bottom line
I will support a hard restructuring if it is a **socially responsible restructuring** that preserves the company as a regional economic anchor.

I will **not** support a solution that restores competitiveness by gutting regional employment and treating the state’s role as expendable.

So yes to restructuring.  
No to destruction.  
And no to any deal that sacrifices the company’s future in the region for short-term financial neatness.
