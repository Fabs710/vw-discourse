From a plant-level restructuring and workforce-modeling perspective, the central issue is still the same: **the savings gap has not been quantified tightly enough to prove which restructuring path is feasible**.

## What can be said with confidence

A **no-closure, no-forced-redundancy** solution is **possible in principle**.  
Whether it works in practice depends on:

- the size of the required savings gap,
- how much capacity can be removed without shutting plants,
- how many jobs can leave through attrition, early retirement, and redeployment,
- whether labor-cost concessions are temporary or permanent,
- and how much internal relocation capacity exists across the German footprint.

## What is still missing

No party has yet provided a complete **site-by-site savings bridge** showing:

1. total savings target  
2. savings by measure  
3. headcount impact by site and year  
4. redeployment capacity by site  
5. transition timing and one-time costs  

Without that, claims that closures are “necessary” or “unnecessary” are both incomplete.

## How the options compare structurally

### 1) Full closure / consolidation
Likely effect:
- fastest and deepest fixed-cost reduction
- highest one-time transition cost
- highest workforce disruption
- strongest supply-chain and labor-relations risk

This is usually the clearest way to remove structural cost, but not necessarily the least risky overall.

### 2) No-closure with concessions
Likely effect:
- lower social disruption
- heavier reliance on labor-cost concessions, working-time cuts, and internal flexibility
- slower savings realization
- constrained by how much capacity can be removed without site shutdowns

This can work if the gap is moderate and redeployment is substantial. It becomes harder if the required savings are large and permanent.

### 3) Hybrid compromise
Likely effect:
- partial consolidation of weaker activities
- retention of strategically important sites
- better balance between savings and continuity
- still requires material headcount reduction and operational redesign

This is often the most realistic route where full closure is politically or governance-wise difficult, but the footprint still needs meaningful shrinkage.

## Main operational constraint

The key question is not whether jobs can be reduced. They can.

The key question is whether enough cost and capacity can be removed **without**:
- forced layoffs,
- closures,
- or leaving the company with a still-uncompetitive cost base.

That depends entirely on the size of the gap.

## What a proper site-by-site model needs

To assess the options rigorously, I would need for each plant:

- current headcount by function
- current utilization and shift pattern
- direct labor cost per unit
- allocated fixed overhead
- natural attrition and retirement rates
- redeployment openings elsewhere in the group
- training lead times for transfers
- tooling/product/logistics constraints
- one-time transition costs

## Practical bottom line

Based on the information given:

- A **preserve-all-sites** model is feasible only if the savings gap is not too large and labor accepts a meaningful contribution.
- If the gap is very large, some form of **closure or structural consolidation** becomes increasingly likely.
