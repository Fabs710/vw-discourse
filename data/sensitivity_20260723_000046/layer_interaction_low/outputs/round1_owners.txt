I appreciate the positions that have been set out, and I will be direct.

I agree on the core objective: **this company must regain competitiveness, restore margins, and secure the investment base for the future**. A purely symbolic compromise would be damaging for everyone, including the workforce and the long-term owners. On that point, management is right that structural cost relief is necessary.

Where I am **not** aligned is with any approach that assumes **German plant closures are the default solution** or that the workforce should bear the burden alone. A conflict that drags on and destroys value would be unacceptable. So would a restructuring that permanently weakens the company’s industrial base or turns a turnaround into a reputational and political disaster.

My view is this:

- **No value-destructive escalation**
- **No permanent impairment of the enterprise’s core value**
- **No settlement that merely postpones the problem**
- **No outcome that creates labour peace on paper but leaves the business structurally uncompetitive**

That means I am open to a **serious negotiated restructuring**, but only if it is credible on economics and durable on labour peace.

To management: I understand the pressure to reduce capacity and costs materially. But if the plan depends primarily on plant closures and forced redundancies, then you should not assume there will be easy governance support. The family holding will not back a solution that solves a short-term spreadsheet problem by damaging the company’s industrial and political stability for years. I would want to see whether the same savings can be achieved through a combination of:
- capacity reduction through operational measures,
- working-time flexibility,
- internal redeployment and qualification,
- wage and cost restraint,
- and a broader contribution from management and the wider cost base.

To the works council and union: I hear the line on no closures and no compulsory redundancies. I understand why that boundary is being drawn. But I also need to be clear that **a refusal to engage on meaningful structural change is not a sustainable answer**. If the company cannot lower its fixed cost base enough, then the result will be worse for everyone over time. So if you want to protect sites and jobs, you will need to help produce a package that genuinely changes the economics, not just the optics.

To the state government: your concern about regional stability is legitimate. But the company cannot be governed as if all adjustment can be absorbed elsewhere while the underlying cost problem stays untouched. Any durable arrangement will require the region and workforce to accept some real change, not just declarations of protection.

So my bottom line is this:

**I support a negotiated solution only if it delivers real and durable competitiveness gains, preserves labour peace, and avoids unnecessary destruction of enterprise value.**  
I do **not** support a prolonged confrontation.  
I do **not** support a cosmetically softened deal that fails economically.  
And I do **not** support letting one side’s absolute red lines become the company’s permanent strategic trap.

If management can bring a package that is materially equivalent in savings and durability without resorting to closures, I will look at it seriously. If not, then the discussion has to remain open to stronger structural measures.
