I will respond directly.

## Where I agree
I agree with the works council, the state, and even management on one essential point: **this company needs a durable restructuring, not a cosmetic compromise**.

I also agree that the package has to contain real operational changes:
- capacity reduction,
- working-time flexibility,
- internal redeployment,
- qualification and retraining,
- overhead restraint,
- voluntary exits and early retirement,
- and a fair employee contribution.

So no one should mistake my position for denial that adjustment is needed. Adjustment is needed.

## Where I disagree
I do **not** accept that **plant closures** and **compulsory redundancies** should be treated as the default or inevitable answer.

That is still not proven.

Management keeps saying the cost gap is structural and large. Fine. Then show it in a way that can actually be tested. Right now, the discussion is still being pushed as if closures are the starting assumption and the workforce must prove they are unnecessary. That reverses the burden of proof.

And let me be very clear:  
**If employees are asked to contribute, then management and owners must also contribute visibly and measurably.**  
We will not accept a deal where workers pay through wage restraint, insecurity, and job cuts while the capital side keeps strategic flexibility intact.

## To management
You say the gap may require deeper consolidation. That may be true. But you have **not yet demonstrated** that the same structural savings cannot be achieved through a no-closure or hybrid package.

So I repeat the question:
- What is the exact savings target?
- What comes from labor cost?
- What comes from overhead?
- What comes from capacity reduction?
- What comes from management and owner contribution?
- What is the site-by-site headcount effect?
- What are the one-time transition costs?

Until that is answered, closures remain a **claim**, not a necessity.

I also reject the implication that a temporary labor contribution is somehow unserious. A negotiated contribution can be meaningful if it is paired with:
- binding site security,
- no forced redundancies,
- and a clear investment path.

That is a real deal. What is not a real deal is asking the workforce for permanent sacrifices without security in return.

## To the works council
We are aligned on the main red lines:
- no plant closures in Germany,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- collective wage standards protected,
- and burden-sharing that is actually fair.

I support your insistence that the company’s German footprint must not be broken up as a bargaining tactic.

Where I would slightly sharpen the point is this: if we want to defend all sites, then we also need a package that can withstand economic scrutiny. That means we need the numbers, not just the principle.

So yes, I stand with your position. But I also want us to keep the discussion focused on what can genuinely absorb the adjustment:
- voluntary exits,
- early retirement,
- internal transfers,
- training,
- working-time reductions,
- and labor-cost concessions that are negotiated and limited.

## To the state government
Your role matters. Regional stability is not a side issue; it is part of the economic reality of this company. I support your insistence that workers cannot be the only adjustment variable.

But stability has to be enforced, not just stated. If the state wants to help, then it should support:
- binding site protection,
- binding job security,
- binding investment commitments,
- and a settlement that does not load the whole burden onto labor.

## To the family owners
If you want long-term stewardship, then prove it by protecting the industrial base, not by demanding that workers absorb the entire shock. A workforce that feels sacrificed will not support a turnaround, and that will damage the company for years.

## To investors
You are right to want a credible return to margin and lower fixed costs. I do not dispute that. But credibility is not created by provoking open conflict or by using closures as leverage without proof that alternatives fail.

A deal that is socially explosive and operationally contested is not value-preserving.

## My position remains unchanged
- **No plant closures in Germany**
- **No compulsory redundancies**
- **Collective wage standards protected**
- **A fair, negotiated employee contribution**
- **Capacity reduction through operational means**
- **Redeployment and qualification as core tools**
- **Management and owners must contribute substantially too**

## What would move me
Only one thing would move me off this line:  
a **verified, site-by-site savings bridge** showing that no-closure or hybrid options cannot close the gap, even with serious contributions from management and owners.

That has not happened.
