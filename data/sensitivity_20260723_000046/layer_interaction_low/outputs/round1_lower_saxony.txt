I appreciate that management is at least acknowledging the need for a negotiated settlement. That is the right starting point. But I need to be equally clear: **a restructuring that relies on plant closures in Germany or on compulsory redundancies is not acceptable from our side**.

Why? Because this company is not just a cost equation. It is a central industrial anchor for entire regions, and the state is not a passive investor here. We have a responsibility to the workforce, to suppliers, and to the regional economy. Once a plant is closed, that capacity and trust are gone. The political and social damage is not “transition cost”; it is permanent.

I do agree with one point from management: **there has to be a real improvement in competitiveness**. A cosmetic deal would be irresponsible. But the conclusion you draw from that is too narrow. You are treating closures as the benchmark and then asking others to find their way down from that. I do not accept that framing.

What I would consider a serious basis for discussion is this:

- **no plant closures in Germany**
- **no compulsory redundancies**
- **capacity reductions through negotiated operational adjustments**, not shutdowns
- **internal redeployment, qualification, and working-time solutions**
- **a fair employee contribution**, but not one-sided wage cuts that simply shift the burden onto the workforce
- **management and ownership also contributing** through overhead reductions, investment prioritization, and disciplined capital allocation
- **binding investment commitments** for future products and technologies in Germany
- **co-determination respected throughout the process**, not bypassed

On the workforce side, I hear the union’s insistence on site security and no forced layoffs, and I support that line. That is the condition for any socially sustainable restructuring. At the same time, I would expect the employee side to engage seriously on flexibility, productivity, and cost measures if those are part of a broader package that actually secures the plants.

Where I disagree with management is your suggestion that because the cost problem is real, closures become unavoidable. That is not the only way to create structural savings. It may be the easiest way from a spreadsheet perspective, but it is not the best way for the company’s long-term stability. If the result destroys regional employment and sidelines the state’s role, it will not be a durable solution — it will be a conflict with delayed damage.

So my position is straightforward:

**We are open to a tough restructuring. We are not open to a restructuring that sacrifices German plants and jobs as the price of executive freedom.**

If management wants our support, it needs to bring forward a package that proves competitiveness can be restored **without** plant closures and without compulsory redundancies. Otherwise, I will not treat this as a balanced solution, and I will use the rights and responsibilities that come with our stake accordingly.
