I appreciate the expert’s clarification, because it confirms the central point management has been making: **a preserve-all-sites solution only works if the required savings are moderate**. That is exactly the issue here. We are not dealing with a moderate gap. We are dealing with a structural cost problem in a shrinking and brutally competitive market. So I need to be direct: **I cannot accept a package that protects every current site and freezes the existing cost base through 2030**.

### Where I agree
I agree with several points raised by the employee side, the state, and the expert:
- A cosmetic deal would be useless.
- A negotiated settlement is preferable to open conflict.
- Capacity reduction, working-time measures, redeployment, qualification, and some employee contribution are all legitimate tools.
- Durability matters; a temporary fix is not enough.

### Where I disagree
I disagree very clearly on the following:
- **No plant closures as an absolute rule**
- **No compulsory redundancies as an absolute rule**
- **Binding job and site security through 2030 regardless of economics**
- **A model where management and owners are asked to contribute while the workforce keeps all structural protections**

That combination does not solve the crisis. It delays it.

### To the works council
You say closures are a choice, not a law of nature. That is only partly true. There are choices, yes — but there are also economic limits. If utilization is too low, if fixed costs are too high, and if the product mix is shifting, then keeping every site open while reducing cost elsewhere eventually becomes arithmetic fiction.

You also keep asking for “all sides” to contribute. I agree. But then let’s be honest about what contribution means. For management, it means delivering a credible restructuring. For owners, it means supporting a hard turnaround. For employees, it means accepting that **the company’s cost base has to come down structurally**, which may require site consolidation, lower pay components, or other deep adjustments.

A package that leaves every plant open and simply redistributes pain through overtime limits and voluntary exits is unlikely to close the gap. The expert has made that clear.

### To the union
You ask for exact numbers, and that is fair. But I am not going to pretend we can solve this with a vague menu of temporary tools if the underlying issue is overcapacity.

You want me to prove that closures are necessary. I am telling you the burden of proof cuts both ways. If you are proposing a no-closure, no-forced-redundancy solution, then you need to show how it closes a large permanent cost gap quickly enough and durably enough. The expert’s answer is simple: that is only plausible if the savings target is moderate. Management does not believe it is moderate.

So I will put it this way: **if you want to keep every German site open, then you must offer a package with the same structural savings and the same certainty as a consolidation scenario.** Otherwise, it is not an equivalent solution.

### To the state government
I understand your political responsibility, but I have to say this plainly: **regional stability cannot be bought by preserving all existing structures regardless of competitiveness**. If we keep inefficient capacity in place, the company will weaken further, and that eventually harms the same regions you are trying to protect.

I do not dismiss the social consequences of closures. But I also do not accept a framework where political discomfort becomes a veto on necessary restructuring. If the company does not become competitive, the long-term damage to employment, investment, and regional value will be worse.

### To the family owners
You correctly warn against value-destroying escalation. I share that concern. But value is also destroyed by indecision, by half-measures, and by a settlement that does not restore margins. We need a turnaround that is credible to the market and effective in operations. That means I need governance support for a real structural reset, not a symbolic one.

### What management needs
Let me be concrete about the kind of outcome we need:
- a **material reduction in overcapacity**
- a **permanent reduction in fixed labor cost**
- a **credible path back to target margins**
- **freed-up funds for EV, software, and future product investment**
- a solution that is **durable and enforceable**

If those savings can be achieved through alternatives to closure, I am open to that. But I have not heard a package that demonstrates that yet. What I have heard is a list of tools that may soften the adjustment, not one that clearly closes the gap.

### Minimum acceptable outcome from management’s side
Not a guarantee to keep every site open.  
Not a promise of zero job impact.  
Not a temporary workaround.

My minimum acceptable outcome is:
- a **durable structural cost reduction**
- a **genuine labor-cost contribution**
- **capacity rationalization**
- and an agreement that gives the company room to invest and compete through 2030

### Red line
A settlement that:
- leaves the company structurally uncompetitive,
- protects every site as an untouchable principle,
- and postpones the real adjustment into the future.

That I cannot accept.

### One concrete compromise option
I remain open to a negotiated package that combines:
- selected capacity reductions,
- working-time flexibility,
- internal redeployment and qualification,
- temporary employee concessions,
- management overhead restraint,
- and investment commitments,

**but only if it produces a materially equivalent cost outcome to a site-consolidation scenario and does not simply preserve the current structure under a different name.**

If the employee side wants to avoid closures, then it must help deliver the same savings with the same certainty. If it cannot, then we should stop pretending that a no-closure formula is automatically viable.
