Below is a quantified **plant-level restructuring assessment** of the central question now on the table:

> **Can the company achieve a “durable structural cost reset” in Germany without plant closures and without compulsory redundancies, using capacity reduction, working-time changes, redeployment, and labor-cost concessions instead?**

Short answer: **yes, in principle — but only if the required savings gap is moderate and/or if the labor-cost concession is unusually large.**  
If management’s gap is very large, then a preserve-all-sites package becomes increasingly unlikely to close it without some combination of **site consolidation, stronger wage concessions, or eventual workforce reduction through attrition and voluntary exits far beyond normal levels**.

Because no site-by-site operational data were provided, the analysis below uses a transparent scenario model rather than pretending to know the company’s exact internal numbers.

---

## 1) Analytical frame

For a German OEM with multiple domestic plants, the relevant restructuring question is not just “how many jobs can be cut?” It is:

1. **How much fixed cost can be removed?**
2. **How much of that comes from labor vs. overhead vs. capacity rationalization?**
3. **Can labor be reduced without closures or forced redundancies fast enough to matter?**
4. **Can redeployment absorb the displaced workforce, and where are the bottlenecks?**

In practice, the no-closure route usually depends on four levers:

- **Capacity reduction without formal closure**  
  fewer shifts, lower line rates, model consolidation within a plant, less overtime, less temporary labor

- **Internal redeployment**  
  moving people from declining ICE-related activities into EV, battery, logistics, QA, maintenance, IT, and ramp-up functions

- **Working-time and pay measures**  
  shorter hours, premium moderation, bonus restraint, lower allowances, temporary pay freezes or reductions

- **Voluntary headcount reduction**  
  early retirement, attrition, severance programs, transfer into external entities, partial retirement

The critical issue is whether these levers can generate savings comparable to a closure/consolidation package.

## 2) Base-case assumptions used for modeling

Since exact figures are unavailable, I use a realistic German OEM restructuring framework.

### Assumed German operating footprint
- **4 major German passenger-car plants** plus supporting functions
- Total German workforce in scope: **~45,000–55,000**
- Direct manufacturing workers: **~60–65%**
- Indirect/support staff: **~35–40%**

For illustration, I model a **50,000-person German workforce**.

### Current cost structure assumptions
Typical annual fully loaded labor cost in German automotive manufacturing:
- Direct production employee: **€65k–€85k**
- Indirect/support employee: **€85k–€120k**
- Blended average: **~€80k–€90k**

I use a blended average of **€85k per employee per year**.

That implies total German labor cost of roughly:

- **50,000 × €85k = €4.25bn/year**

This is not total manufacturing cost, but it is the relevant pool for many restructuring levers.

### Savings target scenarios
I evaluate three restructuring gap scenarios:

- **Scenario A: Moderate gap** = **€400m–€600m/year**
- **Scenario B: Material gap** = **€700m–€1.0bn/year**
- **Scenario C: Severe gap** = **€1.2bn+/year**

This is important because the feasibility of no-closure restructuring changes sharply across these bands.

## 4) What each lever can realistically save

## A. Capacity reduction without closure
This includes:
- removing a shift
- reducing line speed
- lowering overtime
- simplifying product mix
- consolidating models on fewer lines
- cutting internal logistics and support intensity

### Typical savings magnitude
For a major plant:
- removing one shift or a large part of one shift can reduce **10%–25%** of site labor-related cost
- but not all of that is cash savings, because fixed overhead remains unless the site is partially resized

### Estimated savings in this model
If the company reduces effective capacity by 10–15% across the German footprint without closing sites:

- workforce impact: **4,000–7,000 FTE equivalent**
- annual savings: **€250m–€500m**

That is meaningful, but often **not enough alone** for a severe restructuring gap.

---

## B. Internal redeployment
This is strongest where:
- EV ramp-up creates vacancies,
- battery/quality/logistics roles expand,
- maintenance and automation support need more staff,
- and there is a skill match.

### Typical constraints
- not all ICE assembly workers can move directly into EV/software/quality roles
- retraining takes **6–24 months**
- some sites have limited local vacancy capacity
- redeployment often shifts cost rather than removes it unless it replaces external hiring

### Estimated redeployment capacity
A realistic German OEM can often redeploy:
- **5%–10% of workforce over 12 months**
- **8%–15% over 24–36 months**
if there is real volume in the receiving functions.

For 50,000 workers:
- **2,500–5,000** might be redeployed over time
- but only **some fraction** of that translates into permanent savings, depending on hiring avoidance

### Estimated savings
- **€50m–€150m/year** in net structural savings, sometimes more if it avoids new hiring at premium pay
- but redeployment is usually a **supporting lever**, not the main savings engine

---

## C. Working-time measures
These include:
- weekly hour reductions
- short-time work
- shift changes
- overtime/premium suppression
- temporary labor reductions

### Estimated savings
Depending on depth:
- a 3%–5% labor cost reduction from working-time/premium changes can yield
  - **€125m–€215m/year** on a €4.25bn wage bill

If the package includes very significant premium and overtime reductions, it may reach:
- **€250m+/year**

### Limitation
Working-time savings are often:
- real,
- fast,
- and politically easier than layoffs,

but they do **not fully solve permanent overcapacity** unless demand stays lower permanently and the operating model is reset.

---

## D. Labor-cost contribution / pay restraint
This is usually the biggest no-closure lever if the workforce accepts it.

Examples:
- base-pay moderation
- bonus cuts
- allowance removal
- legacy benefit changes
- deferred wage increases
- temporary contribution levy

### Estimated savings
A **5%–8%** reduction in blended labor cost can save:

- **€210m–€340m/year**

A larger, unusually aggressive package of **10%** could approach:

- **€425m/year**

But that is large for a German OEM and typically hard to secure durably without offsetting guarantees.

---

## E. Voluntary headcount reduction
This includes:
- early retirement
- attrition
- severance
- partial retirement
- transfer out

### Typical German automotive reality
Voluntary measures can work, but usually:
- slowly,
- unevenly,
- and not enough for a deep reset by themselves.

Typical annual natural attrition:
- **1.5%–3.0%**
Voluntary targeted exits under a package:
- potentially **3%–6%** over 1–2 years

For 50,000 workers:
- **1,500–3,000/year** under strong programs
- perhaps **4,000–6,000** over a few years

### Estimated savings
- **€100m–€250m/year** eventually, but timing is the issue
- if the target is immediate permanent savings, attrition is too slow as a standalone tool

---

## 5) Combined package outcomes

## Scenario A: Moderate savings gap (€400m–€600m/year)
A no-closure package is **feasible** if it combines:

- capacity cuts: **€250m–€350m**
- working-time/premium measures: **€100m–€150m**
- redeployment/hiring avoidance: **€50m–€100m**
- labor-cost contribution: **€100m–€150m**

### Total
- **~€500m–€750m/year**

### Assessment
This can work **without closures and without forced redundancies**, provided:
- the savings target is near the low end,
- the labor-cost contribution is real,
- and the company accepts slower implementation.

---

## Scenario B: Material gap (€700m–€1.0bn/year)
Here a preserve-all-sites deal becomes **possible but strained**.

A plausible maximum package might be:

- capacity cuts: **€300m–€450m**
- labor-cost contribution: **€200m–€300m**
- working-time/premium reduction: **€125m–€200m**
- redeployment/hiring avoidance: **€75m–€150m**

### Total
- **~€700m–€1.1bn/year**

### Assessment
This is the most realistic upper boundary for a no-closure solution **if**:
- labor accepts significant concessions,
- management commits to investment and site security,
- and the company can actually eliminate the relevant shifts/complexity.

But this is a **hard bargain**. The margins for error are small.

---

## Scenario C: Severe gap (€1.2bn+/year)
A no-closure, no-forced-layoff package is **unlikely** to close this gap.

Even an aggressive combination usually tops out around:
- **€800m–€1.1bn/year** in credible durable savings

To get beyond that, the company usually needs one or more of:
- site consolidation,
- major model concentration,
- larger headcount reduction than voluntary flows can deliver,
- or a much deeper wage reset than is typically sustainable.

### Assessment
If management’s real target is in this range, then the employee-side proposal is probably **not sufficient by itself**.

## 6) Site-by-site implications under a no-closure package

Using the four-site model:

### Plant 1: High-volume core assembly
- Best candidate for **shift trimming and line consolidation**
- Redeployment easier if it remains a strategic core plant
- Could absorb some EV production
- Potential savings: **€120m–€180m/year**

### Plant 2: Powertrain / transition site
- If it is transitioning away from ICE, redeployment can be stronger
- If EV/battery-related work is real, it may absorb labor well
- Potential savings: **€90m–€150m/year**
- Risk: if product transition is delayed, overcapacity remains

### Plant 3: Legacy ICE-exposed or low-utilization plant
- Most vulnerable economically
- In a no-closure deal, savings come from:
  - major volume reduction,
  - product simplification,
  - and large labor-cost concessions
- Potential savings: **€100m–€170m/year**
- This is often the hardest site to stabilize without structural consolidation

### Plant 4: Specialty/logistics/support-heavy site
- More flexible if support functions can be centralized or digitized
- Potential savings: **€50m–€100m/year**
- May absorb some redeployed workers if there is true growth in logistics/QA/parts

### Overall site-level conclusion
A preserve-all-sites plan can work if:
- Plant 1 and 2 absorb growth,
- Plant 3 is materially resized,
- Plant 4 is rationalized,
- and overhead is reduced across all sites.

But if every site is protected equally, the economics usually fail.

---

## 7) Headcount impact under competing options

## Option 1: Plant closures / consolidation
Typical outcome:
- direct closure-related displacement: **3,000–8,000 jobs**
- wider indirect impact: **5,000–12,000 jobs**
- savings: potentially **€800m–€1.5bn/year** depending on scale

This is the strongest cost reset but politically and labor-relations-wise the most disruptive.

## Option 2: No closures, no forced redundancies
Typical outcome:
- direct workforce reduction through attrition/voluntary exits/redeployment: **2,500–6,000**
- temporary or permanent redeployment: **2,000–5,000**
- savings: **€500m–€1.1bn/year** depending on labor concessions

This is viable only if the savings target is not too large.

## Option 3: Hybrid compromise
- one plant or one major line is consolidated,
- no forced redundancies,
- strong voluntary exit program,
- wage restraint,
- some site guarantees for remaining plants

Typical outcome:
- displacement: **3,000–7,000**
- savings: **€700m–€1.3bn/year**

This is often the most workable middle path if a deep savings target exists.

## 8) Redeployment capacity: what is realistic?

This is where no-closure plans are often overestimated.

### Likely absorbable groups
- logistics
- quality
- maintenance
- warehousing
- certain EV assembly roles
- internal services
- selected indirect staff

### Difficult-to-absorb groups
- specialized ICE powertrain labor
- highly site-specific skilled trades
- excess indirect functions where no growth function exists
- older workers with limited retraining runway

### Rough redeployment rule of thumb
At most sites, **20%–30% of displaced workers** can be redeployed relatively cleanly in the near term.  
A higher share is possible only if:
- there is major new volume,
- a large EV ramp,
- or another nearby site can absorb labor.

So if 5,000 workers become surplus:
- perhaps **1,000–1,500** can be redeployed quickly
- the rest need attrition, voluntary exit, working-time reduction, or some form of structural reduction

This is why “redeployment” is essential but usually not sufficient.

---

## 9) What this means for the management claim

Management’s claim, in operational terms, is:

> “We need a durable structural cost reset, and a preserve-all-sites model will not deliver enough.”

That claim is **credible if** the required savings are in the **upper half of Scenario B or in Scenario C**.

But the works council and union are also right to say:

> “No-closure restructuring is not impossible; it depends on the size of the gap and the contribution asked from labor.”

That is also correct.

So the real disputed variable is not ideology. It is:
- the **size of the gap**,
- the **credibility of redeployment**,
- and the **depth of labor contribution management can actually secure**.

---

## 10) Bottom-line assessment

### My expert judgment
A **no-closure, no-forced-redundancy solution is operationally feasible only if the required annual savings are roughly in the €500m–€1.0bn range and if the labor side accepts a meaningful, durable contribution.**

If the required savings are **well above €1bn/year**, the no-closure route becomes much less plausible, because:
- voluntary exits are too slow,
- redeployment saturates,
- working-time cuts are not enough,
- and labor-cost concessions alone rarely fill the entire gap.

### In plain language
- **Moderate restructuring gap:** no-closure package can work.
- **Large restructuring gap:** no-closure package becomes fragile.
- **Very large restructuring gap:** some form of structural consolidation is usually needed.
