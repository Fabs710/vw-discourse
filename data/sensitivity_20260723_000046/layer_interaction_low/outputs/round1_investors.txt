From the perspective of dispersed capital-market shareholders, the non-negotiable point is not the method; it is the **outcome**.

We can live with a restructuring that avoids plant closures if it **credibly delivers the same structural savings, a lower fixed-cost base, and a clear path back to competitive margins**. What we cannot support is a settlement that protects all sites and all legacy guarantees while leaving the company’s cost base uncompetitive. That would be a cosmetic compromise, and the market will punish it.

So, on the arguments so far:

- **To management:** we agree that the current cost position is not sustainable and that the company needs durable structural change, not a short-term patch. But credibility matters. If “restructuring” just means a protracted confrontation with strikes, uncertainty, and half-measures, that destroys value. You need to be precise about the savings, the timing, and the enforceability of the package. If you cannot show how the plan restores margins, investors will not give you the benefit of the doubt.

- **To the works council and union:** we hear the concern about closures and forced redundancies. But an absolute no to closures cannot be the starting point if it prevents the company from regaining competitiveness. We are not interested in protecting legacy structures for their own sake. If you want capital to stay patient, you need to accept a meaningful employee contribution and real operational change. A deal that preserves every entitlement but leaves the business structurally weak is not acceptable.

- **To the state government:** your concern for regional stability is understandable, but regional politics cannot override economic reality indefinitely. If the company remains uncompetitive, the long-run result is worse for jobs, investment, and shareholder value alike. Support for a negotiated solution is welcome, but only if it actually fixes the economics.

- **To the family owners:** we agree with the emphasis on durable competitiveness and avoiding value-destroying escalation. Where we would press you is this: the company needs a settlement that is not just politically survivable but financially convincing. If the family blocks the only credible route to cost reduction, then it risks protecting control at the expense of enterprise value.

Our position is straightforward:

1. **We support a negotiated settlement only if it materially restores competitiveness.**  
2. **We do not support a cosmetic compromise that leaves margins structurally impaired.**  
3. **We are open to alternatives to closures, but only if the savings are real, durable, and enforceable.**  
4. **We want deal certainty.** Prolonged conflict, strike risk, and open-ended uncertainty are value destructive.

So the question for the other stakeholders is not whether they can declare red lines. It is whether they can produce a package that:
- reduces capacity enough,
- lowers labour and fixed costs enough,
- and does so with enough certainty to restore confidence in the business.

If the answer is no, then from an investor standpoint the company is simply postponing the necessary adjustment. And that would be unacceptable.
