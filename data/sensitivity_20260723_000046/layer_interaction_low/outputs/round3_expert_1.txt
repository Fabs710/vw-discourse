From a plant-level restructuring and workforce-modeling perspective, the discussion is still missing the core evidence needed to choose among the options.

## What can be said with confidence
- A **no-closure / no-forced-redundancy** restructuring can be operationally feasible **in principle**.
- Whether it is feasible **in practice** depends on:
  - the size of the savings gap,
  - the amount of idle or underutilized capacity,
  - how much headcount can be removed through attrition, early retirement, and redeployment,
  - whether labor-cost concessions are temporary or structural,
  - and how much production can be consolidated without breaking logistics or quality.

## What is not yet proven
No party has provided a quantified bridge showing:
- the required savings target,
- savings by measure,
- site-by-site headcount effects,
- redeployment capacity by plant,
- and the timing of each measure.

Without that, claims that closures are “necessary” or that closures are “unnecessary” are both incomplete.

## How a proper comparison should be built
A serious site-by-site model would need, for each plant:
1. current headcount by function
2. current utilization and shift pattern
3. direct labor cost per vehicle or per output unit
4. fixed overhead allocated to the site
5. natural attrition and retirement rates
6. redeployment absorptive capacity at other German sites
7. training lead times for transfer into eligible roles
8. constraints from tooling, product mix, and logistics
9. likely one-time transition costs

## Likely operational implications of the three option types

### 1) Full closure / consolidation
Likely effects:
- fastest and deepest fixed-cost reduction
- highest one-time transition cost
- highest implementation risk
- strongest disruption to supply chain and labor relations
- greatest need for relocation, severance, and qualification support

This is usually the most certain way to remove cost, but not necessarily the least risky overall.

### 2) No-closure with concessions
Likely effects:
- lower social disruption
- higher dependence on labor-cost concessions and internal flexibility
- slower to realize savings
- usually limited by how much capacity can be removed without closure
- can work if the savings gap is moderate and redeployment is substantial

This model often fails if management needs large, permanent fixed-cost reduction quickly.

### 3) Hybrid compromise
Likely effects:
- partial consolidation of weak activities
- retention of strategically important sites
- better balance between savings and continuity
- still requires material headcount reduction and site-level redesign
- often the most realistic outcome where governance makes full closure hard

This is usually the most workable path if the gap is meaningful but not catastrophic.

## Main workforce-modeling constraint
The key question is not whether jobs can be reduced. They can.
The key question is whether enough jobs can be removed **without**:
- forced layoffs,
- closure,
- or a cost structure that still leaves the company uncompetitive.

That depends on the gap.

## Bottom line
Based on the information given:
- **A preserve-all-sites solution is possible only if the savings gap is not too large and labor accepts a material contribution.**
- **If the gap is very large, closure or some form of structural consolidation becomes increasingly likely.**
