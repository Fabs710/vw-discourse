## 2) Scenario comparison: what each option typically delivers

### A. Full closure / consolidation
**Industrial effect**
- biggest immediate reduction in fixed cost
- strongest reduction in structural overcapacity
- fastest route to capacity rationalization if execution goes smoothly

**Cost profile**
- high one-time costs:
  - severance
  - early retirement
  - relocation
  - decommissioning
  - dual-running / ramp-down costs
  - possible strike-related disruption
- savings are strong in run-rate terms, but realized later than management often presents

**Workforce effect**
- largest gross job loss
- some redeployment possible, but usually incomplete
- hardest politically and industrially

**Analytical note**
A closure plan is only superior if the **NPV of incremental run-rate savings** clearly exceeds:
1. transition costs,
2. disruption risk,
3. lost flexibility,
4. and the value of an alternative settlement that keeps sites open.

---

### B. No-closure with concessions
**Industrial effect**
- preserves all sites
- relies on:
  - shift reduction
  - working-time flexibility
  - voluntary exits
  - redeployment
  - wage restraint / labor-cost concessions
  - overhead cuts
  - product specialization and volume reallocation

**Cost profile**
- lower one-time cost than closures
- savings are usually more gradual
- structural savings can be meaningful, but only if concessions are durable and utilization improves

**Workforce effect**
- avoids forced redundancies
- gross headcount can still fall materially through attrition, early retirement, and voluntary exits
- redeployment burden is high

**Analytical note**
This option can work only if the following are true:
- enough attrition exists,
- enough internal vacancies exist,
- labor-cost concessions are sizable and durable,
- capacity can be reduced without keeping excess fixed cost alive.

If utilization remains weak, this model often underdelivers on permanent savings.

---

### C. Hybrid compromise
**Industrial effect**
- preserves strategic sites
- consolidates weaker overlaps
- may remove one shift, one line, or one function rather than an entire plant
- often the most realistic bargaining outcome

**Cost profile**
- moderate one-time costs
- moderate to strong permanent savings
- lower disruption than full closure, stronger savings than pure no-closure

**Workforce effect**
- headcount reduction is still real
- more likely to be manageable through voluntary exits, retirement, transfers, and retraining
- still requires painful choices, just less extreme ones

**Analytical note**
From a plant-economics perspective, the hybrid is often the most plausible equilibrium if:
- there is some genuine overcapacity,
- but not enough to justify outright site closure,
- or closures are too disruptive to be value-maximizing.

---

## 3) The key economic test: savings bridge vs. transition cost

The decision should hinge on a simple test:

### Net present value logic
For each scenario:

**Net benefit = run-rate savings − one-time costs − disruption costs − residual inefficiency**

Where:
- **run-rate savings** = labor + overhead + capacity savings at full implementation
- **one-time costs** = severance, retraining, tooling, closure, relocation, dual-running
- **disruption costs** = strike risk, quality losses, supplier disruption, missed output
- **residual inefficiency** = remaining excess fixed cost from keeping too much footprint

A closure plan is justified only if it beats the alternatives on this net basis.

---

## 4) Redeployment capacity: the limiting constraint

A major practical constraint is not just headcount reduction, but **where displaced workers can actually go**.

### Redeployment can absorb labor only if:
- there are open roles elsewhere in the group
- skills are transferable within a reasonable retraining period
- geography is acceptable
- pay/classification differences are manageable
- the company is willing to fund qualification and transition support

### In most auto restructurings, redeployment is limited by:
- product specialization
- line-specific skills
- plant geography
- age profile and mobility
- certification requirements for technical roles

So even when a company says “we can avoid layoffs through redeployment,” the real question is:
**how many FTEs can actually be absorbed, by site and by year?**

### Hybrid

## 7) Most likely conclusion, analytically

Absent extraordinary redeployment capacity, the most likely economic hierarchy is:

1. **Hybrid restructuring**  
   Often best balance of savings, feasibility, and disruption.

2. **Closure-led restructuring**  
   Highest run-rate savings, but only superior if disruption and social cost do not overwhelm the benefit.

3. **Pure no-closure package**  
   Viable only if the savings gap is modest and labor-cost / capacity concessions are large enough and durable enough.

So analytically, **the no-closure option is possible in principle, but not automatically sufficient**.  
And **closures are not automatically necessary, but may become necessary if the savings gap remains large after realistic concessions and redeployment**.
