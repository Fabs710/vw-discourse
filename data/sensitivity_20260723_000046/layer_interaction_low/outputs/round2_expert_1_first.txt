From a plant-level restructuring and workforce-modeling perspective, the key issue is this:

**A no-closure, no-forced-redundancy solution is operationally possible only if the required cost and capacity reduction can be achieved through a combination of demand-adjusted volume cuts, major internal redeployment, working-time flexibility, and a sufficiently large labor-cost concession.** Whether that is feasible depends on the size of the gap management must close.

### What I can assess

#### 1) If all German sites stay open, where can savings realistically come from?
The main levers are:

- **Capacity reduction without closure**
  - Fewer shifts
  - Lower line rates
  - Consolidating production within fewer lines or fewer models per site
  - Extending planned downtime / removing overtime
  - Reducing indirect labor tied to complexity, support functions, and overlap

- **Internal redeployment**
  - Moving staff from declining ICE-related activities into EV, battery-related, software, logistics, quality, and maintenance roles
  - This works only if there are enough internal vacancies and the skill mismatch is not too large

- **Working-time measures**
  - Reduced weekly hours
  - Short-time work mechanisms
  - Limits on overtime, premiums, and shift patterns
  - These can reduce cost quickly, but only if labor agreements allow them and they are durable enough

- **Qualification and conversion**
  - Retraining can preserve employment, but it does not instantly remove overcapacity
  - It is a medium-term tool, not a substitute for immediate structural savings

- **Compensation restraint / labor-cost contribution**
  - This can be meaningful if it affects base pay, allowances, bonuses, or legacy protections
  - In large manufacturing restructurings, this is often the only way to close a major cost gap without headcount cuts

#### 2) What is hard to achieve without closures or layoffs?
There are limits:

- If a plant is structurally underutilized, keeping it open while cutting cost often just spreads fixed overhead over fewer units.
- If product allocation shifts away from a site, redeployment cannot absorb all affected labor unless there are real growth areas inside the same site or nearby sites.
- If the workforce is large and the product mix is declining, **voluntary attrition alone is usually too slow** to close a large gap.
- If management needs a large permanent reduction in fixed cost, **capacity consolidation is often unavoidable** somewhere in the system, even if that is done through shift removal or line simplification rather than a formal closure.

### Practical assessment of the “preserve all sites” option

A package that keeps all German plants open and avoids forced layoffs is most credible if it includes all of the following:

1. **Explicit volume and capacity reductions**
   - Not just promises of efficiency
   - Must specify which shifts, lines, or production volumes come out

2. **A quantified workforce path**
   - How many jobs are reduced by attrition, transfer, retirement, or voluntary exit
   - By when
   - In which occupational groups

3. **A redeployment matrix**
   - Which functions can absorb workers
   - What retraining is required
   - How many workers can realistically be moved within 12, 24, and 36 months

4. **A labor-cost package**
   - Otherwise the savings burden falls entirely on headcount and productivity
   - If wage restraint is modest, the size of the required workforce reduction increases

5. **A strict implementation timeline**
   - A “preserve all sites through 2030” commitment is only credible if market demand and product allocation support it
   - Otherwise, the agreement risks becoming non-implementable

### Key risk points

- **Redeployment capacity may be overstated**
  - In automotive manufacturing, new EV and battery-related roles often require different technical skill profiles.
  - Not all body shop, assembly, logistics, or indirect staff can be absorbed without a real conversion plan.

- **Voluntary measures may be insufficient**
  - Attrition, early retirement, and internal transfer usually work best when the reduction target is moderate.
  - They are rarely enough for a deep structural correction.

- **Labor flexibility may reduce headcount only temporarily**
  - Working-time reductions help cash flow and utilization, but they do not permanently fix overcapacity unless production volume truly stays lower.

- **A site-preservation promise can conflict with operational reality**
  - If demand keeps falling or model allocation changes, the company may later face another round of restructuring unless the agreement includes genuine structural changes.

### Bottom line

**The employee-side position is operationally viable only if management’s required savings are not so large that they exceed what can be delivered by internal redeployment, shift reduction, and labor-cost concessions.**  

If the company needs a very deep permanent cost reset, then preserving every German site and prohibiting forced redundancies becomes increasingly difficult to reconcile with the economics. In that case, the gap usually has to be closed by some combination of:
- stronger labor-cost concessions,
- larger capacity cuts,
- or some form of site consolidation.

### What would be needed to assess feasibility properly
To judge whether the no-closure package is workable, I would need:

- headcount by plant, function, and skill group
- current and projected utilization by site
- product and model allocation through 2030
- vacancy and retirement forecasts
- retraining lead times and conversion costs
- wage bill by labor category
- overhead allocation and fixed-cost structure
- quantified savings target from management

Without that data, the right answer is not “yes” or “no,” but:

**A preserve-all-sites deal can work only if the required savings are moderate and the workforce can be reduced mainly by redeployment, attrition, working-time changes, and meaningful labor-cost concessions. If the savings target is large, the package becomes increasingly unlikely to be sufficient without some form of structural consolidation.**
