I hear the position clearly, but I need to be equally clear: **a solution that preserves every current site and every current guarantee as if nothing has changed is not credible** given the cost position we are facing.

I agree on one point: **we need a negotiated, durable settlement**, not a symbolic fight. A cosmetic package will not solve the structural problem, and I am not interested in that either. But the condition for any agreement is that it must produce **real, lasting cost and capacity reduction**. If it does not, we will be back in the same crisis within a very short time, only with less room to invest in future products, software, and EV competitiveness.

Where I disagree is your blanket rejection of closures and compulsory redundancies as an absolute starting point. That position may be understandable politically, but it does not answer the economic reality. German factory costs are too high, European demand is shrinking, and our competitors are not standing still. If we cannot bring the cost base down structurally, we will lose more jobs over time, not fewer.

So let me be direct about what management needs from this process:

- **A genuine structural reduction in capacity**
- **A sustainable labour-cost contribution**
- **A credible path to improving the brand’s return-on-sales**
- **Freedom to reallocate investment into the technologies that matter to 2030**

I am open on the exact instrument. That means I am not saying every adjustment must be a closure. But I am saying the outcome must be equivalent in effect: fewer sites operating at lower cost, lower production capacity, and a materially lighter fixed-cost base. If you want to avoid closures, then you need to bring forward an alternative that achieves the same savings in a durable way.

On job security: I cannot support a blanket guarantee that freezes the structure through 2030 regardless of economics. What I can discuss is a negotiated framework with transition measures, retraining, internal redeployment, and social safeguards — but only alongside **real capacity adjustment and a meaningful employee contribution**.

On co-determination: yes, that is part of the reality, and any plant decision will require the necessary majority. But that does not change the underlying problem. Blocking change does not make the cost crisis disappear.

So my challenge back to you is this: **if you reject closures and forced redundancies, what exact package do you propose that delivers the same level of structural savings, quickly enough, and with enough certainty for owners, investors, and the business?** If you can produce that, we can talk. If not, then we should be honest that the current stance leaves us without a viable path.
