I’ll be direct.

We all agree on the diagnosis: the company has a structural competitiveness problem, the cost base is too high, and a cosmetic deal would fail. Where we fundamentally disagree is on the conclusion that **German plant closures and compulsory redundancies must be ruled out in advance**.

That position is not credible if the goal is to restore the business to a sustainable return-on-sales level by 2030.

## Where I agree
I agree with the works council, union, state, family owners, and investors on several points:
- the restructuring must be **durable**
- the package must be **quantified**
- we need **shared sacrifice**
- a prolonged conflict would destroy value
- capacity adjustment, redeployment, qualification, working-time changes, and productivity measures all belong in the toolset

So we are not debating whether action is needed. We are debating whether the company is allowed to use the instruments that actually close a structural gap.

## Where I disagree

### 1) “No closures” as an absolute is not defensible
The works council and union keep treating plant closures as if they were a moral failure rather than a possible industrial necessity. I reject that.

If the German footprint is structurally too large for demand, utilization, and cost competitiveness, then capacity must come out somewhere. If you insist that no German plant may be closed under any circumstances, then you are not solving overcapacity — you are freezing it into the structure.

I am open to alternatives. But I am not open to a line that says closures are impossible regardless of the economics.

### 2) “No compulsory redundancies” cannot be an unconditional veto
I would prefer negotiated solutions. I do not want forced layoffs as the first resort.

But I will not accept a framework that removes management’s ability to execute a serious restructuring if the alternative package falls short. If there is no credible path to durable savings without compulsory measures, then those measures cannot be ruled out in advance.

That is not a threat. That is fiduciary realism.

### 3) A no-closure package must be proven, not asserted
Everyone keeps saying “test the alternatives.” Fine — I support that. But testing is not the same as assuming the answer.

I want a side-by-side comparison with hard numbers:
- exact savings required
- exact savings from a no-closure package
- exact savings from capacity consolidation or closures
- timing to 2030
- durability of the measures
- impact on investment capacity and margins

If the no-closure route truly closes the gap, I will consider it seriously. If it does not, then the hard options remain on the table.

## To the works council
You say closures and compulsory redundancies are red lines. I hear the political and social reason for that. But your red lines do not repair the cost base.

You also say management must prove that alternatives fail. I agree with the principle. But you are demanding a conclusion before the evidence is complete. That is backwards.

If you want binding site guarantees through 2030, then show how the required savings are achieved **without** making the company structurally uncompetitive. Right now I do not see that.

## To the union
You say workers are prepared to contribute, but not by losing jobs. I appreciate the willingness to discuss wage restraint, flexibility, redeployment, and productivity. That is constructive.

But “we contribute, except where it matters most” is not enough.

If the package is to be credible, then I need:
- a quantified labour-cost contribution
- capacity adjustment at a level that actually matters
- implementation certainty
- and a path that restores margins, not just moods

If the result preserves every site, rules out compulsory adjustment, and still leaves the company under water, then it is not a restructuring.

## To the state
You are right that legitimacy matters. You are also right that a prolonged confrontation would damage the region and the company.

But legitimacy is not the same as preserving every existing structure. If a no-closure package is real, measurable, and durable, I will support it. If it is only politically attractive but economically insufficient, then it would fail the very workers and region you want to protect because it postpones the necessary correction.

So yes: use your influence to push for a negotiated solution. But do not use it to block every hard decision before the economics are settled.

## To the family holding
I appreciate your concern about value destruction from a maximalist shutdown strategy. On that point, we agree.

But you cannot simply ask management to “be careful” while simultaneously expecting a major turnaround. We need owner backing for a solution that is hard enough to work. If the package is too soft, we all lose. If it is too hard and triggers needless escalation, we also lose.

What I need from you is support for a credible structural package — not an indefinite preference for comfort over change.

## To institutional investors
You are right to insist on measurable, durable margin repair. That is exactly my point.

Where I push back is on the suggestion that the instrument does not matter much as long as the outcome is good. In this situation the instrument matters a great deal, because some instruments actually deliver structural change and others merely defer it.

So yes, I will support an alternative package if it produces equivalent savings. But “equivalent” means equivalent:
- in scale
- in timing
- and in durability

Not softer in effect, and not slower in landing, unless the gap is still fully closed.

## My position, plainly stated
Management’s position remains:
- restore competitiveness
- reduce overcapacity
- lower labour costs structurally
- protect the brand’s return-on-sales target
- free funds for future products and technology to 2030

I remain open on how that is achieved.

But I am **not** open to a deal that:
- rules out closures in advance
- rules out compulsory redundancies in advance
- preserves all guarantees regardless of the economics
- and leaves the company with an uncompetitive cost base

That would not be a restructuring. It would be a postponement with better PR.

## What I need next
If the employee side and state want to prove that hard instruments are unnecessary, then bring a package with:
1. exact savings target
2. exact workforce contribution
3. exact capacity reduction
4. exact productivity measures
5. exact management and owner contribution
6. a clear timeline and verification mechanism through 2030

If that package closes the gap credibly, I will treat it seriously.

If it does not, then the hard options stay on the table.

## Bottom line
I am not here to preserve the status quo. I am here to restore this company’s competitiveness.

If we want a future industrial base in Germany, then we need a restructuring that is real, durable, and financially sufficient. Anything less is not responsible.
