We are aligned on the diagnosis, but not yet on the acceptable means.

From the perspective of capital-market shareholders, the priority is straightforward: **restore competitive margins, reduce the cost base durably, and do it with enough certainty that the market can underwrite the turnaround.** Continued erosion is not acceptable. A cosmetic agreement that preserves the current structure and merely postpones the adjustment would be value-destructive.

## Where I agree
- **Management is right** that this is not a temporary issue. The German cost base appears structurally too high, and the brand is not earning its cost of capital.
- **The family owner is right** that a prolonged public conflict and industrial instability destroy value too.
- **The state and labour are right** that a purely one-sided approach can become politically and operationally unworkable.

## Where I disagree
### 1) “No closures” is too absolute
I understand the concern, but a blanket refusal to consider plant closures is not compatible with restoring competitiveness if the footprint is genuinely oversized. If closures are the only way to achieve durable savings at the required scale, then excluding them in advance simply protects inefficiency.

What matters to us is not whether a site is spared for symbolic reasons, but whether the final structure is **economically sustainable**. If there is a credible alternative that delivers the same savings without closures, fine. But that has to be proven, not assumed.

### 2) “No compulsory redundancies” cannot become a veto on adjustment
We prefer negotiated solutions over forced cuts because strikes and prolonged confrontation destroy near-term value. But if the workforce insists on full employment guarantees while also rejecting major site changes, we would be left with a structurally uncompetitive cost base. That is not acceptable.

### 3) A shared-sacrifice package must still be measurable
I am open to working-time changes, redeployment, qualification, productivity measures, and temporary employee contributions. But these only matter if they produce **quantified, durable savings** sufficient to close the margin gap. We will not support vague promises or a settlement that is politically attractive but financially thin.

## What would move us
We would support an agreement if it delivers all of the following:
1. **A credible, quantified path to margin improvement**
2. **Durable reduction in capacity and fixed costs**
3. **Clear implementation certainty**
4. **Minimal disruption and strike risk**
5. **No structural impairment of the company’s cost position**

That may involve closures, consolidation, labor-cost adjustments, or a combination. We are agnostic on the instrument. We are not agnostic on the outcome.

## Our limit
What we will not support is:
- a deal that leaves the cost base uncompetitive,
- margins structurally impaired,
- or the company locked into overcapacity because the governance structure makes hard choices impossible.

## Message to management
If you want investor confidence, bring a plan that clearly shows:
- where the savings come from,
- when they land,
- how durable they are,
- and why they are enough to restore returns.

Without that, the market will treat the restructuring as incomplete.

## Message to labour and the state
We are not asking for gratuitous destruction. But we are also not prepared to finance a settlement that preserves every legacy arrangement while the business continues to underperform. If there is a negotiated alternative that truly closes the competitiveness gap, we will support it. If there isn’t, then the company needs to confront the hard restructuring openly.

## Bottom line
We are willing to back a tough, negotiated restructuring. We are not willing to back a compromise that is merely comfortable. The final package must credibly restore competitive margins, even if that requires difficult structural changes.
