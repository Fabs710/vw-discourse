I will be direct.

## Our position
The family holding supports a **serious, durable restructuring**. We do **not** support a cosmetic compromise that preserves today’s comfort and erodes tomorrow’s value.

That said, I am **not** prepared to endorse an unconditional “no closures, no compulsory redundancies” rule as a governing principle. Nor am I prepared to endorse a maximalist closure-first strategy. Both are wrong if taken as absolutes. The only question that matters is: **what package actually restores competitiveness, margins, and investment capacity through 2030?**

## Where I agree
I agree with management, investors, labour, and the state on several points:
- the company has a structural cost and capacity problem;
- a purely symbolic agreement would fail;
- workforce, management, and ownership must all contribute;
- a prolonged conflict would destroy value;
- the package must be quantified and durable, not rhetorical.

That is the common ground.

## Where I disagree with labour and the state
You keep treating plant closures and compulsory redundancies as if they were morally disqualifying in advance. I do not accept that framing.

I would strongly prefer a solution that avoids closures and forced layoffs. But I will not support turning that preference into a **pre-commitment** if the economics do not close. If the alternative package is genuinely sufficient, then great — we should prefer it. If it is not sufficient, then refusing hard instruments in advance is not stewardship; it is risk transfer onto the enterprise.

I also reject the idea that “binding site security through 2030” can be offered as an unconditional guarantee regardless of the savings gap. Guarantees are only credible if the underlying economics are credible.

## Where I disagree with management
At the same time, management cannot simply say “closures are available” and expect that to count as a plan.

That is not enough. The family holding does **not** support forcing through value destruction just to demonstrate decisiveness. A closure-first posture can trigger a long labour conflict, reputational damage, and political escalation that destroys the very value it claims to protect.

So management must do the hard work:
- quantify the gap;
- compare alternatives honestly;
- show what the no-closure package delivers;
- show what it does not deliver;
- and show why, if it falls short, it falls short.

If management cannot do that, then it is not presenting a governance-grade restructuring case.

## To the works council and union
I respect your insistence on job security and industrial capability. But “no closures” and “no compulsory redundancies” cannot be treated as self-justifying answers. They are outcomes that still have to be made economically credible.

You say management has not proven alternatives fail. Fair enough. But you also have not proven that your preferred route closes the gap at the necessary scale and duration.

If you want the family holding to back a no-closure settlement, then bring a package that is:
1. quantified,
2. enforceable,
3. materially shared,
4. and demonstrably sufficient to restore competitiveness.

That means real contributions, not just principles.

## To the state
I understand your regional mandate, and I do not dismiss it. A destructive conflict would hurt the region as well as the company.

But the state should not confuse political opposition to closures with a viable industrial strategy. If the non-closure route is credible, I will support it. If it is not, then a rigid no-closure stance becomes a recipe for a weaker company and a weaker region later.

## To investors
You are right to focus on risk-adjusted value and durable savings. I agree with that.

Where I will push back is if “durable” becomes shorthand for “use the harshest instrument available.” That is too blunt. Durable value comes from the right mix of:
- capacity adjustment,
- productivity improvements,
- working-time flexibility,
- workforce contribution,
- management restraint,
- and owner discipline.

If that mix closes the gap, I prefer it. If it does not, then we need to be honest about that too.

## What would move the family holding
We can support a settlement if it clearly shows:
- a credible savings target;
- a side-by-side comparison of non-closure and structural options;
- shared sacrifice across all constituencies;
- verified implementation milestones;
- and a path to durable margins and future investment.

That is the test.

## My bottom line
I am not here to defend comfort. I am here to protect the long-term value of the enterprise.

So:
- I support a **quantified, serious no-closure test**;
- I support **binding milestones and verification**;
- I support **real contributions from labour, management, and ownership**;
- I do **not** support an unconditional veto on all hard instruments before the numbers are proven;
- and I do **not** support a reckless closure strategy that destroys value and labour peace.

Bring the numbers. Then we can judge whether the no-closure route is enough. If it is, I will prefer it. If it is not, then the hard options remain part of the governance reality.
