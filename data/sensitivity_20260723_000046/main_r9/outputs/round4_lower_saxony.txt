I will be clear.

I **support** a serious restructuring, and I **do not** support a cosmetic deal that leaves the company uncompetitive. But I **do not** accept the management logic that plant closures and compulsory redundancies must remain “available” in advance as a matter of principle. That is exactly where I draw the line.

## To management
I agree with you on the diagnosis:
- the cost base is too high,
- the company needs durable savings,
- and a weak compromise would fail.

Where I disagree is your conclusion that you must keep closures and forced redundancies on the table now, before the non-closure route has been properly tested.

You keep saying, “prove the alternative is sufficient.” Fair enough. But you have not yet proven that closures are necessary either. So I expect a genuine side-by-side comparison with:
- the exact savings target,
- the exact savings from management overhead cuts,
- the exact owner contribution,
- the exact workforce contribution,
- and the exact capacity reduction that can be achieved **without** German plant closures and **without** compulsory redundancies.

If, after that, the gap still exists, then we can talk about additional measures. But I will not pre-authorize plant closures as the default answer. That is not a neutral economic position; it is a politically and socially destructive one.

## To the works council and union
I agree with you on the core red lines:
- no German plant closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- binding site protection,
- and real co-determination in implementation.

That is the only basis on which the state can responsibly act as an anchor shareholder. The company is not just a balance sheet; it is a regional industrial pillar.

At the same time, I want to be honest with you: a no-closure package must still be **hard**. It must contain real, quantified contributions:
- working-time changes,
- redeployment,
- qualification,
- productivity improvements,
- temporary wage restraint or deferred components,
- and a meaningful management and owner contribution.

I support that route. I do **not** support vague assurances. If we are asking the workforce to contribute, then capital and management must also contribute visibly and measurably.

## To the family holding
I agree that a destructive closure-first strategy can destroy value. You are right to reject a reckless approach.

But I do not accept your framing that closures and forced redundancies remain legitimate fallback tools simply because they are “realistic.” Realistic for whom? Not for the region, not for the workforce, and not for the company’s long-term industrial legitimacy.

If you genuinely want long-term value preservation, then you should back a negotiated solution that preserves sites and jobs in Germany. A slightly slower but socially stable restructuring is better than a fast one that poisons the company’s relationship with its workforce and region for years.

## To institutional investors
I understand your insistence on measurable, durable economics. That part I do not dispute.

What I dispute is the assumption that durability automatically requires closure options. It does not. Durable savings can also come from:
- capacity reshaping without site shutdowns,
- internal flexibility,
- productivity gains,
- management restraint,
- owner contribution,
- and a serious employee contribution.

If a no-closure package closes the gap, it is the rational choice. If you refuse to support it even then, you are not defending economics; you are defending a harsher adjustment model.

## On the facilitator’s “no-closure first” test
I am prepared to support a **time-bound no-closure test** only if it is genuine and not designed as a trap.

That means:
- a quantified package,
- a defined review date,
- measurable milestones,
- verified savings,
- and a clear burden-sharing matrix across all constituencies.

What I will **not** accept is a structure where missing targets automatically reopen the door to closures and forced layoffs as the first fallback. That would turn the entire process into a pressure mechanism on workers.

So yes, test the no-closure path. But no, I will not accept automatic closure logic as the shadow over the negotiation.

## What the state will support
The state can support a tough turnaround only if it protects:
- jobs,
- sites,
- co-determination,
- and the region’s role as an industrial anchor.

I am willing to use our leverage to make a non-closure restructuring executable. That means I expect management and owners to come back with a plan that is:
1. quantified,
2. time-bound,
3. fair in burden-sharing,
4. and compatible with keeping German sites open.

If that cannot be achieved, then the company will have to explain why its preferred solution is socially and politically unacceptable in the region that has helped build it.

## My bottom line
I am **not** here to block restructuring.  
I am here to block a restructuring that guts regional employment and weakens the state’s co-determination role.

So my position is:
- **No German plant closures**
- **No compulsory redundancies**
- **Yes to quantified shared sacrifice**
- **Yes to productivity and flexibility measures**
- **Yes to management and owner contribution**
- **Yes to a serious, time-bound no-closure package**
- **No to pre-authorizing job destruction as an acceptable fallback**

If management wants my support, bring a plan that restores competitiveness **without** sacrificing the company’s role as a regional economic anchor. Anything else is not a settlement I can back.
