We agree on the diagnosis: the business is structurally under pressure, the cost base is too high, and a cosmetic compromise would be value-destructive. We also agree that a prolonged strike or a chaotic confrontation would be bad for margins and bad for the share price.

But we do **not** agree that the way to solve this is to pre-emptively rule out hard instruments.

## To management
We support your insistence on **durable, quantified savings** and on a side-by-side comparison of options. That is the right standard. What we need from you is not rhetoric about seriousness, but a number-backed package showing:

- the exact savings gap,
- what management overhead cuts contribute,
- what ownership contributes,
- what the workforce contribution delivers,
- what non-closure measures really achieve,
- and where, if anywhere, the gap remains.

Where we disagree is on tone and sequencing. You are right that closures and compulsory redundancies cannot be ruled out in advance if they are the only credible way to restore competitiveness. But if you use them too early, or as a default threat, you increase execution risk and the probability of a destructive conflict. That would also destroy value.

So: keep hard instruments available, yes. But treat them as **fallbacks after a real test**, not as the opening move.

## To the works council and union
We understand your red lines, but from a capital-markets perspective they are too absolute.

A no-closure, no-forced-layoff package is acceptable **only if it is economically equivalent**: same scale of savings, same durability, same timing, same credibility. So far, we have not seen that proven. Assertions about social legitimacy are not enough to offset structural underperformance.

What we **do** support:
- capacity reduction,
- working-time changes,
- redeployment,
- qualification,
- productivity improvements,
- wage restraint or deferred components,
- and some form of employee contribution.

What we **do not** support:
- a package that preserves every site by principle while leaving the cost base uncompetitive,
- or a settlement that shifts pain to the balance sheet and postpones the correction.

If you want investors to back a no-closure path, show that it closes the gap in hard euro terms, not just in political terms.

## To the regional state
We agree that legitimacy matters and that a reckless closure-first strategy could do lasting regional damage. We also agree that a weak compromise would simply defer the pain.

Your role should be to push for a **credible, enforceable, quantified** package, not to hard-code political preferences that prevent adjustment. We need you to support a process that proves whether the alternative package works. If it does, fine. If it doesn’t, you should not be used as a shield for structural underperformance.

## To the family holding
We broadly agree with your point that value destruction from a maximalist shutdown strategy would be a mistake. We also agree that the package must be fair and durable.

Where we push back is on any implication that “no closures” should be the presumed outcome unless disproven. The burden of proof cuts both ways. If a non-closure route is truly equivalent, support it. But don’t treat preservation of every site as a default investment case if the economics don’t clear.

## Our position
We will support a restructuring if it meets three tests:

1. **It materially restores margins and return on sales**
2. **It is durable through 2030**
3. **It is credible in execution, including low strike and litigation risk**

That means we are open to a non-closure package, but only if it is quantified and proven to be sufficient. If it is not sufficient, then hard structural measures must remain available.

## What we want next
We want management to present:
- a real savings bridge,
- a side-by-side comparison of closure and non-closure scenarios,
- a clear timeline,
- and a decision point where the company says plainly whether the no-closure route closes the gap or not.

That is the standard we will judge by.

## Bottom line
We are not here to defend harshness. We are here to defend **risk-adjusted value**.

So:
- yes to decisive cost reduction,
- yes to quantified burden-sharing,
- yes to testing alternatives seriously,
- no to cosmetic settlement,
- no to preserving structural overcapacity by default,
- and no to precluding the tools that may ultimately be required to restore competitive margins.
