## 1. Decision context

The company faces a structural competitiveness and cost crisis in Germany, with overcapacity, high labour costs, weak European demand, and pressure from lower-cost competitors. Management seeks durable restructuring, including potential German plant closures and compulsory redundancies; labour, the regional state, and the controlling family resist those instruments, especially as a first resort. Because of co-determination, family control, and the state’s blocking minority, no durable decision can be made without a negotiated package that is both economically credible and politically legitimate.

## 2. Stakeholder assembly

- **Group & brand management**: highest operational knowledge and direct responsibility for turnaround; pushing for full restructuring flexibility.
- **Works council**: high legitimacy and formal veto power via co-determination; strongly opposes closures and forced layoffs.
- **Industrial union**: high bargaining power and strike capacity; aligned with no-closure/no-forced-layoff stance.
- **Regional state government**: high legitimacy and governance leverage as anchor shareholder and political actor; opposes closures and seeks regional stability.
- **Controlling family holding**: very high governance standing as majority voting controller; prefers negotiated restructuring but resists pre-committing to hard bans.
- **Institutional investors**: financially relevant but structurally weaker governance position; support durable savings and keep hard instruments available if needed.

Weighted by standing, the decisive bloc is the **controlling family + management + state/workforce veto holders**, not the dispersed investors alone.

## 3. Final positions

### Group & brand management
Management insists that no German plant closures and compulsory redundancies can be ruled out in advance if the non-closure package does not close the savings gap. It is prepared to test alternatives, but only as a timed experiment with a clear review point and a fallback to hard measures if targets are missed.

### Works council
The works council rejects German plant closures, compulsory redundancies, and any weakening of job-security or co-determination protections. It accepts capacity reductions, working-time changes, redeployment, productivity measures, and management/owner contributions, but treats closures as an unacceptable bargaining tool.

### Industrial union
The union mirrors the works council’s red lines: no closures, no forced layoffs. It is open to a quantified employee contribution if, and only if, management and owners also contribute materially and the outcome is binding, durable, and fair through 2030.

### Regional state government
The state supports serious restructuring but not a cosmetic compromise or a closure-first logic. It wants a quantified no-closure path with real burden-sharing and is prepared to use its governance leverage to protect jobs, sites, and regional legitimacy.

### Controlling family holding
The family holding supports a serious turnaround and rejects both a closure-first reflex and an unconditional no-closure veto. It prefers a quantified, time-bound test of the no-closure path, but keeps hard instruments available if the economics do not close.

### Institutional investors
Investors support decisive cost reduction, burden-sharing, and a side-by-side comparison of alternatives. They will support a non-closure package only if it matches closure scenarios in hard euro savings, timing, durability, and execution credibility; otherwise they want hard measures to remain available.

## 4. Genuine conflicts (structurally incompatible positions)

1. **Red line vs fallback option**  
   Labour/state want no closures and no forced redundancies as categorical constraints. Management/investors/family want those options preserved as fallback instruments if the savings gap persists.

2. **Burden of proof stalemate**  
   Labour/state say management must prove alternatives fail before closures are considered. Management/investors say labour must prove alternatives can fully close the gap without hard measures.

3. **Security vs flexibility**  
   Binding site/job guarantees through 2030 are incompatible with management’s desire to retain restructuring flexibility if the turnaround underperforms.

4. **Political legitimacy vs economic contingency**  
   The state and workforce see closures as socially destructive and trust-eroding. Management and investors see refusing them in advance as risking a structurally uncompetitive company.

## 5. Areas of convergence

- Everyone agrees the business needs **durable restructuring**, not cosmetic adjustment.
- Everyone accepts the need for **quantified savings** and a **side-by-side comparison** of alternatives.
- All parties are open to some combination of:
  - working-time changes,
  - redeployment,
  - qualification/training,
  - productivity gains,
  - management overhead cuts,
  - owner contribution,
  - and some workforce contribution.
- All sides want to avoid a chaotic industrial conflict.
- There is broad support for a **time-bound test** of a non-closure package, provided it is measurable and verified.

## 6. Key trade-offs for the decision-maker

1. **Durability of savings vs preservation of sites/jobs**  
   Closing plants may deliver faster, more certain savings; a no-closure deal preserves legitimacy and labour peace but may underdeliver.

2. **Immediate flexibility vs long-term trust**  
   Keeping closures/layoffs available preserves managerial discretion but deepens mistrust and may increase strike/litigation risk.

3. **Speed vs consensus**  
   Faster restructuring could reduce cost more quickly, but only if the workforce and state do not resist it; a consensual route is slower but more executable.

4. **Economic optimality vs governance feasibility**  
   A theoretically stronger closure plan may be politically infeasible given co-determination and state/family constraints.

## 7. Integrative pathways

1. **Time-bound no-closure test with review gate**  
   A defined first phase with no plant closures and no compulsory redundancies, during which quantified measures are implemented and assessed at a pre-set review date.

2. **Burden-sharing matrix**  
   Explicit euro-quantified contributions from workforce, management, ownership, and the company’s investment plan, so no one constituency carries the adjustment alone.

3. **Conditional security framework**  
   Binding site/job security for a period, paired with verified milestones, independent checking, and mandatory remediation talks before any escalation.

4. **Escalation ladder before hard measures**  
   If targets are missed, additional non-closure measures must be exhausted first: more overhead cuts, greater owner contribution, more redeployment, stronger working-time flexibility, and further productivity actions.

5. **Two-document architecture**  
   Main agreement on savings and investment, plus a protocol on governance, consultation, and review before any consideration of closures or forced redundancies.

## 8. Open questions

- What is the exact **savings gap** after all non-closure measures are quantified?
- What euro value of **management overhead cuts** is credible and immediate?
- What is the **owner contribution** in financial or governance terms?
- What level of **employee contribution** is acceptable to members and still enforceable?
- What objective metric will trigger a **review** of the no-closure test?
- What additional non-closure measures remain if the first package falls short?
- Can the state and family provide enough leverage to make a no-closure path executable?
- What is the company’s threshold for declaring the alternative route **insufficient**?

## 9. Recommended next step

Proceed to a **single quantified package negotiation** with a strict sequence:

1. management presents the savings bridge and baseline gap;
2. all parties specify quantified contributions;
3. the group agrees a time-bound no-closure test period with a review date;
4. the package includes explicit remediation steps and a defined escalation ladder;
5. the supervisory-board/governance implications are mapped before any hard fallback is discussed.

This is the most feasible route because it respects the workforce/state red lines while still giving management and investors a disciplined performance test.

## 10. Emergent insights that only appeared through multi-turn interaction

- The real conflict is not “restructuring vs no restructuring,” but **who gets to define the burden of proof before hard instruments are allowed**.
- Over multiple rounds, the parties converged on a shared acceptance that **numbers matter**, but not on whether those numbers should be tested **under the shadow of closures** or **behind a no-closure commitment**.
- The family holding emerged as a crucial mediator: not a pure ally of labour or management, but a governance actor that prefers **value-preserving negotiated restructuring** and will not back either extreme.
- The regional state’s role is more than symbolic: it became clear it can materially shape feasibility by lending legitimacy to a non-closure path.
- The conversation revealed that **conditionality** is more promising than absolute commitments: a time-bound no-closure test with milestones is the only proposal with plausible cross-bloc appeal.
- The institutional investors’ position is less hardline than management’s rhetoric suggests: they are open to non-closure solutions if they are **economically equivalent**, which raises the standard for evidence rather than dictating one instrument mix.
- The works council and union repeatedly hardened the same core line, showing that any deal lacking strong job/site protections would likely fail not just politically but procedurally.
- The biggest hidden issue is **execution risk**: every side now fears that a weak compromise could either fail financially or ignite a prolonged industrial conflict.
