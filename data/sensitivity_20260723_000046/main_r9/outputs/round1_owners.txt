I appreciate the clarity from all sides. Let me be equally direct.

I agree on the diagnosis: this company has a real competitiveness problem, and it will not be solved by slogans or by pretending the cost base is fine. The brand must return to sustainable margins if we want to protect investment, innovation, and the long-term value of the business.

But I also need to say plainly where I disagree.

## What I support
I support a restructuring that is:
- economically credible,
- durable,
- negotiated rather than chaotic,
- and capable of restoring competitiveness over more than one bargaining cycle.

I also support the principle that labour should contribute if the company is asking for sacrifices. If the workforce is asked to adjust, then the package must be broader than just demanding more from employees while everyone else stands still.

## What I do not support
I do **not** support a plan that:
- permanently impairs the enterprise’s value,
- destroys trust without a workable alternative,
- or weakens governance control through a destabilising confrontation.

In that sense, I am not indifferent between “hard restructuring” and “no change.” Value erosion from continued underperformance is real. But so is value erosion from a breakdown in labour peace, reputational damage, and an approach that creates long-term industrial instability.

## On plant closures
I understand management’s argument that the current footprint may be too large for demand and cost realities. I do not dismiss that lightly.

But I am not persuaded that plant closures should be treated as the first or default instrument. Closure is the most destructive option: it is irreversible, politically explosive, and risks creating a conflict that drags on far longer than any savings are worth. If there is a credible path to equivalent durable savings through capacity reduction, working-time flexibility, redeployment, and negotiated labour-cost measures, that is materially preferable.

So my position is:
- **I am open to structural cost and capacity reduction.**
- **I am not automatically endorsing plant closures.**
- If closures are proposed, they must be clearly justified as unavoidable and part of a package that preserves long-term value, not merely short-term accounting relief.

## On compulsory redundancies
Here I am more cautious than management. Compulsory redundancies are not something I would want to see used casually. They raise the risk of escalation, legal friction, and loss of the social compact that has historically supported this company.

That said, I am also not prepared to make a blank cheque commitment that could prevent any workforce adjustment whatsoever. The decisive question is whether a negotiated alternative can actually deliver the needed savings.

## On the employee side’s red lines
I hear the insistence on no closures and no compulsory redundancies. I understand why the works council and union are taking that line. But from an ownership and governance perspective, a blanket refusal to consider any site change can become a blocker to necessary restructuring.

If the employee side wants binding site security, it needs to show how the required cost reduction will still be achieved in a way that is durable and credible. Otherwise, the burden simply shifts to the company’s future balance sheet and ultimately to all stakeholders, including the workforce.

## What would move me
I would support a settlement if it contains all of the following:
1. **A credible, quantified path to sustainable margin improvement**
2. **Durable cost and capacity reductions**
3. **A serious employee contribution**
4. **Protection of the company’s long-term industrial capability**
5. **A negotiated solution that avoids destabilising conflict**

I am not setting a preference for one instrument over another here. I am setting the standard: the result must work economically and must not destroy the company’s ability to govern itself and invest for the future.

## My message to management
Management should not assume that the owners will simply rubber-stamp a maximalist shutdown strategy. A plan that is too harsh, too destructive, or too politically unworkable can damage value as much as it restores it. If you want support, bring a solution that is robust, not merely severe.

## My message to labour and the state
I respect the commitment to the German industrial base. But preserving every site in its current form cannot be the only criterion. We need a package that keeps the enterprise competitive enough to justify that industrial base in the first place.

## Bottom line
I support a tough restructuring if it is truly durable and value-restoring. I do not support a path that sacrifices long-term enterprise value, undermines governance stability, or turns restructuring into a prolonged conflict. If there is a negotiated alternative that achieves the necessary savings without irreversible damage, that is the direction I would favor. If not, then the company must be honest about the hard choices still on the table.
