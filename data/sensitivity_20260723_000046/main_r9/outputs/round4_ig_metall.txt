I will be equally clear.

## Where I agree
I agree with the works council, the state, and the family holding on one central point: **this cannot be solved by a cosmetic deal**. The company needs a **durable restructuring** with real numbers behind it.

I also agree that a **shared-sacrifice package** is the only legitimate way forward:
- workforce contribution,
- management restraint,
- owner contribution,
- productivity gains,
- working-time flexibility,
- redeployment,
- qualification,
- and investment commitments.

That is the union’s constructive line, and it remains available.

## Where I disagree
### 1) No closures is not a slogan; it is a boundary
I do **not** accept German plant closures as a negotiating instrument here.  
A plant closure is not “just another tool.” It destroys jobs, capability, supplier networks, and trust. If management wants to claim it is necessary, it must prove — not merely assert — that every serious alternative fails.

So far, that proof has not been provided.

### 2) No compulsory redundancies remains non-negotiable
I also do **not** accept forced layoffs.  
Workers must not be made to carry the crisis by losing their livelihoods while management keeps the option of shifting the burden downward.

If the company needs adjustment, then adjustment must come first through:
- capacity reduction without site shutdowns,
- working-time changes,
- internal redeployment,
- qualification,
- productivity measures,
- and fair contributions from management and ownership.

### 3) Burden-sharing must be real, not symbolic
I am prepared to accept employee contributions, including:
- temporary wage restraint,
- deferred components,
- flexibility,
- and perhaps working-time concessions,

**but only if** management and owners also contribute in a material way.  
If workers give and management only asks, that is not restructuring. That is a transfer of pain onto labour.

---

## To management
You keep saying the cost base is too high and overcapacity must be reduced. I do not dispute the diagnosis.

What I do dispute is your leap to:  
**therefore closures and forced redundancies must remain available in advance**.

That is not a conclusion; it is a choice. And it is a choice I reject unless you can show, transparently and in detail, that:
1. the exact savings gap is quantified,
2. the non-closure package was genuinely tested,
3. management overhead cuts are substantial,
4. owners are contributing materially,
5. and the gap still remains after those measures.

Bring the numbers. Not pressure. Not slogans. Not “fiduciary realism” as a substitute for proof.

---

## To the works council
We are aligned on the essentials:
- no closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- binding site and job security,
- strong co-determination,
- and shared burden.

I support that line.  
But I also want us to remain serious and quantifiable. The employee side should not be asked to accept vague “flexibility” while the other side keeps hard instruments in reserve.

So I support a **hard no-closure, no-forced-layoff package** only if it is:
- numerically credible,
- enforced,
- and backed by real management and owner contributions.

---

## To the state
You are right that legitimacy matters. A solution that destroys industrial trust will damage the region for years.

But legitimacy is not preserved by merely opposing closures rhetorically. Use your leverage to force management and owners into a genuine alternative route with quantified commitments and binding implementation.

That means:
- no closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- binding site security,
- and real burden-sharing from capital and management.

---

## To the family holding
You say you prefer negotiated solutions and want to protect long-term value. I agree with that objective.

But value is not protected by keeping closures and forced layoffs as the default fallback. That only deepens mistrust and raises the risk of a prolonged conflict. If you want stability, back a settlement that protects jobs and sites and shares the burden fairly.

---

## To institutional investors
You say you are not defending harshness, only risk-adjusted value. Fine.

Then here is the test:
- If a **no-closure, no-forced-layoff** package closes the gap durably, you should support it.
- If you refuse it even then, your argument is not about economics; it is about preserving a harsher adjustment logic.

I want that made explicit.

---

## What would move the union
I remain open to a settlement if it contains all of the following:
1. a quantified savings target,
2. a real employee contribution,
3. significant management cost cuts,
4. a meaningful owner contribution,
5. capacity reduction **without** site closures,
6. no compulsory redundancies,
7. binding site and job security through 2030,
8. and commitments for future products and investment.

That is the package we can recommend to members.  
Anything else is not durable and not fair.

---

## What I reject
I reject:
- using closures as leverage from the outset,
- using forced redundancies as the fallback threat,
- making workers carry the adjustment alone,
- and treating “durability” as code for “the workforce must absorb the pain.”

---

## Bottom line
We are prepared to contribute.  
We are **not** prepared to surrender jobs, sites, and wage standards.

If management and owners want a real settlement, they must prove that a **no-closure, no-forced-layoff** path can close the gap fairly. If they cannot, then the conflict will continue — and it will be because they chose to keep job destruction on the table.
