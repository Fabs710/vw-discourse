We are aligned on the diagnosis, but not on the evidentiary standard.

## Where we agree
- The company has a **real structural competitiveness problem**.
- A **cosmetic settlement** would destroy value.
- A **prolonged strike or chaotic confrontation** would also destroy value.
- Any package must be **quantified, durable, and implementation-credible**.
- Workforce flexibility, redeployment, working-time changes, training, productivity measures, and some employee contribution all belong in the solution.

## Where we disagree
### 1) “No closures” cannot be a prior
We do **not** accept an absolute no-closures rule.  
If the German footprint is structurally oversized, then capacity has to come out somewhere. If a no-closure package can truly deliver equivalent durable savings, we would support it — but that needs to be demonstrated with hard numbers, not asserted as a principle.

A package that preserves every site by default while leaving the cost base uncompetitive is not acceptable to capital markets.

### 2) “No compulsory redundancies” cannot be absolute
We prefer negotiated adjustment. Forced layoffs are value-destructive because they raise conflict risk and execution risk. But an unconditional veto on workforce reduction would leave management without a credible fallback if the alternative package falls short.

We are **not asking for forced layoffs**. We are saying they cannot be ruled out in advance if they are part of the only credible route back to competitive margins.

### 3) The test is not fairness; it is durable economics
I understand the concern about burden-sharing. But the market will not reward a deal because it feels balanced. It will reward a deal if it:
- lowers fixed costs materially,
- reduces overcapacity,
- restores return-on-sales,
- and is believable through 2030.

If the package merely softens the pain without repairing the economics, we will not support it.

## What would move us
We would support a settlement if it includes:
1. **A quantified savings target**
2. **A side-by-side comparison of closure vs. non-closure scenarios**
3. **Clear timing and milestones**
4. **A credible path back to target margins**
5. **Low execution risk and limited strike exposure**

## Our line
We will not back:
- a cosmetic compromise,
- a deal that preserves overcapacity,
- or a structure that leaves margins structurally impaired.

## To management
Bring us the numbers in full. Not just the preferred scenario, but the alternatives, the timing, the durability, and the point at which you would declare the non-closure route insufficient.

## To labour and the state
If you want a no-closure outcome, prove it can close the gap. If it can, we are open. If it cannot, then insisting on site protection as an absolute only postpones the correction and risks a larger value loss later.

## Bottom line
We are not here to defend harshness. We are here to defend **risk-adjusted value**.  
That means we support the solution that **credibly restores competitiveness**, even if it is uncomfortable.
