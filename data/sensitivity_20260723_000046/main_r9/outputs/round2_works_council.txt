We are now at the point where words must become facts.

I will respond clearly to the positions on the table:

## Where I agree
Yes, the company has a real competitiveness problem.  
Yes, the German cost base must be addressed.  
Yes, we need a solution that is durable, not a cosmetic patch.

I also agree that the package must be quantified. Fine — then quantify it properly. Show us the exact gap, the exact savings requirement, and the exact contribution expected from each side.

## Where I disagree
I do **not** accept the conclusion that plant closures or compulsory redundancies are necessary.

Management and the capital side keep repeating “durable savings” as if that automatically means shutting sites and throwing people out. It does not. That is a choice — and one we reject.

A company of this scale has other instruments:
- capacity reductions without closures
- working-time adjustments
- temporary or deferred wage components
- redeployment across the group
- training and qualification
- productivity improvements
- investment shifts to secure future output

Those are the instruments that must be put on the table first and tested seriously.

## On management’s position
You say you cannot preserve every guarantee while remaining competitive. But what you are actually asking for is a one-sided burden shift: jobs and sites should pay for management’s strategic and industrial mistakes.

That is not acceptable.

If you want workforce cooperation, then bring a plan that:
1. keeps all German plants open,
2. rules out compulsory redundancies,
3. preserves co-determination in shaping the restructuring,
4. and includes binding job and site security through 2030.

Without those conditions, there is no basis for trust.

## On the institutional investors
You say you are “agnostic on the instrument.” Then stop treating closures as the default answer. If an alternative package can produce the needed savings, you should support it.

But if your definition of “credible” only means “harsh enough for the market,” then you are not measuring sustainability — you are measuring pain. That is not our standard.

## On the family holding
You are right that a prolonged conflict destroys value. Exactly. That is why a closure strategy is so dangerous. It would trigger escalation, undermine labour peace, and damage the company’s legitimacy for years.

If you want long-term value protection, then back a settlement that preserves the industrial base rather than tearing it apart.

## On the state
I appreciate your clear line against closures and forced redundancies. That must remain non-negotiable.

But I want to be equally clear: site protection cannot become a vague political promise. It must be written into a binding agreement with:
- protection of every German plant,
- no operational layoffs,
- and a real role for the works councils in implementation and monitoring.

## Our position for round 2
We are prepared to discuss a **serious quantified contribution** from the workforce — but only if the company commits in return to:

- **no German plant closures**
- **no compulsory redundancies**
- **binding site guarantees through 2030**
- **preservation of co-determination**
- **investment commitments for future products and technologies**

That is the basis for further discussion. Anything else is not a restructuring we can support; it is a dismantling.

## What I want to see next
If management claims that no-closure and no-forced-layoff alternatives are insufficient, then put the numbers on the table:
- what savings are required,
- what alternative measures have been tested,
- what contribution is expected from management and ownership,
- and why the current social partnership model cannot deliver it.

Until then, I reject any attempt to present closures or compulsory redundancies as inevitable. They are not inevitable. They are a decision — and we will oppose them.
