I’ll be direct.

We are not going to solve a structural competitiveness crisis by declaring the hard instruments off-limits in advance and then hoping the cost gap disappears on its own. I respect the concern for jobs and sites. I do **not** accept the claim that German plant closures and compulsory redundancies can be ruled out categorically before we have proven whether the alternative package actually closes the gap.

## Where I agree
I agree on several points:
- This cannot be a cosmetic deal.
- The package must be quantified and durable.
- Shared sacrifice is necessary.
- Management, ownership, and labour all have to contribute.
- A long strike or chaotic confrontation would damage everyone.

I also agree that capacity can be adjusted through more than one instrument:
- working-time changes
- redeployment
- qualification
- productivity gains
- overhead cuts
- temporary or deferred employee contributions

So no, this is not only about closures. But it **is** about whether the company has the freedom to use the instruments that actually restore competitiveness if the softer measures do not close the gap.

## Where I disagree with the works council and union
Your position is clear: no closures, no compulsory redundancies, binding protection through 2030. I understand the political logic, but I reject it as a starting rule.

Why? Because it does not answer the actual economic question:
- How much fixed cost comes out?
- How much overcapacity is removed?
- How quickly?
- How durable is it to 2030?

If the answer is “we will try flexibility and hope it is enough,” that is not a restructuring. That is postponement.

You say management has not proven that alternatives fail. Fair enough. But you have also not proven that alternatives succeed at the scale required. So let’s be honest: this is a mutual evidentiary gap. The difference is that management has to deliver a viable company, not just a protected status quo.

## Where I disagree with the state
I respect the state’s concern for regional stability and industrial legitimacy. But legitimacy is not preserved by insulating every site from adjustment regardless of economics.

A plan that keeps all sites open while leaving the cost base structurally uncompetitive may feel politically safer today, but it risks a weaker company, lower investment, and less industrial strength tomorrow. That is not a win for the region.

If the state wants to prevent unnecessary closures, then support a package that actually closes the cost gap. Do not demand that management accept a structurally inferior setup just because it is politically easier.

## Where I agree and disagree with the family holding
You are right about one thing: a destructive closure-first approach can destroy value. I do not want random or politically blind cuts either.

But I do not share the view that a no-closure path should be the default unless proven impossible. It has to be proven sufficient. That is a different standard.

I can support a negotiated no-closure route **if** it is genuinely equivalent in savings, timing, and durability. What I cannot support is turning preference into veto.

## Where I agree and disagree with investors
You are right to insist on measurable, risk-adjusted value. That is exactly why I am not prepared to accept a deal that preserves overcapacity and leaves the return-on-sales problem unresolved.

But the same logic cuts both ways: if the no-closure package is materially insufficient, then investors cannot ask management to pretend otherwise just to avoid conflict.

So yes, I support side-by-side numbers. But those numbers must be honest about:
- the savings gap
- the implementation timeline
- the durability through 2030
- and the execution risk if the company is forced into a weak compromise

## My response to the facilitator’s framing
The proposed “no-closure first” test is acceptable only as a **test**, not as a pre-commitment to no closures forever.

I am willing to sequence the process:
1. quantify the gap
2. test the full non-closure package
3. verify management, owner, and workforce contributions
4. assess whether the package closes the structural cost gap

If it does, then fine — we can discuss protections around that outcome.

If it does **not**, then the company cannot be trapped by a rule that forbids the very instruments needed to restore competitiveness. I will not sign up to a framework that makes management responsible for the turnaround but removes the tools required to execute it.

## On the claim that closures are “a choice”
Yes, they are a choice. So are wage restraint, flexibility, productivity concessions, and ownership contributions. The question is not whether something is a choice. The question is whether it is sufficient.

If the non-closure package is sufficient, I will support it. If it is not, then refusing closures in advance is not principled; it is a refusal to confront the economics.

## What I need to see next
Bring me a real, quantified package with:
- the exact savings target
- the exact employee contribution
- the exact management overhead cuts
- the exact owner contribution
- the exact capacity reduction
- the timing and durability of each measure
- and the point at which the alternative route is judged insufficient

Without that, “no closures” is just a slogan.

## Bottom line
I remain committed to:
- restoring competitiveness
- reducing overcapacity
- lowering labour costs structurally
- protecting future investment capacity
- getting the brand back to target by 2030

I am open on the instrument mix.

But I will **not** accept:
- an unconditional ban on closures
- an unconditional ban on compulsory redundancies
- or a settlement that leaves the company structurally uncompetitive

If the employee side wants to avoid hard measures, then bring a package that actually closes the gap. If it does not, then the hard options remain on the table. That is not a threat; it is reality.
