Let me respond directly, because we are now past generalities.

## First: what I agree with
I agree on three points that have been repeated across the table:

1. **This company has a structural competitiveness problem.**
2. **A purely cosmetic deal would be a failure.**
3. **We need a negotiated settlement, not uncontrolled conflict.**

I also agree that capacity adjustment, redeployment, qualification, working-time flexibility, and some form of workforce contribution all belong in the package.

So we are not debating whether there is a problem. We are debating whether we have the courage to solve it at the scale required.

## Where I disagree — and strongly
### 1) “No plant closures” as an absolute is not credible
The works council and union keep repeating that closures are off the table. I understand why you say that. I also understand the political pressure from the state. But I need to be blunt: **a blanket refusal to consider closures is not a solution to overcapacity**.

If the footprint is too large for demand, costs, and utilization, then we have to resize it. That may involve closures, consolidation, or equivalent structural measures. If you rule out the hard instruments in advance, you are not protecting the company — you are protecting inefficiency.

### 2) “No compulsory redundancies” cannot be an unconditional veto
I do not want forced layoffs as a first resort. But I will not accept a position where the workforce can insist on full guarantees while refusing the scale of adjustment needed to restore margins.

If no credible alternative delivers the required savings, then management must retain the option of compulsory measures. I am not saying that is the preferred path. I am saying it cannot be ruled out absolutely if we are serious about competitiveness.

### 3) A “shared-sacrifice” package must actually close the gap
I hear a lot of language about fairness, trust, and social partnership. Those matter. But they do not restore return on sales.

What matters is this:
- How much cost comes out?
- How much capacity comes out?
- When does it land?
- Is it durable through 2030?
- Does it free up investment for future products and technology?

If the answer is vague, then we do not have a restructuring. We have a postponement.

## To the Works Council
You say management is asking the workforce to pay for its own mistakes. That is not a serious answer to the situation we are in.

We are facing a shrinking market, intense low-cost competition, and a German cost base far above target. Those are not abstract management talking points; they are hard business realities.

If you want binding site guarantees through 2030, then show how the required savings are achieved without closures and without overloading the balance sheet. Right now, I do not see that.

## To the Trade Union
You say you will not accept forced layoffs or a one-sided burden shift. I hear that. But then I need a credible alternative that produces equivalent structural savings.

Saying “we reject closures” is not enough. Saying “we reject compulsory redundancies” is not enough. The company still needs a cost base that can compete.

If you have a package that delivers:
- quantified labor-cost reduction,
- capacity adjustment,
- higher utilization,
- and durable savings,
then bring it. If not, then you are asking management to sign up to a competitive disadvantage and call it partnership.

## To the State Government
You say closures are politically unacceptable. I recognize the regional impact, but your job is not only to defend the status quo. It is also to help secure the industrial future of this company.

A plan that preserves every site but leaves the company structurally uncompetitive would not protect the region in the long run. It would simply delay worse outcomes.

So yes: support alternatives if they are real. But do not pretend that an outcome without closures is automatically sustainable just because it is easier to defend publicly.

## To the Family Owner
I appreciate that you are not endorsing a maximalist shutdown strategy. That said, we cannot let governance drift into indecision.

You are right that a destructive confrontation would destroy value. But so would a settlement that leaves the cost base unrepaired. I need the owners to back a solution that is hard enough to work, not merely acceptable in public.

## To Institutional Investors
You are right to insist on measurable, durable savings. I agree with that completely.

Where I push back is on the idea that the instrument matters less than the outcome. In practice, the instrument matters a great deal because some instruments are the only ones that actually deliver structural change.

If the alternative package does not achieve the same cost and capacity effect as closures or equivalent deep restructuring, then it is not comparable.

## My position, clearly stated
Management’s objective remains unchanged:

- **durable reduction in cost and capacity**
- **restoration of the brand’s return on sales**
- **freed-up funds for investment to 2030**
- **a structurally sustainable industrial footprint**

I am open on the method. That is true. But I am not open to a settlement that:
- preserves every site by default,
- eliminates the possibility of compulsory adjustment,
- and leaves the company with an uncompetitive cost base.

That would be a cosmetic deal, and I will not sign off on that.

## What I would need to see next
If the employee side wants to prove that closures and forced layoffs are unnecessary, then bring a package with numbers:

1. the exact savings target,
2. the exact labor-cost contribution,
3. the exact capacity reduction,
4. the exact productivity measures,
5. the timeline to 2030,
6. and the proof that it is durable.

If that package closes the gap credibly, I will consider it seriously. If it does not, then the hard options remain on the table.

## Bottom line
I am not here to preserve comfort. I am here to restore competitiveness.

If we want a future for this company, we need a restructuring that is real, structural, and durable. Anything less is just delay dressed up as consensus.
