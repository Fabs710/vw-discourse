I appreciate that all sides are at least acknowledging the same basic reality: this company needs a durable solution, not a temporary patch. I also agree that a purely cosmetic response would be irresponsible.

But I need to be equally clear: **a restructuring that relies on plant closures in Germany or compulsory redundancies is not acceptable from the state’s point of view.** That is not just a political preference; it would damage the regional economy, industrial capacity, and the company’s legitimacy as a long-term anchor employer. It would also create the kind of social and political backlash that makes any turnaround harder, not easier.

### Where I agree
I agree with management that the company faces serious structural pressure:
- the market is tougher,
- cost levels are too high,
- and the future investment burden is real.

I also agree with the union and works council that this cannot be solved by loading the entire adjustment onto workers. A one-sided approach would break trust and destabilize the company for years.

### Where I disagree
What I cannot accept is the idea that **closures are simply the default answer** if the cost gap is large enough. That logic is too narrow. A German industrial company of this importance has other levers:
- capacity reductions without site shutdowns,
- working-time adjustments,
- redeployment and qualification,
- investment shifts to secure future production,
- negotiated labour-cost contributions,
- and productivity measures that do not destroy the industrial base.

If management says there is no credible alternative, then I would ask: have all shared-sacrifice options really been exhausted, or are closures being used because they are the fastest unilateral solution?

### What would move me
I could support a restructuring if it met three conditions:

1. **No German plant closures**
   - The state will not support a plan that permanently guts regional employment.

2. **No compulsory redundancies**
   - Adjustments must be handled through natural turnover, redeployment, qualification, and negotiated instruments.

3. **A genuine social partnership package**
   - That means a fair employee contribution, yes, but also clear contributions from management and owners, plus credible investment commitments for the future.

### On management’s point
Management is right that the outcome must be economically credible. But credibility does not mean the most brutal option. It means a package that the workforce will actually implement and that the company can sustain politically, socially, and industrially.

### On the works council and union
I support the insistence on binding site protection and the rejection of compulsory redundancies. That is the only basis on which a settlement can retain legitimacy. At the same time, I would urge the employee side not to dismiss capacity adjustments, flexibility, and negotiated contributions out of hand if they help avoid closures and protect the broader footprint.

### Bottom line
The state is prepared to back a tough restructuring **only if it preserves sites and jobs in Germany** and keeps the company’s co-determination and regional anchor role intact. If the proposal remains centered on closures and dismissals, I will oppose it.

If management wants my support, bring forward a plan that achieves real cost reduction **without** destroying plants or forcing people out. Otherwise, there is no basis for agreement from my side.
