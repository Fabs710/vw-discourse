## To management
You still have not met the burden of proof for closures or compulsory redundancies.

I agree with you on the diagnosis:
- the company needs a durable restructuring,
- the cost base is too high,
- and cosmetic fixes are useless.

I also agree that a purely symbolic employee contribution would be insufficient. But what you are still doing is **treating plant closures and forced layoffs as if they were the default “serious” answer** without proving that a non-closure package cannot work.

That is not convincing.

What I need from management is not another general claim about “structural savings.” I need:
- a plant-by-plant and line-by-line cost picture,
- the savings from shift reductions and working-time changes,
- the savings from voluntary exits and redeployment,
- the savings from management restraint and owner-side contribution,

If that residual gap is real, quantify it. Then we can argue about how to close it. But do not pretend closures are justified before that analysis is complete.

## To the family holding
I understand your concern about value destruction, and I share part of it: a frozen cost structure is not sustainable.

But I reject the implication that the only way to protect value is to keep closure threats on the table from the start. That approach destroys trust and makes the workforce pay for uncertainty they did not create.

If you want value protection, then support a serious non-closure restructuring:
- capacity consolidation inside the existing footprint,
- temporary employee contribution,
- management compensation restraint,
- reduced payouts,
- owner-side support,
- and binding investment commitments.

If that package does not close the gap, then you can argue the next step. But you cannot use closures as a bargaining shortcut and call that prudence.

## To institutional investors
Your point about fully loaded savings is correct. That is exactly why we insist on a quantified non-closure package first.

You are right that wage concessions alone do not fix a structural problem. We are not proposing wage concessions alone. We are proposing a **shared-sacrifice package** with real operational changes:
- shift cuts,
- utilization management,
- voluntary exits,
- redeployment,
- retraining,
- temporary wage and bonus moderation,
- management restraint,
- and owner contribution.

What I do not accept is the idea that because a non-closure package is harder to execute, closures automatically become the better answer. Harder does not mean better. In a co-determined German industrial setting, it also means more conflict, more delay, and higher execution risk.

So yes: show us the numbers. But do not pre-judge the answer.

I do **not** accept closure triggers as a framework if they are just a delayed threat.

If you want a real deal, then the logic must be:
1. first exhaust the non-closure package,
2. then verify the savings,
3. then discuss any remaining gap.

Not the other way around.
