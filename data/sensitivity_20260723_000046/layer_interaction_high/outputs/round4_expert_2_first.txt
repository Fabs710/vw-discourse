Here is the expert assessment, limited to plant-network economics and German co-determination realities.

## Bottom line
The works council’s core challenge is analytically valid: **closures are not the only way to generate structural savings**. But the counterpoint is equally valid: in a German automotive network, a **non-closure package has to be very large, tightly executed, and numerically proven** to be economically equivalent to a true footprint reduction. In many cases, it can reduce cost materially, but **it often cannot fully substitute for closure-level savings** if there is genuine plant overcapacity.

So the right analytical question is not “closures or no closures,” but:
1. **How large is the excess fixed-cost burden?**
2. **How much can be removed by utilization, shift, and labor measures alone?**
3. **What residual gap remains that only structural footprint changes can close?**

## 1) What a non-closure package can realistically deliver
A well-designed alternative package can usually produce savings through six channels:

- **Shift reduction / line consolidation**  
  This is often the biggest lever without formal closure. Removing a shift can cut direct labor, energy, logistics, maintenance, and some overhead.

- **Working-time flexibility**  
  Shorter hours, account-based reductions, or altered shift patterns can reduce payroll burn and smooth demand mismatch.

- **Voluntary exits / early retirement / attrition**  
  This is the cleanest workforce-reduction tool in Germany, especially where age structure permits it.

- **Internal redeployment and reskilling**  
  Useful for absorbing volume declines, but it does not remove all cost; it often shifts cost rather than eliminates it unless excess labor is truly eliminated.

- **Temporary employee contributions**  
  Pay deferrals, bonus restraint, or limited wage moderation can help, but these are usually **incremental**, not transformational, unless they are very substantial and multi-year.

- **Management and owner-side contributions**  
  Important for deal legitimacy and cash support, but again usually not enough on their own to solve a deep structural gap.

### Analytical conclusion
A non-closure package can plausibly cover:
- a **cyclical downturn**,
- a **moderate overcapacity problem**, or
- a **bridge period** until product launches / EV ramp-up.

It is much harder for it to fully solve:
- **persistent low utilization**,
- **duplicated fixed-cost base across several plants**,
- or **a structurally uncompetitive German cost position** versus lower-cost European or non-European competitors.

## 2) Why closures remain economically potent
Plant closures are so contentious because they attack the **fixed-cost base** directly:

- plant overhead
- facilities maintenance
- utilities and infrastructure
- plant logistics
- indirect labor and support functions
- local complexity costs
- duplicated management layers

Even if the production volume can be reallocated, closures often unlock savings that are difficult to replicate via pure labor concessions. In automotive manufacturing, if a plant is underutilized, the cost penalty is not just wages — it is the **unit absorption of fixed overhead**. That is why management often insists on footprint action.

### Analytical conclusion
If a plant is genuinely redundant, a non-closure package must somehow eliminate:
- the same amount of fixed cost, and
- the same amount of structural capacity,
without actually removing the site.

That is possible in theory, but in practice it often requires:
- deep decontenting,
- major line consolidation,
- very significant headcount reduction,
- and durable underutilization of the remaining site.

If those conditions are not met, the package risks becoming a **cost deferral**, not a cost solution.

## 3) The key benchmark: “fully loaded savings”
The right benchmark is not gross wage savings. It is **fully loaded, recurring savings**:
- direct labor
- indirect labor
- overhead
- maintenance
- utilities
- logistics
- depreciation absorption effects
- administrative complexity
- inventory and changeover inefficiencies

This matters because a “non-closure” deal can look large in wage terms while still leaving too much plant-level fixed cost intact.

### What management should quantify
A credible management case should show, by plant and by function:
- current utilization
- break-even volume
- fixed vs variable cost split
- headcount by direct/indirect categories
- achievable headcount reduction without closure
- savings from shift cuts
- savings from model allocation changes
- residual cost gap after employee concessions

Without that, the closure argument is not yet analytically proven.

## 4) What the works council is right about
The works council is correct that management often overstates closure as the only serious option because closure is the cleanest leverage point in bargaining. In a German co-determined environment, however, management must distinguish between:
- **economic necessity**
and
- **negotiating posture**.

There is often room to reduce cost materially through:
- voluntary departures
- transfer programs
- tighter shift systems
- output concentration
- investment allocation
- temporary work-time moderation

So yes: **non-closure should be quantified before closure is treated as inevitable**.

## 5) What the works council cannot assume
What it cannot assume is that keeping all sites open is automatically compatible with competitiveness.

A site can remain open and still be economically weak if:
- utilization stays low,
- product assignment is insufficient,
- duplicate capacities persist,
- or the labor-cost reduction is too small relative to the fixed-cost burden.

In other words, “no closure” is not the same as “no footprint reduction.”  
A plant can stay legally open while becoming much smaller, less complex, and less employment-intensive.

## 6) German co-determination and what it means here
Under German co-determination, a closure or major operational restructuring is not just a managerial arithmetic problem; it is a governance and bargaining problem.

Important practical points:
- Works councils can **delay, contest, and materially shape** restructuring.
- A supervisory board with parity co-determination and a two-thirds requirement for certain decisions gives labor a **real blocking position**.
- This does not eliminate management’s need to restructure, but it means **forced solutions are expensive and slow**.
- Therefore, the most realistic path is usually a **package deal**: labor concessions, site retention, capacity consolidation, and investment commitments.

### Practical implication
From a restructuring standpoint, the most credible non-closure solution is not “nothing changes.”  
It is:
- fewer shifts,
- fewer lines,
- fewer jobs,
- more flexible work rules,
- lower complexity,
- and conditional investment.

That is often the only way to preserve sites while still moving the economics meaningfully.

## 7) My assessment of the specific dispute
### If the question is:
**Can a non-closure package be economically equivalent to closures?**

**Answer:** Sometimes partially, but not always fully.

### More precisely:
- If the overcapacity is **moderate** and the plants are still strategically needed, then yes, a non-closure package can often substitute.
- If the overcapacity is **large and persistent**, then a non-closure package is usually only an interim solution unless it includes very deep capacity cuts inside the network.

### If the question is:
**Should management be required to prove the non-closure gap first?**

**Answer:** Yes, analytically that is the correct sequence.  
A serious restructuring case should quantify:
- the savings achievable without closure,
- the savings gap versus target,
- and the operational consequences of preserving all sites.

### If the question is:
**Can the workforce demand “keep every German plant open, no forced redundancies” as a starting position?**

**Answer:** Yes, as a bargaining position.  
But economically, that position only becomes sustainable if the package:
- materially reduces capacity,
- lowers fully loaded cost,
- and preserves competitiveness on a recurring basis.

Otherwise it becomes a postponement of the same problem.

## 8) The most defensible neutral formulation
The most defensible analytical statement is:

> A non-closure package should be modeled first and can, in some cases, substitute for closures. But if the company’s German network is structurally overbuilt, then non-closure measures will usually need to be unusually deep to match the savings from actual footprint reduction, and they may still leave a residual competitiveness gap.
