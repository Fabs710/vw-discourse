## Independent restructuring assessment

I’ll answer the questions directed at my area: **what a credible non-closure package would need to contain, how to compare it with closure economics, and what information is missing**.

### Short answer
A **non-closure package can be economically credible**, but only if it delivers:

1. **material recurring cost reduction**, not just temporary relief  
2. **real capacity reduction**, not only labor concessions  
3. **lower fixed-cost absorption risk** through shift and line consolidation  
4. **measurable headcount reduction**, mainly via voluntary exit and attrition  
5. **verified plant-by-plant economics**

If the German network is genuinely overbuilt, then a non-closure solution often **cannot fully replace** closure-level savings unless it becomes very deep: fewer shifts, fewer lines, lower indirect labor, reduced support functions, and substantial workforce shrinkage.

---

## What management must prove
The burden of proof is not “closures are better.”  
The burden of proof is:

> **What is the fully loaded, recurring savings gap after all non-closure measures are applied?**

A proper case needs, by plant and by function:

- current utilization
- break-even volume
- direct labor vs indirect labor
- overhead and support headcount
- maintenance, utilities, logistics
- savings from shift elimination
- savings from product/line consolidation
- achievable voluntary exit volume
- wage moderation / bonus restraint
- management and owner contributions
- residual gap to target margin

Without that, closure arguments are not analytically complete.

---

## What a non-closure package can realistically deliver
In German automotive manufacturing, the main non-closure levers are:

### 1) Shift reduction / line consolidation
Often the biggest lever without formal closure.  
This can remove:

- direct labor
- some indirect labor
- energy and utilities
- maintenance burden
- local logistics costs
- some overhead absorption

But it only works if production volume is actually consolidated, not just “spread thinner.”

### 2) Working-time flexibility
Useful for bridging demand weakness, but usually **incremental** unless it is large and durable.

### 3) Voluntary exits / early retirement / attrition
This is the cleanest way to reduce headcount in Germany.  
It can be meaningful if age structure allows it.

### 4) Internal redeployment and reskilling
Useful for avoiding forced layoffs, but it does not automatically reduce cost unless it eliminates excess labor from the system.

### 5) Temporary employee contributions
Pay moderation, bonus restraint, or deferrals can help, but they are usually not transformational unless they are substantial and multi-year.

### 6) Management and owner-side sacrifice
Important for deal legitimacy and cash support, but typically not enough alone to solve structural overcapacity.

---

## Why closures remain economically powerful
Closures directly attack the **fixed-cost base**:
- plant overhead
- facilities and maintenance
- utilities and infrastructure
- plant logistics
- indirect labor
- administrative complexity
- duplicated management layers
- inventory and coordination inefficiencies

If a plant is underutilized, the problem is not just wages.  
It is **fixed-cost absorption per vehicle**.

That is why management often prefers closure: it removes structural complexity rather than trying to compress it.

---

## What “economically equivalent” would actually mean
For a non-closure package to be genuinely equivalent to closure, it must remove roughly the same recurring burden that closure would remove, without the site shutting.

That usually requires:
- deep line consolidation
- a major reduction in shifts
- material indirect headcount cuts
- product allocation changes
- substantial voluntary workforce reduction
- lower complexity costs
- and durable underutilization of remaining capacity

If those are not achieved, the deal is often a **cost deferral**, not a solution.

## My judgment on the current dispute
### The works council is right about one thing
Management cannot simply assert that closure is the only serious route.  
A non-closure package must be **quantified first**.

### Management and investors are right about one thing
A non-closure package is only credible if it is **deep enough to fix the economics**, not just preserve the current footprint with minor concessions.

### The state government is right about one thing
A plant closure is a permanent industrial loss and should not be treated as a casual negotiating lever.

### The family holding is right about one thing
If the company preserves too much cost and complexity, value destruction continues.

So the real issue is not ideology. It is whether the non-closure package can close the **fully loaded cost gap**.

## What I would call a credible non-closure package
A credible package would typically include all of the following:

- shift cuts and line consolidation
- headcount reduction through voluntary exits and attrition
- redeployment and retraining
- working-time changes
- temporary employee contribution
- management compensation restraint
- reduced payouts
- owner-side contribution
- binding investment commitments
- product allocation to the German plants
- quantitative milestone reviews

If the company has genuine overcapacity, I would still expect this package to be **very large** to avoid closures.
