I will be direct.

I hear the repeated red lines from the works council and the union: no plant closures, no forced redundancies, no weakening of co-determination, and job guarantees through 2030. I also hear the state government’s preference for preserving the regional footprint, and the family holding’s and investors’ insistence that this cannot become a slow decline dressed up as stability.

Here is my position.

## Where I agree
I agree that:
- a **cosmetic deal is worthless**;
- a **one-sided workforce sacrifice is not acceptable**;
- management, owners, and the shareholder side must also contribute;
- working-time flexibility, redeployment, training, and voluntary exit programs should be part of any solution;
- a prolonged industrial conflict would damage the company.

Those points are not in dispute.

## Where I disagree
I do **not** accept that:
- every German plant must remain open no matter what,
- forced redundancies can be ruled out categorically,
- or a blanket guarantee through 2030 can be treated as credible before the economics are fixed.

That is the core problem in your proposals: they define the outcome first and leave the cost base unresolved.

You keep asking management to prove that closures are necessary. I am telling you the reverse is also true: **you must prove that preserving all sites can deliver the required structural savings**. So far, I have not heard a package that credibly does that.

## The economic reality
The company needs:
- lower fixed costs,
- lower labour costs,
- lower structural overcapacity,
- and enough margin improvement to fund future products and technology.

If we only shift volumes, trim overtime, and offer voluntary exits, but do not remove enough capacity and cost from the system, then we have not solved the problem. We have postponed it.

And postponement is not neutral. It means:
- weaker margins,
- less investment capacity,
- more pressure on future model allocation,
- and a higher risk of harsher measures later.

## On your proposed alternatives
I am open to them, but only if they are **quantified and sufficient**.

### 1) Capacity reduction without site closures
That can be part of the answer. But I need to know:
- how much capacity is actually removed,
- from where,
- at what cost,
- and with what lasting effect.

Reducing overtime and third shifts is helpful, but it will not on its own close the gap.

### 2) Working-time flexibility
Yes, but not as a substitute for structural adjustment. Flexibility helps if it produces a real and durable cost effect.

### 3) Voluntary exits and retraining
Yes, but voluntary programs alone rarely deliver the scale required in a crisis of this size.

### 4) Management restraint and owner-side contribution
I agree these must be on the table. But let’s be realistic: they do not solve the industrial cost problem by themselves. They can help finance the transition; they cannot replace the transition.

## What I need to see
If you want to avoid closures and compulsory redundancies, then bring a package that delivers all of the following:

1. **A material and durable labour-cost contribution**
   - not symbolic,
   - not one-off,
   - not merely deferred.

2. **A real capacity reduction**
   - one that changes the economics of the footprint.

3. **Binding productivity improvements**
   - not aspirations.

4. **A credible investment plan**
   - so the company does not cut its future while fixing its present.

5. **A settlement that is enforceable**
   - not a list of promises that evaporate when markets move.

## On job guarantees
I will be blunt: **an unconditional guarantee through 2030 across the full current footprint is not credible if the business case does not support it**. I cannot sign up to a promise that locks in the current structure regardless of market reality.

That does not mean I want disorder or arbitrary cuts. It means the guarantee has to be tied to a package that actually restores competitiveness. Without that, it is just a delay mechanism.

## On forced redundancies and closures
I am not announcing closures as a preference. I am saying they cannot be ruled out in advance if we do not get a solution that is economically equivalent.

If the employee side wants to avoid them, then the burden is to deliver an alternative that is genuinely equivalent in financial and structural terms. Not equivalent in rhetoric. Equivalent in results.

## To the works council and union
You say closures would destroy trust. I understand that. But trust also erodes when the company keeps operating with a cost base that guarantees future weakness. You cannot protect trust by protecting uncompetitive structures indefinitely.

If you want to keep every site open, then show me the numbers that make that sustainable.

## To the state government
Your emphasis on regional industrial policy is understandable. But the company’s competitiveness cannot be subordinated to preserving every existing site at all costs. A regionally anchored company that steadily loses margin, investment capacity, and market position is not being protected; it is being weakened.

## To the family holding and investors
I agree with your insistence on value creation and durability. But I also want to be clear: if the response to the crisis becomes prolonged confrontation for its own sake, then that will destroy value as well. I will not support a strategy that trades a structural solution for indefinite industrial war.

## My bottom line
I remain prepared to negotiate a deal that avoids immediate closures **if** it delivers:
- meaningful capacity reduction,
- meaningful labour-cost relief,
- shared sacrifice,
- and a credible path back to target margins.

But I will not agree to a package that:
- keeps every site open by default,
- preserves the current cost base,
- and replaces hard choices with promises.

So the next step is not more red lines. It is a concrete, numbers-based counterpackage that shows how the company reaches competitiveness without closures **and** without leaving the structure intact.

If you cannot produce that, then you should not be surprised if management continues to press for stronger structural measures.
