I will be equally direct.

The state government shares the assessment that this company needs a durable restructuring. But I do **not** accept that this automatically means plant closures in Germany or forced redundancies as the first, default, or preferred instrument.

## Where I agree
I agree with management, the family holding, and investors on three points:

- **The status quo is not sustainable.**
- **A purely cosmetic compromise would fail.**
- **The company needs real competitiveness gains and a lower cost base.**

I also agree that employee-side contributions can be part of a settlement, provided they are **limited, fair, and tied to binding commitments** on investment and future product allocation.

## Where I disagree
I disagree with the way management is still framing the choice.

You are presenting closures and compulsory job cuts as if they were the unavoidable price of realism. I do not accept that.  
That is not an economic law; it is one restructuring path among others, and in this company it carries very high political, social, and industrial risks.

What I will not support:
- **German plant closures as the starting point**
- **forced redundancies as a routine lever**
- **a restructuring that weakens the company’s regional industrial base**
- **any outcome that sidelines the state’s co-determination and blocking role**

Those are not bargaining points for me. They are red lines.

## On management’s argument
Management keeps saying that if all sites are protected, the company cannot become competitive. That is too simplistic.

A serious alternative package can still generate major savings through:
- capacity reduction without site closure,
- working-time flexibility,
- shift and line adjustments,
- redeployment across plants,
- retraining,
- voluntary exits,
- and shared burden from management, owners, and the shareholder side.

Before anyone talks about closures, I expect to see that these options have been fully quantified and seriously exhausted.

If management cannot show that, then closure talk is premature.

## On the union and works council
I understand and largely support the workforce’s core position:
- no closures,
- no forced redundancies,
- no dismantling of the collective framework.

That said, I also want to be honest: the workforce cannot be asked to carry the burden alone. If there is to be a negotiated contribution, then it must be **temporary, proportionate, and matched by visible sacrifice from management and ownership**.

That includes:
- compensation restraint,
- reduced payouts,
- investment commitments,
- and a credible plan for future production in Germany.

## On the family holding
I agree that prolonged conflict would destroy value. That is precisely why I am not prepared to back a hard closure strategy that would trigger exactly that kind of conflict.

Preserving value is not only about cutting costs quickly. It is also about preserving:
- skills,
- trust,
- industrial peace,
- regional legitimacy,
- and long-term capacity to produce in Germany.

A settlement that damages those assets may save money on a spreadsheet and lose value in the real economy.

## On investors
You are right that the company must return to sustainable margins. But I do not accept the implication that the only credible route is to make jobs and sites disposable.

If the investor side wants my support, it must accept that:
- **deal certainty is not the same as closure certainty**
- and **structural change is not the same as plant liquidation**

You want a durable solution. So do I. But durability requires social legitimacy, not just financial discipline.

## What I will support
I am prepared to support a package if it includes all of the following:

1. **No plant closures as the chosen outcome**
   - closures must remain the absolute last resort, not the plan.

2. **No forced redundancies**
   - workforce reduction, if needed, must be handled through social and voluntary means.

3. **Real capacity adjustment**
   - yes, capacity must come down, but through operational reorganization, not site destruction.

4. **Shared burden**
   - management, owners, and shareholders must contribute materially.

5. **Binding future commitments**
   - investment in German sites,
   - future product allocation,
   - training and transformation programs,
   - and protection of co-determination.

## What I need to hear next
If management wants support from the state shareholder, then I need a **numbers-based restructuring plan without closures** that answers three questions:

- How much savings are required exactly?
- How much comes from management/owners/shareholders?
- How much comes from temporary labor-side measures and capacity changes?

And crucially:
- what concrete guarantees are offered for jobs, sites, and investment in Germany?

If that can be shown credibly, I will engage constructively.

If the answer is still “closures or nothing,” then my answer is also clear: **I will not accept that framing, and I will use the rights available to me under the special statute.**

The company is a regional economic anchor. I will support a tough restructuring that preserves its future. I will not support one that amputates the region to solve a short-term management problem.
