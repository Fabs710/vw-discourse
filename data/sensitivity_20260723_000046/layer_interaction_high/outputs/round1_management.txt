Thank you. I hear the clear red lines you have set, and I want to respond just as clearly.

First: I do **not** dispute that the company has a responsibility to preserve a viable industrial base in Germany. Nor do I dispute that any restructuring must be negotiated, not imposed by declaration. We are in a co-determined system, and I understand the power that gives the workforce and the union.

But I have to be equally direct: **keeping every plant open at all costs is not a strategy if the result is a permanently uncompetitive cost base**. And a deal that simply freezes the current structure while delaying the pain would fail the company, the workforce, and the future of the brand.

The facts are blunt:
- our German cost base is too high,
- the European market is shrinking,
- competition is intensifying,
- and the brand is not meeting its return targets.

If we do not make **structural** changes now, we will lose far more later — not in theory, but in orders, margins, and investment capacity.

So where do I agree?

I agree that:
- **purely cosmetic cuts are useless**;
- **voluntary measures, working-time flexibility, internal redeployment, and training** should be part of the package;
- the solution must be **durable**, not a one-year patch;
- and if employees are asked to contribute, then management and owners must also be part of the burden-sharing.

Where I disagree is on your central premise that **all German plants must remain open and no compulsory job reductions can occur under any circumstances**. I cannot accept that as a starting point, because it predetermines the outcome before we have even addressed the cost problem. If we lock in every site and every position, then we are left with slogans, not restructuring.

Let me be explicit:
- **No forced redundancies as a blanket principle is not compatible with the scale of adjustment we need.**
- **A guarantee through 2030 covering the whole existing footprint is not credible if the business case does not support it.**
- **Any employee contribution must be meaningful enough to restore competitiveness**, not symbolic.

That said, I am not here to demand closures for their own sake. I am open to a solution that achieves the required savings **without immediate closures**, if — and only if — it delivers the same structural result:
- substantial capacity reduction,
- durable labour-cost relief,
- real productivity improvements,
- and a credible path back to target margins.

So the question is not whether there must be change. There must be. The question is whether the workforce and the union are prepared to accept a package large enough to actually solve the crisis.

On that basis, here is the line I am drawing:
1. We need a **material and sustained labour-cost contribution**.
2. We need **capacity to come out of the system**, one way or another.
3. We need **binding rules that improve competitiveness for years, not months**.
4. We need a settlement that frees up resources for future products and technology.

If you want to avoid closures, then come back with a package that is genuinely equivalent in economic effect. If you cannot, then you should not expect management to rule out stronger structural measures.

I am prepared to negotiate. I am not prepared to sign up to a deal that leaves the company structurally unfit for the next decade.
