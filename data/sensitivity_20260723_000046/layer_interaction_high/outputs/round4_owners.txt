## First, where I agree

I agree with management, the union, the works council, the state government, and investors on one essential point: **this cannot be a cosmetic outcome**.

A deal that merely postpones the problem, preserves too much cost, and leaves the company structurally weak would be unacceptable. That would destroy value over time and would ultimately harm everyone, including the workforce and the region.

I also agree that:
- management and owners must contribute,
- working-time flexibility, redeployment, training, and voluntary exits belong in the package,
- temporary employee contributions can be part of a fair solution,

## Where I disagree, and strongly

I do **not** accept the working assumption from the labour side and the state government that:
- German plant closures are categorically off the table,
- forced redundancies are never permissible in any circumstances,
- and a blanket preservation of the current footprint through 2030 is a credible starting point.

That is not a restructuring answer. It is a pre-committed outcome that may simply preserve an uncompetitive structure.

### To the works council and union
You are right to demand that management prove its case. But you are still not answering the central economic question: **can a non-closure package really remove enough fixed cost and capacity to restore competitiveness on a durable basis?**

You keep saying:
- shift cuts,
- working-time changes,
- voluntary exits,
- redeployment,
- management restraint,
- owner contribution.

Good. Those are legitimate tools. But the question is not whether they sound acceptable. The question is whether they are **sufficient**.

If you want to rule out closures, then show:
- how much recurring savings come out,
- how much capacity is actually removed,
- how indirect cost is cut,
- and how the remaining footprint reaches target margins.

Without that, “no closures” is just a political position, not a viable restructuring.

### To the state government
You are right that a plant closure is not a neutral spreadsheet item. It is a permanent industrial loss.

But you are overreaching when you treat closures as basically illegitimate from the start. The state cannot use its blocking position to freeze the company into a cost structure that undermines its future. Protecting the industrial base also means protecting the company’s ability to earn its capital cost and invest.

### To management
You are also not getting a blank cheque.

You have still not presented a sufficiently quantified non-closure alternative that proves closures are unnecessary. Repeating “structural savings” is not enough. I need to see:
- plant-by-plant cost data,
- the fixed versus variable cost split,
- the savings from utilization measures,
- the headcount reduction path,
- and the residual gap after labor, management, and ownership contributions.

If that package closes the gap, I will support it. If it does not, then stronger structural measures remain legitimate.

### To institutional investors
Your risk-adjusted value language is right. So is your warning that a closure-heavy strategy can trigger conflict and execution failure.

But you also cannot hide behind conditionality forever. At some point the package must actually deliver lower fixed cost, higher margin, and a credible path to future investment. The right standard is not “whatever sounds toughest,” but “what really works.”

## What I think the economics require

This company faces:
- German factory costs above target,
- a shrinking European market,
- intense competition,
- and a brand missing its return-on-sales goal.

That means we need:
1. **material capacity reduction**
2. **material labour-cost relief**
3. **durable productivity improvement**
4. **management and ownership contribution**
5. **a credible path to fund future products and technology**

I am open on **how** that is achieved. I am **not** open to preserving every current site and every current guarantee by default and expecting the economics to fix themselves.

## On the compromise ideas

### 1) Time-limited no-closure freeze with hard review points
This is only acceptable if it is **real**:
- a defined period,
- quantified savings targets,
- measurable capacity reduction,
- and automatic reopening of the issue if targets are missed.

I do not support a permanent closure ban.  
I do not support a freeze that is just a delayed veto over necessary adjustment.

### 2) Site preservation with capacity consolidation
This is the most promising route if it actually means:
- fewer shifts,
- fewer lines,
- lower utilisation where needed,
- real headcount reduction through voluntary means first,
- and a smaller, more efficient footprint inside the existing site network.

Keeping a site “open” is not meaningful if it remains structurally underutilised and expensive. I care about the economics, not the label.

### 3) Burden-sharing tied to investment and governance commitments
Yes, but only if the burden is meaningful and the commitments are binding.

That means:
- employee concessions,
- management compensation restraint,
- reduced payouts,
- owner-side contribution,
- investment commitments,
- and product allocation that supports German operations.

If the sacrifice is one-sided, the deal is not durable.

## My line on closures and layoffs

Let me be precise.

I do **not** want closures or forced redundancies as the first move. They are costly, destabilising, and politically and operationally risky.

But I also will **not** support a settlement that:
- rules them out categorically in advance,
- leaves the company unable to restructure if the non-closure package fails,
- or freezes an uncompetitive footprint in place.

So my position is:

- **Open to a serious non-closure package**
- **Open to a time-limited freeze if it is hard-edged**
- **Not open to a permanent no-closure promise**
- **Not open to a deal that permanently impairs enterprise value**
