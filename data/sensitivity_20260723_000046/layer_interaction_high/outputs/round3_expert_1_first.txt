## Expert assessment: can a non-closure package realistically replace plant closures, and under what conditions?

Short answer: **possibly, but only if the arithmetic is sufficient on a fully loaded basis**. In German automotive, “no closures” can be a viable restructuring outcome **only when** the company can extract enough savings from **capacity consolidation, shift/working-time changes, voluntary exits, overhead reduction, procurement/productivity improvements, and labour-cost concessions** to close the gap to target margins **without relying on plant shutdown economics**. If it cannot, then an absolute no-closure stance is economically unstable.

### My judgment
Based on the scenario as given, management’s claim of structural overcapacity is plausible. In that case, the decision is not about whether cost reduction is needed, but **where the cost comes out**:

- **Plant closures** are the most direct way to remove **fixed cost** and **excess capacity**.
- A **non-closure package** can work only if it achieves **equivalent fixed-cost relief** through:
  - sustained lower utilisation,
  - removal of duplicate lines/shifts,
  - deep voluntary attrition,
  - significant labour-cost moderation,
  - and real overhead compression.

If the alternative package only trims variable labour cost while leaving the plant network and support structure largely intact, it will usually **not** be enough in a shrinking European market.

---

## What matters economically

### 1) Fixed-cost absorption
The core issue is not just wage cost. It is **fixed-cost absorption** across:
- plant overhead,
- maintenance,
- utilities,
- logistics,
- quality,
- engineering support,
- and indirect labour.

If volumes fall and the footprint stays intact, unit costs rise sharply. This is why closures or deep consolidation often become necessary in underutilised OEM networks.

### 2) Labour cost alone is not the whole gap
In a high-cost German OEM environment, labour concessions can help, but they rarely solve the problem by themselves unless the adjustment is substantial and durable. Typical temporary measures:
- wage moderation,
- working-time reduction,
- reduced bonuses,
- voluntary exits,

can improve P&L, but **do not fully fix structural underutilisation**.

### 3) Capacity reduction without closure is possible, but not free
You can reduce capacity while keeping sites legally open by:
- eliminating shifts,
- idling lines,
- concentrating models,
- moving volumes between plants,
- and reducing headcount through attrition/voluntary exits.

However, if the plants remain physically open with near-full overhead structures, the savings are often **less than management initially hopes**.

---

## What a credible non-closure package would need to include

A package would have to be quantified in three layers:

### A. Capacity and footprint actions
- removal of one or more shifts,
- consolidation of production on fewer lines,
- elimination of duplicate activities,
- transfer of models out of the weakest sites,
- shutdown of non-core or inefficient sub-processes.

### B. Workforce and labour-cost actions
- large voluntary exit program,
- hiring freeze,
- redeployment,
- reduced overtime,
- temporary working-time reductions,
- wage restraint or temporary concessions.

### C. Management/ownership actions
- overhead reductions,
- procurement savings,
- reduced discretionary spend,
- compensation restraint,
- possibly capital support for restructuring.

Without all three, the package is usually too weak.

## My view on the positions in the room

### Works council / union line
Their position is understandable, but from a restructuring perspective it is only viable **if** they are prepared to accept:
- real capacity reduction,
- permanent headcount reduction through voluntary means,
- and measurable productivity gains.

If they insist on “every site open” **and** “no forced redundancies” **and** “no material cost base change,” then the package will likely not close the gap.

### Management / investors / family holding line
They are correct that a cosmetic compromise is not enough. The risk is that they underestimate how much of the alleged savings from closures depends on:
- execution,
- labour conflict,
- and the ability to actually reallocate production.

A closure plan that triggers long industrial disruption can destroy value quickly. So “closure” is not automatically superior; it is only superior if it is **implementable** and the savings are **larger and more durable** than the non-closure alternative.

### State government line
From a restructuring standpoint, the state can influence feasibility, but it cannot change the arithmetic. If it blocks closures, the alternative must still produce the required savings. Otherwise the company will end up with an unresolved structural cost problem.

## What would determine whether the non-closure route is enough

You need a bottom-up model with at least these elements:

1. **Current utilisation by plant and line**
   - actual capacity vs. output
   - shift pattern
   - indirect labour ratio

2. **Fully loaded cost by site**
   - direct labour
   - indirect labour
   - overhead
   - maintenance
   - utilities
   - logistics
   - depreciation/carrying cost where relevant

3. **Savings from non-closure measures**
   - per site and per year
   - one-time vs recurring
   - gross vs net after transition costs

4. **Headcount reduction path**
   - voluntary exits only vs compulsory element
   - timing
   - severance / restructuring cost
   - skill mix consequences

5. **Impact on EBITDA / ROS**
   - before and after
   - sensitivity to volume decline
   - sensitivity to EV mix and market weakness

Without that, nobody can honestly say whether “keep all sites open” is feasible.

---

## My practical conclusion

### A non-closure deal is economically plausible only if:
- the group has **significant spare capacity** that can be removed by shift and line consolidation,
- labour concessions are **material, durable, and measurable**,
- voluntary exits are **large enough** to cut indirect cost as well as direct cost,
- and management/owners contribute enough to make the package credible.

### A non-closure deal is not plausible if:
- sites remain broadly intact,
- fixed cost remains mostly unchanged,
- labour concessions are temporary and modest,
- and volume erosion continues.

In that case, closures or equivalent structural exits become hard to avoid.

---

## My bottom line
The right question is **not** “closures or no closures?”  
The right question is:

> Can a non-closure package remove enough fixed cost, labour cost, and duplicate capacity to restore target margins on a durable basis?

My assessment: **it can, but only if it is deep enough to look like restructuring, not accommodation**. If the package is shallow, it will fail.
