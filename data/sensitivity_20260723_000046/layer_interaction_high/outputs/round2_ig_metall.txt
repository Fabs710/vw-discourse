I’ll respond directly and plainly.

We agree on the diagnosis that this cannot be a cosmetic exercise. The company needs a durable solution, and yes, management and owners must contribute. We also agree that working-time flexibility, redeployment, training, voluntary exits, and temporary employee contributions are legitimate tools.

But I need to correct the direction of the debate: **“structural change” cannot be a code word for plant closures, compulsory redundancies, and the erosion of the collective wage framework.** That is where we stop.

## Where we agree
- The company needs real competitiveness gains, not slogans.
- A one-sided solution that puts all pain on the workforce is unacceptable.
- Management restraint, lower payouts, and owner-side responsibility are part of any credible package.
- Capacity must be adjusted somehow.
- A prolonged open conflict would hurt everyone.

## Where we disagree
### 1) Plant closures
I reject the idea that closures are the necessary or acceptable starting point.  
The fact that the cost base is high does **not** prove that German plants must be shut. That is a managerial choice, not an economic law.

If the company wants savings, then it must first exhaust alternatives that preserve industrial sites:
- volume shifts between plants,
- temporary line reductions,
- shortened working time,
- internal transfers,
- reskilling,
- voluntary instruments,
- and productivity measures.

If those have not been fully quantified, then closure talk is premature.

### 2) Forced redundancies
We oppose compulsory layoffs. Full stop.  
If the workforce is expected to contribute, then that contribution must be socially responsible, negotiated, and balanced — not imposed through dismissals.

### 3) “Equivalent savings” must be proven, not asserted
Management and investors keep saying savings have to be “material enough.” Fine. Then show the numbers.  
We are not accepting vague claims that only closures can work.

If there is a serious alternative, it should be measurable:
- how much from management compensation restraint,
- how much from owner-side contributions,
- how much from productivity and overhead reduction,
- how much from working-time and capacity adjustments,
- how much from voluntary exit programs.

If that calculation is not complete, then closures are not justified.

## On the investor argument
I want to be very clear: the line that “we would rather see tougher structural action than a slow decline dressed up as peace” is exactly the kind of thinking that can trigger a destructive strike and a deadlock under co-determination.

That is not prudence. It is escalation.  
And if investors push for a settlement that destroys trust and industrial peace, they will not get deal certainty — they will get resistance.

## On management
You are right that the status quo is not an option. But you are wrong if you think the workforce will accept a restructuring that is basically:
- wage pressure for employees,
- job cuts for employees,
- insecurity for employees,
while management and owners present themselves as neutral observers.

That will not happen.

If you want a durable settlement, then the burden must be shared in a way that is visible and enforceable:
- management restraint,
- reduced payouts,
- temporary employee contribution,
- binding investment commitments,
- and a real employment framework.

## Our position
We are prepared to discuss a serious package that includes:
1. **Temporary, limited employee contribution**
   - only if paired with equal sacrifice elsewhere,
   - only if time-limited,
   - only if it protects jobs and sites.

2. **Capacity reduction without closures**
   - flexible production allocation,
   - reduced hours,
   - internal redeployment,
   - voluntary instruments.

3. **Binding job and site guarantees**
   - no forced redundancies,
   - no plant closures,
   - protection of the collective framework through 2030.

4. **Management and owner contribution**
   - lower payouts,
   - compensation restraint,
   - investment commitments,
   - and a credible industrial plan.

## What we will not accept
- German plant closures
- compulsory redundancies
- open-ended insecurity
- dismantling of collective protections
- a deal where employees finance their own displacement

## To the state government
Your rejection of closures as a default instrument is welcome. But this has to translate into real pressure on management, not just balanced statements. If the regional footprint matters, then use your influence to keep the company at the table with alternatives.

## To the family holding
You say you will not accept value destruction. Neither will we. But value destruction also comes from tearing up trust and provoking a confrontation that the company does not need. A negotiated solution preserving jobs and sites is not sentimentalism; it is industrial strategy.

## Bottom line
If management’s answer is still closure and forced layoffs, then we are not close.

If management wants a settlement, it must come back with:
- concrete savings from non-closure measures,
- real burden-sharing from all sides,
- and binding guarantees for jobs, sites, and collective standards.

Until then, our line remains:
**no plant closures, no forced redundancies, and no shift of the full crisis burden onto the workforce.**
