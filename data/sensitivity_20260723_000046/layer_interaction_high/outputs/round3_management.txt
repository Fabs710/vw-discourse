I will respond directly.

## First: where I agree
I agree with all sides on one fundamental point: **this cannot be a cosmetic exercise**. If we end up with a deal that preserves the current cost base and only buys a few months of peace, that will fail. It will fail the company, the workforce, the owners, and the region.

I also agree that:
- management and owners must contribute,
- working-time flexibility, redeployment, training, and voluntary exits belong in the package,
- prolonged conflict would be damaging,
- and the solution must be durable, not symbolic.

## Where I disagree — and strongly
I do **not** accept the repeated claim that:
- every German plant must stay open,
- forced redundancies are off the table in all circumstances,
- and a blanket guarantee through 2030 can be treated as credible before the economics are fixed.

That is not a restructuring plan. It is a precondition that leaves the structural problem unsolved.

### To the works council
You say closures are a choice, not an economic law. On that narrow point, yes. But neither is preserving all current sites an economic law. If you want every German plant kept open, then you have to show how the required savings and capacity reductions are achieved **without** leaving the footprint uncompetitive.

So far, I have not heard a numbers-based alternative that closes the gap.

You keep asking us to “prove closures are necessary.” I am telling you the counterpart is also true: **prove that no-closure can deliver equivalent savings and structural correction.** Not in slogans. In hard numbers.

### To the union
I hear your warning that closures would provoke strike action and destroy trust. That risk is real. But refusing all structural adjustment and then threatening escalation if management insists on reality does not solve anything either.

I am open to a negotiated package. I am **not** open to a package that pretends capacity reduction can happen without meaningful labor-cost relief and structural footprint change.

### To the state government
Your role as a blocking shareholder and political actor matters. But it cannot be used to freeze the company into an uncompetitive structure. If the business does not improve materially, the regional base is weakened anyway — just more slowly and with less investment. I will not support a deal that trades competitiveness for political comfort.

### To the family holding
I agree with you that a prolonged stalemate destroys value. I also agree that a blunt closure-first strategy that becomes ungovernable would be self-defeating. So yes, execution risk matters.

But value is also destroyed by preserving too much capacity, too much cost, and too much rigidity. We cannot reduce that risk by denying it exists.

### To institutional investors
Your standard is the right one: does the package actually restore margins and competitiveness? That is the real test. I agree that a cosmetic compromise is worthless.

Where I differ from any interpretation of your position that would simply keep every option open is this: **risk-adjusted value is not created by endless conditionality**. At some point, the package must actually remove cost and capacity.

## What we need in concrete terms
Here is the reality:

- German factory costs are above target.
- The European market is shrinking.
- Competition is intensifying.
- The brand is missing its return-on-sales target.
- We need funds for future products and technology to 2030.

That means we need:
1. **material capacity reduction**
2. **material labour-cost contribution**
3. **durable productivity improvement**
4. **shared burden from management, owners, and employees**
5. **enforceable commitments that actually change the economics**

## On the “no closures now” idea
I will say this carefully: I am open to a **time-limited freeze on closures and forced redundancies** only if it is tied to:
- a quantified restructuring package,
- measurable capacity reduction,
- a real employee contribution,
- management/owner contribution,
- and clear trigger points if the economics are not delivered.

But I will not accept a freeze that simply delays the same problem and turns every concession into a dead end.

If there is no credible structural correction, then I will not rule out stronger measures later.

## What a serious package must contain
A serious package must include all of the following:

### 1) Capacity consolidation
Not just shift trimming. Not just idle rhetoric.  
I mean:
- fewer active lines,
- lower utilization where necessary,
- redistribution of volumes,
- reduction of duplicate capacity,
- and a footprint that matches demand.

### 2) Labor-cost relief
Not symbolic. Not temporary PR.  
I mean:
- working-time changes,
- restrained wage growth,
- temporary employee contribution,
- lower burden on the cost base,
- and voluntary exits at scale.

### 3) Management and ownership contribution
This must be visible:
- compensation restraint,
- reduced payouts,
- overhead discipline,
- procurement and logistics savings,
- and capital discipline from the ownership side.

### 4) Investment linkage
Savings must not just disappear into a black hole. They must free funds for:
- EV programs,
- software,
- battery-related capabilities,
- and future product allocation.

### 5) Verification and enforcement
No more “trust us.”  
We need:
- milestones,
- review dates,
- quantified savings by source,
- and consequences if targets are missed.

## On the specific compromise ideas

### “No closures now” with triggers
This is only acceptable if the triggers are real and the package is real.  
If it is just a closure ban with no credible economic correction, then it is not a compromise. It is a postponement.

### Site preservation with capacity consolidation
This is the most serious route if — and only if — it actually reduces cost and capacity enough.  
Keeping a site “open” means nothing if it remains structurally underutilized and expensive. I am interested in outcomes, not labels.

### Burden-sharing tied to investment and governance commitments
Yes, but burden-sharing has to be large enough to matter.  
A small temporary concession in exchange for major structural rigidity is not a deal I can support.

## My position, clearly
I remain prepared to negotiate a deal that:
- avoids immediate closures,
- avoids unnecessary conflict,
- and preserves industrial capabilities,

**if** it delivers real, durable competitiveness.

But I will not agree to:
- preserve every current site by default,
- rule out structural workforce reduction in all circumstances,
- or sign a 2030 guarantee before the economics are fixed.

## To the works council and union: what I need from you
If you want me to stay away from closures, then give me a **credible counterpackage** with:
- quantified savings,
- concrete capacity reduction,
- a defined employee contribution,
- and enforceable productivity gains.

If you can do that, I will engage seriously.

If you cannot, then you should not be surprised that management continues to press for structural measures, including closures and workforce reduction.

## Bottom line
We all agree the company needs a durable restructuring.  
The dispute is whether the workforce side is willing to accept the scale of structural change needed to make that restructuring real.

My answer is straightforward:

- **No cosmetic deal**
- **No preservation of the current cost base**
- **Yes to a negotiated package if it is structurally sufficient**
- **No to a settlement that guarantees weakness**

If the next round does not produce a numbers-based alternative that materially reduces cost and capacity, then we are not discussing a real solution yet.
