We share the same starting point as management, the family holding, and even the state: **the company needs a durable restructuring, not a cosmetic truce.** If the package does not credibly restore margins and lower the structural cost base, we will not support it.

But we need to be equally clear: **a closure-heavy strategy is not automatically value-maximizing**. If it triggers a prolonged strike, co-determination blockage, political escalation, and execution failure, then the “decisive” option can destroy more value than it creates. We are not here to endorse the most aggressive plan; we are here to back the plan that best restores **risk-adjusted value**.

## Where we agree

### With management
- The current German cost base is too high.
- The status quo is not sustainable.
- Cosmetic concessions are worthless.
- Management, owners, and shareholders must contribute.
- The package must be measurable and enforceable.

### With the family holding
- A frozen cost structure is not acceptable.
- Long-term competitiveness matters more than short-lived peace.
- Burden-sharing has to be real.
- The company cannot be allowed to drift into slow decline.

### With the state government
- Prolonged industrial conflict would be harmful.
- The regional footprint and industrial base matter.
- A negotiated solution is preferable to open confrontation.

### With the workforce side
- Temporary employee contributions, working-time flexibility, redeployment, reskilling, and voluntary exits are legitimate tools.
- Management restraint and reduced payouts should be part of the deal.
- A one-sided burden on employees would be unstable and likely unworkable.

## Where we disagree

### 1) “No closures” as an absolute
We do **not** support a blank cheque for keeping every German plant open regardless of economics. If the non-closure alternative cannot deliver equivalent, durable savings, then an absolute no-closure line is not credible.

That said, we also do **not** support closures as a first reflex. The burden of proof is on management to show that closures are truly necessary after non-closure measures are fully quantified.

### 2) “No forced redundancies” as an absolute
We are not comfortable with absolute guarantees if they lock in an uncompetitive structure. Workforce reduction may be necessary. The question is whether it is achieved through voluntary, social, and negotiated means first, and whether compulsory measures remain a last resort.

### 3) Job guarantees through 2030 across the full current footprint
A blanket guarantee is not credible unless the underlying economics are fixed. We would not support a promise that simply postpones the problem and weakens the company’s ability to adapt.

## What we need to see

The debate now has to move from positions to **numbers**. We want a package that answers:

- What exact savings are required?
- How much comes from management restraint, overhead, procurement, and owner-side contribution?
- How much comes from labor measures?
- How much capacity is actually removed?
- How quickly do the savings land?
- How is execution risk controlled?
- What happens if targets are missed?

If those answers are not there, then none of the proposals is yet investable.

## Our view on the compromise options

### 1) No closures now with a quantified trigger framework
This is the most sensible route **if** it is genuinely hard-edged:
- a time-limited freeze on closures and forced redundancies,
- a binding restructuring package,
- clear savings milestones,
- and automatic reopening of the issue if performance is not delivered.

We can support that structure only if the triggers are real, measurable, and tied to margin outcomes.

### 2) Site preservation with capacity consolidation
This is preferable to a closure-led approach **if** it produces real cost and capacity reduction.
Keeping a site open is not the same as preserving every line, shift, and legacy arrangement. If capacity can be consolidated across sites, that may achieve the needed economics with less value destruction.

### 3) Burden-sharing tied to investment and governance commitments
This is attractive only if it is balanced:
- employee concessions,
- management restraint,
- owner contribution,
- binding investment,
- and measurable product allocation.

If it is merely employees giving ground while management promises vaguely, then it is not credible.

## Our bottom line

We will support the package that most credibly restores **competitive margins**, not the package that sounds toughest.

That means:
- real and durable cost reduction,
- measurable capacity reduction,
- enforceable burden-sharing,
- minimal execution risk,
- and a path back to target returns.

We are open to a non-closure deal if it is **quantified and sufficient**.  
We are open to stronger structural action if it is the only way to restore competitiveness.  
What we will **not** support is a cosmetic compromise that protects appearances while leaving the company structurally weak.

## What we want next
Management should come back with a **numbers-based restructuring table**:
- savings required,
- savings by source,
- capacity reduction by site,
- labor contribution,
- management/owner contribution,
- and the margin impact.

If that table shows that a non-closure package is enough, we can support it.  
If it does not, then the non-closure line is not credible — and if the closure route is too conflict-heavy to execute, then management has to find another structurally sufficient solution.
