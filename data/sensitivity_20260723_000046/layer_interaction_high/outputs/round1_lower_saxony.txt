We share the same basic diagnosis: the company needs a durable restructuring, not a cosmetic fix. I also agree that a purely temporary patch would only postpone the problem and make the eventual correction harsher.

But I need to be equally clear where I **do not** follow management.

### What I agree with
- The current cost base is too high.
- The business needs stronger competitiveness and more investment capacity.
- Capacity has to be reduced somewhere, and the company may need painful adjustments.
- Employee contributions can be part of the solution, if they are fair, limited, and tied to a credible future plan.

### What I cannot accept
- **German plant closures as a default restructuring instrument.**
- **Forced redundancies as the first or main lever.**
- A package that shifts the burden mainly onto employees while protecting everyone else from meaningful sacrifice.
- Any outcome that weakens the company’s regional industrial footprint in a way that permanently damages jobs, skills, and supplier networks.

This is not just a political preference. It is a matter of long-term industrial policy and shareholder responsibility. A restructuring that destroys the company’s anchor sites may look efficient on paper, but it can also damage trust, know-how, and social legitimacy in a way that undermines the company’s future.

### My position
If management wants the regional state government to support a restructuring, it must be able to show all of the following:

1. **No plant closures as the starting point**  
   We need alternatives first: working-time adjustments, internal reallocation, volume balancing, investment shifts, product allocation, and voluntary instruments.

2. **No compulsory redundancies if avoidable**  
   If jobs must be reduced, that should be done through socially responsible means, not blunt dismissals.

3. **A binding industrial future for the German sites**  
   A real commitment to investment, product allocation, and technology programs is essential.

4. **Shared burden across all sides**  
   If employees are asked for concessions, then management, the shareholder side, and capital providers must also contribute visibly and materially.

5. **Preservation of co-determination and the state’s veto role**  
   Any attempt to sideline the workforce or remove the blocking rights that protect regional interests would be fundamentally unacceptable.

### On your point about “equivalent economic effect”
That is the right test in principle, but I reject the implication that only closures can deliver it. Equivalent savings can also come from a negotiated package of:
- temporary wage restraint or deferred compensation,
- longer working-time flexibility,
- capacity reductions without site closures,
- voluntary exit programs,
- targeted redeployment,
- and management restraint on payouts and overhead.

If the numbers still do not work after that, then management has to explain why it has not exhausted these options before reaching for closures. Given the company’s importance to the region, I will not treat plant closure as an acceptable shortcut.

### To be blunt
If management comes back with a plan that still depends on closing German plants or hollowing out employment guarantees, it will face resistance from my side. I am prepared to support a tough restructuring. I am **not** prepared to support regional industrial retreat disguised as competitiveness.

What I can support is a negotiated package that:
- preserves the sites,
- secures jobs as far as possible,
- restores competitiveness,
- and keeps the company rooted in the region.

That is the standard I will apply.
