I’ll speak plainly.

We agree on the core diagnosis: this company needs a durable restructuring, not a cosmetic patch. We also agree that management, owners, and shareholders must contribute, and that working-time flexibility, redeployment, training, voluntary exits, and temporary employee contributions are legitimate tools.

But I need to correct the direction of this debate: **“structural change” cannot be a polite label for plant closures, compulsory redundancies, and erosion of the collective wage framework.** If that is the real plan, then say so openly — because the workforce will not accept it.

## To management
You still have not shown that closures are necessary. You keep saying “capacity reduction” and “structural savings,” but that does not answer the key question: **why should the burden be carried through closures and forced layoffs instead of a negotiated non-closure package?**

If you want to talk seriously, bring us a quantified alternative based on:
- capacity consolidation across existing sites,
- reduced shifts and working-time adjustments,
- internal redeployment,
- retraining and reskilling,
- voluntary departures only,
- management compensation restraint,
- reduced payouts,
- and a real owner/shareholder contribution.

If that package leaves a gap, then show the gap. But do not jump straight to closures as if they were economically neutral. They are not — they trigger conflict, destroy trust, and weaken the industrial base.

## To the family holding
You are right about one thing: a frozen cost structure is not sustainable. But I do **not** accept your implication that the only credible answer is to keep “stronger structural measures” on the table in the form of closures and layoffs.

That is not prudence; it is escalation.

You say you reject value destruction. So do we. But closure-first logic also destroys value by provoking strike action, legal blocking, and long-term industrial damage. If you want value protection, then support a deal that:
- keeps every German plant open,
- rules out forced redundancies,
- and preserves the collective framework.

If you cannot support that, then your “durable restructuring” is just another word for making workers pay for management’s strategic failures.

## To institutional investors
Your position is clearer, but still not acceptable in its current form. You say you will back whichever package restores value. Fine. But then you also refuse any blank check on no closures, no forced redundancies, and job guarantees. That leaves the workforce exposed as the only adjustable variable.

We are not offering a cosmetic deal. We are offering a **hard but negotiated non-closure restructuring** with enforceable obligations on all sides.

What would make such a package credible:
- quantified savings from capacity reduction without site closure,
- measurable productivity gains,
- a temporary and limited employee contribution,
- management restraint and reduced payouts,
- owner-side financial contribution,
- and binding investment commitments.

If you want “risk-adjusted value,” then accept that workforce sacrifice cannot be the only lever.

## To the state government
I appreciate that you are resisting closures and forced redundancies. That is the right line.

But your role cannot stop at “preference.” If you truly want to preserve the regional industrial base, then use your blocking position to prevent a settlement that shifts the entire adjustment burden onto the workforce.

A plant closure is not a spreadsheet item. It is a permanent loss of skills, supply-chain depth, and industrial legitimacy. If management insists on using that weapon, I expect the state to use the powers it has.

## To the works council
We remain aligned on the core line: no closures, no forced redundancies, no dismantling of co-determination, no unilateral erosion of job security.

I also agree with you that capacity reduction can happen inside open sites through:
- shift reductions,
- overtime cuts,
- line adjustments,
- temporary working-time reductions,
- redeployment,
- and voluntary instruments.

Where I differ slightly is this: I will not accept a framework that quietly turns “utilization management” into permanent shrinkage without safeguards. If we make temporary concessions, they must remain temporary, fair, and tied to binding commitments on jobs, sites, and investment.

## On the moderator’s compromise options

### 1) “No closures now” with triggers
I do **not** support closure triggers as the framework. That is just a delayed threat hanging over the workforce from day one. It undermines trust and turns every concession into a bridge to future layoffs. If you want a deal, it has to be a real deal, not a conditional ultimatum.

### 2) Site preservation with capacity consolidation
This is the only direction that is even remotely workable, but only if it truly means:
- every German site remains open,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- and production is reallocated through negotiated working-time, shift, and product-line changes.

That is the serious alternative. I am prepared to discuss it.

### 3) Burden-sharing tied to binding investment and governance commitments
This is also constructive — but only if the burden-sharing is **matched** by visible sacrifice from management and owners, and only if it protects jobs and sites. No burden-sharing without protection is just a wage cut with nicer language.

## What we are prepared to discuss
Let me be equally clear about what the union can support:
- temporary working-time reduction,
- shift adjustments,
- internal redeployment,
- retraining,
- voluntary early exit programs,
- limited employee contribution,
- management pay restraint,
- reduced payouts,
- owner-side contribution,
- investment commitments,
- and future product allocation to German sites.

We can also discuss **capacity reduction through utilization management**:
- fewer shifts,
- less overtime,
- adjusted line usage,
- targeted volume shifts between sites,
- but **not** plant closure.

## What remains non-negotiable
- **No German plant closures**
- **No forced redundancies**
- **No dismantling of the collective wage framework**
- **No weakening of co-determination**
- **No deal where workers finance their own displacement**

## Bottom line
If management, owners, and investors want a deal, then bring a **numbers-based non-closure package** that protects every German site and spreads the burden fairly.

Until then, our line remains unchanged:
**keep every German plant open, no forced layoffs, and no unilateral shift of the crisis onto the workforce.**

If needed, I am prepared to escalate industrial action rather than let this become a restructuring where employees bear all the pain.
