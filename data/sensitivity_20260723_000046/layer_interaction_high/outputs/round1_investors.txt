From the perspective of dispersed institutional shareholders, the only question that matters is whether this process credibly restores **risk-adjusted value**. We are not attached to any particular site, legacy arrangement, or political narrative. We are attached to **margins, cash flow, and a sustainable return on capital**.

### Where we agree
We agree with management, the family holding, and partly with the state government on the core diagnosis: **the current cost base is not competitive** and cannot remain unchanged. If the company keeps carrying excess capacity and structurally high labour costs, then the problem will simply reappear in weaker margins, weaker earnings, and a weaker valuation.

We also agree that:
- a purely cosmetic settlement is worthless;
- a prolonged conflict would destroy value;
- and any restructuring must be durable, not a temporary patch.

### Where we disagree
We do **not** support an outcome that merely preserves the current footprint while leaving the economics broken.

So to the works council and union: your red line against closures and compulsory redundancies is understandable politically, but if it results in a deal that leaves the company structurally uncompetitive, it is not acceptable from an investor standpoint. We do not invest to subsidize inefficiency indefinitely. If savings are not real, then the company’s capital cost, valuation, and strategic flexibility will continue to deteriorate.

To management: we understand the need for hard action, but a blunt closure-first approach is not automatically value-maximizing either. If closures trigger a prolonged strike, operational disruption, political backlash, and legal deadlock under co-determination, then the short-term savings may be outweighed by destruction in output, reputation, and execution risk. We will not support a value-destructive confrontation just because it looks decisive on paper.

To the state government and family holding: we agree that stability matters, but stability is not a substitute for competitiveness. Preserving every legacy arrangement and blocking meaningful restructuring would amount to defending a shrinking asset base.

### Our standard
We will support whichever package most credibly delivers:
1. **A durable return to competitive margins**
2. **A material and verifiable cost reduction**
3. **A lower structural capacity base**
4. **No endless industrial conflict**
5. **Deal certainty**

That means we are open to either:
- closures with credible execution and no drawn-out disruption, or
- a negotiated alternative that achieves **equivalent savings** in a real and enforceable way.

But we will not support:
- a cosmetic compromise that avoids all pain but fixes nothing;
- open-ended guarantees that lock in uncompetitive costs;
- or a strike-driven stalemate that burns value while everyone claims victory.

### On employee concessions
If the workforce wants to avoid closures, then the alternative must be economically meaningful, not symbolic. Temporary wage restraint, working-time flexibility, voluntary exits, capacity reduction, and productivity improvements can all be part of a serious package. But they must be large enough to change the earnings trajectory. If they are merely marginal, then they do not solve the problem.

### On management and owners
If employees are asked for sacrifice, then shareholders, the family holding, and management should also show discipline:
- restraint on distributions,
- restraint on top-end compensation,
- and clear investment commitments tied to the restructuring.

But let’s be honest: that does **not** replace the need for real operational change. It only makes the deal more credible.

### Bottom line
We are **not** backing a settlement that keeps the cost base uncompetitive or margins structurally impaired. That is our limit.

So our message to all parties is simple:
- bring a **credible, durable, numbers-based plan**;
- prove that it restores competitive margins;
- and avoid a prolonged conflict that destroys value.

If the only available outcome is a face-saving compromise without real savings, we would rather see tougher structural action than a slow decline dressed up as peace.
