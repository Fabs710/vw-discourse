I will respond directly.

## First: what I agree with
I agree with the union, works council, state government, family holding, and investors on one basic point: **a cosmetic deal is useless**.

If the result is just a temporary pause, a few voluntary exits, and some accounting relief while the German cost base stays structurally uncompetitive, then we have not solved anything. We would simply postpone the crisis and burn more value.

I also agree that:
- management and owners must contribute,
- working-time flexibility, redeployment, training, and voluntary exits belong in the package,
- and the package must be measurable and enforceable.

## Where I disagree — and this is the core issue
I do **not** accept the repeated insistence that:
- every German plant must stay open,
- forced redundancies are categorically off the table,
- and co-determination can be used to block any meaningful footprint adjustment.

That position may be understandable politically. It is **not sufficient economically**.

### To the works council
You keep saying we have not proven closures are necessary. Fair enough — but you also have not proven that a no-closure package can close the gap **on a fully loaded basis**.

That is the key point.  
Not slogans. Not symbolic guarantees. **Numbers.**

If you want to rule out closures, then show me:
- how much capacity comes out,
- how much fixed cost comes out,
- how many jobs come out,
- and how the remaining footprint reaches competitiveness.

If that is possible, I will engage seriously. If it is not, then “no closures” is not a solution; it is a refusal to solve the problem.

### To the union
You are right that closures and forced redundancies would trigger conflict and damage trust. I do not dismiss that risk.

But you cannot simply say “there is a third path” and leave it at that. A third path has to be **deep enough to be a restructuring**, not a soft landing.

You mention shift cuts, working-time changes, voluntary exits, productivity gains, and temporary wage moderation. Good. That is the right direction. But is it enough?

At this point, I still do not see a quantified package that:
- removes enough duplicate capacity,
- cuts enough indirect cost,
- lowers enough labour cost,
- and does so durably enough to restore the brand’s return-on-sales target.

### To the state government
I respect your blocking position. I also respect your concern for the industrial base. But if you use that power to freeze the footprint entirely, you do not preserve value — you trap the company in a high-cost structure that eventually weakens the region anyway.

Industrial policy is not the same as industrial protection at any price.

### To the family holding
I agree with you that a frozen cost structure is value destruction. I also agree that a closure-first strategy can provoke a destructive conflict if it is not executable.

But your position still underestimates one thing: **the scale of the structural correction required**. If the non-closure alternative does not materially reduce the cost base, then “no closures” becomes an expensive illusion.

### To institutional investors
Your emphasis on risk-adjusted value is correct. So is your warning that a closure-heavy strategy can fail if it sparks prolonged industrial disruption.

But you also cannot hide behind conditionality indefinitely. At some point, the package must actually deliver lower fixed cost and higher margin. The question is not whether closures are elegant. The question is whether the alternative is sufficient.

## What the economics require
Let me be explicit.

We face:
- German factory costs above target,
- a shrinking European market,
- intense competition,
- and a brand that is missing its return-on-sales target.

That means the company needs:
1. **material capacity reduction**
2. **material labour-cost relief**
3. **durable productivity improvement**
4. **management and owner contribution**
5. **a credible path to fund future products and technology to 2030**

If a non-closure package can do that, I will not insist on closures as an end in themselves.

But if the package leaves:
- too much fixed overhead,
- too much indirect labor,
- too much duplicate capacity,
- and too little recurring savings,

then I will not pretend it is enough.

## On the compromise ideas

### 1) “No closures now” with triggers
I can support a **time-limited freeze** only if it is paired with:
- hard, quantified savings targets,
- a real capacity-reduction plan,
- and consequences if the plan does not deliver.

What I will **not** accept is a closure ban that simply removes all pressure and turns “review” into a meaningless ritual. A freeze is only credible if it is tied to hard numbers.

### 2) Site preservation with capacity consolidation
This is the most promising route — but only if “site preservation” does not become a legal fiction.

If sites remain open but:
- shifts are cut,
- lines are consolidated,
- volumes are moved,
- support functions are compressed,
- and headcount is reduced materially,

then we are talking about real restructuring.

If, however, “site preservation” means the company keeps all structures and hopes a few voluntary exits will fix the gap, then that will fail.

### 3) Burden-sharing tied to investment and governance commitments
Yes — but only if the burden is substantial enough to matter.

That means:
- employee concessions,
- management compensation restraint,
- reduced payouts,
- owner-side contribution,
- binding investment commitments,
- and product allocation that supports German operations.

I will not endorse a deal where the workforce gives ground and the company receives only symbolic relief.

## What I need from the workforce side
If you want me to stay away from closures, then give me a credible counterpackage with:
- quantified savings,
- actual capacity reduction,
- measurable productivity gains,
- and a defined employee contribution that is meaningful, not decorative.

In plain terms: show me the gap is closed.  
If you can do that, then I will support a non-closure solution.

If you cannot, then I will not rule out stronger structural measures later.

## My line, clearly
I remain prepared to negotiate a deal that:
- avoids immediate closures,
- avoids unnecessary conflict,
- preserves key industrial capabilities,
- and protects future investment,

**if** it delivers real, durable competitiveness.

But I will not agree to:
- preserve every current site by default,
- rule out structural workforce reduction in all circumstances,
- or sign a 2030-style guarantee before the economics are fixed.

## What comes next
We need a **numbers-based restructuring table**:
- savings required,
- savings by source,
- capacity reduction by site,
- labor contribution,
- management and owner contribution,
- and margin impact.

If that table shows a non-closure package is enough, I will engage on that basis.

If it does not, then the refusal to consider closures or compulsory measures is not a responsible position; it is a blockade.

## Bottom line
- I agree that cosmetic compromise is worthless.
- I agree that management and owners must contribute.
- I agree that labor flexibility and voluntary measures should be used first.
- I do **not** agree that closures or compulsory measures can be ruled out in advance.
- I will support a non-closure package **only if** it is economically equivalent on a fully loaded, durable basis.

That is the standard.
