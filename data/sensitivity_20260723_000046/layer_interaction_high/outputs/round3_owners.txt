I will be direct.

The family holding does **not** support a deal that freezes the current cost structure and calls that “stability.” That would be a slow value destruction exercise. We also do **not** support a blunt closure-first strategy that triggers prolonged industrial conflict and makes implementation fail. Both extremes are wrong.

## Where I agree

### With management
You are right that:
- the cost base is too high,
- the company needs structural change,
- cosmetic concessions are worthless,
- and margin repair must be real, not symbolic.

I also agree that the company must regain flexibility to invest in future products and technology. Without that, the business erodes.

### With the works council and union
You are right that:
- a one-sided workforce sacrifice is unacceptable,
- management and owners must contribute,
- and working-time flexibility, redeployment, training, and voluntary exits are legitimate tools.

I also agree that prolonged conflict would damage everyone.

### With the state government
You are right that this cannot become a regional industrial retreat. A deal that destroys trust, skills, and industrial legitimacy would also destroy value.

### With investors
You are right on the core test: the package must actually restore competitive margins. A cosmetic compromise is not enough.

## Where I disagree

### To the works council and union
Your position remains too absolute.

- “No German plant closures”
- “No forced redundancies”
- “Every German plant must stay open”
- “Job guarantees through 2030”

Those are not a restructuring answer. They are a pre-committed outcome that may simply preserve an uncompetitive structure.

I understand the bargaining logic. But as a controlling shareholder, I cannot accept a settlement that locks in permanent weakness. If the economics do not work, then the company needs room for structural adjustment. That may include closures or workforce reductions if no equivalent package is proven.

### To the state government
You are right to reject reflex closure talk, but you are overreaching when you treat closures as basically off-limits. The state cannot use its blocking position to freeze the company into a cost structure that undermines its future.

### To management
You are also not getting a blank cheque.

You keep insisting on “structural savings,” but you still have not shown a sufficiently quantified non-closure alternative that proves closures are unnecessary. Repeating the words “capacity reduction” and “competitiveness” is not the same as delivering a credible plan.

## What I will support

The family holding will support a **numbers-based restructuring package** that meets all of the following:

1. **Real and durable cost reduction**
   - not temporary optics,
   - not deferred pain.

2. **Meaningful capacity reduction**
   - the footprint cannot remain untouched.

3. **Shared burden**
   - management, owners, and employees all contribute.

4. **No value-destructive instability**
   - no uncontrolled strike spiral, no governance breakdown, no reputational self-harm.

5. **Protection of long-term governance and enterprise value**
   - we will not accept any outcome that permanently impairs control or the company’s industrial future.

## What I need to see next

If you want the family holding to back a non-closure route, then bring me the numbers:

- exact savings required,
- savings by source,
- what comes from management restraint and overhead,
- what comes from procurement/logistics,
- what comes from labor-side measures,
- what capacity is actually removed,
- and how the margin target is reached.

If that package truly closes the gap, I am open to it.

If it does not, then closures and/or stronger structural measures remain on the table. I am not going to pretend otherwise.

## My line on the compromise options

### 1) “No closures now” with triggers
I would consider a **time-limited** no-closure arrangement only if:
- the trigger framework is precise,
- the savings are measurable,
- the package is enforceable,
- and the workforce side accepts that failure to deliver means the issue reopens.

But I will not support a permanent closure ban dressed up as a temporary peace.

### 2) Site preservation with capacity consolidation
This is the most promising route if it is real, not rhetorical.  
Keeping every site “open” on paper is meaningless if the economics stay broken. I care about the cost structure, not the label.

### 3) Burden-sharing tied to investment and governance commitments
Yes, but only if the burden is meaningful and the commitments are binding.  
Employee concessions must be matched by visible management restraint, owner-side contribution, and a credible investment plan.

## What I will not accept
- a cosmetic compromise,
- a permanent guarantee that freezes an uncompetitive structure,
- a deal that leaves the company unable to invest,
- or a settlement that shifts the entire adjustment burden onto shareholders while preserving all legacy protections.

## Bottom line

The family holding’s position is simple:

- We want a **durable restructuring**
- We want **real competitiveness gains**
- We want **shared sacrifice**
- We want **labor peace if it is economically credible**
- But we will **not** support a settlement that merely postpones decline

So: bring a quantified, enforceable, non-closure alternative that truly restores margins. If you cannot, then stronger structural options remain legitimate.
