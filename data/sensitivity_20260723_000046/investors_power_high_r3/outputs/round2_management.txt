Let me respond directly, because the gap between the positions is still too wide.

## First: what I agree with
I agree with everyone on one central point: **this company needs a durable solution, not a cosmetic one**.

I also agree that:
- the current situation is serious,
- labour cannot be expected to carry everything alone,
- and any restructuring must be credible through 2030.

I further agree that a deal built only on confrontation would be damaging. Prolonged strikes hurt output, reputation, and value creation. That is exactly why we need a solution that actually changes the cost base.

## Where I disagree — clearly
### 1) “No plant closures” cannot be a starting line if the economics do not work
To the Works Council and the union: I understand your red line. I also understand why you are drawing it. But I cannot accept it as an absolute if it prevents the company from fixing structural overcapacity.

This is not a symbolic dispute. The company has:
- too much cost in Germany,
- too much capacity relative to a shrinking market,
- and a brand that is missing its return-on-sales target.

If the answer is simply “keep every plant open and protect every job through 2030,” then you are asking management to preserve an uncompetitive structure and hope it becomes competitive on its own. It will not.

### 2) “No compulsory redundancies” is not enough unless the alternative delivers equivalent savings
I am open to voluntary measures, redeployment, working-time changes, and negotiated instruments. But I will not sign up to a package that says “no dismissals” while leaving the same headcount, the same fixed cost burden, and the same overcapacity in place.

If you reject closures and forced redundancies, then you must bring something that is **genuinely equivalent** in economic effect:
- durable labour-cost relief,
- meaningful capacity reduction,
- real productivity gains,
- and clear margin improvement.

Not temporary pressure relief. Not accounting effects. Real structural change.

### 3) The state government’s position is understandable, but it does not solve the cost problem
I respect the concern for regional stability and the industrial base. But saying “no closures” does not remove the need to cut costs. It only shifts the question to how.

If you want to avoid closures, then the package must still deliver the savings. Otherwise, you are defending a geography, not a business.

### 4) To the investors and family holding: I agree with the diagnosis, but not with passivity
You are right that a weak compromise would destroy value. You are right that the economics must work. Where I push back is on the idea that governance constraints somehow make the restructuring impossible. They do not.

What they mean is that we need a negotiated solution that is tough enough to pass the test of the board, the market, and the workforce. That is exactly why management is pushing for a structural package now.

## What management needs to protect
Our objectives remain unchanged:
- reduce overcapacity,
- lower labour costs structurally,
- restore the brand’s return-on-sales trajectory,
- and free up funds for product and technology investment through 2030.

Those are not optional goals. Without them, the company will continue to underperform, and every stakeholder here will end up with a weaker business.

## What I am prepared to accept
I am **not** insisting that plant closures are the only route.

I am prepared to support an alternative package if it delivers the same durable outcome, for example through:
- substantial and lasting labour-cost contributions,
- capacity reductions of sufficient scale,
- working-time adjustments,
- productivity and flexibility gains,
- voluntary departures,
- and other structural savings that are contractually enforceable.

But I need to be explicit: **the package must materially reduce the cost base**. If it only preserves all current structures and asks for a temporary concession, then it is not a restructuring.

## What I am not prepared to accept
I will not accept:
- a purely cosmetic compromise,
- a “peace at any price” arrangement,
- a deal that preserves every site regardless of economics,
- or a settlement that postpones the problem until the next crisis.

I am also not interested in a process where management is told to absorb strategic failure indefinitely while the company’s competitiveness deteriorates. That would be irresponsible.

## On labour’s contribution
I hear the point that employees should not pay alone. I accept that. But labour contribution has to be **substantial and durable**, not symbolic.

That means if the workforce wants site security, then the package must give the company:
- real cost relief,
- real flexibility,
- and a real path back to acceptable margins.

A contribution that is too small, too temporary, or too reversible will not move the company to where it needs to be.

## On governance and decision-making
We all know the governance reality here:
- a plant decision requires a two-thirds supervisory-board majority,
- co-determination gives employees an effective veto,
- and there are additional blocking positions in the system.

That means no one gets unilateral victory. Fine. But it also means the employee side cannot simply declare the necessary restructuring off limits and expect management to pretend that the numbers have changed.

The board will have to judge whether a deal is economically credible. So will the owners and investors. That test remains.

## My bottom line
Management is willing to negotiate a serious package. But the package has to do the following:
1. **Cut costs structurally**
2. **Reduce overcapacity meaningfully**
3. **Improve the brand’s margin path**
4. **Create room for investment to 2030**
5. **Be durable, not temporary**

If the employee side wants to avoid closures, then it must bring a package that is just as effective. I am open to that.

If it cannot, then I will say plainly: closures and stronger restructuring measures remain on the table because the business cannot afford a deal that leaves it uncompetitive.

So let us stop pretending the choice is between “nice compromise” and “management harshness.” The real choice is between:
- a **credible structural reset**, or
- a **deal that fails the company by preserving too much of the old cost base**.

That is the standard I will use.
