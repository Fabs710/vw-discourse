As dispersed institutional shareholders, we are not here to defend any particular site or to preserve capacity for its own sake. We are here to protect the risk-adjusted value of the equity. That means one thing above all: **a credible, durable return to competitive margins**.

So let me be direct.

### Where we agree
We agree with management on the core diagnosis:
- the cost base is too high,
- overcapacity is a real problem,
- and cosmetic measures will not restore returns.

We also agree with the union, works council, and state government on one point:
- a deal that simply shifts the burden onto employees while leaving the company’s strategic execution weak would be unstable and value-destructive.

### Where we disagree
We do **not** support an outcome that leaves the company structurally uncompetitive.

That means:
- no settlement that merely postpones the problem,
- no “peace” bought by avoiding hard decisions,
- no arrangement that preserves too much capacity, too much cost, and too little margin improvement.

If the outcome is a cosmetic compromise that protects all sites in name but leaves the cost base intact in reality, that is not a compromise from our perspective — it is a destruction of shareholder value.

### On plant closures and job cuts
We are not ideologically attached to closures. If management can achieve the same economics through other durable measures, we would welcome that.

But we need to be clear: **the economics have to work**.

So we would support:
- plant closures, if they are genuinely necessary to remove structural overcapacity,
- or alternative measures, if they deliver the same durable cost reduction with comparable certainty.

What we will not support is a “no closures, no redundancies” framework **unless** it is paired with savings that are just as large, just as durable, and just as credible.

### On labour’s contribution
We expect a meaningful employee contribution if it helps secure the turnaround. That can be reasonable, but only if it is:
- substantial,
- durable,
- and tied to measurable restructuring outcomes.

Temporary or symbolic concessions are not enough. Nor is a deal where labour gives up compensation while management still fails to close the competitiveness gap.

### On the state and governance constraints
We understand the political sensitivity and the governance veto points. But from a capital-market perspective, those constraints are part of the problem, not a substitute for a solution.

If governance blocks the company from taking the steps required to restore competitiveness, then the market will price that risk in. We will not pretend that a politically comfortable arrangement is financially acceptable.

### What we need to see
To support any restructuring package, we need:
- a **clear, quantified path** back to target margins,
- **durable cost reduction**,
- **credible capacity rationalization**,
- and **deal certainty**.

That last point matters: prolonged strike risk and endless confrontation destroy value. We would rather back a hard but settled restructuring than a prolonged stalemate.

### Bottom line
We are prepared to support a tough solution — including closures if necessary — **provided it is economically credible and durable**.

What we will not support is:
- a weak compromise,
- a structurally impaired cost base,
- or a settlement that protects every constituency except the shareholders who finance the business.

If the parties can produce a package that genuinely restores competitiveness, we will back it. If not, we will treat that as a failure of governance and capital discipline.
