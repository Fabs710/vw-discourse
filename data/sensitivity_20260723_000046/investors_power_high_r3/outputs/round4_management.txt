I will respond plainly.

## To the works council and union
I hear your insistence on **no closures** and **no compulsory redundancies**. I understand the political and social logic behind it.

But I do **not** accept the claim that there is “no equivalence” in economic terms. There absolutely is an economic equivalence test, because the company has a finite cost base and finite capacity. If we do not reduce both structurally, then we do not restore competitiveness. That is not a slogan; it is the arithmetic of this business.

What I **do** agree with is this:
- a cosmetic deal is worthless,
- labour must contribute,
- and any solution must be durable.

What I **do not** agree with is the idea that security must be granted **first, unconditionally, and permanently**, while management is asked to hope that savings somehow appear later. That would not be restructuring. That would be postponing the crisis and loading all the risk onto the company, owners, and future investment.

## On your “binding through 2030” demand
A blanket guarantee through 2030, with no credible restructuring flexibility, is not acceptable if it blocks the structural changes we need. You are effectively asking us to sign away the ability to fix overcapacity while promising that future competitiveness will emerge by goodwill.

It won’t.

If you want binding protection, it has to be tied to **binding, measurable contributions** that genuinely change the cost base:
- meaningful wage moderation,
- reductions in special payments,
- working-time flexibility,
- capacity consolidation,
- voluntary exits,
- redeployment,
- early retirement,
- and productivity improvements.

If those are substantial enough, then yes, we can talk about avoiding closures. If they are not, then “security” is just another word for freezing the problem in place.

## On plant closures
I am not saying closures are the preferred solution. I am saying they must remain on the table because they are the clearest structural lever if the non-closure alternatives do not produce equivalent savings.

The works council keeps saying closures are a threat tactic. No: they are a consequence of a business that has to become competitive again. If we can achieve the same savings and capacity reduction without closures, I am open to that. But you have not yet proposed a package that proves it.

That is the gap in your position: you reject the outcome, but you have not matched the economics.

## On compulsory redundancies
I prefer to avoid compulsory redundancies. They are disruptive and costly. But I will not join a blanket taboo if the numbers do not work.

Again: if voluntary instruments, redeployment, and working-time measures can get us there, excellent. If they cannot, then I will not pretend that ignoring the shortfall is a solution.

## To the regional state government
I respect your concern for regional stability, and I also accept that industrial conflict would be damaging.

But your position cannot be: “No closures, no forced layoffs, preserve every plant, and somehow the economics will sort themselves out.” That is not responsible governance.

If you want to block destructive outcomes, then help produce a package that actually reduces the structural cost burden. Support qualification, redeployment, and flexible adjustment. Help make a real deal work. But do not ask management to carry the entire burden while the operating model remains too expensive.

## To the family holding and investors
You are right to demand a serious outcome. I agree that a weak compromise is unacceptable.

Where I differ from any impatience for an easy “big cut” is this: a disorderly restructuring that triggers prolonged strikes, operational chaos, and brand damage can destroy value faster than a measured but real package.

So yes, we need structural change. But it has to be **durable and implementable**. I am not interested in theater. I am interested in restoring margins, freeing funds for product and technology, and getting the brand back on track.

## On the mediator’s bridge ideas
I will be very clear:

### Time-bound non-closure pact with triggers
Not enough if it is merely a pause. A moratorium with future triggers does not solve the core issue. If the trigger mechanism simply reintroduces closure pressure later, then it is not a settlement; it is deferred conflict.

### Sequenced package
Sequencing is secondary. The only question is whether the final package delivers:
- real cost reduction,
- real capacity reduction,
- and a durable improvement in margins.

### Capacity reduction without shutdowns
This is the only route I am prepared to explore seriously **if** it is economically real. If it is just internal reshuffling, then it is not enough.

## What I need from the other side
If you want me to move off my current position, bring me something concrete:

- exact labour-cost contribution;
- exact capacity reduction;
- exact productivity gains;
- exact timing;
- exact impact on return-on-sales;
- exact mechanism for ensuring delivery.

Without those numbers, I cannot say that a non-closure solution is enough.

## My bottom line
My position remains unchanged:

- We need a **structural, durable cost and capacity reduction**.
- Cosmetic compromise is not acceptable.
- I am open to avoiding closures **only if** the alternative is economically equivalent.
- I am not prepared to rule closures out in advance when the company’s competitiveness is at stake.
- I am not prepared to accept a “no forced redundancies” absolute if it blocks a real restructuring.

So let’s stop pretending that rejection of closures is itself a plan. It is not. A plan is something that closes the cost gap, reduces overcapacity, and restores the brand’s competitiveness through 2030.

If the employee side can deliver that without closures, I will support it. If not, I will continue to insist that closures remain a legitimate part of the restructuring toolkit.
