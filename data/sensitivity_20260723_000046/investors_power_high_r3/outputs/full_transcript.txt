[Works Council (Betriebsrat) (Plant-level employee representation under co-determination) — Round 1]
As Works Council, I will be very clear from the outset:

We will not accept plant closures in Germany and we will not accept compulsory redundancies. That is the line.

The workforce has already carried a great deal of the burden of this transformation. We have supported investment, productivity improvements, and painful adjustments in the past. What we cannot accept is that management now tries to solve its strategic mistakes by tearing up historic job-security commitments and threatening entire sites.

Our priorities are:

1. Keep every German plant open.
2. Protect every job through to 2030 with binding guarantees.
3. Avoid forced layoffs and preserve co-determination and collective bargaining rights.
4. Secure a restructuring path that is fair and shared, not one-sidedly imposed on employees.
5. Ensure that any savings come through negotiated, reversible measures, not irreversible destruction of industrial capacity.

If management believes it needs savings, then we are prepared to discuss alternatives:
- capacity adjustments,
- working-time solutions,
- voluntary instruments only,
- temporary contribution from labour under strict conditions,
- and clear commitments on future products, investment, and location security.

But let me be equally clear: no plant closure, no operational dismissals, no fait accompli. If those are on the table, then we are in direct conflict.

We are ready to negotiate seriously, but only on a solution that secures the future of the sites and the people who built this company. Without binding guarantees, there is nothing to discuss.

[Expert: independent industrial restructuring ana — Round 4]
Yes. Within industrial restructuring practice, the key question is not whether a non-closure deal can produce *some* savings — it can — but whether it can realistically match the **full economic effect** of a plant closure package: permanent fixed-cost removal, capacity reduction, overhead absorption improvement, capex avoidance, and reduced operational complexity.

## Bottom line
A **non-closure package can sometimes approximate a meaningful share of closure savings**, but it **usually does not fully match** a true plant closure unless several unusually strong conditions are met:

1. there is substantial **overcapacity** that can be removed through shifts, lines, or platforms without keeping duplicate fixed costs alive;
2. the site-specific fixed cost base is mainly **labor**, not buildings, utilities, maintenance, logistics, and local overhead;
3. labor accepts **durable** concessions, not temporary ones;
4. product allocation can be simplified so that the remaining footprint operates at a higher utilization rate;
5. management is able to realize **headcount and overhead reductions** without maintaining hidden standby capacity.

If any of those conditions fail, a non-closure deal typically falls short of closure-equivalent economics.

---

## How closure savings usually work

A plant closure delivers savings in several buckets:

### 1) Fixed operating cost elimination
This is the biggest item:
- direct plant labor
- supervision
- maintenance
- site services
- utilities and security
- local engineering, planning, and support roles
- plant-level overhead allocation

In automotive, the truly structural gain comes from eliminating a full cost center, not just reducing wages.

### 2) Capacity rationalization
Closure removes installed capacity outright. That improves:
- network utilization
- line balance
- overhead absorption
- planning simplicity
- inventory efficiency

If the company has excess European capacity, closing a plant can lift the utilization of the remaining plants, which magnifies savings beyond the closed site itself.

### 3) Capex avoidance
A closed site usually avoids:
- refresh capex
- compliance capex
- tooling renewal
- EV platform adaptation
- brownfield maintenance investment

A non-closure package may defer some capex, but if the site remains open it usually does not eliminate it.

### 4) Working capital and logistics effects
Closure often enables:
- shorter product complexity
- fewer variants
- lower interplant shuttle costs
- leaner inventories

These are real, but usually secondary to fixed-cost removal.

---

## What a non-closure package can realistically deliver

A strong non-closure package can include:
- voluntary exits and early retirement
- reduced working time
- wage moderation / special-pay cuts
- redeployment
- shift reductions
- line consolidation
- product reallocation
- site-level productivity programs
- management overhead cuts

### Typical achievable effect
In practice, a well-designed non-closure package can often capture roughly:

- **20% to 50%** of the cost benefit of a full plant closure, sometimes more in special cases
- **50% to 70%** if the main cost issue is excess labor and the remaining network can absorb volume efficiently
- **Less than 20%** if the site carries heavy fixed overhead, dedicated tooling, or unique product/process costs that persist unless the site is shut

Those are broad industrial ranges, not company-specific data.

---

## Why “no closure” often fails to equal closure economics

### 1) Fixed costs remain
If the site stays open, many fixed costs stay alive:
- security
- maintenance
- utilities
- compliance
- local management
- residual overhead

Even after workforce reductions, the company may still carry a partially loaded plant structure.

### 2) Underutilization persists
A plant operating at lower volume but still open often suffers from:
- weak labor productivity
- poor fixed-cost absorption
- higher unit cost
- complexity penalties

You can reduce headcount without fully solving the cost-per-unit issue.

### 3) Redundancy elimination is incomplete
Closure allows the company to remove:
- entire teams
- duplicated functions
- plant-level decision layers
- site support infrastructure

Non-closure packages often leave some of this intact.

### 4) Durability is weaker
Temporary wage moderation or working-time concessions are not equivalent to a closed site unless they are:
- long-dated
- legally or contractually robust
- linked to irreversible capacity reduction

---

## What would need to be true for equivalence

A non-closure deal could come close to closure-equivalent savings if it includes all of the following:

### A) Large permanent headcount reduction
Not just attrition — a durable reduction in productive labor and support staff.

### B) Strong labor-cost reset
Examples:
- pay moderation
- bonus reduction
- lower progression
- reduced allowances
- changes to guarantees

### C) Actual capacity removal
Not merely lower shifts. The company would need to eliminate:
- one full shift system or more
- line duplication
- duplicate tooling
- some product families
- cross-site overhead

### D) Product concentration
Remaining output must be concentrated onto fewer plants, so utilization rises materially.

### E) Capex discipline
The company must credibly avoid major reinvestment in the preserved sites.

If those five elements are achieved, a non-closure package can be **economically serious**. But at that point it begins to resemble closure economics through other means.

## Specific comparison: closure vs. non-closure

### Full closure
Pros:
- maximum fixed-cost elimination
- clean capacity reduction
- high durability
- clear overhead reduction

Cons:
- severance and closure costs
- transition disruption
- possible supply-chain reconfiguration cost
- political and labor conflict
- potential temporary volume loss

### Non-closure package
Pros:
- avoids immediate social and operational shock
- may preserve know-how and optionality
- can be faster to implement if negotiated well
- lower immediate closure cash cost

Cons:
- savings are usually incomplete
- residual fixed costs remain
- requires ongoing labor cooperation
- risk of underperformance and later renegotiation
- may preserve too much capacity

## Core impasse

The central blockage is **not** whether restructuring is needed. All sides accept that it is.

The impasse is over **what must be irrevocably excluded up front** versus **what must remain available as a fallback if the economics are not met**.

More specifically, the conflict concentrates on four linked points:

1. **Plant closures**
   - Works council, union, and regional state government want them excluded.
   - Management, investors, and the family holding want them retained as a legitimate restructuring tool.

2. **Compulsory redundancies**
   - Employee-side and state actors want them excluded.
   - Management and capital-side actors want them retained as a fallback if voluntary measures fall short.

3. **Binding security vs. economic flexibility**
   - Labour wants binding site/job security through 2030 as the precondition for negotiation.
   - Management and owners want economic reforms first or at least simultaneously, not after security is locked in.

4. **Meaning of “equivalence”**
   - Labour says closure is not socially equivalent to any cost-saving alternative.
   - Management and investors say any alternative must be economically equivalent to closure on fixed costs, capacity, and durability.

So the core impasse is: **Can a non-closure package be made deep and durable enough to replace closure-based restructuring, while still giving labour and the state a binding no-closure, no-forced-layoff assurance?**

## Facilitator compromise positions

### 1) Deep non-closure package with independent equivalence test
A compromise position is to create a **time-limited restructuring test phase** in which:
- closures and compulsory redundancies are **not used during the test period**,

- management commits to transparent target figures for fixed-cost reduction and capacity reduction,
- an **independent advisor** verifies whether the non-closure package closes a specified percentage of the economic gap versus closure,
- a pre-agreed threshold determines whether the parties move to a second phase.

### 2) No-closure / no-forced-layoff pact with conditional escalation only after failure of agreed milestones
A second compromise is a **binding no-closure, no-compulsory-redundancy pact** through a defined period, but only if:

- state participation is used to reinforce compliance and mediation.

This preserves the workforce’s red lines while giving management a structured route to revisit the deal **only if the agreed plan demonstrably fails**.

### 3) Site-by-site differentiated settlement
A third compromise is to avoid an all-or-nothing company-wide rule and instead negotiate:
- **site-specific agreements** based on actual capacity and product allocation,
- stronger guarantees for sites that can absorb volume and modernization,

This can reduce deadlock by shifting the debate from abstract principles to **plant-level economics**.
