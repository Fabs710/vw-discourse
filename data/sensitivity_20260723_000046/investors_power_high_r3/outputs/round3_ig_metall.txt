I will respond directly.

## First: what we agree on
I agree with several points raised by management, the state government, and even the investors and family holding:

- **The company needs a real restructuring.**
- **A cosmetic deal is not enough.**
- **Employees will need to contribute.**
- **The outcome has to be durable, not just a short truce.**
- **Long-term investment in products and technology matters.**

On that, we are not at odds.

## Where we fundamentally disagree
### 1) Plant closures are not the “default” solution
I reject the logic that closures are somehow the natural first answer and everything else is secondary.

Management says closures may be necessary if alternatives fail. Fine — but that cannot mean closures are the benchmark from day one. It means management has to **prove, with numbers**, that every other serious tool has truly been exhausted.

Those tools are not vague:
- capacity reductions across sites,
- shift and working-time changes,
- voluntary departures,
- early retirement,
- redeployment,
- training and qualification,
- productivity and flexibility agreements,
- temporary labour-cost moderation,
- and management/owner contributions.

If those can deliver the required savings, then closures are not justified.

### 2) Compulsory redundancies are unacceptable
I want to be unambiguous: **forced layoffs are a red line**.

If headcount has to come down, then it must be through:
- voluntary exit programs,
- natural attrition,
- redeployment,
- qualification,
- and negotiated working-time tools.

The workforce will not accept being told that management’s strategic correction requires people to be forced out. That would transfer the cost of restructuring entirely onto employees, and that is exactly what we will not allow.

### 3) “No closures, no redundancies” is not rhetoric — it is the condition for a fair deal
The works council is right on the principle, and I will reinforce it.

A durable settlement cannot be built on fear. If employees are expected to contribute, then they need certainty that:
- their sites are not being sacrificed,
- their jobs are not being forcibly cut,
- and the collective bargaining framework remains intact.

Without that, labour’s contribution is not a fair trade. It is simply a one-sided sacrifice.

## To management
You say you need structural cost reduction and overcapacity relief. I do not dispute that.

What I dispute is your implication that the only credible path runs through closures or forced redundancies. That is not a restructuring necessity; it is one possible choice.

If you want our serious engagement, then bring forward:
- a quantified cost gap,
- the savings target,
- the measures tested,
- and the economic reason why a non-closure package would or would not work.

Right now you are still arguing in broad terms. We need hard numbers.

And let me say this clearly: **we will not sign a blank cheque for “competitiveness.”** Workers have already carried a lot of transformation burden. They will not be asked to carry all the pain again while management and owners remain largely insulated.

## To the investors and family holding
You are right about one thing: a weak compromise that leaves the company structurally uncompetitive is not acceptable.

But you are wrong if you think labour’s insistence on no closures and no forced redundancies is somehow a refusal to restructure. It is not. It is a refusal to accept that restructuring must mean industrial damage.

If you want value preserved, then the package must be:
- economically real,
- durable,
- and socially stable.

A strike-driven, trust-destroying solution may satisfy a spreadsheet for one quarter, but it will damage productivity, morale, and the company’s long-term industrial base.

## To the state government
I appreciate your support for avoiding closures and forced redundancies. But support in principle is not enough. If this is serious, then use your position to make sure the company does not turn social damage into an economic strategy.

## On labour’s contribution
Let me be equally clear: **we are prepared to contribute — but only fairly.**

That can include:
- temporary wage moderation,
- reduced bonuses or special payments,
- working-time flexibility,
- voluntary efficiency measures,
- and other negotiated instruments.

But those contributions must be matched by:
- **site security**
- **job security**
- **management contribution**
- **owner/shareholder contribution**
- **investment commitments**
- **future-product commitments**

We will not accept a deal where workers pay twice:
1. through lower incomes, and
2. through insecurity and plant closures.

## On the mediator’s bridge proposals
### Proposal 1: “No forced layoffs, conditional site review”
I do **not** accept this as currently framed. A “conditional review” can become a closure mechanism later. If “site review” means the threat returns after a short pause, then it is not a guarantee.

If there is to be any review at all, it must be based on jointly agreed measures and without an open-ended gun to the head of the workforce.

### Proposal 2: “Employment security package with measurable offsetting savings”
This is closer, but only if the order is right:
1. binding no-closure and no-forced-layoff commitments,
2. then negotiated employee contributions,
3. then jointly enforced productivity and investment commitments.

Not the other way around.

### Proposal 3: “Capacity reduction without closure as first resort”
This is the only direction that has any real potential — **if** it is actually without closure and without forced redundancies.

That means:
- shift reductions,
- site-specific product allocation,
- reduced overtime,
- voluntary departure pathways,
- internal transfers,
- temporary underutilization,
- and negotiated flexibility.

It does **not** mean closure by another name.

---

## My clear position in this round
The union’s line remains:

- **No German plant closures**
- **No compulsory redundancies**
- **Preserve the collective bargaining framework**
- **Employees can contribute, but only in a fair shared-sacrifice package**
- **Management, owners, and shareholders must also take responsibility**

If management can present a serious package that meets those conditions, we can negotiate it.

If not, then let us be honest: the conflict will continue, and we will use the tools we have. The workforce will not be made to finance its own insecurity.

## Bottom line
A durable settlement is still possible. But it must be built on **shared sacrifice**, not on workers absorbing the restructuring costs alone.

If that principle is not accepted, then there is no basis for agreement.
