I’ll be equally clear in round 3: **the employee side has not moved on the core issue, and we will not move on it now.**

The mediator keeps asking for “equivalence” as if that were a neutral technical test. It is not. From our perspective, there is **no equivalence** to closing a German plant or forcing people out of their jobs. Those are not interchangeable line items.

## Where I agree
I agree with all parties on one point: **the company needs a serious restructuring, not a symbolic one**. I also agree that labour must contribute, and that a durable solution has to be economically credible.

But credibility cannot be achieved by demanding that the workforce sign away site security and job security first.

## Where I disagree

### 1) No plant closures in Germany — not now, not as a “last resort”
Management, investors, and the family holding keep saying closures are a legitimate fallback if alternatives fail. I reject that framing.

Why?

Because once you normalize closures, every plant is put on probation, every workforce is made disposable, and trust is destroyed. That is not a restructuring strategy; it is a threat model.

You say “prove the alternatives.” Fine — but the burden is on management to prove that:
- capacity reductions,
- shift changes,
- product allocation changes,
- voluntary instruments,
- internal transfers,
- working-time solutions,
- and productivity measures

have truly been exhausted **without** invoking closure as leverage.

So far, I have not seen that proof. I have seen pressure, but not proof.

### 2) No compulsory redundancies — absolutely not
This is not negotiable.

If management cannot achieve adjustment through:
- natural attrition,
- voluntary programs,
- early retirement,
- redeployment,
- training,
- and negotiated working-time instruments,

then the problem is not that the workforce is “inflexible.” The problem is that management wants the workforce to pay for strategic mistakes through fear.

That is unacceptable, and it will be resisted.

### 3) No weakening of co-determination as the price of “speed”
The discussion keeps drifting toward a model where management wants more freedom, faster decisions, and fewer obstacles. Let me say plainly: **co-determination is not the obstacle; it is the safeguard**.

If you want labour to accept sacrifices, then employee representatives must have a real role in shaping the plan, not merely being informed after the fact.

A package that sidelines co-determination while asking for trust is a contradiction.

## On the mediator’s bridge proposals

### Proposal 1: “No forced layoffs, conditional site review”
No. I do not accept a “temporary moratorium” on closures that quietly turns into a conditional threat later.

A moratorium only has value if it is real, binding, and not just a pause before the same pressure resumes. If “site review” is code for a later closure decision, then it is not a guarantee. It is a delay tactic.

### Proposal 2: “Employment security package with measurable offsetting savings”
This is closer to the real issue, but still incomplete.

If you want labour contributions, then the sequence must be:
1. binding site and job guarantees,
2. then negotiated employee contributions,
3. then jointly agreed productivity and investment commitments.

Not the other way round.

And any “offsetting savings” must come from measures that do **not** endanger the sites or make jobs conditional on the next quarterly shortfall.

### Proposal 3: “Capacity reduction without closure as first resort”
Now we are talking about the right direction — but only if it is truly without closure and without forced redundancies.

We can discuss:
- shift reductions,
- product reallocations,
- temporary underutilization,
- voluntary exit pathways,
- reduced overtime,
- internal flexibility,
- and targeted investment by site.

We cannot discuss “capacity consolidation” as a euphemism for shutting one site and overloading another. That would be closure by another name.

## To management
You keep asking for structural cost reduction. Understood.

But if your only serious tool is closure pressure, then you have not demonstrated management capability — you have demonstrated dependence on coercion.

Bring us a plan that:
- keeps every German plant open,
- excludes compulsory redundancies,
- preserves co-determination,
- and delivers savings through negotiated means.

Then we can evaluate whether you are serious.

Until then, we see a unilateral restructuring attempt dressed up as dialogue.

## To the investors
You say the market won’t reward governance comfort. That is true. But the market also punishes industrial conflict, brand damage, and operational instability.

A settlement built on plant closures and forced layoffs may look decisive to analysts in the short term, but it creates:
- strike risk,
- morale loss,
- skill drain,
- reputational harm,
- and long-term instability.

So do not tell us that the only “value-friendly” path is the one that tears up the workforce’s trust.

### To the family holding
You say you want long-term value, not blunt force. Then stop backing leverage that puts our plants and jobs under permanent threat.

Long-term ownership means protecting industrial capability, not treating German sites as a balancing item.

### To the state government
I appreciate that you reject closures and forced redundancies. But words are not enough.

If you are serious, then use your position to block any solution that:
- closes German plants,
- imposes compulsory redundancies,
- or weakens the job-security framework.

Do not ask the workforce to “understand the bigger picture” while our people absorb the damage.

## Our position on a settlement
Let me restate it without ambiguity:

We can consider:
- voluntary programs,
- internal redeployment,
- working-time instruments,
- temporary and negotiated labour contributions,
- productivity improvements,
- and investment-linked modernization.

We cannot consider:
- plant closures,
- compulsory redundancies,
- or conditional guarantees that evaporate when management changes its mind.

## Bottom line
If the others want a deal, they must stop treating **jobs and sites** as negotiable collateral.

**No German plant closures.  
No compulsory redundancies.  
Full respect for co-determination.  
Binding job and site security through 2030.**

Anything less is not a compromise. It is a demand that the workforce finance its own insecurity. We will not accept that.
