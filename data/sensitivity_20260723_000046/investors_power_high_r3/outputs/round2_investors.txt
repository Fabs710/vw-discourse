We are aligned with the diagnosis that this cannot end in a cosmetic settlement. The company needs a durable reset of the cost base and a credible path back to competitive margins. On that point, we agree with management, the family holding, and the state government.

Where we disagree is on the assumption that “no closures, no compulsory redundancies” can be treated as an absolute if it blocks the economics. It cannot. If those red lines prevent meaningful structural improvement, then they are not a responsible protection of the company — they are a veto on competitiveness.

### Our position, clearly

#### What we support
- A restructuring that materially improves margins and cash generation
- Durable, not temporary, cost reduction
- Meaningful capacity rationalisation
- A negotiated labour contribution if it is substantial and enforceable
- Investment and product commitments that support the turnaround
- Deal certainty, because prolonged conflict destroys value

#### What we do not support
- A settlement that preserves too much cost and capacity
- A purely symbolic compromise
- Any outcome that leaves the company structurally uncompetitive
- Prolonged strike-driven stalemate
- A package where labour gives concessions but the cost base remains impaired

### To the works council and union
We respect your insistence that employees should not carry the full burden alone. That is correct. A one-sided burden shift is unstable and value-destructive.

But your “no closures, no compulsory redundancies” line is only acceptable if you can show an alternative package that delivers **equivalent savings with equal durability and certainty**. So far, we have not seen that. Working-time changes, voluntary exits, and temporary moderation may help, but if they do not materially reduce the structural cost base, they do not solve the problem.

We are not asking for the destruction of sites for its own sake. We are saying the economics must be fixed. If that can be done without closures, fine. If it cannot, then closures remain a legitimate tool.

### To management
We agree that the current structure is too expensive. But “closures may be necessary” is not enough by itself. Investors need quantified, credible evidence:
- how much cost comes out,
- by which measures,
- on what timeline,
- and how that translates into return-on-sales improvement.

If management cannot show that a non-closure package is truly equivalent, then it should say so plainly. If it can, then we would support it. Ambiguity is what we will not accept.

### To the state government
We understand the political and regional sensitivity. But the market will not reward governance comfort if the result is an uncompetitive company. A settlement that protects every site in the short term but weakens the business long term will be priced as a failure.

We can support job and site protection only if it sits within a package that actually restores competitiveness. Protection without performance is not a shareholder-friendly outcome.

### To the family holding
We agree with you that value cannot be preserved by avoiding hard choices. Where we part company is on the idea that labour’s red lines can be accommodated without clear economic equivalence. If the cost base is not structurally fixed, then long-term value erodes. That is our threshold.

### Our bottom line
We are prepared to back a tough, negotiated restructuring — including closures if necessary — provided it is:
- economically credible,
- durable,
- and capable of restoring competitive margins.

We will not back:
- a weak compromise,
- a deal that merely postpones the adjustment,
- or a settlement that leaves the company structurally impaired.

If the other parties want our support, bring a package with numbers, durability, and deal certainty. If not, we will treat that as a failure to protect equity value.
