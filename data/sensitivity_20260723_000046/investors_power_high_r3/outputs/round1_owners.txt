As the controlling family holding, I want to be very clear: **we will not back a hollow compromise that preserves every promise but leaves the company structurally uncompetitive**. That would erode the long-term value of the business, and we are not prepared to accept that.

At the same time, I do **not** want this to become a destructive public confrontation that damages the brand, the workforce, and the enterprise itself. Our interest is durable competitiveness, not symbolic victory.

### Where I agree
I agree with management and the institutional investors on the core diagnosis:
- the cost base is too high,
- overcapacity is real,
- and the company needs a durable return to margin and cash generation.

I also agree with the works council, union, and state government on one important point:
- a one-sided burden shift onto employees is not a stable solution.

A restructuring built only on threats and labour concessions would be brittle, politically explosive, and likely to fail over time.

### Where I disagree
I cannot accept the idea that **“no closures, no compulsory redundancies”** is a binding starting point if it prevents a real restructuring. If those red lines make it impossible to achieve the necessary savings, then they are not a solution; they are a veto on competitiveness.

I also cannot accept the opposite extreme:
- plant closures as the default answer,
- irreversible destruction of capacity without exhausting alternatives,
- or a restructuring that permanently weakens the company’s industrial base.

The family holding’s position is simple: **the outcome must restore competitiveness and preserve the enterprise’s long-term value**. We are not committed to any single mechanism. We are committed to the result.

### What we can support
We can support a package that delivers:
- durable cost reduction,
- meaningful capacity adjustment,
- labour cost relief,
- investment in future products and technologies,
- and labour peace for a meaningful period.

That can include a mix of:
- working-time adjustments,
- voluntary departures,
- site-specific productivity agreements,
- temporary or phased wage moderation,
- targeted capacity reductions,
- and, if absolutely necessary, closures of genuinely redundant capacity.

But any such solution must be economically real, not cosmetic.

### What we need from labour
If the workforce wants site and employment security, then it must be prepared to contribute in a way that is:
- substantial,
- durable,
- and tied to measurable restructuring outcomes.

Not slogans, not symbolic pauses, not short-lived concessions. Real contribution for real security.

### What we need from management
Management must show that it is not simply outsourcing its strategic failures to the workforce. If it wants our support for tough measures, it must present:
- a credible industrial plan,
- clear numbers,
- and a restructuring that can survive through 2030.

We will not support a plan that is just a labour confrontation in different clothing.

### To the works council, union, and state government
I respect the concern for sites, jobs, and regional stability. But protecting every current structure cannot be the governing principle if it undermines the company’s future.

If alternatives to closures can deliver the same durable economic effect, then they should absolutely be used. But if they cannot, then the enterprise must not be held hostage by absolutist positions.

### Bottom line
Our threshold is straightforward:
- **yes** to a credible restructuring that restores competitiveness;
- **no** to a weak compromise that delays the problem;
- **no** to value-destructive instability;
- **no** to permanent impairment of the company’s long-term earnings power.

We are open to a negotiated solution, but only if it is tough enough to work.
