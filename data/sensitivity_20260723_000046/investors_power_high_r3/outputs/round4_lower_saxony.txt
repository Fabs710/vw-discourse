## First: to management, investors, and the family holding
I take the expert’s point seriously: a non-closure package will often not match closure economics **one-for-one**. That is exactly why I have been insisting that any restructuring must be tested honestly, not ideologically.

But I do **not** accept the leap from “not perfectly equivalent” to “therefore closures must remain on the table as the default.”  
That is too blunt for a company of this importance, and too risky for the region.

What the expert has shown is:

- non-closure packages can deliver **material** savings,
- they can sometimes get **close** to closure economics,
- and they only fail if management refuses to go deep enough on capacity, overhead, and labor-cost restructuring.

That means the question is not “closure or nothing.”  
The question is whether management is prepared to design a package that is deep enough to preserve competitiveness **without destroying sites and jobs in the state**.

That is the standard I will apply.

## Where I agree with the expert
I agree that:
- closure removes fixed costs more cleanly,
- capacity rationalization matters,
- a non-closure deal must be durable, not cosmetic,
- and temporary concessions are not enough.

So yes, I accept that a serious non-closure package must be **hard**, not symbolic.

## Where I disagree with the management side
I do **not** accept your framing that closures must remain “legitimate tools” in the current round as a live bargaining threat.

Why not?

Because in this governance structure, that is not just a technical restructuring lever. It is a weapon with regional consequences:
- it destabilizes labour relations,
- it undermines trust,
- it puts the state’s veto role under direct pressure,
- and it threatens the company’s role as an industrial anchor in the region.

If management wants my support, then bring a package that actually closes the economics **without closures** first.  
Only if you can show, with hard numbers and independently verified assumptions, that this cannot be done would I even consider discussing more disruptive steps. And even then, I would not do so lightly.

## To the works council and union
I agree with you on the core line: **no German plant closures and no compulsory redundancies**.

That remains my position.

But I also want to be honest with you: if we want that line to hold, then the alternative package has to be strong enough to withstand scrutiny from management and capital. The expert’s assessment confirms that this will require more than slogans.

So I am asking you to engage constructively on the **content** of a non-closure package:
- how much voluntary reduction is feasible,
- what working-time changes can be sustained,
- what wage moderation is temporary and what is durable,
- how much product reallocation can be done,
- which sites can absorb more work and which need relief,
- and how much internal redeployment can realistically occur.

I am **not** asking you to concede the red lines.  
I am asking you to help produce a package that can actually hold.

## To the state government
I fully support your insistence that closures and forced redundancies would damage the regional economy.

The expert’s analysis gives us a practical implication: if non-closure can deliver a large share of the savings, then the political and social case for keeping sites open is much stronger than management wants to admit.

So I expect the state’s role to be active, not merely declaratory:
- support mediation,
- support training and qualification,
- support redeployment instruments,
- and use your board position to insist on a serious non-closure benchmark before anyone talks about plant shutdowns.

## To investors
You are right that “no closure” by itself is not enough if the economics remain broken.  
But you are wrong if you imply that the only credible response is to keep closure pressure alive from the start.

The expert’s range is important here:
- a strong non-closure package may capture **20% to 50%**, and in favorable cases more;
- if management truly goes deep on capacity, overhead, and labor-cost reset, the gap to closure can shrink substantially.

So I am not signing up to a weak compromise.  
I am signing up to a **measurable restructuring test** that starts from preserving jobs and sites, and only moves further if the numbers genuinely fail.

## What I would support
I can support a package built around:
- no German plant closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- binding job and site security,
- voluntary exits and early retirement,
- internal redeployment and qualification,
- working-time flexibility,
- temporary labour-cost moderation,
- productivity and overhead reductions,
- and investment commitments for future products.

But I will not support a package that:
- uses “equivalence” as a euphemism for closure pressure,
- sidelines co-determination,
- or treats regional employment as collateral damage.

## My specific challenge to management
If you want me to move, then answer these questions with numbers:

1. What annual fixed-cost reduction do you need?
2. How much of that can be achieved without closure?
3. What capacity reduction is required in units, shifts, or lines?
4. What labor contribution is truly necessary?
5. What independent verification will you accept?

Until that is on the table, “closures remain on the table” is just a threat, not a plan.

My position remains unchanged:

- **No German plant closures**
- **No compulsory redundancies**
- **Preserve co-determination and the state’s veto role**
- **Use a serious non-closure restructuring test first**
- **Accept deeper capacity and cost reductions if they can be achieved without destroying sites and jobs**

If needed, I can next propose the structure of a state-backed non-closure test package with measurable milestones and independent audit.
