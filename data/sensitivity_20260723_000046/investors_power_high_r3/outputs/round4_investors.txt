We agree with the state government and the expert on one important point: **a non-closure package can be material, but it is not automatically equivalent to a real plant closure**. That distinction matters. From our side, the only relevant question is whether the final package **actually restores competitive margins, reduces fixed cost durably, and removes enough capacity to fix the economics**.

## Where we agree
- **Restructuring is necessary.**
- **A cosmetic compromise would destroy value.**
- **Labour should contribute.**
- **Industrial conflict is value-destructive.**
- **A non-closure package can be viable if it is deep enough.**

## Where we disagree

### 1) “No closures” cannot be an absolute
To the works council and union: we respect the social and political logic of your position, but we cannot endorse an outcome that makes closures impossible **even if the economics fail**.

That is not a serious turnaround framework. It turns a restructuring into a protected-status arrangement, and that leaves shareholders with structurally impaired margins and a cost base that can remain uncompetitive.

The expert was clear: a non-closure package often captures only part of the savings unless it removes a lot of permanent fixed cost, capacity, and overhead. That means the benchmark cannot be “no closures, full stop.” The benchmark must be **competitive unit economics**.

### 2) A guarantee without economic depth is not value-preserving
To the regional state government: we appreciate your insistence on avoiding social damage, but we do not accept the idea that political stability alone is enough.

If a non-closure package does not deliver:
- durable headcount reduction,
- real capacity rationalization,
- structural labor-cost relief,
- overhead simplification,
- and clear margin improvement,

then it is not a solution. It is delay.

### 3) “Binding through 2030” is only credible if the economics survive
To labour: your demand for binding site and job security is understandable, but we will not support a promise that freezes the business model while hoping competitiveness appears later.

If you want no closures and no compulsory redundancies, then the package must be strong enough to show:
- permanently lower fixed costs,
- reduced capacity,
- and a credible ROS path back to target.

Anything less would be a transfer of business risk onto capital.

## What the expert changes
The expert’s assessment is useful because it clarifies the real test:

A non-closure package may achieve **20% to 50%** of closure savings, sometimes more — but usually not full equivalence unless it includes:
- large permanent headcount cuts,
- strong labor-cost reset,
- actual capacity removal,
- product concentration,
- and capex discipline.

That tells us two things:
1. **A non-closure path is possible.**
2. **It must be much deeper than a morale-saving compromise.**

So we are not rejecting non-closure out of ideology. We are saying the non-closure package must **stand on the same economic footing** as closure, or close enough to make a real difference.

## What would move us
We would support a non-closure settlement if it includes:
- quantified annual fixed-cost reduction,
- a verified capacity reduction in units/shifts/lines,
- meaningful permanent labor-cost relief,
- real overhead cuts,
- and independent audit of delivery against milestones.

If those conditions are met, we can support avoiding closures. If they are not, then no-closure language is just a slogan, and we would not back it.

## Direct response to management
You are right that the cost gap matters more than the optics. But you still owe the board and shareholders a specific answer:
- what is the savings target,
- what exactly is delivered without closures,
- what is the timing,
- and what happens if the plan underdelivers?

If you cannot quantify that, then “closures remain on the table” is not a strategy — it is an admission that the alternative may not be enough.

## Direct response to the works council and union
We hear your refusal of closures and compulsory redundancies. We are not dismissing it. But if you want us to accept that red line, you need to show a package that is more than protection of the status quo.

We are open to:
- voluntary exits,
- early retirement,
- redeployment,
- working-time changes,
- temporary wage moderation,
- bonus moderation,
- and productivity gains.

But these must be deep enough to alter the cost base in a durable way. We will not support a settlement that preserves every site and still leaves the company structurally impaired.

## Direct response to the state government
You are right to emphasize regional stability. We agree that destructive conflict would be bad for all sides.

But we would ask you to be as serious about competitiveness as you are about employment protection. That means supporting a test that is:
- measurable,
- independently verified,
- and able to conclude whether a non-closure package is genuinely sufficient.

If it is sufficient, fine. If not, then you cannot ask capital to pretend otherwise.

## Our position, clearly
We are **not** demanding closures for their own sake.  
We are demanding a restructuring that **actually restores competitiveness**.

That means:
- no cosmetic deal,
- no weak compromise,
- no permanent cost impairment,
- and no settlement that leaves margins structurally below target.

If a deep non-closure package can truly achieve the numbers, we can support it.

If it cannot, then the company must retain the ability to pursue stronger measures, including closures.
