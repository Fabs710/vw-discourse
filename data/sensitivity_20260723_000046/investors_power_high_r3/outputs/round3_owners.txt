I will be direct.

We agree on the diagnosis: **the current cost structure is not sustainable, and a cosmetic compromise would destroy value.** The company needs a durable restructuring that restores margins and protects long-term competitiveness.

Where I part company with several of you is on the idea that **“no closures” and “no compulsory redundancies” can stand as absolutes regardless of economics**. They cannot. But equally, I will not support a destructive approach that triggers a prolonged labour war, damages the brand, and erodes the industrial base we are trying to preserve.

## Where I agree

### With management and investors
You are right that:
- the cost base is too high,
- overcapacity is real,
- temporary relief is not enough,
- and the solution must be durable through 2030.

I also agree that the company cannot simply preserve every current structure and hope for a turnaround.

### With the works council, union, and state government
You are right that:
- labour cannot carry the whole burden,
- a one-sided burden shift would be unstable,
- and prolonged conflict would be value-destructive.

I also agree that any restructuring must preserve implementation capacity and legitimacy. A deal that looks “efficient” but detonates labour peace may prove false economy.

## Where I disagree

### 1) I do not accept closures as the default answer
Management keeps saying closures may be necessary if no equivalent savings are found. That is a valid statement in principle, but it is not yet a conclusion. I do **not** support treating closures as the starting point.

The burden of proof is on management to show:
- the exact cost gap,
- the capacity gap,
- what alternatives were tested,
- and why those alternatives are insufficient.

Until that is shown clearly, closures remain a last resort, not a first reflex.

### 2) I do not accept a weak “no closure” promise that leaves the economics unchanged
To labour and the state: I hear your insistence on no closures and no forced layoffs. I understand why. But I will not support a package that merely preserves the status quo under a different label.

If plant security is to be preserved, then the alternative must deliver:
- real and durable cost reduction,
- real capacity rationalisation,
- real productivity gains,
- and measurable margin improvement.

If it does not, then it is not a solution; it is postponement.

### 3) I do not accept compulsory redundancies as the easy path
Forced layoffs would be a serious mistake unless there is no credible alternative. They would damage trust, invite conflict, and likely destroy more value than they create. I am therefore not inclined to back them as a routine restructuring instrument.

That said, I will not pre-commit to a blanket prohibition if the economics collapse and every serious alternative fails. Governance cannot be reduced to slogans.

## What I would support
I would support a package built around the following principles:

- **Meaningful structural cost reduction**
- **Capacity reduction that is economically real**
- **A substantial labour contribution**
- **Voluntary departures, redeployment, early retirement, and working-time flexibility**
- **Investment commitments for future products and technology**
- **A durable peace through 2030**
- **No unnecessary destruction of sites or industrial capability**

That is the right test: not whether a proposal is comfortable, but whether it restores competitiveness without permanently impairing the enterprise.

## On the mediator’s bridge proposals

### Proposal 1: “No forced layoffs, conditional site review”
I would not support a moratorium that simply postpones the same fight. A review must be tied to hard, agreed economic criteria and a credible alternative package. Otherwise it becomes delay, not solution.

### Proposal 2: “Employment security package with measurable offsetting savings”
This is closer to the mark. But the sequencing matters less to me than the substance.

If labour wants site and job security, then the package must produce:
- quantified savings,
- enforceable commitments,
- and a durable effect on margins.

If that happens, I can support avoiding closures.

### Proposal 3: “Capacity reduction without closure as first resort”
This is the most promising path, provided it is not just internal reshuffling. It has to genuinely reduce the fixed-cost base and excess capacity. If it does, it deserves serious support.

## My view of the parties

### To the works council
You are right to defend the workforce. But I will be frank: **“no closures, no redundancies” is not enough unless you can show an equivalent economic result.** Protecting every site in all circumstances is not a governance strategy.

### To the union
I acknowledge your openness to wage moderation, flexibility, and voluntary instruments. That is constructive. But those measures must be substantial and durable. Temporary gestures will not restore competitiveness.

### To management
Stop speaking in abstractions. If you want support for difficult measures, bring the numbers:
- how much savings are required,
- which measures deliver them,
- what has already been tested,
- and what the threshold is for saying an alternative is truly sufficient.

### To the state government
I respect your role and your concern for regional stability. But stability cannot become a shield for preserving an uncompetitive structure. Help produce a package that is both socially and economically credible.

### To investors
You are right that a weak compromise destroys value. I agree. But do not confuse toughness with value creation. A deal that causes long industrial conflict can be just as destructive as an overly soft one.

## My bottom line
The controlling family holding will support **a credible, durable restructuring that restores margins and competitiveness**.

We will **not** support:
- a cosmetic compromise,
- a settlement that simply delays the problem,
- or a destructive conflict that permanently damages the enterprise.

If the employee side can bring a package that is truly equivalent in economic effect to closures, then I am prepared to back avoiding closures.

If not, then management must be ready to make the hard case with evidence, not rhetoric.

I do **not** want this company weakened by hesitation. But I also do **not** want it damaged by blunt force. The solution must protect long-term value, not merely satisfy short-term pressure.
