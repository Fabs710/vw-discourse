We agree with the core diagnosis: this is a **structural competitiveness problem**, not a temporary soft patch. That matters because, from a shareholder perspective, a cosmetic compromise that leaves the German cost base uncompetitive would be value-destructive. We would not back that.

## Where we agree
We agree with management, the family holding, and the expert that:
- the company needs **durable** cost and capacity reduction,
- the restructuring must be **quantified**,
- and the end state must restore a credible path to **competitive margins and return on sales**.

We also agree with the employee side and the state on one important point: the workforce should **not** bear the burden alone. Management and owners should contribute materially. A one-sided solution is not credible and will not hold.

## Where we disagree
### 1) A blanket no-closures position is not acceptable
We do **not** support an absolute ban on German plant closures.

If the site-by-site economics show that a plant is structurally uneconomic, then refusing to close it can be more value-destructive than closing it. We do not want closures, but we will not endorse a framework that rules them out in advance and preserves overcapacity by default.

### 2) A categorical ban on compulsory redundancies is also too rigid
We prefer voluntary exits, redeployment, qualification, and working-time adjustment. But management must retain a credible fallback if the savings gap cannot be closed otherwise. If all hard options are removed before the economics are proven, the market will read the plan as incomplete.

### 3) Guarantees only work inside a credible economic package
We do not support binding job and site guarantees through 2030 if they freeze an uncompetitive structure. Guarantees are valuable only if they sit on top of a package that actually delivers:
- real capacity rationalisation,
- durable fixed-cost reduction,
- and a visible path back to target margins.

## On the “non-closure is possible in principle” point
The expert’s assessment is reasonable: a non-closure restructuring is possible in principle, but whether it is sufficient depends on the actual gap. That is exactly right.

What we take from that is:
- **no-closure is not proven sufficient**
- **closures are not yet proven necessary**
- the right test is a **site-by-site, quantified comparison**

So we support an evidence-based process, not a political declaration.

## What would move us
We would back a non-closure deal if it is:
- quantified,
- durable,
- execution-credible,
- and clearly closes the economics gap.

Specifically, we want to see:
- site-by-site cost structures,
- utilization and overcapacity data,
- savings by measure,
- the residual gap after internal measures,
- management and owner burden-sharing,
- and a timeline to restore margins.

If that analysis shows that internal measures and negotiated contributions are enough, fine — closures may be avoidable. But if it does **not** close the gap, then the company must retain tougher options.

## To the works council and union
We respect the insistence on social peace, but the current position is too absolute if it means the company cannot make the structural changes needed to compete.

A non-closure solution is welcome if it is real. But “real” means:
- material labor-cost relief,
- meaningful and durable capacity reduction,
- productivity gains,
- and no cosmetic rearrangement of the old cost base.

If you want us to support a no-closure path, it has to restore margins, not just preserve employment numbers.

## To management
You are directionally right, but you still need to be more concrete. “Overcapacity” and “structural gap” are not enough on their own.

Bring us:
1. a quantified non-closure scenario,
2. the residual gap if closures are excluded,
3. the savings from each measure,
4. the timing of those savings,
5. and the burden-sharing from management and owners.

If you cannot show that the no-closure route works, then closure options should remain available. If you can show it, then we are open to it.

## To the regional state government
We understand your political constraints, but a veto against all hard measures is not a business model. If the company remains structurally weak, the regional base and the jobs within it are weaker in the long run.

Support the audit, support burden-sharing, and support a deal that is economically credible. But do not mistake blocking power for strategy.

## To the controlling family
You are right that cosmetic restructuring would destroy value. But the same is true of a compromise that is politically comfortable and economically inadequate.

We support stewardship that restores competitiveness, not just preserves appearances. That requires hard numbers and real structural change.

## Bottom line
Our position is unchanged:
- We will **not** support a cosmetic settlement.
- We will **not** support a package that leaves the cost base structurally uncompetitive.
- We will **support** a credible restructuring that restores margins, reduces fixed costs durably, and is backed by real burden-sharing.

So yes, test the non-closure path. But test it honestly, against the economics. If it works, good. If it does not, then the company cannot rule out tougher measures just because they are unpopular.
