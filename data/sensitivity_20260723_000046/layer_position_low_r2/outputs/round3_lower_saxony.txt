I will be direct.

The state government shares the diagnosis that this company needs a durable restructuring. We do **not** deny the competitiveness problem, and we do **not** want a cosmetic settlement that merely delays the next crisis.

But I **do** disagree with management, parts of the investor side, and the family holding on the central remedy. A restructuring that relies on **German plant closures** or **compulsory redundancies** is not acceptable to us. That is not a starting point for negotiation; it is a line we will not cross.

## Where I agree
I agree with:
- the need for structural change,
- the need for real savings, not symbolism,
- the need for burden-sharing,
- the need for investment in future products,
- and the expert’s point that the question must be tested with numbers, not slogans.

I also agree that the workforce cannot simply be asked to absorb everything. Management, owners, and the company itself must contribute.

## Where I disagree
### On closures
The expert is right that non-closure restructuring is possible in principle. That matters. It means closures are **not automatically necessary**.

Management has not yet proven that closing German plants is the only credible route. “Overcapacity” is not a sufficient argument by itself. It may justify capacity reduction, yes. But capacity reduction can happen **within the existing network** through:
- product allocation changes,
- shift reductions,
- working-time flexibility,
- voluntary exits,
- redeployment,
- qualification,
- and temporary cost contributions.

If those tools are not enough, that still does **not** give management a blank cheque to close plants without first exhausting real alternatives.

### On compulsory redundancies
I also reject the idea that forced layoffs must remain a normal fallback. Once compulsory redundancies are treated as routine, the trust base of co-determination is damaged. That is not a minor labour-relations issue; it is a strategic risk for the company’s long-term stability.

### On 2030 guarantees
The employee side is right to insist that guarantees matter. But I want to be precise: guarantees are not a reward for the workforce; they are a necessary condition for any fair restructuring package.

If the company wants temporary contributions from employees, then it must provide real security in return:
- no German plant closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- preserved co-determination,
- and a stable job-security framework.

Without that, “concessions” become one-sided pressure.

## What I take from the expert assessment
The expert has made an important point: the real issue is whether the verified savings gap can be closed through internal measures and burden-sharing.

That is exactly why I am not prepared to accept closure talk as the default answer. The burden of proof is still on those who want irreversible damage to sites and jobs.

The expert also says a no-closure deal may be insufficient if the gap is very large. Fine. Then show that clearly, with site-by-site evidence. Until then, closures are a political preference, not a proven necessity.

## What we will support
The state government is prepared to support a **tough negotiated restructuring** if it contains all of the following:
- binding no-closure commitment for German sites,
- binding no-compulsory-redundancy commitment,
- substantial capacity reduction inside the existing network,
- working-time flexibility,
- voluntary exits,
- redeployment and qualification,
- temporary and fair employee contributions,
- management and owner contributions,
- and investment commitments tied to the deal.

That is a serious package. It is not soft. It is not a denial of reality. It is a way to restore competitiveness **without hollowing out the company’s regional foundation**.

## To management
You need to stop presenting closures as if they were the only adult option.

If you want our support, bring:
1. a **quantified non-closure scenario**,
2. a **site-by-site cost and capacity picture**,
3. a transparent comparison of savings by measure,
4. and a clear burden-sharing proposal from management and owners.

If that audit shows a gap that truly cannot be closed otherwise, then we can discuss what that means. But you are **not** there yet.

## To the works council and union
We support your core red lines:
- no closures,
- no forced redundancies,
- protection of co-determination,
- and no one-sided sacrifice by the workforce.

At the same time, I am being honest: if we want to defend sites and jobs politically and in governance, then a meaningful employee contribution will likely be necessary. That contribution must be temporary, fair, and tied to binding security.

## To the family holding and investors
You are right that cosmetic restructuring is not enough. But you are wrong if you assume that the only credible path is to keep closures and layoffs as a standing threat.

A company like this destroys value if it destroys trust. A durable solution needs:
- industrial peace,
- skill retention,
- stable labour relations,
- and a viable footprint in Germany.

That is what long-term value looks like.

## My bottom line
The regional state government will **not** back a settlement based on German plant closures or compulsory redundancies.

We are ready to back a serious restructuring that:
- preserves sites,
- protects employment,
- restores competitiveness,
- and keeps the company anchored in the region.

If management wants to test whether the no-closure path is sufficient, then let’s do it properly:
[SUMMON: independent site-by-site restructuring audit with plant economics, capacity utilization, savings scenarios, and a closure-versus-non-closure comparison]

Until then, I do not accept that closures are necessary. I accept only that they are being demanded. That is not the same thing.
