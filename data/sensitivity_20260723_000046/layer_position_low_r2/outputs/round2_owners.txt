I will be direct.

I agree with everyone on one central point: **the company has a structural competitiveness problem, and a cosmetic response would destroy value**. That is exactly why I am not prepared to back a weak compromise that preserves the old cost structure under a new label.

But I do **not** share the employee side’s categorical view that plant closures and compulsory redundancies are off the table as a matter of principle. If the economics require structural capacity reduction, then pretending otherwise would be irresponsible stewardship — not only toward the business, but also toward the workforce in the medium term.

## Where I agree
- Management is right that this is structural, not cyclical.
- The company needs durable savings, not temporary optics.
- Employees should not bear the burden alone.
- A negotiated solution is preferable to a prolonged conflict.
- Investment in future products must remain protected.

## Where I disagree

### 1) “No closures” as a principle
I do **not** accept an absolute ban on German plant closures.

If there is real overcapacity, then some footprint adjustment may be necessary. Keeping every site open regardless of economics would mean preserving value destruction. That is not acceptable for a long-term owner.

That said, I am **not** saying closures should be the first move. They are a last resort if other measures cannot close the gap. But they cannot be ruled out in advance.

### 2) “No compulsory redundancies” as an absolute
I do **not** accept a categorical ban on compulsory redundancies either.

I prefer voluntary instruments, redeployment, qualification, and working-time flexibility. But if those tools do not deliver the required structural savings, then management must retain a fallback. Removing every hard option before the numbers are proven would leave the company without execution capacity.

### 3) Guarantees through 2030 before the economics are fixed
I also do not accept binding guarantees that freeze the current structure while the company remains uncompetitive.

Guarantees have to be part of a credible restructuring, not a substitute for it. If the company cannot restore margins, then promises of permanent stability are just deferred damage.

## What I would support
I am prepared to support a package that delivers:
- real and durable capacity reduction,
- meaningful cost savings,
- productivity improvement,
- labor-cost contributions that are material, not symbolic,
- and investment commitments tied to execution.

I would also support a solution that avoids closures **if** it genuinely closes the economics gap. But that has to be proven, not asserted.

## To management
You need to be more specific than “overcapacity” and “structural gap.” Put the numbers on the table:
- what savings are required,
- what each measure delivers,
- what remains if closures are excluded,
- and why alternative measures are or are not sufficient.

If you can show a credible path without closures, I will support that. If you cannot, then you should not be surprised if more difficult measures become necessary.

## To the works council and union
I respect the insistence on social peace and the protection of jobs. It matters. But you are overreaching if you treat every closure or redundancy as illegitimate from the outset.

That position may be politically understandable, but it is not a solution to structural overcapacity. A company that refuses all hard adjustments may preserve comfort for a while, but it will erode its future.

## To the regional state government
I understand your blocking position and your responsibility for regional employment. But a veto cannot be used to force an uneconomic status quo. If the business remains structurally weak, the long-term damage to the regional base will be worse.

A responsible anchor shareholder should help shape a viable restructuring, not simply block the hard decisions.

## Bottom line
The family holding supports a restructuring that restores competitiveness and preserves long-term industrial capability.

We will **not** support a settlement that is merely designed to avoid conflict while leaving the company overstaffed, overpriced, and unable to invest.

So my position is simple:
- show us a **credible, quantified, durable** restructuring,
- include **real burden-sharing** from all sides,
- and prove that the cost gap can be closed.

If you can do that without closures, fine. If you cannot, then the company must keep the option of tougher measures open.
