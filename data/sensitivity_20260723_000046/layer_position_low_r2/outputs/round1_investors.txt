We are aligned on one essential point: **the current cost base and capacity structure are not acceptable if the company is to restore margins and fund future investment**.

That said, I need to be equally clear about what will and will not support value creation from a capital-markets perspective.

### Where we agree
- Management is right that this is **not** just a cyclical problem.
- The company needs a **durable** restructuring, not a cosmetic one.
- Employees should not be expected to absorb **all** of the burden.
- A negotiated settlement is preferable to a prolonged, value-destroying conflict.

### Where we disagree
I do **not** accept an outcome that preserves all German sites and job structures if that leaves the company with an uncompetitive cost base. If the result is simply:
- no closures,
- no hard headcount reduction,
- and only temporary or symbolic concessions,

then that is not a restructuring. It is postponement. From where we sit, that would be unacceptable because it would keep margins structurally impaired and undermine returns.

I also disagree with the idea that plant closures are automatically “value destruction.” If a site is genuinely uneconomic and overcapacity is real, then avoiding all closure decisions can destroy more value over time by keeping fixed costs too high. The test is not whether a measure feels painful; it is whether it credibly restores competitiveness.

### To management
Your diagnosis sounds credible, but your case will only hold if you can show:
- a **quantified** path back to competitive margins,
- durable fixed-cost reduction,
- real capacity rationalisation,
- and execution certainty.

If you cannot demonstrate that, then the market will treat this as another round of delay, and that will continue to weigh on valuation.

### To the works council and union
I understand the refusal of cosmetic burden-sharing, but your current red lines are too rigid if they imply that no site can close and no compulsory redundancies can ever occur. That is not compatible with restoring competitiveness if the overcapacity is structural.

I would support alternatives to closures **only if** they genuinely deliver equivalent economics:
- substantial and durable capacity reductions,
- meaningful labour-cost relief,
- credible productivity gains,
- and no hidden reversion to the old cost structure.

If those conditions are not met, then “no closures” becomes a demand to preserve value destruction, not employment.

### To the regional state government
Your concern about regional employment is understandable, but a blocked restructuring that leaves the business weak is not a long-term answer for any stakeholder, including the region. Protecting every site regardless of economics may preserve headlines, but it can also prolong underperformance and eventually force a worse outcome.

### To the controlling family
You are right that a weak compromise would be unacceptable. But I would add: a plan that relies on political manageability while leaving the economics unresolved is also unacceptable. Ownership control does not substitute for a credible industrial solution.

### Our bottom line
We will support **tough measures** if they are:
- clearly necessary,
- durable,
- economically credible,
- and likely to restore competitive margins in a reasonable timeframe.

We will **not** support a settlement that is mainly designed to avoid conflict while leaving the company overcosted and underperforming.

If management can present a restructuring with real capacity cuts, real cost reduction, and execution certainty, we are prepared to back it. If not, we will view the outcome as value-destructive and price it accordingly.
