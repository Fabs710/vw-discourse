I’ll be equally direct.

## Where I agree
I agree with everyone on one thing: this company needs a **durable** restructuring, not a cosmetic patch. I also agree that we should not make decisions on slogans alone. The numbers matter.

And I agree with the expert’s core point: **a non-closure restructuring is possible in principle**. I have never denied that. The question is not whether internal measures exist. The question is whether they are **sufficient, durable, and execution-credible** to restore competitiveness.

## Where I disagree — strongly

### 1) “No closures” is not a strategy
The works council and union keep repeating a principle as if repetition makes it economic. It doesn’t.

If internal measures can close the gap, then present the evidence.  
If they cannot, then a categorical no-closure stance is simply a refusal to deal with reality.

I will not accept a settlement that preserves overcapacity and leaves the brand structurally uncompetitive just to avoid difficult decisions.

### 2) “No compulsory redundancies” as an absolute is also not credible
Voluntary exits, redeployment, reskilling, working-time flexibility — all good. We should use them.

But if those measures do not deliver enough, then management must retain a credible fallback. Without that, there is no real restructuring power, only hopes and slogans.

A company with a shrinking European market and a cost base above target cannot be locked into a framework that prevents it from adjusting.

### 3) Binding guarantees before the economics are settled are the wrong sequence
The state government and employee side keep asking for binding site and job guarantees first.

I reject that order.

Guarantees only make sense **after** we have a package that materially reduces cost and capacity. Otherwise we are simply freezing the problem and asking investors, owners, and the business to absorb it indefinitely.

## On the proposed “test phases” and “conditional” models

### Time-limited non-closure test phase
I am open to testing non-closure measures first — but only if the test is real, quantified, and time-bound.

That means:
- a defined period,
- measurable savings targets,
- full transparency on site economics,
- and a clear review point.

What I will not accept is a test phase that becomes a permanent delay while costs continue to outrun the market.

### Conditional no-closure commitment
A conditional no-closure promise sounds attractive, but it is only useful if it actually delivers the needed savings.

If the target is missed, we cannot pretend the economics disappear. The company must have a path to complete restructuring.

### Site-protection with exception procedures
I do **not** support a framework that rules out hard measures forever and then creates a formal exception process later. That is just a slower route back to the same unresolved problem.

If a site is uneconomic after a proper audit and after internal measures have been exhausted, then that reality cannot be legislated away.

## On the audit
I support the independent audit — but it must be honest and complete.

It must test:
- site-by-site fixed costs,
- utilization,
- product allocation,
- labor-cost reduction potential,
- overhead reduction,
- and the savings gap after internal measures.

And it must answer one simple question:  
**Does a non-closure restructuring close the gap, yes or no?**

If yes, fine — that would be a valid basis for a serious agreement.  
If no, then the employee side must stop pretending that no closures is a strategy rather than a preference.

## To the works council
You say capacity reduction can happen entirely inside the current network. That may be true in some areas. It is not yet proven true across the whole footprint.

You also say closures are a political excuse. No — closures are an economic option if the numbers require them. I would prefer not to use them. But I will not rule them out in advance while the company remains structurally overbuilt.

If you want to avoid closures, then bring a package that:
- materially reduces cost,
- materially reduces capacity,
- and restores margin trajectory.

Not just a list of protected principles.

## To the union
You are right that the workforce should not bear the burden alone. I agree. Management, owners, and the company itself must all contribute.

But I reject the implication that any workforce contribution is acceptable only if closures are taken off the table first. That is backwards.

The question is not whether employees contribute. The question is whether the total package is enough to secure the company’s future.

If you want meaningful security, you need meaningful economic adjustment.

## To the regional state government
I respect your political position. I understand the importance of employment, the industrial base, and regional stability.

But a veto is not a restructuring plan.

If the company’s economics do not improve enough, then protecting every site today simply transfers the crisis into the future, with less room to invest and fewer options left. That is not stewardship.

If you want to help secure jobs in the long term, help us get a package that actually restores competitiveness.

## To the controlling family
You are right that cosmetic restructuring destroys value. I agree.

But refusing hard decisions can also destroy value. Value is not preserved by sentiment. It is preserved by a business that can compete, fund its future, and earn its cost of capital.

If the numbers show that structural adjustment requires hard choices, then long-term stewardship means supporting them.

## To institutional investors
You are right to insist on execution credibility. I share that concern.

But credibility is not just about keeping the threat of closures alive. It is about delivering a plan that the business can actually execute and that the workforce can actually live with.

A settlement that looks decisive but triggers prolonged conflict and fails to lower the cost base enough is not value-enhancing. It is delay with extra damage.

## What would move me
I will be very specific.

I would move if the employee side and state can show, with numbers, that a non-closure package can deliver:
- a **durable and substantial cost reduction**,
- a **real reduction in capacity**,
- a **credible path back to target margins**,
- and sufficient flexibility to fund investment to 2030.

If that can be demonstrated, I will take it seriously.

If it cannot, then I will continue to insist that plant closures and compulsory redundancies cannot be taken off the table.

## My bottom line
I am not here to preserve every current structure. I am here to restore competitiveness.

That means:
- lower fixed cost,
- lower overcapacity,
- lower labour cost,
- higher margins,
- and investment capacity for future products and technology.

So I will say it plainly:

- **No cosmetic deal.**
- **No guarantee that freezes an uncompetitive structure.**
- **No exclusion of hard options before the numbers prove they are unnecessary.**

Bring me a quantified non-closure plan that truly closes the gap, and I will evaluate it.  
Bring me a principle without an economics case, and I will reject it.
