As the independent restructuring auditor, my mandate is to assess **whether a non-closure restructuring can close the economic gap** and, if not, what the residual risk and trade-offs are.

## Core factual assessment

### 1) A non-closure solution is possible in principle
That is economically plausible in many automotive restructuring cases if enough of the following are available:
- product reallocation across sites,
- shift reductions and working-time flexibility,
- voluntary exits and natural attrition,
- redeployment and reskilling,
- overhead reduction,
- supply-chain and logistics simplification,
- temporary labour-cost concessions,
- and investment/product commitments that improve utilization.

So the employee side is correct on the narrow point that **closure is not automatically required by definition**.

### 2) But “possible in principle” is not the same as “sufficient in practice”
Whether a no-closure package works depends on four things:
1. **Size of the savings gap**
2. **How much of that gap is structural vs cyclical**
3. **How much capacity can be removed without site closure**
4. **Whether concessions are durable enough to last through the next product cycle**

If the gap is large and structural, internal measures may be insufficient. If the gap is moderate and driven by utilization and overhead, a negotiated non-closure package can work.

### 3) The decisive issue is residual gap after internal measures
From a method standpoint, the proper test is:
- baseline site economics,
- quantify overcapacity by plant,
- model savings from each internal measure,
- subtract one-time implementation costs,
- and then identify the **residual gap**.

Only if the residual gap remains material after realistic internal measures does closure or major footprint change become economically relevant.

## On the stakeholder positions

### Works council / union / state
Their position is internally coherent if the goal is to preserve the domestic industrial base:
- no closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- preserve co-determination,
- seek burden-sharing,
- insist on independent verification.

The weak point is that this position only remains credible if the internal-measures package truly closes the gap. If not, it can become a **delay strategy** rather than a restructuring solution.

### Management / family / investors
Their position is also coherent from an economic-risk perspective:
- keep hard options available,
- require quantified savings,
- avoid freezing an uncompetitive structure,
- insist on execution credibility.

The weak point is sequencing: if they demand all hard options remain open before proving the no-closure gap, employees will reasonably view that as leverage rather than neutral analysis.

## What an independent audit would need
To determine whether non-closure restructuring is sufficient, I would require at minimum:

- plant-level P&Ls or equivalent cost accounts
- capacity utilization by line and model
- fixed vs variable cost split
- transfer pricing and overhead allocation methodology
- labor cost per vehicle by site
- logistics cost by site and lane
- product allocation and future volume assumptions
- capex commitments by platform/site
- one-time restructuring cost estimates
- sensitivity cases for volume, mix, and FX
- labor-relations constraints and implementation timing

## Method-based conclusion
At this stage, without the underlying numbers, the only defensible conclusion is:

- **A no-closure restructuring is plausible but unproven.**
- **Closures are not yet proven necessary.**
- **A site-by-site audit is the correct next step.**
- **Any settlement that ignores residual gap analysis is high-risk.**

## Practical interpretation
If the audit shows that internal measures close the gap with acceptable timing and durability, then a non-closure package is economically defensible.

If the audit shows a significant residual gap after realistic internal measures, then the company will need harder structural measures somewhere in the network. That could mean:
- site consolidation,
- partial footprint reduction,
- or, in the most severe case, closure.

I cannot responsibly pre-judge which of those is necessary without the plant economics.
