I will respond directly and without ambiguity.

## Where I agree
I agree with the works council on the core principles:
- **no German plant closures**
- **no compulsory redundancies**
- **no one-sided burden on the workforce**
- **binding security, not vague promises**
- **restructuring must be durable and economically credible**

I also agree with management, the family, and investors on one point: this cannot be a cosmetic exercise. The company has a structural competitiveness problem. Pretending otherwise would be irresponsible.

## Where I disagree
I do **not** accept the repeated framing that credibility requires keeping closures and forced redundancies “on the table” as a normal fallback.

That is not a solution. It is a threat position.

A restructuring can be credible **without** plant closures and **without** compulsory redundancies, provided management and owners accept real burden-sharing and we work through genuine internal capacity and cost measures first.

## On the latest “staged escalation” idea
I reject any model in which the last stage is “if savings are still missing, then closures or forced layoffs come back into play.”

That is not a compromise ladder. It is a delayed ultimatum.

If the company wants the workforce to contribute, then the direction must be clear from the outset:
- no closures,
- no forced layoffs,
- no dismantling of the collective wage framework.

Anything else simply turns employee concessions into a down payment on job destruction.

## On the independent audit
An independent audit is sensible, and I support it — but only if its mandate is correct.

It must:
- test **non-closure options first**
- quantify savings from internal capacity reduction, working-time changes, redeployment, qualification, and productivity measures
- include management and owner contribution scenarios
- **not** assume closures as the default baseline
- be jointly agreed and transparent

If the audit is used to pre-legitimize closures, we will reject it.

### [SUMMON: Independent industrial restructuring auditor]
We need a neutral industrial economist or restructuring auditor to verify:
- overcapacity assumptions,
- site-by-site cost structures,
- non-closure savings potential,
- and the effect of alternative labor and capacity measures.

That is the only way to turn this from assertion into evidence.

## To management
You keep saying the cost base is structurally too high. Fine. Then show the numbers in a way that proves a non-closure deal cannot work.

Specifically:
- what exact savings are required,
- what internal measures deliver,
- what labor-cost contribution is needed,
- what management and owner contribution is on offer,
- what residual gap remains **after** those steps.

If you cannot show that a non-closure scenario fails, then your closure talk is premature.

And let me be equally clear: the workforce will not accept a settlement where employees give up wages, time, and guarantees while management preserves all strategic freedom and owners preserve all upside.

## To the controlling family
I disagree with the idea that “stewardship” means leaving closures available as a bargaining club.

A long-term owner should understand that durable industrial value depends on:
- trust,
- skill retention,
- social peace,
- and stable collective bargaining.

If you want a durable solution, then back a package that protects:
- all German sites,
- jobs,
- co-determination,
- and the collective wage framework.

If you insist on retaining the option of closures and compulsory redundancies, then you are not asking for a deal; you are asking for unilateral leverage.

## To institutional investors
I hear your concern about capital discipline. But your argument still overstates the need for “execution credibility” through hard rights against the workforce.

Credibility can also come from:
- transparent milestones,
- independently verified savings,
- capacity rationalisation within the existing network,
- temporary employee contributions,
- and binding management/owner concessions.

A plan that devastates labour relations may look decisive on paper and still destroy value in practice.

## To the regional state government
Your blocking position matters, and I support you using it. But support for a deal must not be confused with permissiveness toward indefinite ambiguity.

If management wants workforce concessions, then the state should insist on:
- binding site protection
- binding no-forced-redundancy commitments
- clear investment commitments
- preserved co-determination
- and a fair burden-sharing package from management and owners

You should not be the party that asks employees to contribute while leaving the door open for closures later.

## What the union is prepared to discuss
We remain prepared to discuss a real package of:
- temporary employee contributions
- bonus moderation or suspension
- working-time flexibility
- voluntary exits
- internal redeployment
- reskilling and qualification
- productivity improvements
- capacity reduction **inside the current network**

But I need to stress: this is only meaningful if it is tied to **binding security**.

We are **not** prepared to accept:
- plant closures
- compulsory redundancies
- permanent wage erosion
- or a restructuring that leaves workers carrying the full adjustment burden

## The condition for movement
If management wants movement from us, it must bring a package that includes:
1. **A binding no-closure commitment for all German sites**
2. **A binding no-compulsory-redundancy commitment**
3. **Preservation of the collective wage framework**
4. **A jointly agreed independent audit of non-closure options**
5. **Material contributions from management and owners**
6. **Investment and product commitments through 2030**

Without that, there is no basis for serious wage or flexibility concessions.

## Bottom line
Let me be completely plain:

- The workforce will contribute.
- The workforce will accept change.
- The workforce will not accept being the only side that pays.
- The workforce will not accept closures.
- The workforce will not accept forced layoffs.
- The workforce will not accept the erosion of the collective agreement as the price of “competitiveness.”

If management wants a durable settlement, it must stop treating job destruction as the default answer and start negotiating a real non-closure restructuring.

If it refuses, then we will defend our position with every legitimate means available.
