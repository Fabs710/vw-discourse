We are aligned with the basic diagnosis: this is a **structural competitiveness problem**, not a temporary dip. That matters. A cosmetic settlement that leaves the German cost base uncompetitive would destroy value, and we would not support it.

## Where we agree
We agree with management, the family holding, and the expert on the key point:  
- the company needs **durable** cost and capacity reduction,  
- the restructuring must be **quantified**,  
- and the outcome must restore a credible path to **competitive margins and return on sales**.

We also agree with the employee side and the state on one important principle: the workforce should **not** bear the burden alone. If management and owners ask for sacrifice, they must contribute materially as well.

## Where we disagree
### 1) A blanket “no closures” stance is not acceptable
We do **not** support an absolute ban on plant closures. If a German site is genuinely uneconomic and the overcapacity is structural, then refusing to close it can be more value-destructive than the closure itself.

That said, closures are not the goal. They are a **fallback**. We want the least destructive option that still restores competitiveness. But we will not endorse a package that rules out structural remedies in advance and preserves overcapacity by default.

### 2) A categorical ban on compulsory redundancies is also too rigid
We prefer voluntary exits, redeployment, qualification, and working-time adjustment. But management must retain a credible execution fallback. If all hard options are permanently removed before the savings gap is closed, the market will read the plan as non-binding and incomplete.

### 3) Guarantees are only valuable if they sit inside a credible economic package
We do not support binding job and site guarantees through 2030 if they freeze an uncompetitive structure. Guarantees have value only if the plan actually delivers:
- real capacity rationalisation,
- durable fixed-cost reduction,
- and a visible path back to target margins.

## What the expert said — and what it means
The expert’s assessment is sensible: a non-closure restructuring is **possible in principle**, but whether it is sufficient depends on the actual gap. That is the right framing.

What we take from that:
- “No closures” is **not proven sufficient**
- “Closures are necessary” is also **not yet proven**
- The decision should be driven by a **site-by-site, quantified comparison**

So the next step must be evidence, not repetition of red lines.

## What would move us
We would support a non-closure deal if it is:
- **quantified**
- **durable**
- **execution-credible**
- and clearly closes the economics gap

Specifically, we want to see:
- site-by-site cost structures,
- utilization and overcapacity data,
- savings by measure,
- the residual gap after internal measures,
- management and owner burden-sharing,
- and a timeline to restore margins.

If that analysis shows that internal measures and negotiated contributions are enough, fine — then closures may be avoidable. But if it does **not** close the gap, then the company must retain tougher options.

## To the works council and union
We respect the insistence on social peace, but your current position is too absolute if it means the company cannot make the structural changes needed to compete.

A non-closure solution is welcome if it is real. But “real” means:
- material labor-cost relief,
- meaningful capacity reduction,
- permanent productivity gains,
- and no cosmetic rearrangement of the old cost base.

If you want us to back a no-closure path, then it has to be strong enough to restore margins, not just preserve employment numbers.

## To management
You are directionally right, but you still need to be more concrete. “Overcapacity” and “structural gap” are not enough on their own.

Bring us:
1. a quantified non-closure scenario,
2. the residual gap if closures are excluded,
3. the savings from each measure,
4. the timing of those savings,
5. and the burden-sharing from management and owners.

If you cannot show that the no-closure route works, then closure options should remain available. If you can show it, then we are open to it.

## To the regional state government
We understand your political constraints, but a veto against all hard measures is not a business model. If the company remains structurally weak, the regional base and the jobs within it are weaker in the long run.

Support the audit, support burden-sharing, and support a deal that is economically credible. But do not mistake blocking power for strategy.

## To the controlling family
You are right that cosmetic restructuring would destroy value. But the same is true of a compromise that is politically comfortable and economically inadequate.

We support stewardship that restores competitiveness, not just preserves appearances. That requires hard numbers and real structural change.

## Bottom line
Our position is unchanged:

- We will **not** support a cosmetic settlement.
- We will **not** support a package that leaves the cost base structurally uncompetitive.
- We will **support** a credible restructuring that restores margins, reduces fixed costs durably, and is backed by real burden-sharing.

So yes, test the non-closure path. But test it honestly, against the economics. If it works, great. If it does not, then the company cannot rule out tougher measures just because they are unpopular.
