I’ll be direct.

## Where I agree
I agree with management, the expert, and to a degree the state government on the central diagnosis: **this is a structural competitiveness problem, not a temporary one**. The company cannot keep carrying a cost base and capacity footprint that the market no longer supports.

I also agree that a **cosmetic settlement would destroy value**. If we walk out of this with only a short-lived patch, we will have bought a later crisis at a higher price.

And I agree with the employee side on one important point: **the workforce should not bear the burden alone**. That would be both unjust and economically counterproductive.

## Where I disagree
I do **not** accept the employee side’s attempt to turn “no closures” and “no compulsory redundancies” into absolute principles.

That position may be understandable politically, but it is not enough for a company facing structural overcapacity. If the numbers show that the current footprint cannot be made competitive through internal measures alone, then ruling out hard options in advance is simply refusing the problem.

At the same time, I do **not** accept management’s tone that closures or forced redundancies are effectively the default answer. They may be necessary, but they have not yet been proven necessary. They are a fallback, not a starting ideology.

## On the expert assessment
The expert is right about one thing: **a non-closure restructuring is possible in principle**. That is true.

But “in principle” is not a decision. The question is whether a non-closure package can close the actual gap in this company, in this market, at this cost level, and on a durable basis.

So I take from the expert:
- we should test the non-closure path seriously,
- but we should not pretend it is automatically sufficient.

That is the correct standard.

## On the state government
I understand your political and regional obligations. I also understand why you resist closures and compulsory redundancies.

But I have to be blunt: **a veto is not a restructuring plan**. If the company’s economics do not improve enough, then protecting every current site simply delays the damage and spreads it over a longer period. That is not stewardship.

So I cannot support a position that treats closures as illegitimate before the site-by-site economics have actually been tested.

## On the works council and union
You are right to insist on serious burden-sharing, real guarantees, and negotiated change.

Where I disagree is your insistence that any future discussion of closures or compulsory redundancies must be ruled out categorically. That is too rigid. If we want a durable business, we need the ability to make hard decisions if the evidence demands it.

I also reject the implication that long-term value is protected by freezing the current industrial footprint. Long-term value is protected by restoring competitiveness, even if that requires painful adjustment.

## On management
Management is directionally right, but still too vague.

“You have overcapacity” is not enough. You need to quantify:
- the required savings gap,
- what each measure delivers,
- what remains if closures are excluded,
- and whether a non-closure package is truly sufficient.

If you want support from the owners, then bring a business case, not just a conclusion.

## What I would support
I would support a **structured, evidence-based process** that tests a non-closure solution first, but does not preclude harder measures if the economics do not work.

That means:
- a quantified, independent audit,
- site-by-site cost and capacity data,
- a serious non-closure scenario,
- burden-sharing from all sides,
- and a defined decision point if the gap remains.

[SUMMON: independent industrial restructuring auditor with site-by-site plant economics, capacity utilization, and non-closure versus closure savings comparison]

## What I would not support
I would not support:
- a blanket no-closure commitment before the numbers are proven,
- a blanket ban on compulsory redundancies,
- guarantees that freeze the current footprint regardless of economics,
- or a weak compromise that preserves overcapacity and erodes margins.

## Bottom line
My position is simple:

- **Test a real non-closure restructuring.**
- **Make the numbers transparent.**
- **Require meaningful contributions from management, owners, and employees.**
- **Do not rule out hard measures if the evidence shows they are necessary.**

That is the only responsible path for long-term value, competitiveness, and stability.

If the parties want to move this forward, the next step is not more rhetoric. It is the audit and the numbers.
