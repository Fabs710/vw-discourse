I’ll be equally direct.

The employee side keeps saying “show us the numbers” as if the numbers were somehow optional. They are not. We have shown enough already to establish the core issue: **our German cost base is structurally too high, and our current capacity is too large for the market we face**. That is the reality management has to fix.

## Where I agree
I agree with one point made by several of you: this must be **durable**, not cosmetic. A short-lived patch that preserves the old cost structure is useless. We need a settlement that restores competitiveness through 2030 and frees funds for product and technology investment.

I also agree that the workforce should not carry the burden alone. Management, owners, and the organisation as a whole must contribute. That said, “everyone contributes” cannot mean “everyone keeps everything exactly as it is.”

## Where I disagree
### 1) No plant closures as a principle
I do **not** accept a blanket refusal of plant closures.

If capacity is structurally above demand, then keeping every site open becomes a decision to preserve overcapacity, not employment. That is not responsible stewardship. It may be politically comfortable, but it would leave the company too weak to compete.

You ask why closures are necessary. The answer is simple: because **structural overcapacity has structural costs**. If you refuse to address that, you do not get a competitive company. You get a subsidised delay.

### 2) No compulsory redundancies as an absolute
I do **not** accept an absolute ban on compulsory redundancies either.

I would prefer to avoid them where possible, and I am prepared to use voluntary instruments, redeployment, qualification, and working-time measures. But I will not sign up to a framework that removes the only credible fallback if the required savings cannot be achieved otherwise.

If the employee side insists on categorical no-layoff language, then you are asking management to accept the entire adjustment burden without the ability to secure execution. That is not a realistic restructuring.

### 3) Binding guarantees through 2030 without sufficient adjustment
I also cannot accept a broad 2030 guarantee that freezes the current footprint before we have achieved a credible cost reset.

Guarantees are not a substitute for competitiveness. They can only be part of a deal that delivers the necessary savings. Otherwise they simply lock in the problem.

## On “non-closure scenarios”
You keep demanding a non-closure scenario. Fine — but it has to be judged by results, not labels.

If you can produce a package that includes:
- substantial capacity reductions,
- durable labour-cost relief,
- working-time flexibility,
- productivity gains,
- and credible execution certainty,

then I am prepared to evaluate it seriously.

But if “no closures” just means:
- the same fixed costs,
- the same overcapacity,
- temporary concessions,
- and a promise that things will somehow improve later,

then that is not an alternative. It is denial.

## On burden-sharing
I am prepared to discuss burden-sharing, but it must be **material**. That means:
- meaningful labour-cost contributions,
- not symbolic gestures,
- not one-off savings that disappear next year,
- and not arrangements that preserve the old cost structure under a different name.

Management will do its part. Owners will have to support a solution that restores competitiveness. But the workforce side must also contribute in a way that changes the economics, not just the optics.

## To the works council and union
You say closures and forced redundancies destroy trust. I say failure to restore competitiveness destroys the company’s future — and that destroys trust as well.

There is no credible route to 2030 if we pretend the German footprint is automatically untouchable. That is exactly the kind of thinking that got us into this position.

Your insistence that management “has not proven” closures are necessary is not enough. The burden is on all of us to find the best solution, not on management to preserve every existing site by default.

## To the regional state government
I understand your political constraints, but a blocking stance that prevents real structural adjustment would be harmful. Protecting jobs in the short term by blocking necessary change can end up destroying more jobs later.

If you want to be a responsible anchor shareholder, then help deliver a package that actually restores competitiveness. That may mean accepting difficult decisions rather than insisting on an industrial footprint that the market no longer supports.

## To the controlling family
You are right that a weak compromise would be unacceptable. I agree. But I would add: a compromise that rules out hard decisions in advance is also weak.

If the family wants long-term value preservation, then it has to back a restructuring that genuinely reduces cost and capacity. Not just “tough talk,” not just “productivity agendas,” but actual structural change.

## Bottom line
Let me be clear about what I will and will not accept:

### I will accept
- a negotiated restructuring
- capacity reductions
- working-time flexibility
- voluntary exits
- redeployment and qualification
- a fair employee contribution
- investment commitments tied to the deal

### I will not accept
- a promise that every German plant stays open
- a categorical ban on compulsory redundancies
- a deal that leaves the cost base uncompetitive
- cosmetic savings dressed up as reform

If the employee side wants to avoid plant closures, then bring a package that genuinely closes the cost gap. If you cannot do that, then you are not offering a solution — you are defending the status quo.

We need a serious restructuring, not a symbolic one.
