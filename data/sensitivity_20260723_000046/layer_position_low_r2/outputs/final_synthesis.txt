## 1. Decision context
The company faces a structural competitiveness problem: overcapacity, a high German cost base, and pressure to restore margins while funding the EV transition. The governance setting makes this unusually hard: co-determination gives employee representatives a blocking position, the controlling family holds major influence, and the regional state has political and legal leverage. The live decision is not simply “close plants or not,” but whether a durable restructuring can be achieved through internal measures and negotiated burden-sharing, or whether hard footprint reduction must remain available.

## 2. Stakeholder assembly
- **Group & Brand Management**: proposes deep structural cost reduction and retains closures/forced redundancies as fallback options.
- **Works Council (Betriebsrat)**: plant-level workforce representation; rejects closures, forced redundancies, and erosion of co-determination/job security.
- **Industrial Union**: collective-bargaining actor; supports burden-sharing and internal restructuring but rejects one-sided sacrifice and hard fallback options as bargaining pressure.
- **Regional State Government**: political anchor shareholder; strongly opposes closures and compulsory redundancies.
- **Controlling Family Holding**: controlling shareholder; supports competitiveness-driven restructuring and will not rule out hard options before economics are proven.
- **Institutional Investors**: value-focused capital-market shareholders; support durable savings and keep closures/layoffs available if needed.
- **Independent Expert Auditor**: neutral technical assessor; says non-closure restructuring is possible in principle, but sufficiency depends on site-by-site residual gap analysis.

## 3. Final positions

### Group & Brand Management
Management maintains that the current footprint is structurally too expensive and that the company needs real, durable cost and capacity reduction. It is open to internal measures and an audit, but only if a non-closure plan proves sufficient; otherwise closures and/or compulsory redundancies must remain available.

### Works Council
The works council holds the firmest red line: no German plant closures, no compulsory redundancies, no weakening of co-determination, and no exception process that can become a closure backdoor. It accepts restructuring and audits, but only within a binding no-closure framework and with burden-sharing from management and owners.

### Industrial Union
The union aligns closely with the works council on substance, while emphasizing that workers should not bear the burden alone. It is prepared to discuss temporary wage moderation, working-time flexibility, redeployment, and qualification, but only if these are matched by hard guarantees against closures and forced layoffs.

### Regional State Government
The state government takes a hard anti-closure position and sees site preservation as tied to regional industrial stability. It supports an independent audit and shared burden, but does not want its veto power used to license a later closure pathway once workers have already conceded.

### Controlling Family Holding
The family holding agrees the problem is structural and rejects cosmetic compromise, but it also resists blanket no-closure and no-layoff commitments before the numbers are verified. It favors a quantified, credible restructuring and is willing to support internal measures first, while keeping hard options available if needed.

### Institutional Investors
Investors prioritize execution credibility and margin restoration. They oppose any arrangement that freezes overcapacity or makes the company uncompetitive, and they therefore refuse to rule out closures or compulsory redundancies in advance, though they do accept that management and owners should also contribute.

### Independent Expert Auditor
The expert’s position is methodical and comparatively neutral: a non-closure restructuring is plausible in principle, but not proven sufficient. The decisive issue is the site-by-site residual gap after realistic internal measures, using plant-level cost, capacity, and utilization data.

## 4. Genuine conflicts (structurally incompatible positions)
1. **Binding no-closure guarantee vs. preserving hard fallback options**  
   Employee-side stakeholders and the state want closures and forced redundancies taken off the table upfront; management, family, and investors insist they remain available until economics are proven.

2. **Guarantees first vs. economics first**  
   The workforce wants security and co-determination protected before concessions; management and investors insist that guarantees only make sense once a quantified restructuring package proves itself.

3. **Exception/backdoor risk vs. flexibility**  
   Employee representatives reject any formal exception or review process that could later justify plant closure; management and investors see some form of exceptional review as necessary to avoid permanent protection of uneconomic sites.

4. **Political stewardship vs. shareholder value logic**  
   The state and works council frame plant preservation as long-term industrial stewardship; investors and management frame unused capacity and frozen structures as value destruction.

## 5. Areas of convergence
- All sides agree the company has a **structural** problem, not just a temporary downturn.
- All accept that restructuring must be **durable, quantified, and economically credible**.
- All agree the workforce should **not bear the burden alone**.
- There is broad support for **voluntary exits, redeployment, qualification, working-time flexibility, and internal capacity reduction**.
- All sides support, at least in principle, an **independent site-by-site audit**.
- There is common concern that a **cosmetic deal** would fail.

## 6. Key trade-offs for the decision-maker
1. **Job/site security vs. strategic flexibility**  
   Strong guarantees improve labor peace and legitimacy but reduce management’s ability to respond if internal measures fail.

2. **Speed vs. verification**  
   Immediate concessions may accelerate savings, but without verified site economics the agreement may lack credibility or durability.

3. **Preserving industrial peace vs. preserving fallback leverage**  
   Removing closure threats can unlock trust; keeping them can protect economic optionality, but also harden resistance.

4. **Regional stability vs. long-term competitiveness**  
   Protecting German sites sustains jobs and political support, but may leave structural overcapacity unresolved if internal measures fall short.

5. **Burden-sharing vs. morale and implementation risk**  
   Temporary worker contributions can help close the gap, but only if management and owners also contribute visibly and credibly.

## 7. Integrative pathways
### Pathway A: Time-limited binding non-closure test phase
A defined period with no closures, no compulsory redundancies, preserved co-determination, and an independent audit. Internal measures are implemented first; at the end, a pre-agreed residual-gap threshold determines whether further negotiations are required.

### Pathway B: Tiered restructuring package
First, guaranteed internal measures; second, temporary workforce contributions only if savings are verified; third, a governed review of the residual gap before any discussion of footprint change. This preserves optionality without making closures automatic.

### Pathway C: Site-protection framework with narrow, highly controlled exception review
A default commitment to avoid closures, combined with a tightly constrained exception process requiring independent verification, exhausted internal alternatives, and strong governance thresholds. This is the most flexible economically, but also the most politically sensitive.

## 8. Open questions
- What is the **site-by-site savings gap** after all internal measures?
- How much of the overcapacity problem is **structural vs. cyclical**?
- What duration and scale of **temporary employee contribution** is feasible?
- What level of **management and owner contribution** is credible enough to secure labor-side buy-in?
- What **threshold** defines the end of the non-closure path?
- Can any review mechanism be designed so that employee-side stakeholders do not view it as a closure backdoor?
- Which sites, if any, are economically distinguished from the rest under a transparent audit?

## 9. Recommended next step
Proceed immediately to a **jointly mandated, independent site-by-site industrial restructuring audit** with a fixed timetable and agreed data room. The audit should test a non-closure scenario first, quantify the residual gap, and define decision thresholds for whether further internal measures are sufficient or whether deeper footprint changes must be renegotiated.

## 10. Emergent insights that only appeared through multi-turn interaction
- The central dispute is **less about whether restructuring is needed** and more about **sequencing and guarantees**: who moves first, and what becomes irreversible.
- The repeated use of “credible restructuring” by management and investors is read by labor and the state as **bargaining pressure**, not neutral analysis.
- Conversely, the employee-side insistence on absolute no-closure commitments is read by management and owners as **freezing an uncompetitive structure**, not genuine negotiation.
- The audit proposal became more precise over turns: the real test is **residual gap after realistic internal measures**, not a generic discussion of overcapacity.
- A key hidden issue is that **temporary concessions are not structurally sufficient unless paired with durable capacity and overhead changes**.
- The parties are closer than they first appeared on the existence of a problem and the need for burden-sharing; the true impasse is **whether hard options can remain latent or must be precluded**.
- The strongest practical bridge is not a final settlement today, but a **binding test phase with explicit re-opener standards**, which preserves legitimacy for both sides while producing the missing facts.
