I will be direct.

## Where I agree
I agree with management, the expert, and in part the state government on the diagnosis: this is a **structural competitiveness problem**, not a temporary fluctuation. The company cannot keep a cost base and capacity footprint that the market no longer supports.

I also agree with the employee side on one point that matters: **the workforce must not carry the adjustment alone**. A one-sided solution would be unstable, unfair, and value-destructive.

And I agree with everyone that a cosmetic deal is unacceptable. If we leave this room with a patch that merely postpones the problem, we will have failed.

## Where I disagree
I do **not** accept the employee-side attempt to turn “no closures” and “no compulsory redundancies” into absolute, non-negotiable principles.

That position may be understandable, but it is not a sufficient answer to structural overcapacity. If the numbers show that the current German footprint cannot be made competitive through internal measures alone, then ruling out hard options in advance is not stewardship. It is avoidance.

At the same time, I do **not** accept management’s tone that closures or forced redundancies should be treated as the default answer. They are not the starting point. They are fallback options if, and only if, the economics truly require them.

## On the state government
I understand your political constraints and your wish to protect jobs and regional stability. But I have to be blunt: **a veto is not a restructuring plan**.

If the company remains structurally too expensive, then protecting every site without exception merely delays the damage. It does not solve it. That said, your insistence on real numbers is correct: no irreversible decision should be taken without a proper, site-by-site test.

## On the works council and union
You are right that overcapacity does not automatically equal closures. Capacity reduction can and should happen through:
- product allocation changes,
- shift reductions,
- working-time flexibility,
- voluntary exits,
- redeployment,
- qualification,
- and temporary employee contributions.

But I reject the idea that these tools are automatically sufficient. They may be enough; they may not. We do not know yet. What I cannot support is a framework that makes any harder decision impossible before the evidence is in.

## On management
Management is directionally right, but still too abstract.

You say the cost base must come down materially and durably. I agree. But I want the **actual gap** quantified clearly:
- site by site,
- measure by measure,
- and with a credible residual gap after internal levers.

Without that, closure talk is not yet a business case; it is a position.

## On the expert assessment
The expert is helpful and correct on the main point: a non-closure restructuring is **possible in principle**, but whether it is sufficient depends on the actual numbers.

That is exactly the standard I want applied.

So I do **not** accept the employee side’s claim that closures are unnecessary by definition.  
And I do **not** accept management’s implied claim that closures are inevitable by definition.

The truth lies in the audited economics.

## What would move me
I would support a serious non-closure restructuring if it is:
- **quantified**
- **independently verified**
- **durable**
- and **execution-credible**

That means I would need to see:
1. site-by-site cost and capacity data,
2. realistic savings from internal measures,
3. management and owner burden-sharing,
4. a clear investment commitment,
5. and a residual gap that is either closed or clearly proven to remain.

If the non-closure path works, I will support it.  
If it does not, then hard options cannot be ruled out in advance.

## What I will not support
I will not support:
- a blanket no-closure commitment before the economics are proven,
- a blanket ban on compulsory redundancies,
- guarantees that freeze an uncompetitive structure through 2030,
- or a settlement that preserves overcapacity while asking the company to fund its future at the same time.

That would be value-destructive.

## Bottom line
My position is simple:

- **Test a real non-closure scenario first.**
- **Do it transparently and site by site.**
- **Require contributions from employees, management, and owners.**
- **Do not rule out hard measures unless the numbers truly justify doing so.**

That is the only responsible route if we want long-term value, competitiveness, and stability.
