We are listening closely, but our position remains anchored in one thing: **credible, durable improvement in margins and capital returns**. Anything else is noise.

## Where we agree
We agree with management, the family holding, and the state on the central diagnosis: this is **structural**, not cyclical. The current cost base and capacity footprint are not consistent with a competitive business. A cosmetic settlement would be value-destructive.

We also agree with the employee side on one important point: the workforce should **not** be asked to absorb all of the burden while management and owners preserve the status quo elsewhere. That would not be a durable settlement, and the market would not reward it.

## Where we disagree

### 1) “No closures” as an absolute
We do **not** accept a blanket ban on plant closures.

If a site is genuinely uneconomic and overcapacity is structural, then refusing to close it can destroy more value over time than the closure itself. The right question is not whether a closure is painful; it is whether the overall package restores competitiveness.

So if the alternative to closures is simply:
- the same fixed costs,
- temporary flexibility,
- voluntary exits,
- and wishful thinking,

then that is not an acceptable restructuring.

### 2) “No compulsory redundancies” as an absolute
We also do **not** accept a categorical exclusion of compulsory redundancies.

We prefer voluntary instruments, redeployment, and working-time changes. But from a capital-markets perspective, management must retain execution credibility. If the only fallback is permanently blocked, the market will discount the plan as non-binding.

That said, we are not demanding layoffs for their own sake. We are demanding **a credible path to durable cost reduction**.

### 3) Guarantees first, economics later
We cannot support binding site-and-job guarantees through 2030 if they are used to freeze an uncompetitive structure.

Guarantees have value only if they sit inside a package that actually delivers:
- meaningful capacity reduction,
- lower fixed costs,
- and a visible route back to target margins.

Without that, guarantees become a delay mechanism, not a solution.

## What would move us
We would support a negotiated package if it is **quantified, durable, and execution-credible**. Specifically, we would look for:

- clear evidence of **capacity rationalisation**
- a **material and lasting** reduction in fixed costs
- a **quantified** contribution from labour costs
- management and owner contributions, not just employee concessions
- investment commitments tied to restructuring milestones
- a timeline that returns the brand to a **competitive return-on-sales path**

If those conditions are met, we can support a non-closure solution as well. But the burden is on the deal to prove it can close the economics gap.

## To the works council and union
We respect the insistence on social peace, but your current red lines are too rigid if they imply that the company must preserve every site and every employment guarantee regardless of economics.

That would not be a protection of value; it would be a transfer of future pain onto shareholders and, ultimately, the company itself.

If you want to avoid closures, then the alternative has to be real:
- not symbolic cuts,
- not short-lived concessions,
- not a rebranding of the old cost structure.

We would look favorably on a serious package of:
- voluntary exits,
- working-time flexibility,
- internal redeployment,
- qualification,
- temporary labour-cost contributions,
- and substantial capacity reduction.

But if that package does not materially change the economics, then it is not enough.

## To management
You are directionally right, but you still need to make the case in a way capital markets can underwrite.

“Overcapacity” and “structural gap” are not substitutes for a quantified plan. We want to see:
- what savings are required,
- what each measure delivers,
- what the residual gap is if closures are excluded,
- and how execution risk is being managed.

If you cannot show that, then the market will assume the restructuring is politically constrained and financially incomplete.

## To the regional state government
We understand the political constraints, but a veto that blocks all meaningful adjustment is not a long-term answer. If the business remains weak, the regional base becomes weaker too.

A responsible anchor shareholder should support a settlement that restores competitiveness, even if it is uncomfortable.

## To the controlling family
You are correct that cosmetic restructuring is unacceptable. But the same is true of a compromise that is politically elegant and economically inadequate.

We want a plan that is tough enough to matter and credible enough to hold. That is the standard.

## Bottom line
We will support:
- a **durable** restructuring,
- real capacity reduction,
- material cost discipline,
- and execution certainty.

We will **not** support:
- a cosmetic settlement,
- a deal that preserves structural overcost,
- or a package that leaves margins impaired and valuation under pressure.

If the parties can produce a quantified, credible path back to competitive margins, we are open on the form it takes. If they cannot, we will treat the outcome as value-destructive.
