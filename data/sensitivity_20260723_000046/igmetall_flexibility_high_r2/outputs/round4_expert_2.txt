# 2) The correct financial comparison

The right metric is **risk-adjusted net present value (NPV)** over 3–5 years:

\[
\text{NPV} = \sum \frac{\text{Recurring annual savings} \times \text{Probability of realization}}{(1+r)^t} - \text{One-time restructuring costs} - \text{Delay costs} - \text{Conflict costs}
\]

Where:
- **recurring savings** = permanent annual run-rate improvement
- **one-time costs** = severance, closure, relocation, write-downs, retraining, retooling
- **delay costs** = lost savings from implementation slippage
- **conflict costs** = strike losses, overtime premiums, expediting, missed volume, productivity loss
- **probability of realization** = chance the package is fully implemented and sustained

---

# 4) The savings gap test

The key question is not whether the non-closure route saves money. It probably does.

The question is whether it closes the **economic gap** versus closures.

## Rule of thumb
A non-closure package is economically credible only if it achieves:
- at least **~80%–90%** of the closure case’s risk-adjusted NPV benefit,
- and does so with sufficient durability to 2030.

If it lands materially below that, then it may be politically preferable but not economically equivalent.

---

# 5) Why closures often still win on pure economics

Even though closures are socially and industrially harder, they often produce:
- larger fixed-cost removal
- clearer capacity rationalization
- less dependence on continuous bargaining
- stronger credibility with capital markets

However, this advantage is partly offset by:
- high upfront cash cost
- asset write-downs
- longer conflict
- strike risk
- productivity drag
- reputational and recruitment damage

So closure is not automatically superior. It is superior only if:
- the structural savings are large enough,
- conflict does not erase too much value,
- and the company can actually execute the closure plan.

---

# 6) Why non-closure packages often fail to be truly equivalent

The usual weakness is not the first-year saving. It is **durability**.

A non-closure package often relies on:
- temporary wage moderation
- voluntary programs that taper off
- internal redeployment that eventually saturates
- capacity reduction without full permanent fixed-cost elimination

That means the package may look good for 2–3 years and then fade, unless:
- the labor-cost concessions are durable,
- the capacity cuts are real,
- and enforcement is embedded in binding agreements.

---

# 7) Conflict risk must be explicitly included

The summaries correctly emphasize that conflict risk is part of the economics.

A closure-led plan may show larger nominal savings, but if it triggers:
- prolonged strikes
- reduced output
- lost market share
- lower morale
- higher defect / rework rates

then the realized benefit can shrink sharply.

By contrast, a negotiated non-closure package may:
- deliver smaller nominal savings
- but achieve much higher realized value because execution is smoother

That is why the comparison must be **risk-adjusted**, not headline-only.
