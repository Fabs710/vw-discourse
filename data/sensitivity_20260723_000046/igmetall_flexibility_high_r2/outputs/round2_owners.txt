I agree with the core diagnosis from management and the economist: **this is a structural competitiveness problem, not a temporary one**. If the German cost base remains materially above target, then the company will continue to underperform, and that is not acceptable for the long-term owner.

I also agree with the state and the employee side on one important point: **a prolonged, value-destroying industrial conflict would be deeply damaging**. It would erode margins, delay restructuring, harm the brand, and weaken the company’s social legitimacy. So no one should pretend that a brute-force approach is costless.

Where I disagree is with the employee side’s attempt to make **“no plant closures” and “no compulsory redundancies”** an absolute starting boundary. If that boundary simply prevents any serious cost reset, then it is not a responsible position; it is a veto on necessary adjustment. I cannot support a settlement that preserves all current structures while leaving the company uncompetitive.

At the same time, I am not handing management a blank cheque for the most disruptive option. A closure-led shock strategy may be economically defensible in theory, but it is only acceptable if it is truly necessary and if no equivalent negotiated alternative exists. I do **not** support unnecessary destruction of value through open-ended conflict or a careless assault on the company’s industrial base.

So my position is:

- **Yes** to a durable restructuring that restores margins and competitiveness.
- **Yes** to serious employee contributions and meaningful capacity reduction.
- **Yes** to negotiated alternatives if they are economically equivalent.
- **No** to a cosmetic compromise that postpones the problem.
- **No** to a package that leaves the German cost base structurally uncorrected.
- **No** to any outcome that permanently weakens enterprise value or governance stability.

The economist’s point is the key one: a non-closure solution can work only if it is **deep and durable**. That means management and labour now need to move from principles to numbers. I want a side-by-side comparison of:

1. a closure/layoff scenario with quantified annual savings and one-time costs, and  
2. a non-closure scenario with quantified capacity reduction, labor-cost relief, productivity gains, and timeline.

If the non-closure option genuinely closes the gap, then that deserves serious consideration. If it does not, then the employee side should stop treating closures as taboo and management should stop pretending that vague “alternatives” are enough.

To be explicit: **I will not endorse a settlement that is designed to be politically comfortable but economically insufficient.** But I also will not support a destructive showdown if a credible negotiated route can deliver the required savings.
