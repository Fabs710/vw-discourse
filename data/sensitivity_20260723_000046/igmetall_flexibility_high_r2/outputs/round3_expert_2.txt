Below is a **restructuring finance comparison matrix** focused on the economics of the two paths, not on strategy or labor politics.

## Core comparison matrix

| Dimension | Closure-led restructuring | Non-closure negotiated restructuring |
|---|---:|---:|
| **Annual recurring savings** | **High**. Usually the largest structural savings because fixed overhead and plant-level cost are removed. Illustrative range: **€700m–€1.5bn+ p.a.** | **Medium**. Can be substantial, but usually lower unless capacity reduction and labor-cost relief are deep. Illustrative range: **€400m–€1.0bn p.a.** |
| **One-time restructuring costs** | **High**. Severance, shutdown costs, asset write-downs, logistics changes, relocation, and disruption. Illustrative range: **€1bn–€3bn+** | **Medium**. Lower shutdown cost, but still includes voluntary exits, retraining, productivity investment, and concession management. Illustrative range: **€0.3bn–€1.5bn** |
| **Execution risk** | **Medium to high**. Clearer economics, but exposed to co-determination friction, strike risk, legal/process delays, and implementation drag. | **High** unless the package is very deep and binding. Savings leakage is the main risk. |
| **Likelihood of full delivery** | **Moderate**, depending on governance approval and industrial conflict. | **Moderate to low** unless the package includes enforceable capacity and cost measures. |
| **Impact on regional employment** | **High negative** at affected sites; also spillovers to suppliers and local demand. | **Lower immediate job loss**, but still usually implies headcount reduction via attrition, redeployment, or voluntary exits. |
| **Industrial-relations stability** | Usually weaker in the short run. | Better if genuinely negotiated, but only if the deal is credible and durable. |
| **Long-run competitiveness** | Usually strongest on pure fixed-cost reset. | Can be competitive only if it includes real footprint reduction, durable labor-cost relief, and productivity gains. |

## Savings gap
On a general industrial basis, the **closure-led route usually outperforms** the non-closure route on recurring savings by roughly:

- **€200m to €700m+ per year**, depending on:
  - number of sites closed,
  - how much fixed overhead is removed,
  - whether production is successfully consolidated,
  - and how much labor-cost relief the non-closure deal achieves.

That is the **economic gap** the non-closure package must close if it is to be truly equivalent.

## How to read the two options

### 1) Closure-led restructuring
This is the cleaner **cost-reset mechanism**.
- Best when the problem is **structural overcapacity** and persistent fixed-cost burden.
- Delivers more durable recurring savings.
- But it comes with high one-time costs and significant execution/relations risk.

### 2) Non-closure negotiated restructuring
This can work, but only if it is **deep**, not symbolic.
It typically needs all of the following:
- real capacity reduction,
- working-time or shift changes,
- durable labor-cost relief,
- voluntary exits and redeployment,
- productivity improvements,
- and a binding implementation framework.

If those elements are weak, the result is usually **not economically equivalent** to closures.

## Bottom line
From a finance and plant-planning perspective:

- **Closure-led restructuring** = higher recurring savings, higher one-time costs, higher immediate disruption.
- **Non-closure restructuring** = lower one-time costs, lower employment damage up front, but usually lower savings and higher risk of underdelivery.

So the key question is not whether the non-closure option is politically preferable. It is whether it can produce:
1. **enough recurring savings**,
2. **with acceptable execution risk**,
3. **over a 3–5 year horizon**.

If it cannot close the savings gap, then it is not economically equivalent.
