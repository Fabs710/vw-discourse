We agree on the core diagnosis: **the company has a structural competitiveness problem, not a temporary one**. A cosmetic settlement would be value-destructive, and we would not support it.

Where we differ is on the insistence that **“no closures” and “no compulsory redundancies”** can stand as a hard boundary **before** a financially equivalent alternative is shown. From capital markets, that is not acceptable. Our support depends on a credible path back to competitive margins, not on preserving legacy structures.

## What we agree with
- Management is right that the status quo is not viable.
- The economist is right that this is a **recurring cost-base** problem and that temporary concessions alone are unlikely to be enough.
- The employee side is right that prolonged industrial conflict destroys value.

## What we disagree with
### To the Works Council and Union
We do **not** accept a settlement that protects every plant and rules out compulsory job reductions if the result is only modest or temporary relief. That would leave the cost base structurally impaired, which would continue to pressure margins, valuation, and future investment capacity.

If you want closures off the table, then you need to show a package that is **financially equivalent**:
- real capacity reduction,
- durable labour-cost relief,
- measurable headcount alignment,
- and a fixed-cost reduction that is not just deferred.

“Investment commitments” and “job security through 2030” are not, by themselves, savings. If the economics do not close, then the plan is not credible.

### To Management
We agree that deep restructuring is necessary. But we are **not** giving a blank cheque for the most disruptive path.

The expert’s point is important: closure may be stronger economically, but a non-closure solution can be acceptable if it is **deep, durable, and implementable**. Management should therefore quantify both paths clearly:
1. closure/layoff scenario,
2. non-closure negotiated scenario.

If management cannot prove that closures are necessary, then it should not frame them as the default. Execution risk matters, and an all-out confrontation can destroy value too.

### To the Regional State and Family Owners
We agree that value destruction from open conflict must be avoided. But social legitimacy cannot become a veto on hard restructuring. The relevant question is whether the company can return to competitive margins. If not, everyone’s long-term position worsens.

## Our position
We are prepared to support a solution that is tough, durable, and economically credible. But it must satisfy these conditions:

1. **Clear, quantified return to competitive margins**
2. **Structural, not temporary, cost reduction**
3. **Deal certainty and execution credibility**
4. **No cosmetic compromise that preserves excess capacity**

## What would move us
A non-closure alternative could gain our support only if it demonstrates, in numbers:
- annual recurring savings close to the closure case,
- real capacity shrinkage,
- enforceable labour-cost relief,
- a credible implementation timetable,
- and a convincing mechanism to prevent cost leakage.

Absent that, we would see the employee-side position as a delay strategy, not a solution.

## Bottom line
We will not support an outcome that leaves the cost base uncompetitive or margins structurally impaired.  
We will support a serious restructuring if it is economically equivalent, durable, and credible.  
If not, decisive structural cuts remain necessary.
