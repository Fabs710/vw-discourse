[SUMMON: Industrial economist / restructuring specialist — to quantify and compare savings paths, including non-closure alternatives vs closure-led restructuring]

[Expert: Industrial economist / restructuring spe — Round 2]
From an industrial-economics and restructuring perspective, the key question is not whether restructuring is needed — the scenario already makes that clear — but **whether a non-closure package can credibly deliver savings comparable to a closure-led package**, and if not, **how large the residual gap is**.

I can assess that at the level of economics and restructuring logic. I cannot determine legal admissibility, labor-relations tactics, or governance enforceability in this setting.

## 1) The economic benchmark

For a large automotive manufacturer with German plants, the relevant cost problem usually has four parts:

1. **Fixed manufacturing overhead** tied to plant footprints, utilities, maintenance, logistics, and support staff.
2. **Labor cost per vehicle**, including wages, bonuses, allowances, and paid time not fully matched by output.
3. **Underutilized capacity**, which raises unit costs sharply when volume falls.
4. **Product-mix weakness**, meaning the plants are making models or trims with insufficient margin.

In that setting, a restructuring must usually create savings from one or more of these channels:

- **capacity removal**
- **headcount reduction**
- **labor-cost moderation**
- **product reallocation / better mix**
- **asset rationalization**
- **productivity improvement**

The central economic issue is this:  
**If the company has structural overcapacity, then purely temporary concessions rarely close the full gap unless they are large and durable.**

## 2) Closure-led restructuring: what it typically achieves

A plant closure can deliver savings in three main ways:

- elimination of plant fixed costs,
- removal of excess labor tied to that site,
- and a durable reduction in capacity.

For a large automotive plant, the annual cash cost avoided by closure can be substantial, but the actual magnitude depends on:
- plant size,
- whether production is transferred elsewhere,
- severance and transfer costs,
- stranded asset write-downs,
- and whether the lost output can be replaced profitably.

### Typical economic effect
A closure-led package is often strongest when:
- the plant is structurally underutilized,
- the model assigned to it has weak demand,
- alternative plants have spare capacity,
- and the savings are permanent.

In that case, closure can produce a **durable step-down in cost base**, not just a temporary relief.

### Quantitative order of magnitude
Without company-specific data, I would treat the following as a **rough benchmark**, not a forecast:

- **Per major plant closure:** often **hundreds of millions of euros per year** in gross fixed-cost and labor-cost relief, but net savings may be materially lower after restructuring charges.
- **One-time costs:** severance, transfer, and write-down costs can easily run into **the high hundreds of millions or more** depending on site size and labor arrangements.
- **Net annual savings after transition:** very roughly, one major closure might yield **tens to low hundreds of millions of euros** in net annual benefit if the production can be redeployed efficiently.

The key point is not the exact number. The key point is that **closures create durable savings because they permanently remove the capacity-cost structure**.

## 3) Non-closure alternatives: what they can and cannot do

A non-closure alternative can still produce meaningful savings, but economics is stricter here.

### A. Labor-cost measures
These include:
- wage moderation,
- bonus reductions,
- working-time adjustments,
- removal of allowances,
- reduced pay for reduced hours,
- and delayed increments.

These measures can be effective if the company is currently above market labor cost. But they are usually **only part of the solution**.

#### Economic limitation
If the company’s problem is not just wage level but **overcapacity**, then wage cuts alone do not solve the fixed-cost burden. They reduce unit cost, but do not eliminate plant overhead.

### B. Capacity reduction without closure
This can include:
- fewer shifts,
- lower output targets,
- consolidation of model lines,
- and partial idling of lines.

This can reduce variable and semi-fixed costs, but it often leaves a large share of fixed costs in place unless one also shrinks the site footprint materially.

#### Economic limitation
If capacity is reduced but the plant remains open, the company may still carry:
- maintenance,
- security,
- support staff,
- depreciation,
- and underabsorbed overhead.

So the savings are usually **less than closure**, unless the plant is radically resized.

### C. Productivity measures
These include:
- shorter changeover times,
- better line balancing,
- higher automation,
- lower scrap,
- improved logistics,
- and better workforce deployment.

These are economically valuable, but they are usually **incremental**, not transformational, unless there is major process redesign.

#### Typical range
In mature automotive manufacturing, productivity gains of **low single-digit percent per year** can be meaningful. But if the gap is structural and large, productivity measures alone rarely close it.

### D. Product-mix shift
Moving German plants to:
- higher-margin models,
- niche EVs,
- premium derivatives,
- or specialized variants

can improve profitability without closure.

But this depends on:
- product pipeline,
- investment timing,
- platform allocation,
- and market demand.

This is potentially powerful, but it is not instant savings. It is a **restructuring plus industrial strategy** path, not just a labor-cost measure.

### E. Asset rationalization
Selling, mothballing, or consolidating assets can generate some benefit, especially if there is excess real estate or duplicated support functions.

But this usually provides **one-off balance sheet relief**, not enough by itself to solve a recurring cost problem.

## 4) Which path is usually economically stronger?

If the company truly has **structural overcapacity**, then the economic ranking is usually:

1. **Closure or major footprint consolidation**  
   strongest recurring savings
2. **Radical resizing plus product shift**  
   potentially comparable, but only if implementation is large and credible
3. **Labor-cost concessions plus productivity gains**  
   useful, but often insufficient alone
4. **Temporary protections without footprint change**  
   lowest economic impact

So the real comparison is not “closure versus no closure.”  
It is:

> **How much of the structural cost gap can be eliminated by non-closure measures, and how much still remains?**

## 5) A practical comparison framework

To decide whether a non-closure alternative is economically equivalent, I would compare the two paths on four metrics:

### 1. Annual recurring savings
- Closure-led: likely highest
- Non-closure: depends on wage relief + capacity reduction + productivity gains

### 2. One-time restructuring cost
- Closure-led: highest upfront
- Non-closure: lower upfront, but sometimes higher ongoing leakage if overcapacity remains

### 3. Execution risk
- Closure-led: high labor conflict risk, but economically clear if executed
- Non-closure: lower conflict risk if negotiated, but higher risk of under-delivering savings

### 4. Durability
- Closure-led: more durable
- Non-closure: only durable if concessions and capacity changes are structurally locked in

## 6) A quantified illustration

Because no company-specific model is provided, I will use a simplified illustration.

Assume the company needs to improve operating performance by, say, **€1.0 billion per year** at the German operations to restore competitiveness.

A possible closure-led package might produce:
- €500–700 million from plant footprint reduction and labor elimination,

## 1) Core economic principle

If the company has **structural overcapacity**, then a non-closure package must do more than reduce wages. It must also remove enough **fixed cost** and **semi-fixed capacity cost** to match what closures would otherwise eliminate.

That means the relevant comparison is not:

- “closures vs no closures”

but:

- **net present value of recurring savings**
  minus
- **one-time restructuring costs**
  adjusted for
- **probability of successful implementation**

A non-closure package is only economically equivalent if:

## 2) What usually drives the gap

### Closure-led package
A plant closure usually removes:
- site fixed overhead,
- plant labor,
- support functions,
- maintenance and utilities,
- underabsorbed capacity cost.

But it also creates:
- severance and social plan costs,
- transfer/logistics costs,
- write-downs,
- ramp-up costs at receiving sites,
- potential disruption to supply.

### Non-closure package
A non-closure package can reduce:
- labor cost,
- working time,
- shift count,
- some overhead,
- some headcount through attrition,
- some capacity through partial line reduction.

But it usually leaves:
- most site fixed cost,
- more overhead leakage,
- more underutilization,
- more execution risk that savings will erode over time.

So the economic question is whether the non-closure package removes **enough recurring cost** to offset the fact that the plant footprint remains.

## 4) The exact savings gap: how to calculate it

The right calculation is:

### For each option:
**Expected NPV = Σ [Savings_t × Probability_t / (1+r)^t] – One-time costs**

Where:
- `Savings_t` = recurring annual savings in year t
- `Probability_t` = probability-adjusted realization of those savings
- `r` = discount rate
- One-time costs include severance, write-offs, transfer, retraining, logistics, consultants, etc.

### Savings gap:
**Gap = NPV(closure-led) – NPV(non-closure)**

If the gap is positive, closure is economically stronger.  
If the gap is near zero, non-closure may be equivalent.  
If the gap is negative, the non-closure package is better economically.

---

## 5) Probability adjustment matters

This is where many negotiations become misleading.

A non-closure package often looks attractive on paper, but its **realization probability** is lower because:
- wage concessions may expire,
- productivity measures may not stick,
- headcount reductions may be slower than planned,
- future bargaining can reopen the deal,
- underutilized capacity continues to create hidden cost.

A closure-led package has high certainty once executed, but with higher upfront cost and labor conflict risk.

### Practical probability ranges
For modeling purposes only:
- Closure implementation success: **80%–95%**
- Non-closure full-savings success: **50%–80%**

That difference can erase a large part of the apparent savings advantage of a non-closure deal.

---

- One-time cost: **€500m**
- Probability-adjusted realization: **90%**

Approximate 5-year undiscounted value:
- Savings: €200m × 5 = **€1.0bn**
- Probability-adjusted savings: **€900m**
- Minus one-time cost: **€500m**
- Approximate net value: **€400m**

### Non-closure package

- One-time cost: **€150m**
- Probability-adjusted realization: **65%**

- Minus one-time cost: **€150m**
- Approximate net value: **€402.5m**

### Result

- probability-adjusted = €360m

Now the closure-led package is clearly superior by about **€190m**.

So the exact gap is highly sensitive to:
- annual recurring savings,
- durability,
- and execution confidence.

## How to read the two options

### 1) Closure-led restructuring
This is the cleaner **cost-reset mechanism**.
- Best when the problem is **structural overcapacity** and persistent fixed-cost burden.
- Delivers more durable recurring savings.
- But it comes with high one-time costs and significant execution/relations risk.

### 2) Non-closure negotiated restructuring
This can work, but only if it is **deep**, not symbolic.
It typically needs all of the following:
- real capacity reduction,
- working-time or shift changes,
- durable labor-cost relief,
- voluntary exits and redeployment,
- productivity improvements,
- and a binding implementation framework.

If those elements are weak, the result is usually **not economically equivalent** to closures.

## Bottom line
From a finance and plant-planning perspective:

- **Closure-led restructuring** = higher recurring savings, higher one-time costs, higher immediate disruption.
- **Non-closure restructuring** = lower one-time costs, lower employment damage up front, but usually lower savings and higher risk of underdelivery.

So the key question is not whether the non-closure option is politically preferable. It is whether it can produce:
1. **enough recurring savings**,
2. **with acceptable execution risk**,
3. **over a 3–5 year horizon**.

If it cannot close the savings gap, then it is not economically equivalent.

# 2) The correct financial comparison

The right metric is **risk-adjusted net present value (NPV)** over 3–5 years:

\[
\text{NPV} = \sum \frac{\text{Recurring annual savings} \times \text{Probability of realization}}{(1+r)^t} - \text{One-time restructuring costs} - \text{Delay costs} - \text{Conflict costs}
\]

Where:
- **recurring savings** = permanent annual run-rate improvement
- **one-time costs** = severance, closure, relocation, write-downs, retraining, retooling
- **delay costs** = lost savings from implementation slippage
- **conflict costs** = strike losses, overtime premiums, expediting, missed volume, productivity loss
- **probability of realization** = chance the package is fully implemented and sustained

## Closure-led package — illustrative profile

- **Implementation probability:** moderate to high in theory, but conflict can reduce realized value

- **Recurring savings:** medium to high, but usually below closure case
- **One-time cost:** lower than closures
- **Implementation probability:** can be higher if negotiated, but durability is weaker unless contractually locked in
- **Time to savings:** can be faster if immediately agreed, but may decay after guarantee period
