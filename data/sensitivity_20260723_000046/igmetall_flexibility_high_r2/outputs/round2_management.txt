The economist’s intervention confirms what management has been saying from the start: **a real solution must remove structural cost and capacity, not just hand out temporary concessions.** That is the central point, and I’m glad it is now on the table in economic terms rather than slogans.

### Where I agree
I agree with the expert on three things:

1. **This is a structural overcapacity problem.**  
   That means labor-cost tweaks alone will not be enough.

2. **Non-closure alternatives can work only if they are unusually deep and durable.**  
   A modest package would be economically inadequate.

3. **Execution credibility matters.**  
   A deal that looks nice on paper but leaves us with the same cost base is not a solution.

### Where I disagree
I disagree strongly with the idea that the employee side can simply set “no closures, no compulsory redundancies” as a fixed boundary and then demand that management somehow conjure up an equivalent restructuring around it.

That is not a restructuring framework. It is a political veto on the hardest part of the adjustment.

The economist’s comparison is actually quite clear:
- closure-led restructuring is more likely to deliver the full cost reset;
- non-closure alternatives only work if they are deep enough to remove a large share of fixed cost and overcapacity;
- and smaller compromises will be insufficient.

That means the burden is now on the employee side to explain **how** they intend to close the gap without closures. Not in generalities. In numbers.

### To the Works Council and Union
You keep repeating “no closures, no compulsory redundancies.” I understand the position, but I do **not** accept it as the starting point for a viable deal unless you can produce a package that is economically equivalent.

So here is the challenge back to you:

- How much capacity will actually be removed?
- How much annual cost relief will your proposal deliver?
- How quickly?
- And what is the binding mechanism that prevents the company from being trapped in another three years of high-cost underutilization?

If your answer is “investment, guarantees, and negotiated flexibility,” that is not enough by itself. Those are inputs, not savings.

I am prepared to discuss:
- working-time flexibility,
- targeted labor-cost concessions,
- internal reallocation,
- and plant-specific restructuring measures.

But I am **not** prepared to accept:
- a blanket 2030 job guarantee,
- preservation of all sites as a principle,
- and a promise that “something else” will somehow close a gap that the expert says is likely substantial.

### To the regional state
I take your point about legitimacy and industrial stability. But we cannot confuse legitimacy with immobility. If we do not reduce the cost base materially, there will be no stable future to protect.

A solution that avoids closures is acceptable **only if it produces comparable structural savings**. If not, then blocking necessary change simply delays a worse outcome later.

### To the controlling family owners
You are right that open-ended conflict destroys value. I am not asking for conflict for its own sake. I am asking for a durable restructuring that restores competitiveness. If a negotiated non-closure alternative can really do that, then fine. But we need hard evidence, not wishes.

### My position, stated plainly
- **I will not support a cosmetic compromise.**
- **I will not support a settlement that leaves the German cost base materially above target.**
- **I am open to alternatives to plant closures only if they deliver equivalent, durable savings.**
- **If they do not, then closures and other hard measures remain on the table and must be treated as real options, not taboo.**

### What I need next
If the employee side wants to keep closures off the table, then bring a **fully quantified package** that shows:
- plant-by-plant capacity reduction,
- annual recurring savings,
- labor-cost contribution,
- productivity gains,
- timeline to effect,
- and how it restores the brand’s return-on-sales target.

Without that, we are not discussing an equivalent alternative — we are discussing delay.

And let me be clear: **delay is not acceptable**. The market is moving now, not in 2030.
