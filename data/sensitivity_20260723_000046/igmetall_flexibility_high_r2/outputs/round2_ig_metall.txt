I will be equally direct.

## Where I agree
I agree with the Works Council on the core red lines:

- **No plant closures in Germany**
- **No compulsory redundancies**
- **No dismantling of the collective bargaining framework**

And I agree with several others on one point as well: **this company needs a real, durable restructuring**, not a temporary patch. A deal that merely postpones the problem is not acceptable.

## Where I disagree
### To management
You are still arguing as if closures and forced layoffs are the only serious option. I do **not** accept that as established fact.

If you want to claim that, then put the numbers on the table:
- What exact savings come from closures?
- What exact savings come from wage contributions, working-time changes, capacity adjustments, and internal redeployment?
- What is the quantified gap between those two paths?

Until that comparison is made transparently, “we need closures” is just a preference dressed up as necessity.

And let me be clear: **workers will not be made the sole shock absorber** for strategic and management failures. If management wants employee contributions, then management, owners, and the company itself must also contribute materially.

### To investors and owners
I hear the insistence on “credible economics.” Fine. But credibility is not only about cutting costs on paper. A settlement that blows up industrial peace, destroys trust, and weakens the company’s social legitimacy is not credible either. That is a real economic cost.

If you support a closure-first strategy without proving that no equivalent alternative exists, then you are choosing confrontation risk and long-term damage, not value creation.

### To the regional state
Your opposition to closures and mass redundancies is important, but it cannot remain only declaratory. If you believe there is a better path, then use your blocking position to insist on a negotiated restructuring that preserves sites and jobs.

## What the union is prepared to discuss
We are not saying “nothing changes.” That would not be responsible.

We are prepared to discuss:
- **temporary and negotiated employee contributions**
- **working-time flexibility**
- **capacity reductions through socially responsible means**
- **targeted efficiency measures**
- **reallocation of production**
- **a fair contribution from the workforce only if matched by real contributions from management and owners**
- **investment commitments for German plants**
- **a binding employment perspective through 2030**

What we are **not** prepared to discuss:
- plant closures
- compulsory redundancies
- or a solution that preserves shareholder comfort by pushing all pain onto employees

## On the collective wage agreement
Let me underline one point that is non-negotiable in practice: **the collective wage framework must remain intact**. If management wants relief, it must be negotiated in a way that does not create a precedent for dismantling wage standards across the group.

That means any employee contribution has to be:
- limited in scope,
- tied to binding job and site security,
- time-bound,
- and matched by contributions from the other sides.

## The key test
So the real test is simple:

### If management can produce a quantified alternative without closures and forced layoffs
that delivers comparable structural savings, then we can negotiate seriously.

### If management cannot do that
then it should stop pretending that closures are unavoidable and admit it is trying to force workers to pay for the adjustment alone.

That will not be accepted.

## Bottom line
We are open to a fair, shared-sacrifice settlement.  
We are not open to plant closures, forced redundancies, or the erosion of collective bargaining.  
If the other side refuses a balanced path, then we will defend our position with the means available to us, including industrial action.
