I will respond plainly.

## Where I agree
I agree with management, investors, the owners, and the economist on one central point: **this company needs a real, durable restructuring**. A cosmetic fix is worthless. If the German operations remain structurally too expensive, then the company will keep bleeding competitiveness.

I also agree that **industrial peace matters**. A prolonged conflict would damage everyone, including the workforce. That is precisely why we have already signaled willingness to discuss **real contributions from employees**.

## Where I disagree
### To management
You are still treating **plant closures and compulsory redundancies** as if they are the natural reference point. They are not acceptable to us, and nothing said so far changes that.

You have not proven that closing German plants or forcing people out is the only way to achieve the needed savings. What you have proven is that you prefer a faster, harsher route because it shifts the burden onto the workforce.

That is not an argument we accept.

The economist was right about one thing: a non-closure package must be **deep and durable**. Fine. We accept that. But that does **not** mean closures are justified. It means you have to quantify a serious alternative and stop hiding behind generalities.

We are prepared to discuss:
- working-time flexibility,
- temporary wage-related concessions,
- capacity reduction without closures,
- internal redeployment,
- productivity measures,
- and targeted site restructuring.

We are **not** prepared to discuss:
- German plant closures,
- compulsory redundancies,
- or breaking the collective wage framework.

### To investors
You keep saying “credible economics.” We agree with that standard. But credibility does not mean “whatever delivers the biggest cut fastest.” A strategy that detonates industrial relations, destroys trust, and weakens the company’s employer reputation is not economically clean.

If you support a closure-first route, then you must show:
- why the same result cannot be reached otherwise,
- what savings are actually gained after conflict and transition costs,
- and why the long-term damage is acceptable.

So far, that case has not been made.

### To the controlling family owners
Long-term ownership must mean long-term responsibility. A durable company is not built by making workers absorb all the pain while owners preserve strategic optionality. If the workforce is asked to contribute, then capital and management must contribute materially as well.

A settlement that protects margins by sacrificing the workforce and the industrial base is not a responsible family-owner strategy. It is short-termism dressed up as discipline.

### To the regional state
You are right to oppose closures and forced redundancies. But your opposition must have consequences. If the board is being pushed toward a closure-led route, then your blocking position must be used to insist on a negotiated alternative.

A political statement is not enough. We need active support for a non-closure restructuring that keeps German sites open and jobs secure.

## On the economist’s comparison
The expert essentially said:
- closures produce stronger recurring savings,
- non-closure alternatives can work only if they are deep and durable,
- weak compromises are insufficient.

We accept that framing. What we do **not** accept is the leap from “non-closure must be deep” to “therefore closures are justified.”

That leap only works if management proves there is no serious alternative. It has not done so.

So the burden is on management now to show, in numbers, the following:
1. how much savings it expects from closures,
2. what a non-closure package would deliver,
3. what the gap is,
4. and why the gap cannot be closed without violating our red lines.

If they cannot show that, then the closure agenda is a choice, not a necessity.

## Our position remains unchanged
We remain open to a **fair shared-sacrifice package**. That means:
- employees contribute,
- but not alone;
- capacity is reduced,
- but without closures;
- costs come down,
- but without compulsory redundancies;
- job security is preserved,
- and the collective wage framework remains intact.

That is the only route we can negotiate seriously.

## What would move us
Only a package that meets all of the following:
- all German plants remain open,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- real, measurable capacity and cost reduction,
- binding investment commitments,
- and a durable employment perspective through 2030.

If management cannot live within that framework, then it should stop pretending this is a shared restructuring. It is then simply asking the workforce to pay for a decision it has not accepted.

## Bottom line
We are not retreating from our position.

- **No German plant closures**
- **No compulsory redundancies**
- **No dismantling of the collective wage framework**
- **Yes to real, shared sacrifice**
- **Yes to a durable negotiated restructuring**
- **No to workers bearing all the pain**

If the other side wants a deal, it must bring a credible non-closure plan with real numbers and real commitments. Until then, our line stands.
