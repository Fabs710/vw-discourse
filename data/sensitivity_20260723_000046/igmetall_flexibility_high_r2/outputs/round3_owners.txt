I’ll respond plainly.

## Where I agree
I agree with management, the economist, and the investors on the core diagnosis: **this is a structural competitiveness problem**, not a temporary cycle issue. If the German cost base stays materially above target, long-term value erodes. That is unacceptable from an ownership perspective.

I also agree with the Works Council, the union, and the state on one crucial point: **a prolonged industrial conflict would be value-destructive**. It would damage margins, delay implementation, and weaken the company’s reputation and execution capacity. That risk is real and should not be underestimated.

## Where I disagree
I do **not** agree with the employee side’s attempt to make **“no plant closures” and “no compulsory redundancies”** an absolute starting point.

Those are understandable red lines from a labour perspective, but from an ownership and governance perspective they cannot be treated as untouchable if they make a necessary restructuring impossible. If the company cannot restore competitiveness otherwise, then those red lines become a veto on value preservation. I cannot support that.

At the same time, I do **not** support management treating closure-led restructuring as the default answer without proof. A closure-first posture may be economically strong, but it also carries major execution and conflict risk. If a negotiated alternative can close the gap, that is preferable.

So my position is not “closures at any price.” It is:
- **real restructuring must happen,**
- **it must be quantified,**
- **it must be durable,**
- and **it must not permanently impair enterprise value or governance stability.**

## On the arguments from the employee side
The Works Council and union keep saying:
- no closures,
- no forced redundancies,
- no dismantling of job security.

I understand the intent, but I reject the implication that these conditions can stand regardless of economics. They have not yet offered a quantified alternative that proves the same savings can be achieved without closures or compulsory redundancies.

Their request for a plant-by-plant non-closure plan is reasonable in form, but it has to be matched by reality:
- How much capacity is actually removed?
- How much recurring cost is cut?
- How durable are the concessions?
- What prevents the cost base from creeping back up?

Without answers to those questions, “no closures” is not a restructuring plan. It is a refusal to engage with the scale of the problem.

## On management
Management is right to insist that **marginal tweaks are not enough**. The expert made that clear. Structural overcapacity and weak returns mean the company needs durable savings, not symbolic concessions.

But management should not confuse “necessary restructuring” with “the harshest available restructuring.” A package that destroys industrial peace and still does not deliver durable execution is not a win. If a non-closure solution can deliver comparable savings, management should accept it. If not, then harder measures remain on the table.

So I support management’s insistence on hard numbers, but not a blanket claim that closures are inevitable.

## On the regional state
The state is right to emphasize legitimacy, regional impact, and industrial base. Those are not side issues; they matter for long-term value and execution stability.

But I also need to be direct: **political opposition to closures is not the same as an economically credible alternative**. If the state wants to block closures, then it must help force a package that actually closes the savings gap. Otherwise it is only delaying the adjustment.

## On investors
I agree with the investors that the company cannot afford a cosmetic compromise. They are right to insist on structural savings and credible margins.

Where I would caution them is this: supporting the most disruptive route simply because it is numerically clean on paper can be a mistake if it triggers prolonged conflict, weakens execution, or damages the company’s domestic base. Ownership value is protected by durable competitiveness, not by short-term severity.

## What would move me
What would move me is not another statement of principle. It is a **side-by-side quantified comparison** of:
1. a closure-led restructuring, and
2. a deep non-closure restructuring.

I want to see:
- annual recurring savings,
- one-time restructuring costs,
- execution risk,
- and likely impact on regional employment.

If the non-closure package genuinely delivers comparable, durable savings, then I can support it. If it does not, then closures or other hard measures remain legitimate options and should not be ruled out in advance.

## My current position
So, to be explicit:

- **I support a durable restructuring.**
- **I support serious employee contributions.**
- **I support meaningful capacity reduction.**
- **I do not support cosmetic compromise.**
- **I do not support a settlement that leaves the German cost base structurally uncorrected.**
- **I do not support any outcome that permanently weakens enterprise value or governance control.**
