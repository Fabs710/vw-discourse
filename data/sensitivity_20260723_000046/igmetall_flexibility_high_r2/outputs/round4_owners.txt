I’ll be direct.

## Where I agree
I agree with management, the investors, and the expert on the central diagnosis: **the company has a structural competitiveness problem**. This is not a temporary dip, and a cosmetic compromise would destroy value. The German cost base must come down, and it must come down **durably**.

I also agree with the works council, union, and state on one important point: **prolonged industrial conflict would be value-destructive**. It would hurt margins, delay implementation, and weaken the company’s reputation and execution capacity. That risk is real.

## Where I disagree
I do **not** accept the employee side’s attempt to make **“no plant closures”** and **“no compulsory redundancies”** absolute starting points.

Those are understandable labour red lines, but they cannot be treated as untouchable if they prevent a necessary restructuring. If the company cannot restore competitiveness otherwise, then those red lines become a veto on value preservation. I cannot support that.

At the same time, I do **not** support management treating closure-led restructuring as the default answer without proof. If a negotiated alternative can close the gap, that is preferable from an ownership perspective because it may preserve stability and reduce conflict risk.

So my position remains:
- **real restructuring must happen,**
- **it must be quantified,**
- **it must be durable,**
- and **it must not permanently impair enterprise value or governance stability**.

## To the works council and union
You keep saying:
- no plant closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- no dismantling of job security.

I understand the intent. I do **not** accept it as a solution unless you can show, in hard numbers, how the same savings are achieved.

Right now, you are arguing from protections, not from an equivalent restructuring. That is not enough.

If you want closures off the table, then show:
- how much capacity is actually removed,
- how fixed costs come out,
- how headcount is reduced without forced redundancies,
- what durable labour-cost contribution is made,
- and how the cost base reaches a sustainable level by 2030.

Without that, your position is a refusal, not an alternative.

## To management
Management is right that marginal tweaks are not enough. The expert made that clear. But management should also be careful not to confuse **“necessary restructuring”** with **“the harshest available restructuring.”**

A closure-first route may look cleaner on paper, but if it triggers prolonged conflict and still does not deliver durable execution, it can destroy value. That is not a win for owners.

So I support your insistence on hard numbers, but I do **not** support a blanket claim that closures are inevitable.

## To the regional state
You are right to emphasize legitimacy, regional impact, and the industrial base. Those matter.

But I need to be blunt: **political opposition to closures is not the same as an economically credible alternative**. If you want closures off the table, then help force a package that actually closes the savings gap. Otherwise you are only delaying the adjustment.

## To investors
You are right to demand credibility. I share that demand.

Where I would caution you is this: backing the most disruptive route simply because it is numerically clean on paper can be a mistake if it triggers years of conflict, weakens execution, or damages the company’s domestic base. Ownership value is protected by durable competitiveness, not by short-term severity.

## What would move me
What would move me is not another statement of principle. It is a **side-by-side quantified comparison** of:
1. a closure-led restructuring, and
2. a deep non-closure restructuring.

I want to see:
- annual recurring savings,
- one-time restructuring costs,
- execution risk,
- and likely impact on regional employment.

If the non-closure package genuinely delivers comparable, durable savings, then I can support it. If it does not, then closures or other hard measures remain legitimate options and should not be ruled out in advance.

## On the expert input
The expert has confirmed what matters:
- closure-led restructuring usually delivers **higher recurring savings**,
- non-closure restructuring can work only if it is **deep and durable**,
- weak or symbolic concessions are not enough.

That supports management’s concern about competitiveness, but it does **not** prove that closures are the only solution.
