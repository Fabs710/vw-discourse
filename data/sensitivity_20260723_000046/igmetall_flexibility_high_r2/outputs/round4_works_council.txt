I will respond directly.

## First: my position has not changed
I still reject:
- **German plant closures**
- **compulsory redundancies**
- **any dismantling of the job-security framework**
- **a fait accompli presented as “restructuring”**

Those remain red lines.

## Where I agree with others
I agree with management, investors, the owners, the state, and the expert on one point only: **the company needs a durable restructuring**. Cosmetic fixes are useless.

I also accept the expert’s basic economic point that a serious alternative must be:
- deep,
- durable,
- and enforceable.

That is fair. But it does **not** justify closures.

## Where I disagree
### 1) Management keeps framing closures as if they were the reference solution
That is not neutral. It is a preferred option.

You have still not demonstrated that German plant closures are necessary. You have shown that closures are the **easiest** way to cut fixed cost. That is not the same thing.

If you want us to consider any non-closure path, then the burden is on you to prove that the gap cannot be closed otherwise **without violating our red lines**.

So far, that proof is missing.

### 2) Investors and owners are treating “economic equivalence” too narrowly
You keep talking as if only recurring savings on a spreadsheet matter.

They do matter. But so do:
- industrial peace,
- trust,
- execution quality,
- workforce motivation,
- plant know-how,
- brand reputation as an employer,
- and the long-term legitimacy of co-determination.

A closure-led solution may look cleaner on paper and still be worse in reality because it triggers conflict, destroys trust, and weakens implementation.

So no, closures are not automatically “more credible” just because they are harsher.

### 3) The state is right to oppose closures, but must stop speaking as if opposition alone is strategy
I welcome your position against closures and mass redundancies.

But you are also being tested now: will you actually use your blocking position, or just issue warnings?

If the company comes back with a closure plan instead of a genuine alternative, then the state must help block it. Otherwise the workforce will again be asked to carry the cost while political actors preserve flexibility.

## On the expert’s matrix
The expert has at least been honest: closure-led restructuring tends to produce higher recurring savings, but at high one-time and social cost.

That does **not** settle the matter.

What it means is:
- a non-closure solution must be serious,
- weak concessions are not enough,
- and management must bring real numbers.

I accept that.

What I reject is the implied conclusion that because a non-closure plan is hard, closures become justified by default. That logic is exactly what management wants to normalize.

## What a real non-closure package must include
If the other side wants to prove that the business can be fixed without closures and without forced layoffs, then bring a package with all of the following:

- **capacity reduction without site closure**
- **internal redeployment across the German footprint**
- **working-time and shift flexibility**
- **temporary, negotiated employee contributions**
- **real productivity improvements**
- **binding investment commitments**
- **a job guarantee through 2030**
- **full preservation of co-determination rights**

That is the minimum basis for discussion.

Anything less is not an alternative. It is a weakened demand for sacrifice.

## What we will not accept
Let me be absolutely explicit:

- No “voluntary” pressure campaigns that are really forced exits in disguise.
- No hidden layoffs through attrition, outsourcing, or transfer schemes dressed up as social responsibility.
- No deal that keeps all the pain on workers while capital keeps strategic optionality.
- No arrangement that breaks the historic guarantee and then asks us to trust again.

We have been down that road before. Trust was already damaged.

## To management
You say: show the numbers.

Fine. Then show them honestly, including:
- conflict risk,
- delay risk,
- lost productivity,
- reputational damage,
- and the cost of destroying the job-security framework.

Do not compare a closure-led plan to a fantasy non-closure plan that assumes frictionless execution. If you do that, the comparison is rigged.

If you want a fair comparison, then include the full costs of industrial conflict and trust destruction.

## To investors and owners
If you want durable value, then you cannot keep treating the workforce as the adjustment buffer of first resort.

A company that violates its own employment promises may win one restructuring and lose the trust needed for the next decade.

That is not long-term ownership. That is short-term extraction dressed up as discipline.

## To the regional state
You asked what concrete use of blocking rights I expect.

Here is the answer: **use them** if management returns with closures.

Not symbolically. Not after the decision is effectively made. Use them to force a genuine non-closure solution to the table.

Only a package that meets all of these:

- no compulsory redundancies,
- binding job security through 2030,
- meaningful capacity and cost reduction,
- real investment in German sites,

We do not accept the framing that says the choice is between:
- closures and layoffs, or
- an unrealistic status quo.

There is a third route: **deep restructuring without closures and without compulsory redundancies**.

Management has not proven that this route is impossible. Until it does, closures remain a choice — and one we will resist with all available means.
