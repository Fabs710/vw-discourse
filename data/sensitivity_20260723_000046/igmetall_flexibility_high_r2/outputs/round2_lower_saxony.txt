I will be direct.

The economist’s assessment confirms something important: **this is a structural problem, not a cosmetic one**. I do not dispute that the company needs a durable cost and capacity adjustment. But I also do **not** accept the conclusion that German plant closures and compulsory redundancies are therefore the default answer.

That is where I disagree with management and with those investors who are too quickly treating closures as economically “normal.” For a regional anchor company, the economic calculus cannot be separated from execution risk, social legitimacy, and the long-term damage to the company’s domestic base.

## Where I agree
- **Yes**, the company needs a serious restructuring.
- **Yes**, labor cost relief and capacity adjustment must be real and durable.
- **Yes**, temporary patches are not enough.
- **Yes**, the burden cannot fall only on the company.

## Where I disagree

### To management
You are right that the status quo is not acceptable. But you have **not yet proven** that closures and forced layoffs are the only credible way forward.

The economist said something very relevant: non-closure alternatives can work if they are sufficiently deep and durable. That means the question is not “closures or nothing.” The question is whether management is prepared to present a package that actually removes enough cost and capacity **without permanently damaging the company’s regional industrial footprint**.

So I want to be clear:

- I do **not** accept a closure-first logic as the starting point.
- I do **not** accept forced redundancies as an adjustment tool of first resort.
- I do **not** accept that the workforce must bear the main burden while ownership and management ask for patience.

If you want to argue that no non-closure solution exists, then bring the numbers. Plant by plant. Site by site. Show the gap, not slogans.

### To the works council and union
I support your insistence that **the company must not casually sacrifice German sites and jobs**. That is legitimate, and I will not back a proposal that guts regional employment.

But I also have to say this: simply repeating “no closures, no compulsory redundancies” is not yet a full solution. It is a necessary red line, but not a complete restructuring plan.

If you want the state and other shareholders to back a non-closure path, then the alternative must be credible:
- substantial capacity reduction,
- meaningful cost relief,
- working-time and flexibility measures,
- productivity improvements,
- and a clear investment and product commitment for the German plants.

That is the only way to make the non-closure route politically and economically defensible.

### To investors and owners
I understand the demand for competitiveness. But I do **not** accept a framing in which the only “credible” route is the harshest one.

The economist’s comparison also makes clear that open conflict carries real value destruction. So if you insist on closures, you must be able to show that no negotiated alternative can achieve comparable savings. Otherwise you are simply choosing the most disruptive option and calling it necessary.

That is not good stewardship.

## My position on the numbers
I agree with the moderator: we need to move from principles to scenarios.

From my side, the condition for supporting a restructuring is this:

1. **A quantified non-closure option must be presented first and taken seriously.**
2. It must show:
   - how much capacity is removed,
   - what annual recurring savings it delivers,
   - how headcount is aligned without compulsory redundancies,
   - what employee contribution is made,
   - and what binding investment/security package comes in return.
3. Only if that package clearly fails to close the gap should more drastic options be discussed.

I am **not** prepared to give a blank cheque for closures before that test is passed.

## What I would support
I could support a negotiated restructuring if it includes all of the following:
- no plant closure in Germany unless every viable alternative is genuinely exhausted,
- no compulsory redundancies as the primary adjustment instrument,
- meaningful and durable labor-cost and capacity measures,
- binding investment commitments for German sites,
- a real role for co-determination in implementation,
- and a package that preserves the company’s function as a regional anchor.

That is a serious framework. It is not a refusal to change; it is a refusal to destroy the regional base as the first resort.

## Bottom line
My position remains:

- **No to plant closures as a starting point**
- **No to compulsory redundancies**
- **Yes to a serious, quantified restructuring**
- **Yes to shared sacrifice**
- **Yes to a negotiated solution that protects jobs, sites, and long-term competitiveness**

If management can produce a credible non-closure package with real savings, I will engage constructively. If not, then we will have to conclude that the proposal is not a restructuring plan but a decision to shift the burden onto the workforce and the region. That I cannot support.
