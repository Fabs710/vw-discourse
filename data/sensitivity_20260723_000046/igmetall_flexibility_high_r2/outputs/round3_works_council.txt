## Where I agree
I agree with the repeated point that the company has a structural competitiveness problem and needs a durable restructuring. That is not in dispute.

I also agree that a cosmetic compromise would be worthless. If a plan merely delays the problem, it is not a solution.

And yes, I accept the economist’s core framing: a non-closure package must be judged on whether it delivers enough **recurring** savings, not on slogans or intentions.

## Where I disagree
### To management
You keep saying that because the company needs structural savings, closures must remain on the table. That is not a conclusion; it is your preferred route.

You have still not shown that German plant closures are the only credible way to achieve the required savings. You have also not shown that compulsory redundancies are unavoidable. You are asking us to accept the harshest measure first and justify alternatives later. That is not acceptable.

If you want a serious discussion, then bring a **plant-by-plant quantified non-closure plan** that includes:
- real capacity reduction,
- durable labor-cost relief,
- product allocation,
- investment in German sites,
- and a binding job guarantee through 2030.

Without that, your “necessity” claim remains unproven.

### To investors and owners
You say you want credibility. Fine. Then understand this: **industrial peace is part of credibility**. A plan that destroys trust, tears up commitments, and triggers prolonged conflict is not economically clean just because it looks tidy in a spreadsheet.

You are treating closures as if they were a neutral efficiency tool. They are not. They are a profound rupture with long-term costs:
- loss of trust,
- weaker execution,
- lower morale,
- industrial conflict,
- damage to the brand as an employer,
- and permanent erosion of co-determination legitimacy.

If you want sustainable value, then support a solution that protects sites and jobs.

### To the controlling family owners
Long-term ownership should mean long-term responsibility. That means not just defending margins, but defending the industrial base that made this company strong in the first place.

If you endorse closures and forced layoffs as the easy answer, you will be breaking trust with the workforce and with the regions that have supported this company for decades.

### To the regional state
I appreciate your stated opposition to closures and mass redundancies. But I need to be frank: opposition without consequences is not enough.

If you really mean that German plants should stay open, then use your blocking position accordingly. Do not wait for management to present a fait accompli and then call it “balanced.”

## On the economist’s comparison
The expert said something important: a non-closure option is only credible if it is **deep and durable**. That is true.

But that does **not** mean closure is automatically justified. It means management must demonstrate, with numbers, that no non-closure alternative exists that can do the job.

And so far, that has not happened.

### What I take from the expert
- A weak concession package is not enough.
- A serious non-closure package must include real capacity shrinkage.
- Fixed-cost leakage matters.
- Execution risk matters.

I accept all of that.

### What I do **not** accept
I do **not** accept the conclusion that because a non-closure package must be deep, closures are therefore acceptable. That leap is exactly what management is trying to smuggle in.

A serious non-closure package can still be built around:
- shift and working-time changes,
- internal redeployment,
- targeted voluntary instruments,
- temporary wage moderation tied to binding site and job guarantees,
- production reallocation,
- productivity improvements,
- and investment commitments.

What it cannot include is plant closure or forced layoffs. Those are not “just one more measure.” They are our red lines.
