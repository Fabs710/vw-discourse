Below is a **concise comparison matrix** of the two restructuring paths, using **industrial-economics assumptions** rather than company-specific verified figures. I cannot verify the firm’s internal numbers, so I state explicit assumptions.

## Assumptions used
- The company needs a **durable annual cost reduction** in the low-to-mid **€1bn range** to restore competitiveness.
- The comparison horizon is **3–5 years**, because that is the relevant window for restructuring value.
- “Recurring savings” means savings that persist after implementation.
- “One-time costs” include severance, site shutdown, asset write-downs, relocation, dual-running, and conflict/disruption costs where material.
- “Execution risk” reflects the probability that the planned savings actually land in full and on time.

## Concise matrix

| Dimension | Closure-led restructuring | Non-closure negotiated restructuring |
|---|---:|---:|
| **Annual recurring savings** | **High**: typically the largest structural saving, especially if a whole plant is removed and fixed overhead is cut. Rough order: **€700m–€1.5bn+ p.a.** depending on scope. | **Medium**: can be meaningful, but usually below closure-led unless capacity reduction is deep and labor-cost relief is substantial. Rough order: **€400m–€1.0bn p.a.** |
| **One-time restructuring costs** | **High**: severance, shutdown costs, asset impairments, logistics changes, reputational/industrial-relations disruption. Rough order: **€1bn–€3bn+**. | **Medium**: lower direct shutdown cost, but may still involve voluntary exits, retraining, productivity investment, and concession management. Rough order: **€0.3bn–€1.5bn**. |

| **Likelihood of full implementation** | **Moderate** if governance allows it; lower if blocking minorities and workforce vetoes are effective. | **Moderate to low** unless all parties commit to hard, enforceable terms; otherwise partial delivery is common. |
| **Impact on regional employment** | **High negative** in the affected regions: direct job losses at closed sites, plus local supplier spillovers. | **Lower immediate job loss**; employment is spread across sites, but headcount still typically falls through attrition, transfers, reduced hours, or voluntary exits. |
| **Industrial-relations stability** | Often **weaker in the short run** due to conflict risk. | Potentially **better if negotiated**, but only if the deal is credible and durable. |
| **Long-run competitiveness** | Usually strongest on pure cost reset. | Can be competitive if the package includes genuine capacity shrinkage, labor-cost moderation, and productivity gains. |

## Economic interpretation

### 1) Closure-led restructuring
**Evidence-based generalization:** closing a plant removes a chunk of fixed cost decisively. That is why closure-led plans usually produce the **largest recurring savings**.

**Inference:** if the company’s problem is mainly **structural overcapacity plus high fixed cost**, closures are the cleanest way to reset the cost base.

**Uncertainty:** the net value depends heavily on
- one-time shutdown costs,
- whether the plant’s output can be reassigned efficiently,
- and whether conflict erodes the expected gains.

### 2) Non-closure negotiated restructuring
A non-closure package can work **only if it is deep**. That usually means a combination of:
- capacity reduction without formal closure,
- shift and working-time changes,
- wage moderation or temporary labor-cost relief,
- voluntary exits / internal redeployment,
- product-mix shift to higher-margin vehicles,
- and productivity measures.

**Evidence-based generalization:** these tools can materially reduce cost, but they often leave more fixed cost in place than a closure does.

**Inference:** to match a closure-led plan, the non-closure package must be **more than a wage concession**; it must alter the operating footprint.

**Uncertainty:** the key risk is **savings leakage**—concessions may fade, productivity gains may not stick, and the cost base may creep back up by 2027–2030.

## Bottom line
If the question is **purely economic**, closure-led restructuring usually delivers **higher and more durable recurring savings**.

If the question is **economics plus execution**, a negotiated non-closure path can be superior **only if** it achieves:
1. material capacity reduction,
2. durable labor-cost relief,
3. credible productivity gains,
4. and binding implementation discipline.

If it does **not** achieve those four things, then it is typically **not economically equivalent** to closures; it is a softer adjustment with a higher risk of underdelivery.
