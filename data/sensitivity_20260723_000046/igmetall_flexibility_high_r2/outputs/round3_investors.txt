We agree on the diagnosis: this is a **structural competitiveness problem** and a cosmetic truce would destroy value. We also agree that prolonged industrial conflict is economically harmful.

Where we diverge is on the employee side’s attempt to make **“no plant closures” and “no compulsory redundancies”** absolute red lines **before** showing a financially equivalent alternative. From a capital-markets perspective, that is not sufficient.

## To the Works Council and Union
We respect the concern about social damage, but you are still arguing from **constraints**, not from **equivalent economics**.

If you want closures off the table, then show us a package that genuinely restores competitiveness:
- real and durable capacity reduction,
- measurable fixed-cost removal,
- meaningful headcount alignment,
- durable labour-cost relief,
- and a credible path back to target margins.

“Job security through 2030” and “investment commitments” are not, on their own, savings. They may be part of the bargain, but they do not close the gap unless the operating economics improve materially. If the company remains structurally overcosted, then the equity story remains broken.

So our position is simple: **no support for a settlement that preserves legacy structures while leaving margins impaired.**

## To Management
We agree that the status quo is unacceptable, and we accept the economist’s point that closures usually create the strongest recurring savings. But you still need to prove that the non-closure path is insufficient, not merely assert it.

Capital markets will not support a reckless confrontation if a deep negotiated alternative can achieve comparable savings. Execution risk matters. Industrial peace matters. A deal that looks efficient on paper but triggers years of conflict can destroy value too.

So we want the numbers:
1. closure-led package,
2. non-closure package,
3. expected recurring savings,
4. one-time restructuring costs,
5. probability of implementation success,
6. net effect on margins and return on sales.

Without that, “closures are necessary” is still a claim, not a case.

## To the Regional State and Family Owners
We agree that value destruction from open conflict must be avoided. But political resistance cannot become a veto on hard restructuring if the economics do not work otherwise.

At the same time, we do **not** support a default closure strategy if a serious negotiated path can deliver the same result. We are not asking for maximum severity; we are asking for **credible, durable margin repair**.

## Our position
We will support whichever route delivers:
- a **clear, durable return to competitive margins**,
- disciplined and structural cost reduction,
- deal certainty,
- and no cosmetic compromise.

What we will **not** support is:
- a settlement that leaves the cost base uncompetitive,
- a deal that only postpones the problem,
- or a package that relies on guarantees and political comfort while the operating economics remain weak.

## What we want next
Bring the **concise matrix**:
- closure-led scenario,
- non-closure negotiated scenario,
- recurring savings,
- one-time costs,
- execution risk,
- regional employment impact.

Until then, we remain skeptical of any proposal that rules out closures or compulsory redundancies without proving an economically equivalent alternative.
