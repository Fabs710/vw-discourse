I will be equally direct.

## Where I agree
I agree with management, the economist, investors, the owners, and the state on one fundamental point: **this company needs a durable restructuring, not a cosmetic pause**. The competitiveness problem is real, structural, and urgent.

I also agree that **a weak concession package would fail**. If a non-closure path is to be credible, it must be deep, enforceable, and economically meaningful. I am not disputing that.

And yes, I agree that **industrial peace has value**. A prolonged conflict would hurt the company, the sites, and the workforce. That is precisely why we have already shown willingness to make real contributions.

## Where I disagree
### To management
You keep presenting closures and compulsory redundancies as if they are the default “economic truth.” They are not. They are a choice.

You still have **not proven** that German plant closures are necessary. What you have shown is that closures are the fastest way to cut fixed cost. That is not the same as proving they are unavoidable.

And you are still trying to shift the burden onto the workforce first:
- first accept closures,
- first accept forced layoffs,
- first accept tearing up job security,
- and then maybe talk about alternatives.

That sequence is unacceptable.

If you want to argue necessity, then bring the full comparison:
- closure-led package,
- deep non-closure package,
- recurring savings,
- one-time costs,
- implementation risk,
- and the cost of industrial conflict and trust destruction.

If you leave out conflict and execution damage, the comparison is incomplete and biased.

### To investors and owners
You are right that the company needs a cost reset. But you are wrong if you treat the harshest option as automatically the best one.

A closure-led restructuring may look better in a spreadsheet, but it can also:
- destroy trust,
- trigger longer strikes,
- weaken morale and execution,
- damage the employer brand,
- and make future bargaining much harder.

That is not a side issue. It is part of the economics.

So if you want durable value, you should not back a plan that sacrifices the workforce and then assumes loyalty will survive untouched.

### To the regional state
I welcome your rejection of closures and mass redundancies. But I need to say this clearly: **your opposition must have consequences**.

If management comes back with a closure plan, you cannot just say you are concerned. You have to use your blocking position to force a negotiated alternative. Otherwise the state becomes a commentator, not a counterweight.

### To the works council
I support your red lines:
- no German plant closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- no dismantling of the job-security framework.

Those are correct. But I also agree with one point you made implicitly: **a real alternative has to be specific**.

That means we need plant-by-plant and measure-by-measure clarity on:
- capacity reduction without closures,
- internal redeployment,
- working-time and shift flexibility,
- productivity measures,
- temporary employee contributions,
- and investment commitments.

That is the path to a negotiated solution. Not closures. Not forced exits. But also not slogans.

## On the economist’s comparison
The expert has confirmed what we already knew:
- closure-led restructuring usually delivers **higher recurring savings**,
- non-closure restructuring can work only if it is **deep and durable**,
- weak or symbolic concessions are not enough.

That analysis supports our position, not management’s shortcut.

Because the expert did **not** say closures are the only option. The expert said non-closure must be serious. We accept that. So now management must stop hiding behind the easier route and prove why the deeper route cannot work.

## What the union is prepared to discuss
We remain prepared to discuss a **shared-sacrifice package** that includes:
- working-time flexibility,
- temporary labor-cost moderation,
- internal redeployment,
- shift and capacity changes,
- voluntary instruments,
- productivity improvements,
- and investment commitments for German sites.

We are **not** prepared to discuss:
- German plant closures,
- compulsory redundancies,
- or dismantling the collective wage framework.

That position has not changed.

## What would move us
Only a package that:
- preserves every German plant,
- rules out forced layoffs,
- protects the collective wage framework,
- delivers real and durable savings,
- and gives the workforce a binding perspective through 2030.

If you cannot deliver that, then you are not asking for a fair restructuring. You are asking the workers to pay for a strategy they did not choose.

## What I want next
If management is serious, then bring back a **verified non-closure scenario** with:
1. annual recurring savings,
2. one-time costs,
3. implementation risk,
4. capacity reduction by site,
5. and the measures that make it durable.

And include the real cost of conflict, because that is part of the decision.

My position is unchanged:
