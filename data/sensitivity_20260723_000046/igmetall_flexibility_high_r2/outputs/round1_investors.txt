We are aligned on one point: **this cannot be a cosmetic settlement**. If the outcome leaves the German cost base structurally uncompetitive, the company’s margins, valuation, and future investment capacity will remain impaired. That is unacceptable from an investor perspective.

Where I differ is on the implied assumption from the employee side and, to a lesser extent, the state side, that **“no closures / no compulsory redundancies”** can be treated as a starting boundary without first proving an economically equivalent alternative. From capital markets, that is backwards. The test is not whether a plan is socially preferable in the abstract; it is whether it delivers **credible, durable margin improvement** and a competitive cost base.

### To the Works Council and Union
I understand the insistence on social responsibility, and I agree that a prolonged strike or uncontrolled confrontation would destroy value. But I need to be explicit:  
**a package that protects every site and forbids compulsory job reductions, while only offering modest or temporary cost relief, will not restore competitiveness.**

If the employee side wants to avoid closures, it must put something on the table that is financially equivalent:
- real and measurable capacity reduction,
- meaningful and durable labour-cost relief,
- flexibility on working time and staffing,
- and a structure that permanently lowers fixed cost, not just defers it.

A promise of “investment and protection” is not enough if the margin problem remains. We would view that as a delay tactic, not a turnaround plan.

### To Management
I agree with your core diagnosis: the company does need structural action. But I would caution against using that diagnosis as a blank cheque for the most disruptive approach.  
**Plant closures and mass compulsory redundancies may be economically defensible only if management can show that alternative measures cannot deliver comparable savings with less execution risk.**

From an investor standpoint, the best restructuring is not the most brutal one; it is the one that:
- restores return on sales,
- reduces execution risk,
- avoids prolonged industrial disruption,
- and is believable to the market.

If you come back with a plan that depends on an all-out confrontation, that will damage equity value as surely as inaction would.

### To the Regional State and Family Owners
I agree that social legitimacy matters, and I also agree that a purely closure-led shock strategy could destroy value through conflict and reputational damage. But I would not support turning social legitimacy into a veto over hard restructuring.  
**Investor support depends on credible economics, not political comfort.**

If a negotiated alternative can achieve the same savings with less disruption, that is preferable. If not, then the company should not be forced into a settlement that preserves structures at the expense of long-term value.

### Our position
We are prepared to support a solution that is tough, durable, and credible. But our support depends on three conditions:

1. **A clear path back to competitive margins**
2. **A structurally lower cost base, not temporary concessions**
3. **Deal certainty and execution credibility without prolonged destructive conflict**

So, to be direct:  
- We **would not support** a shallow compromise that preserves too much capacity and leaves margins impaired.  
- We **would support** a serious restructuring, whether it comes via closures or a genuinely equivalent negotiated alternative, provided the savings are real and durable.  
- We **do not care about preserving every legacy structure** if that comes at the expense of long-term shareholder value.

If management and labor can produce an alternative that is truly equivalent on cost and capacity, we can back it. If not, then decisive structural cuts become necessary.
