I will be equally direct.

## Where I agree
I agree with management, the expert, the investors, and the controlling owners on one fundamental point: **this company needs a durable restructuring, not a cosmetic pause**. A weak compromise would simply postpone the problem and make the eventual correction harsher.

I also agree that **a serious non-closure plan must be quantified**. If the company remains structurally overcosted, then slogans about social partnership are not enough.

And I agree that **industrial conflict has real economic costs**. A prolonged confrontation would hurt everyone, including the workforce and the regional economy.

## Where I disagree
### On plant closures
I do **not** accept plant closures in Germany as the default answer.

Management keeps speaking as though closures are the natural benchmark and everything else must prove itself against that. That is not neutral analysis; it is a preference for the harshest route. You have not yet demonstrated that closures are unavoidable.

The same applies to the owners and investors: you are right to demand competitiveness, but wrong to treat closures as automatically justified simply because they are the fastest way to cut fixed cost.

For us, plant closures in the state are not a technical detail. They would mean:
- direct job losses,
- damage to supplier networks,
- weakened regional stability,
- and a blow to the company’s role as an industrial anchor.

That is precisely what we cannot accept.

### On compulsory redundancies
I also reject compulsory redundancies as a first resort. If headcount has to fall, then it must be through negotiated, socially responsible instruments first:
- voluntary exits,
- early retirement,
- internal redeployment,
- working-time changes,
- qualification and transfer measures.

Forced redundancies are the point where a restructuring stops being a negotiated adjustment and becomes a blunt transfer of risk onto the workforce.

### On the bargaining logic
I do not accept the argument that because a non-closure solution must be “deep,” closures therefore become legitimate. That leap is not justified.

The expert has said clearly:
- closure-led restructuring usually creates higher recurring savings,
- but non-closure restructuring can work if it is deep, durable, and binding.

That means the correct question is not “how fast can we close sites?” It is: **what exact package can close the savings gap without destroying regional employment and co-determination?**

That is the test. Not your preferred route.

## To management
You still have not given a convincing case that all viable alternatives are exhausted.

If you want us to consider a tougher employee contribution, then bring a **fully quantified non-closure package** that shows:
- site-by-site capacity reduction,
- recurring cost savings,
- fixed-cost removal,
- headcount alignment without forced redundancies,
- investment commitments for German plants,
- and durability through 2030.

If you cannot show that, then the closure agenda is not a necessity. It is a choice.

And I want to be very clear: if your comparison excludes the real cost of conflict, damage to trust, and long-term execution risk, then it is incomplete and biased. Those costs are not externalities. They are part of the decision.

## To the works council and union
I support your core red lines:
- **no German plant closures**
- **no compulsory redundancies**
- **no dismantling of the collective job-security framework**

Those are legitimate and, in my view, necessary protections.

But I also need to be frank: red lines alone do not produce a restructuring. The workforce side must also be prepared to support a package that is genuinely deep, not symbolic. That means discussing:
- working-time flexibility,
- temporary labor-cost moderation,
- internal redeployment,
- productivity improvements,
- and binding capacity reduction without closure.

That is the area where a negotiated solution can still be built.

## To the controlling family owners
Long-term ownership means long-term responsibility. I agree that value must be protected. But value is not protected by reflexively accepting the harshest restructuring path before the numbers are settled.

If closures and forced layoffs are used too quickly, the company may win a short-term cost correction but lose trust, execution quality, and legitimacy. That is a poor long-term strategy.

So I urge you to support a negotiated, quantified alternative first — not a closure-first mindset.

## To the institutional investors
You are right to insist on real savings and no cosmetic compromise. I share that.

But I would caution against a purely spreadsheet view. A plan that looks efficient on paper but destabilizes labour relations and regional confidence can destroy value in ways that are harder to model.

So yes: demand hard numbers. But also weigh the cost of conflict, the risk to execution, and the company’s legitimacy as a major employer.

## What I would support
I would support a restructuring if it includes:
- **meaningful capacity reduction**
- **durable recurring savings**
- **real employee contributions**
- **binding investment commitments for German sites**
- **preservation of co-determination**
- **no compulsory redundancies as the default tool**
- **and no German plant closures unless a truly equivalent alternative is proven impossible**

That is the standard I will hold.

## What would move me
Only a package that demonstrates, in hard numbers, that:
- all German plants remain open,
- jobs are protected as far as possible,
- savings are real and durable,
- and the company’s long-term competitiveness improves without gutting the regional base.

If that can be shown, then I am prepared to support difficult concessions.

If not, then the demand for closures is not necessity. It is preference.

## Bottom line
I am not retreating from my position.

- **No blanket acceptance of German plant closures**
- **No acceptance of compulsory redundancies as the first resort**
- **Yes to a serious restructuring**
- **Yes to shared sacrifice**
- **Yes to a quantified non-closure alternative**
- **No to sacrificing jobs and sites as the default adjustment mechanism**

Bring the numbers — including conflict risk and long-term damage — and then we can judge whether a non-closure path is truly insufficient. Until then, closures remain a choice, and one I will continue to oppose.
