## To the Works Council
I hear your red lines. I do **not** accept them as a substitute for a viable restructuring.

You keep saying:
- no plant closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- no dismantling of job security.

That is a position. It is **not** yet a solution.

You say management has not proven closures are necessary. Fair enough — but you have also not proven that a non-closure package can close the gap. That burden is now on the table. A credible alternative cannot be built on the assumption that the company can remain structurally overcosted while somehow finding competitiveness elsewhere.

If you want closures off the table, then show me, concretely:
- how much capacity is removed,
- how fixed costs come out,
- how headcount is reduced without forced redundancies,
- what labor-cost contribution is made,
- and how the German cost base reaches a sustainable level by 2030.

Not slogans. Not trust appeals. Numbers.

## To the Union
You say you are ready for real contributions. Good. Then let’s be honest about what “real” means.

Temporary wage moderation, working-time flexibility, and voluntary instruments are useful **only if they produce durable structural relief**. They are not enough if they merely smooth the problem for two years and then hand us the same cost base again. That would be a cosmetic truce, and I will not sign up to that.

You also keep asking us to include conflict and trust destruction in the comparison. I agree that conflict has costs. But do not use that as a shield against hard restructuring. A strike risk does not make an uneconomic structure suddenly affordable. It only means the negotiated route has to be robust enough to justify it.

So yes:
- I accept that industrial relations matter.
- I accept that a weak deal is worthless.
- I accept that execution risk must be included.

But no:
- I do **not** accept that this justifies ruling out closures and forced redundancies in advance.
- I do **not** accept that the burden of adjustment can be avoided by redefining cost pressure as a trust issue.

## To the Regional State
You are right on one point: a cosmetic compromise would be irresponsible.

Where I disagree is when you speak as though political opposition alone can preserve the industrial base. It cannot. If German operations remain structurally too expensive, then the base erodes anyway — through lost investment, lost margins, and eventually lost relevance.

You ask us not to gut German capacity. I am not interested in gutting anything. I am interested in keeping the company viable. That may require hard site decisions if a credible non-closure alternative does not exist.

If you want to prevent closures, then use your influence to deliver a package that is actually economically equivalent. Otherwise you are protecting the appearance of industrial strength while leaving the underlying weakness untouched.

## To the Controlling Family Holding
You rightly stress long-term value. I agree — and that is exactly why we cannot preserve an uncompetitive footprint.

Long-term ownership means more than avoiding conflict. It means restoring margin, funding future products, and ensuring the company can invest through 2030. That requires structural cost reduction, not symbolic gestures.

You are cautious about closures at any price. So am I. But the reverse is also true: I will not let “no closures” become a veto on competitiveness.

If a non-closure solution is genuinely equivalent, I’m open. If it is not, then we need to be honest about the consequences.

## To the Institutional Investors
You ask for a clear return to competitive margins. That is exactly what management is trying to deliver.

I agree with you that a cosmetic settlement is unacceptable. I also agree that execution risk matters and that a closure-led route is not automatically better if it triggers prolonged conflict. But you cannot stop there. You still need to answer the central economic question: does the non-closure package actually close the savings gap?

If it does not, then it is not a serious alternative. It is delay.

## On the expert input
The expert has now said twice what I have been saying throughout:
- closure-led restructuring usually delivers higher recurring savings,
- non-closure can work only if it is deep and durable,
- weak concessions are insufficient.

That does **not** mean closures are the only answer. But it does mean the employee side must stop pretending that preserving all sites and rejecting redundancies can coexist with full competitiveness unless there is a quantified mechanism to make that true.

That mechanism still has not been presented.

## My position, plainly stated
I remain firm on the essentials:

- I will not accept a cosmetic compromise.
- I will not accept a deal that leaves the German cost base structurally above target.
- I will not accept a settlement that preserves all legacy structures while demanding investment for the future.
- I am open to alternatives to plant closures **only if** they deliver comparable, durable savings.
- I am open to alternatives to compulsory redundancies **only if** they still produce real headcount and capacity alignment.

## What would move me
Only one thing:
A **verified, plant-by-plant non-closure package** that shows, in hard numbers:
1. annual recurring savings,
2. one-time costs,
3. implementation risk,
4. capacity reduction,
5. headcount reduction,
6. and the path to competitiveness by 2030.

If that package truly matches the economic effect of closures, then I will consider it seriously.

If it does not, then the arguments against closures become political preferences, not economically sufficient solutions.

So I’ll say it as clearly as possible:
