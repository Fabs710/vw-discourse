From an industrial-economics and restructuring perspective, the key point is this:

## Core economic conclusion
A **non-closure restructuring can be economically credible**, but only if it delivers a **similar present-value improvement** to a closure-led plan once you include:
- recurring savings,
- one-time restructuring costs,
- execution delay,
- industrial conflict risk,
- and the probability of full implementation.

If the non-closure plan does **not** close most of the gap on those terms, then it is not economically equivalent, even if it is politically or industrial-relations preferable to some parties.

## What I would compare
I would frame the choice over a **3–5 year horizon**, because that is where most restructuring value is determined.

### 1) Closure-led scenario
Typically gives:
- higher and more durable fixed-cost reduction,
- clearer capacity removal,
- stronger signal to capital markets,
- but also:
  - high one-time severance/closure costs,
  - legal/process friction,
  - longer conflict risk,
  - and possible productivity loss during execution.

### 2) Deep non-closure scenario
Can still deliver substantial savings through:
- voluntary exits and early retirement,
- redeployment,
- shift/working-time changes,
- wage moderation or benefit resets,
- product-mix improvement,
- plant productivity gains,
- asset rationalization,
- and selective capacity reductions without full site closure.

But it usually has two constraints:
- savings may be **less certain** or **slower**,
- and if it depends on repeated bargaining, the durability is weaker unless it is hard-wired into binding commitments.

## Minimum economic test for a credible non-closure package
In practice, I would expect a credible alternative to include all of the following:

1. **Material recurring savings**
   - not a one-off accounting gain,
   - but a durable reduction in the annual cost base.

2. **Real capacity reduction**
   - even if sites stay open,
   - the company still needs fewer units of installed capacity than today.

3. **Headcount reduction without forced layoffs, if possible**
   - but with enough volume to matter financially.
   - If the labor force is large relative to output, modest attrition alone is rarely sufficient.

4. **Durability through 2030**
   - if the savings disappear after a short grace period, the restructuring is only deferred.

5. **Enforcement mechanism**
   - milestone-based triggers, escrow-like commitments, or pre-agreed fallback measures.
   - Without enforcement, the package is too weak for capital to treat as equivalent.

## How to think about the trade-off
The real comparison is not:
- “closures vs no closures”

It is:
- **higher certain savings plus higher disruption**
versus
- **lower disruption plus lower certainty and possibly lower savings**

In economic terms, the closure plan often has:
- higher expected savings,
- but also higher expected implementation cost and risk.

A non-closure package can still win if it:
- closes most of the savings gap,
- avoids a prolonged strike,
- preserves productivity,
- and keeps the investment program on track.
