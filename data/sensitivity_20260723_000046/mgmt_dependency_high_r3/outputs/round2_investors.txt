We have enough information now to be concrete.

## Our position
As capital-market shareholders, we will support **the option that credibly restores competitive margins, durable profitability, and deal certainty**. We are **not** attached to closures as an ideology, but we are also **not** willing to back a compromise that leaves the cost base structurally impaired.

## Where we agree
We agree with management and the controlling family on the core economics:
- the German cost base is too high,
- structural overcapacity is real,
- and temporary or symbolic concessions are not enough.

We also agree with the state and labor on one important point:
- a prolonged strike spiral would be value-destructive,
- and a solution that permanently damages the industrial base would also be bad for value.

So yes: **we want savings, but we want them in a way that is durable and implementable**.

## Where we disagree with labor
The works council and union continue to frame **“no closures, no compulsory redundancies, no framework change”** as the baseline. That is not a financial solution; it is a defensive position.

The finance assessment makes the problem plain:
- non-closure measures can save money,
- but they usually fall short unless they remove enough fixed cost and capacity,
- and without that, savings become partial, fragile, or reversible.

So we cannot support a package that simply preserves all sites and all protections while expecting the market to reward it. That would be a cosmetic settlement, and we would treat it as such.

## Where we disagree with management
At the same time, management should not overread the finance case as a blank cheque for blunt closures.

Yes, closures are powerful economically. But if management walks in with closure-first tactics as the default, it risks:
- a strike,
- governance resistance,
- political backlash,
- and delayed execution that destroys the very savings it wants.

From our standpoint, a closure-based plan is acceptable **only if it is truly necessary and better than the alternatives on a risk-adjusted basis**. Not because it is easiest to announce.

## What we think is credible
The most credible path is a **serious hybrid package**, but only if it is genuinely structural.

That means:
- real capacity reduction,
- some site rationalization if required,
- meaningful labor-cost contribution,
- management and overhead discipline,
- and quantified savings that close the margin gap on a clear timetable.

A hybrid package is not attractive because it sounds balanced. It is attractive only if it:
1. produces durable savings,
2. is implementable without endless renegotiation,
3. and materially improves return on sales.

If it doesn’t, then it is just a slower version of the same problem.

## Our requirements
Before we support any outcome, we need management to show, site by site:
- the cost gap,
- expected savings under closure, non-closure, and hybrid scenarios,
- the one-time charges,
- the employment impact,
- and the strike/delay risk.

And we need the analyst to stress-test whether the proposed hybrid package truly closes the margin gap **after** execution risk and one-time charges.

[SUMMON: management to provide a site-by-site hybrid restructuring proposal with quantified savings, one-time costs, timing, and employment effects; and the analyst to stress-test whether it closes the margin gap with acceptable strike risk]

## Bottom line
Our support goes to the package that gives us:
- **durable cost reduction**
- **credible margin recovery**
- **implementation certainty**
- **minimal value destruction from prolonged conflict**

We will not support:
- a cosmetic no-closure deal that leaves the company overcosted,
- or a rushed closure strategy that triggers unnecessary and avoidable destruction of value.

If the hybrid package is real, quantified, and strong enough, we can back it. If it is merely a political compromise dressed up as restructuring, we will not.
