From the perspective of dispersed capital-market shareholders, the key test is not whether a proposal is politically comfortable; it is whether it **credibly restores margins, competitiveness, and valuation**.

So let me be direct.

### Where I agree
I agree with management, and to a lesser extent the controlling family, on the core diagnosis:  
- the German cost base is too high,  
- capacity appears structurally too large for the market, and  
- cosmetic savings will not be enough.

I also agree with the state shareholder on one important point: a destructive confrontation or an outcome that weakens the industrial base for good would be value-destructive. Prolonged strikes, reputational damage, and continued margin erosion are not acceptable from an investor standpoint either.

### Where I disagree
I do **not** support a settlement that simply preserves the current structure and calls it restructuring. If the package leaves the company with:
- an uncompetitive cost base,
- only temporary or reversible savings,
- or no meaningful capacity reduction,

then that is not a solution. It is delay.

I also do **not** support a “no hard measures ever” stance. I understand the social and political objections, but from a shareholder perspective, if the economics require permanent structural change, then that reality cannot be vetoed indefinitely by slogans.

### What matters to us
We need three things, in this order:

1. **Durable savings at scale**  
   The result must materially improve the return-on-sales trajectory. Anything marginal or temporary is insufficient.

2. **Credibility and timing**  
   Savings need to arrive on a timetable that actually matters for margins and investment capacity, not years later.

3. **Deal certainty**  
   The market will discount endless conflict. A prolonged strike or open-ended negotiation is itself value-destructive.

### On plant closures and compulsory redundancies
We are not ideologically attached to closures. If equivalent savings can be achieved without them, that is preferable. But “equivalent” has to mean genuinely equivalent in:
- scale,
- durability,
- and implementation certainty.

If alternative measures cannot match that, then insisting on no closures or no forced redundancies becomes financially indefensible. The company cannot be asked to preserve every legacy guarantee if that leaves the cost base structurally impaired.

### On labor contributions
A meaningful employee contribution can be acceptable, but only if it is part of a credible full package. It cannot be symbolic, and it cannot simply postpone the same problem.

At the same time, I would caution management: if the burden is placed only on labor while overhead, governance, and operational discipline remain untouched, that will also undermine confidence. Investors want evidence of broad-based discipline.

### On the positions of the other parties
- **Works Council / Union:** your red lines may be understandable, but if they prevent any structurally effective solution, then they are not sustainable. Protecting every site at any cost is not a viable investment case.
- **State shareholder:** your concern for regional impact is valid, but value preservation also requires a competitive company. The state cannot reasonably support a structure that bakes in chronic underperformance.
- **Controlling family:** we welcome support for a rigorous restructuring, but we will judge you by results, not by rhetoric about responsibility. If you have control, then use it to deliver a credible solution, not to preserve ambiguity.
- **Management:** bring us a package that quantifies the gap, shows the savings, and proves implementation. If you cannot, the market will continue to punish the stock.

### Bottom line
We will support **whatever convincingly restores competitive margins and durable profitability**. That could include closures, but it does not have to—if and only if the alternative is truly equivalent and certain.

What we will not support is:
- a cosmetic compromise,
- a prolonged strike spiral,
- or a restructuring that leaves the company structurally uncompetitive.

If the eventual outcome does not materially improve margins and reduce costs in a durable way, then capital will go elsewhere.
