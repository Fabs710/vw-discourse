### 2) Typical restructuring economics, in bounded terms
Without company-specific figures, only ranges are defensible:

#### Closure-based scenario
- **Highest recurring savings** and strongest certainty on fixed-cost removal.
- Typical annual savings can be materially larger than non-closure packages because they remove:
  - entire site overhead,
  - duplicated logistics and support,
  - and a large block of direct labor and fixed manufacturing cost.
- **One-time charges are also highest**:
  - impairment and exit costs can be very large,
  - severance/retirement cost concentration is high,
  - payback may still be attractive if the site is genuinely underutilized.

#### Non-closure scenario
- Savings usually come from:
  - headcount reduction via voluntary exits,
  - wage moderation / deferred pay,
  - working-time flexibility,
  - overhead cuts,
  - capacity reduction through shifts, lines, or product rationalization.
- The key limitation is that it often leaves:
  - site fixed costs,
  - part of the labor structure,
  - and legacy complexity in place.
- So it often closes only **part** of the gap unless the measures are unusually deep.

#### Hybrid scenario
- Can be economically optimal if it combines:
  - one or more selective closures or line eliminations,
  - plus non-closure measures at retained sites.
- Usually offers:
  - more savings than a pure non-closure deal,
  - less social disruption than a full closure strategy,
  - but also more bargaining and execution complexity.
- The risk is that “hybrid” becomes a diluted compromise, which weakens savings without fully avoiding disruption.

### 3) The key analytical test
The right question is not “closure or no closure?”  
It is:

**Does the non-closure or hybrid package close the structural EBIT margin gap after:**
1. one-time charges,
2. implementation delays,
3. lower-than-planned voluntary uptake,
4. and strike/execution risk?

If not, then it is not economically equivalent, even if it is politically easier.

### 4) What would make a non-closure plan credible
A non-closure package can be credible only if it includes most of the following:
- material fixed-cost reduction, not just variable pay restraint
- meaningful and durable capacity removal
- high-certainty labor cost reduction
- binding overhead cuts in management and support functions
- credible voluntary exit uptake
- capital expenditure rationalization
- enforceable implementation milestones

### 5) Decision rule
A rational comparison should be based on:
- **3-year cash impact**
- **5-year NPV**
- **post-restructuring breakeven volume**
- **probability-weighted savings delivery**

That will show whether:
- closure is economically necessary,
- a hybrid can match it,
- or whether the non-closure route leaves a savings gap too large to justify.
