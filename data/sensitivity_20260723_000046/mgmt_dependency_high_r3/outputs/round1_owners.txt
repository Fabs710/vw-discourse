I agree with management on the central diagnosis: the company cannot preserve its long-term value if the German cost base remains structurally uncompetitive and capacity stays above demand. That reality does not disappear because it is politically uncomfortable.

I also agree with the state shareholder on one important point: a solution that permanently weakens the German industrial base would be value-destructive, not value-preserving. As controlling owners, we do not have an interest in “winning” a restructuring at the price of damaging the core asset.

Where I do **not** agree is with any position that turns “no closures” or “no compulsory redundancies” into absolute taboos regardless of the economics. If the numbers truly require structural change, then we cannot rule out every hard measure in advance and still claim to be acting responsibly toward the enterprise.

At the same time, I do **not** support a management approach that treats plant closures and forced layoffs as the default or only serious option. That would be too blunt, too destabilising, and potentially self-defeating. The burden of proof is on management to show that any drastic move is genuinely necessary, not merely convenient.

My position is therefore this:

- **We support a credible restructuring that restores margins and competitiveness.**
- **We do not support cosmetic savings that postpone the problem.**
- **We do not support value-destroying conflict or an open-ended strike spiral.**
- **We are open to alternatives to closures if they deliver comparable, durable savings.**
- **If they do not, then stronger structural measures must remain on the table.**

What I want to see from management is a package that is economically rigorous and governance-ready:
1. a clear quantification of the gap that must be closed,
2. a comparison of closures versus alternative measures,
3. a credible timeline for savings,
4. a labour-cost contribution that is meaningful,
5. and a plan that protects the company’s ability to invest in future products.

To the works council and union: I respect the social concerns, but I cannot endorse an approach that demands full site and job guarantees through 2030 regardless of economic reality. That would lock in weakness. If you want the support of the controlling shareholder, you need to help produce a settlement that is durable, not just defensive.

To the state shareholder: I share your concern about regional spillovers, but the state cannot ask the company to absorb structural losses indefinitely. Regional responsibility includes making sure the company survives as a competitive industrial employer, not just preserving every legacy structure.

So I am not giving a blank cheque to management, and I am not giving a veto to maximalist labor demands either. My support goes to the option that genuinely restores competitiveness while preserving as much value, stability, and industrial substance as possible. If that requires difficult measures, then we should be honest about that now rather than pretend it can be avoided by rhetoric alone.
