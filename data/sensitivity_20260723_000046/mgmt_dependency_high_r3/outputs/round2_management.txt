I will respond directly, because we are past the stage for slogans.

## First: where I agree

I agree with all parties on one foundational point: **this company needs a durable solution, not a temporary patch**. We are not debating whether there is a problem. There is one.

I also agree that **a destructive labor conflict would be value-destroying**. Prolonged strikes, loss of output, and further erosion of customer and investor confidence help no one.

And I agree that **we should test whether alternatives to plant closures can achieve the required savings**. I am not wedded to closures as an ideological goal. I am wedded to the economics.

## Where I disagree, strongly

### 1) “No plant closures” as an absolute
I cannot accept that as a blanket rule.

If the German network is structurally overbuilt, then we cannot pre-commit to preserving every site regardless of market reality. That would not be responsible management; it would be managed decline.

The Works Council and Union keep saying closures are “permanent destruction.” That is true. But so is the permanent destruction of competitiveness if we keep excess capacity and an uncompetitive cost base in place. If we do nothing structural, the damage will simply be slower and more diffuse — but no less real.

### 2) “No compulsory redundancies” as a hard veto
I cannot guarantee that either.

I would prefer a solution that avoids forced redundancies. But I will not pretend that there is an unlimited shield around current headcount if we cannot secure equivalent savings through other measures. If the workforce insists that every adjustment must be voluntary, reversible, and painless, then the numbers will not close.

### 3) Binding job and site guarantees through 2030 as a starting point
That is not compatible with the scale of change we need.

I understand why labor wants security. But if we lock in the current structure through 2030, we lock in the current cost problem through 2030 as well. That is not a restructuring; it is postponement.

## On the options

### Option A: Closure-based restructuring
This remains on the table.

Not because it is the first preference, but because if the savings gap is large enough, then structural overcapacity has to come out of the system somewhere. I do not accept that the mere existence of political resistance makes closures impossible.

That said, I am not here to announce closures as a fait accompli. I am saying: if no alternative delivers comparable savings, then closures remain a necessary tool.

### Option B: Non-closure restructuring with comparable savings
This is the only alternative I am prepared to consider seriously — but only if it is **truly comparable** in three respects:

1. **Scale**  
   It must close the cost gap materially, not symbolically.

2. **Durability**  
   It must be structural, not a temporary deferral.

3. **Certainty of implementation**  
   It must be deliverable without endless renegotiation, workarounds, or legal drift.

If the employee side wants to avoid closures, then it must come with a package that actually changes the economics:
- real capacity reduction,
- meaningful labor-cost contribution,
- productivity increases,
- overhead cuts,
- and a binding implementation path.

### Option C: Forced layoffs / compulsory redundancies
Not my preferred route, but I will not rule it out if necessary.

Again: I prefer voluntary exits, working-time solutions, and negotiated contributions. But “preferred” is not the same as “sufficient.” If the only way to restore competitiveness is a deeper workforce reduction, then we have to be honest about that.

## To the Works Council
You say management has not proven closures are unavoidable. I say the opposite: the burden is on labor to show how you will deliver the same economics without closures or forced reductions.

Right now, your proposal still sounds like:
- keep all sites,
- preserve employment,
- protect guarantees,
- and ask management to find the money elsewhere.

That is not a full solution.

If you want me to take a non-closure path seriously, then bring a quantified package:
- exact capacity reduction,
- exact labor-cost contribution,
- exact savings timeline,
- exact investment commitments,
- and exact governance mechanism to ensure delivery.

Without that, “no closures” is just a slogan.

## To the Union
I acknowledge that strikes are costly for both sides. But if you continue to treat every structural adjustment as a breach of principle, then you are making industrial conflict more likely, not less.

You say management and owners must contribute. Fine — and they will. Overhead, product timing, investment discipline, and management compensation are all part of the picture. But do not confuse those contributions with the scale of structural cost reduction this company requires. They are part of the answer, not the whole answer.

## To the Regional State Government
I understand your political and regional responsibility. But I must be blunt: if the state shareholder uses its blocking position to prevent any meaningful restructuring, it will be blocking the company’s future, not protecting it.

We can and should seek a socially sustainable solution. But preserving regional industrial identity is not the same as preserving every current site and every current guarantee. If the company remains uncompetitive, the regional base will weaken anyway — only more slowly and under worse conditions.

## To the Controlling Family
I appreciate your emphasis on value preservation and your rejection of cosmetic compromise. But value preservation requires more than demanding seriousness from management; it requires support for actual structural change.

If you back a deal that avoids hard decisions but leaves the company overcosted, that is not stewardship. If you back a deal that creates endless confrontation without delivering savings, that is not stewardship either.

You need to support a package that is governance-ready and economically meaningful. I am prepared to be flexible on the form — not on the substance.

## To Institutional Investors
You are right on the key point: **the outcome must materially improve margins and restore competitiveness**. A settlement that looks politically neat but leaves the cost base impaired will not hold in the market.

Where I differ from you is on the idea that closures are the implicit benchmark of seriousness. They are not the only serious answer, but any alternative has to be just as decisive.

## My position, plainly stated

I am prepared to negotiate:
- capacity reductions,
- voluntary exit programs,
- working-time flexibility,
- temporary labor-cost contributions,
- management overhead reductions,
- and investment commitments tied to future product allocation.

I am **not** prepared to accept:
- a deal that preserves all sites regardless of economics,
- a blanket guarantee against plant closures,
- a blanket guarantee against compulsory redundancies,
- or a settlement that simply shifts the burden onto management while leaving the structural cost problem untouched.

## What I need next
Before we go any further, I want a **quantified comparison** of three scenarios:
1. closure-based restructuring,
2. non-closure restructuring with equivalent savings,
3. a hybrid package.

For each, I want:
- total savings,
- timing of savings,
- implementation certainty,
- labor impact,
- investment capacity created,
- and risk of strike / delay / leakage.

If the non-closure route can really match the economics, I will consider it. If it cannot, then we should stop pretending that slogans can replace restructuring.
