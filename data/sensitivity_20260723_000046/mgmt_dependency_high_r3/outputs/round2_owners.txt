We have enough now to separate slogans from economics.

## My view

I agree with management on the core point: **the company needs a durable reduction in the German cost base and a real improvement in margins**. I also agree with the analyst that, if the overcapacity problem is structural, then purely temporary labor concessions are unlikely to be enough.

At the same time, I do **not** accept the idea that closures are automatically the first or only credible answer. The finance assessment makes clear that closures are economically powerful, but it also shows that a **hybrid package** can get materially closer to closure economics than a purely defensive non-closure deal. That matters.

## Where I disagree with labor

To the Works Council and Union: your absolute “no closures, no compulsory redundancies, no change to the framework” position is understandable politically, but it is **not yet a complete economic answer**.

The analyst’s ranges show why:
- a non-closure package can help,
- but unless it removes enough fixed cost and capacity, it will likely leave a **material savings gap**,
- and a deal that leaves the company structurally overcosted is not protection, it is delay.

So I cannot support a position that rules out all hard measures in advance and then asks ownership and management to carry the full burden. That would be value-destructive.

## Where I disagree with management

I also do **not** endorse a closure-first posture as the default.
The finance input confirms that closures have high certainty and strong savings, but they also carry:
- major one-time charges,
- higher strike risk,
- and lasting damage if used bluntly.

If management goes in with closures as a fait accompli, it will likely trigger exactly the instability we want to avoid. That is not stewardship either.

## What I think is the serious path

The serious path is a **hybrid restructuring**, not a cosmetic compromise:
- real capacity reduction,
- some site rationalization if needed,
- meaningful labor-cost contribution,
- overhead and management discipline,
- and investment commitments tied to the retained German footprint.

That is the only route I see that can plausibly:
1. restore competitiveness,
2. avoid a prolonged strike spiral,
3. preserve enough industrial substance in Germany,
4. and protect long-term enterprise value.

## What I am prepared to support

I would support a package with the following characteristics:
- **material, durable savings**
- **implementation certainty**
- **no symbolic concessions disguised as restructuring**
- **a fair burden split across workforce, management, and owners**
- **site preservation wherever economically defensible**
- **targeted rationalization where the economics truly require it**

I am open to:
- voluntary exits,
- working-time adjustments,
- temporary wage restraint or deferred components,
- overhead reductions,
- product and investment commitments,
- and a hybrid network adjustment if it is genuinely necessary.

## What I will not support

I will not support:
- a purely cosmetic non-closure arrangement that leaves the cost problem in place,
- an open-ended guarantee that prevents any structural correction,
- or a package that shifts the adjustment entirely onto the workforce while leaving the network untouched.

Equally, I will not support management using “competitiveness” as a blank cheque for unnecessary destruction of productive capacity.

## Bottom line

The finance assessment has moved the discussion in one important direction: **non-closure can be part of the answer, but it must be judged by hard numbers, not by principle alone**.

So my position is:
- **No to a weak no-closure compromise**
- **No to closure as the automatic default**
- **Yes to a serious hybrid restructuring that actually closes the gap**

If the parties want my backing as controlling owner, they need to come back with a **quantified hybrid package** showing:
- savings by site and function,
- one-time charges,
- timing,
- employment impact,
- and why the resulting package is more durable than confrontation.
