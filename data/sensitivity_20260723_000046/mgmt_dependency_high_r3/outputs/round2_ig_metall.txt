We can be very clear as well.

## Where we agree
We agree with the Works Council on the essentials:
- **no compulsory redundancies**
- **no German plant closures**
- **no dismantling of the collective wage framework**
- **no one-sided burden shifting onto the workforce**

We also agree that a purely symbolic compromise would be pointless. If the company needs real savings, then the package must be structurally meaningful, not a delay tactic.

## Where we disagree
What we do **not** accept is management’s attempt to make closure and forced layoffs the default benchmark for “seriousness.”

That is not the only serious way to restructure a company.

We also do **not** accept the assumption that the workforce must absorb the main pain because labour costs are visible and easy to cut. If management and owners want a durable settlement, then they must contribute visibly and materially too:
- overhead cuts
- management restraint
- investment discipline on the owner side
- and a real commitment to the German sites

## On management’s arguments
Management says the cost gap is structural, and yes, that may be true. But “structural problem” does **not** automatically mean “close plants and cut jobs.”

What has not been proven is that there is no alternative package capable of closing the gap with less social damage.

So the burden of proof remains with management:
- show the quantified gap,
- show why non-closure alternatives are insufficient,
- show comparable savings and timing,
- and show that the burden is being shared fairly.

Until then, closures and compulsory redundancies are not a credible necessity; they are a preferred route.

## On the controlling family
You say you want value preservation. Then do not back a strategy that creates a strike, destroys trust, and tears up the company’s social compact.

A settlement that breaks the workforce and poisons industrial relations is not value-preserving. It may look tough on paper and weak in reality.

If you want my respect as a controlling shareholder, then use your influence to force management away from the closure-first logic and toward a package based on:
- capacity reduction without closure,
- voluntary instruments,
- working-time flexibility,
- and a genuine owner contribution.

## On the state shareholder
You are right to emphasize site preservation and regional spillovers. But that must translate into hard leverage, not just political statements.

The state should use its blocking position to insist that:
- no plant closure is decided over the workforce’s objections,
- no compulsory redundancies are imposed,
- and any restructuring must come with binding employment and investment guarantees.

## On institutional investors
You want durable savings at scale. So do we. But durable does **not** mean destructive.

If you insist that closures are the only “credible” form of seriousness, then you are ignoring the equally real costs of:
- strikes,
- reputational damage,
- loss of skilled labour,
- and the destruction of future production capability.

A deal that protects sites and jobs while still producing real savings is more credible, not less, because it is more stable.

## Our position on the options
### 1) Closure-based restructuring
**Rejected.**  
Not because we deny the need for change, but because closures are permanent destruction, and the company has not shown that alternatives cannot work.

### 2) Non-closure restructuring with capacity reductions
**Open for negotiation.**  
But only if it includes:
- clear job security
- site guarantees
- management and owner contributions
- voluntary and reversible measures
- and a real contribution from labour that is limited, temporary, and fair

### 3) Forced layoffs / compulsory redundancies
**Rejected outright.**

That crosses the line. If the company insists on that route, it is not negotiating a social settlement; it is provoking industrial conflict.

## What we are prepared to discuss
We remain prepared to discuss a serious package that could include:
- temporary wage restraint or deferred wage components
- reduction or suspension of certain bonuses
- working-time adjustments
- voluntary exit programs
- capacity reductions short of plant closures
- a jointly funded restructuring or future-investment contribution
- commitments on future models and volumes for German sites

But this only works if the company also commits to:
- no closures
- no forced redundancies
- protection of the collective wage framework
- and a fair burden split across all sides

## Bottom line
If management comes with a balanced package, we will negotiate hard but constructively.

If management comes with closures and compulsory redundancies, then it should not expect social peace.

And to be absolutely explicit: workers will not be asked to carry the full adjustment while management and owners remain protected. That is not a settlement we will support.
