Yes. The core missing item is now a **quantified restructuring economics comparison**. I can address the analyst question directly.

## 1) What “economic equivalence” means in this context

A non-closure or hybrid package is economically equivalent to a closure-based restructuring **only if**, on a risk-adjusted basis, it delivers:

1. **Comparable annual recurring fixed-cost reduction**
2. **Comparable certainty of delivery**  
   - less reliance on voluntary uptake that may undershoot
   - less exposure to renegotiation later
3. **Comparable speed to cash savings**
4. **Acceptable one-time restructuring charges**
5. **Comparable NPV after implementation risk**
6. **No material hidden cost leakage**  
   - e.g. underutilization at retained sites, duplicate overhead, transfer costs, or future capex burdens

In practice, a package that saves the same headline amount but with lower certainty or slower realization is **not equivalent**. The discount for that risk can be material.

---

## 2) Decision rule: a simple threshold framework

Let:

- **S₍closure₎** = annual recurring savings from closure scenario
- **C₍closure₎** = one-time closure charges
- **p₍closure₎** = probability of full implementation
- **t₍closure₎** = average time to realize savings

For non-closure/hybrid:

- **S₍alt₎** = annual recurring savings from alternative
- **C₍alt₎** = one-time charges
- **p₍alt₎** = probability of full implementation
- **t₍alt₎** = time to realize savings

A non-closure package is economically equivalent only if its **risk-adjusted NPV** is roughly the same:

**NPV ≈ p × PV(annual savings stream) − one-time charges**

So equivalence usually requires:

- **S₍alt₎ ≈ 90–100% of S₍closure₎** if implementation certainty is very high, or
- **S₍alt₎ > S₍closure₎** if implementation certainty is lower or savings arrive later

As a practical rule of thumb:
- if certainty is meaningfully lower, the alternative needs **more than 100%** of closure-scenario nominal savings to be equivalent
- if the non-closure path is slower by 1–2 years, it needs a **substantial uplift** in nominal savings to offset the delay

---

## 3) What typically makes closure stronger financially

Closure-based restructuring usually has three financial advantages:

### A. Higher certainty of permanent fixed-cost removal
A closed plant removes:
- direct labor cost
- site overhead
- maintenance and utilities
- some logistics and indirect support

It is usually a **hard structural cut**, not dependent on recurring bargaining.

### B. Faster de-risking of the cost base
If a plant is closed, the future savings are less exposed to:
- renegotiation
- volume shortfalls
- future productivity slippage
- partial reversion in future bargaining cycles

### C. Greater capacity reduction per euro of savings
If a site is materially underutilized, closure can remove both:
- excess fixed cost
- excess capacity

That can improve network utilization at the retained plants, though only if the remaining sites can absorb the volume without creating new bottlenecks or capex needs.

---

## 4) What can make a non-closure package close the gap

A non-closure or hybrid package can be financially credible if it combines several levers at scale:

- **headcount reduction through voluntary exits/attrition**
- **working-time reduction or flexibility**
- **temporary wage moderation / bonus restraint**
- **overhead cuts in corporate and plant support functions**
- **line consolidation / product transfer**
- **shift reduction and footprint rationalization**
