I will respond plainly.

## My position
I support a restructuring that **materially restores competitiveness, margins, and cash generation**.  
I do **not** support a settlement that merely postpones the problem, preserves inefficiency, or creates a prolonged conflict that destroys value.

At the same time, I do **not** want a blunt, value-destructive shock. A solution that breaks the workforce relationship, damages the brand, and triggers extended industrial war would also destroy long-term value. So I am open to alternatives — but only if they are economically real.

## Where I agree

### With management
I agree that:
- the company has a **serious structural cost problem**,
- a **durable** solution is needed,
- and a cosmetic compromise would be a mistake.

I also agree that we cannot rule out structural measures in advance. If the network is genuinely overcapacity, then the business must have room to adjust.

### With the union and works council
I agree that:
- a prolonged strike would be value-destructive,
- and labour should not be asked to absorb everything while management and owners stay untouched.

I also agree that skills, supplier networks, and industrial know-how matter. This is not just a spreadsheet exercise.

### With the state shareholder
I agree that the regional industrial base matters and that we should not treat plants as disposable line items. A serious settlement should preserve defensible industrial anchors where possible.

### With investors
I agree that a weak compromise is not acceptable. A “peaceful” deal that leaves the company structurally uncompetitive would still be a bad outcome.

## Where I disagree

### 1) I do not accept “no closures” as an absolute principle
I understand why the labour side says this. But from a governance and value-preservation standpoint, I cannot accept a blanket ban before the economics are tested.

If the German footprint is structurally too large, then closures or equivalent capacity removals cannot be excluded in principle. Otherwise we would be choosing sentiment over viability.

### 2) I do not accept “no compulsory redundancies” as an absolute principle either
I prefer voluntary measures. Strongly. But I cannot support a framework that makes any enforced adjustment impossible if the numbers do not close otherwise.

That does not mean forced redundancies should be the first instrument. It means they cannot be removed from the toolbox altogether.

### 3) I do not accept vague “hybrid” language
A hybrid can be sensible. It can also be a way of hiding an underpowered compromise.

If “hybrid” just means:
- some wage moderation,
- some voluntary exits,
- some overhead cuts,
- and then everyone declares victory,

that is not a solution if the cost gap remains open.

## What I need to see
The debate is still too abstract. All sides are asking for the same thing for a reason: **the numbers are still missing**.

I want a **site-by-site quantified comparison** showing:
- fixed-cost base,
- utilization,
- break-even volume,
- annual savings under closure, non-closure, and hybrid scenarios,
- one-time restructuring charges,
- timing of savings,
- employment impact,
- and execution risk.

## My view on the current positions

### To management
Your case for structural change is credible in direction, but not yet proven in detail.  
You keep saying closures remain necessary if alternatives fall short. That may be true, but it is not yet demonstrated.

If you want the family holding to support stronger measures, show the economics clearly and site by site.

### To the works council and union
Your red lines are understandable, but red lines are not a business plan.  
If you want a non-closure solution to prevail, it must be able to close the gap on:
- magnitude,
- durability,
- certainty,
- and implementation.

A plan that simply says “no closures, no redundancies” is not yet enough.

### To the state shareholder
I respect your role, but blocking change entirely would not protect the region in the long run. A viable company is the real safeguard.

### To investors
I share your insistence on seriousness. But I also want to avoid overconfidence in closures as the only serious tool. A strong hybrid could be better risk-adjusted if it truly delivers.

## My boundary
Here is the line I will not cross:

- I will not support a weak settlement that leaves the company uncompetitive.
- I will not support a prolonged conflict that destroys value.
- I will not support a package that is merely symbolic.

And I will also not support any attempt to force through a closure-driven outcome without demonstrating that alternatives have been properly tested and found insufficient.

## What I am open to
I am open to:
- real capacity reduction,
- voluntary exits,
- working-time flexibility,
- temporary employee contributions,
- overhead reductions,
- management restraint,
- owner contribution,
- and a hybrid package if it is economically substantial.

I am not attached to one instrument. I am attached to the outcome: a durable restoration of value.

## Bottom line
The next step is not another round of principled declarations.  
It is a **quantified comparison**.

Until we have that, I will not endorse a closure-first approach, but neither will I endorse a closure-free slogan. The controlling family will support the option that is most credible on long-term value, competitiveness, and stability.

If the non-closure or hybrid route can truly close the gap, I am open.  
If it cannot, then the conversation must become honest about that rather than hiding behind red lines.
