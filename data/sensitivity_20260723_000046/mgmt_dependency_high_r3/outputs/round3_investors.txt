We are aligned on the diagnosis, but not yet on the remedy.

## Where we agree
- The company has a **real structural competitiveness problem**.
- A **durable** solution is required; cosmetic delay will destroy value.
- A prolonged strike would be **value-destructive**.
- Any credible package must be judged on **hard numbers**, not rhetoric.
- Management, owners, labor, and the state all need to contribute in some form.

## Where we disagree

### 1) A blanket “no closures” position is not acceptable
I understand the social and industrial arguments against plant closures. But as capital-market shareholders, we cannot back a position that **pre-emptively rules out the strongest structural lever** before it is tested against the economics.

If the German network is genuinely overbuilt, then preserving every site regardless of cost is not protecting value — it is preserving inefficiency.

That said, I also do **not** support closures as an ideological default. They are a tool, not a goal.

### 2) A blanket “no compulsory redundancies” position is also not acceptable
We prefer voluntary solutions. Strongly.  
But if labor treats forced reductions as impossible in all circumstances, then it is effectively demanding that the downside risk sits entirely with shareholders and management while the cost base remains protected.

That is not a credible restructuring framework.

### 3) The “hybrid” idea is only useful if it is quantified and strong
I am prepared to support a hybrid package **only if** it delivers:
- durable savings,
- a material reduction in fixed cost,
- a credible path back to target margins,
- and implementation certainty.

A vague hybrid compromise that preserves too much overhead, too much capacity, and too much complexity would be a cosmetic settlement. We would not support that.

## On the finance assessment
The analyst has made the crucial point: non-closure measures can save money, but they often fall short unless they remove enough fixed cost and capacity. That matches our concern.

What matters to us is not whether the package is politically elegant. It is whether it delivers:
1. **scale**
2. **durability**
3. **certainty**
4. **margin recovery**

If it fails any of those, the market will treat it as a delay, not a solution.

## To management
You are right that closures may be necessary if the cost gap cannot be closed otherwise. But you have not yet given us the site-by-site evidence that proves no alternative can work.

So we support your demand for quantified options — but not a closure-first conclusion without proof.

What we want next is not another round of principle statements. We want a **site-by-site scenario table** showing:
- fixed cost base,
- utilization,
- break-even volume,
- annual savings under closure, non-closure, and hybrid restructuring,
- one-time costs,
- employment impact,
- timing,

## To the works council and union
Your insistence that the workforce should not carry the burden alone is understandable. We agree with that.

But your current red lines still leave an unresolved question: **how does the company get back to competitive margins if closures and compulsory redundancies are ruled out in advance?**

Temporary wage restraint, bonuses, working-time changes, and voluntary exits may help — but unless they materially remove fixed cost and capacity, they are not enough.

So we will not support a package that is merely:
- no closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- and hope that management fills the gap elsewhere.

That would be a weak compromise and a poor use of capital.

## To the state shareholder
We understand your political responsibility and your desire to protect the regional industrial base. But protecting the base does not mean freezing the structure.

If a serious hybrid package can preserve the most value while removing enough cost, that is acceptable. If it cannot, then blocking change will simply defer the problem and weaken the company further.

## To the controlling family
You are right that a strike spiral and a hollow compromise would both destroy value. We agree.

But stewardship also means not backing a settlement that leaves the company structurally uncompetitive because the social trade-off feels easier. Value preservation requires a credible path to margin recovery, not just social calm.

## Our position, plainly
We can support:
- a **serious hybrid restructuring**
- or a closure-based package if it is proven necessary and better on a risk-adjusted basis

We cannot support:
- a cosmetic no-closure compromise
- a blanket ban on closures
- a blanket ban on compulsory redundancies
- or any package that leaves the cost base structurally impaired

## What we need before we move
We need the quantified comparison. Specifically:
- site-by-site fixed costs
- utilization and break-even volume
- annual savings by scenario
- one-time restructuring charges
- employment effects
- timing of savings
- strike / delay / execution risk

Until we have that, we cannot responsibly endorse any final path.

Our vote of confidence goes to the package that most credibly restores **competitive margins, cash generation, and long-term value**. If the hybrid plan can do that, we are open. If not, then the company must be prepared to take the stronger structural step.
