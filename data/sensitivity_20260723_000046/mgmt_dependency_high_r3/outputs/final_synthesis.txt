## 1. Decision context
The company faces a structural cost and capacity problem in a shrinking, highly competitive European market. Management seeks durable fixed-cost reduction, including possible German plant closures and workforce cuts; labor rejects closures, compulsory redundancies, and erosion of job security. Because of co-determination, family control, and a regional blocking minority, the decision requires a politically durable package, not just an economically plausible one.

## 2. Stakeholder assembly
- **Group & Brand Management**: highest operational mandate to restore competitiveness.
- **Industrial Union**: high bargaining power through collective agreement and strike capacity.
- **Works Council**: high legitimacy and direct co-determination power.
- **Controlling Family Holding**: very high governance power and strategic influence.
- **Regional State Government**: high legitimacy and blocking-minority power, with regional employment interests.
- **Institutional Investors**: significant capital-market legitimacy, but less direct governance power than the above actors.
- **Finance expert / analyst**: no formal power, but high decision relevance because the missing quantified comparison is now central to all positions.

## 3. Final positions

### Group & Brand Management
Management insists that the restructuring must materially reduce structural fixed costs and cannot be constrained in advance by blanket bans on closures or compulsory redundancies. It remains open to non-closure or hybrid alternatives only if they are economically equivalent on a risk-adjusted basis, but it views labor’s current red lines as incompatible with a credible turnaround.

### Works Council
The works council holds firm to three red lines: no German plant closures, no compulsory redundancies, and no dismantling of the job-security framework. It is willing to discuss a fully quantified non-closure package, but only if it preserves every German plant and does not embed closure triggers or staged ultimatums.

### Industrial Union
The union aligns closely with the works council: no closures, no compulsory redundancies, no erosion of collective protections. It is open to temporary labor concessions, voluntary exits, working-time flexibility, and real capacity adjustment, but only if these preserve sites and do not convert into closure by attrition.

### Regional State Government
The state shareholder supports restructuring but rejects closure or forced-redundancy approaches absent proof of necessity. It wants regional industrial capacity, jobs, and co-determination protected, yet it also acknowledges that a non-closure plan must still deliver real, quantified cost reduction and not merely a political postponement.

### Controlling Family Holding
The controlling family wants a durable competitiveness recovery and will not back a weak compromise that only delays the problem. It is open to alternatives, but not to an absolute ban on closures or redundancies; it wants a quantified, economically credible package and will support the option that best preserves long-term value.

### Institutional Investors
Investors insist on durable fixed-cost reduction and reject both cosmetic compromise and blanket exclusions of the strongest restructuring tools. They are open to non-closure or hybrid solutions, but only if the quantified economics show comparable risk-adjusted value to closure-based restructuring.

### Finance Expert
The expert frames the decision as an NPV and implementation-certainty problem: non-closure or hybrid packages are only equivalent if they match closure on recurring savings, timing, certainty, and hidden-cost leakage. The expert’s contribution shifted the discussion from positions to thresholds and clarified that “equivalence” must be judged after discounting for risk and delay.

## 4. Genuine conflicts
1. **Labor red lines vs. management’s need for structural flexibility**: labor excludes closures and forced redundancies; management will not rule them out if alternatives fail.
2. **Job protection vs. durable cost removal**: labor wants binding guarantees through 2030; management says this freezes an uncompetitive structure.
3. **Site preservation vs. network simplification**: non-closure options reduce political damage but may leave excess capacity and overhead.
4. **Sequencing dispute**: labor wants a non-closure solution tested without closure pressure; management wants closure to remain a credible fallback.
5. **Risk allocation**: labor sees closure threats as asymmetric coercion; management/investors see labor’s absolute bans as shifting adjustment risk onto the company.

## 5. Areas of convergence
- All parties accept a **real structural competitiveness problem**.
- All agree that a **cosmetic or purely delaying solution is unacceptable**.
- There is broad recognition that **prolonged industrial conflict would destroy value**.
- All sides now accept the need for a **site-by-site quantified comparison** rather than slogans.
- There is some openness to a **hybrid package** if it is real, measurable, and durable.
- All stakeholders acknowledge that some form of **capacity reduction and burden sharing** is likely necessary.

## 6. Key trade-offs for the decision-maker
- **Certainty vs. legitimacy**: closures provide clearer savings but trigger intense conflict and political risk.
- **Speed vs. durability**: faster cuts may damage trust and implementation, while slower negotiated measures may underdeliver.
- **Scale of savings vs. social damage**: the more savings guaranteed, the greater the likely employment and regional harm.
- **Temporary vs. structural measures**: labor prefers reversible concessions; management wants permanent cost removal.
- **Site preservation vs. competitive network design**: retaining plants may preserve legitimacy but can leave complexity and overhead.
- **NPV value vs. execution risk**: an attractive paper model may fail if voluntary uptake, timing, or follow-through is weak.

## 7. Integrative pathways
1. **Quantified non-closure-first package with objective thresholds**
   - Test a non-closure plan first, but only against pre-agreed savings, certainty, and timing thresholds.
   - If it fails, a second-stage hybrid review follows.
   - This is the strongest procedural bridge, but labor distrusts any fallback that reintroduces closure pressure.

2. **Selective capacity reduction without full plant closure**
   - Use line consolidation, shift reduction, product transfers, and function centralization.
   - This may satisfy the need for structural savings while avoiding the symbolic and political blow of closure.
   - Its weakness is the risk that it becomes closure by attrition or leaves too much overhead intact.

3. **Protected anchor sites plus burden sharing**
   - Designate selected sites as protected, with no closure and no compulsory redundancies for a defined period.
   - Pair this with temporary labor concessions, management overhead cuts, and investment commitments.
   - This may be the most politically viable if the protected sites materially contribute to savings.

4. **Symmetric burden-sharing deal**
   - Combine labor concessions, owner contribution, management cuts, and targeted capacity reduction.
   - This is the most likely route to a stable coalition if the quantified savings are credible enough.
   - It still needs a fallback mechanism that labor can accept and management can trust.

## 8. Open questions
- What is the **actual fixed-cost gap** by site and function?
- How much of that gap can be closed through **non-closure measures alone**?
- What is the **risk-adjusted NPV** of closure vs. hybrid vs. non-closure?
- Which sites are true **anchor sites** and what protection would be credible?
- What level of **voluntary exits and working-time flexibility** is realistic?
- How much **management/owner contribution** is expected or politically feasible?
- What is the **minimum savings threshold** that would make a non-closure solution acceptable?
- What implementation risks arise from **strike action, delay, or future renegotiation**?

## 9. Recommended next step
Commission and table a **site-by-site quantified scenario comparison** covering:
- fixed-cost base
- utilization
- break-even volume
- annual recurring savings
- one-time restructuring charges
- employment effects
- timing of savings
- required capex
- execution risk
- risk-adjusted NPV

Then convene a decision round in which each stakeholder states:
1. which scenario is procedurally acceptable,
2. what minimum threshold would move their position,
3. and what guarantee or contribution they are willing to offer.

## 10. Emergent insights that only appeared through multi-turn interaction
- The central dispute is **not simply about whether restructuring is needed**; it is about **which instruments are legitimate** and **how certainty is allocated**.
- “Non-closure first, closure fallback” emerged as a potential bridge, but labor reinterpreted it as a **staged ultimatum**, revealing that procedure itself is a substantive conflict.
- The discussion moved from broad positions to a more precise recognition that **economic equivalence depends on risk-adjusted NPV**, not just headline savings.
- A subtle but important issue surfaced: **capacity reduction without formal closure can still be perceived as closure by attrition**, making terminology and implementation design critical.
- The state shareholder’s role became clearer as a **dual-purpose actor**: politically protective of jobs and sites, but also required to back a viable company, not just regional preservation.
- The family holding and investors converged on an important nuance: they are not necessarily pro-closure as an ideology, but they want **credible discipline** and do not trust open-ended protection claims.
- The interaction revealed that the decisive missing input is not another principle statement, but a **shared analytic basis** for comparing options under real governance constraints.
