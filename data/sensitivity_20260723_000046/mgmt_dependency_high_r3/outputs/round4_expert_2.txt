Yes. From a restructuring finance and plant economics standpoint, the missing question is not whether a non-closure solution is politically preferable. It is whether it can deliver **comparable risk-adjusted fixed-cost removal** to closure.

## Short answer
A non-closure or hybrid package is economically equivalent to closure **only if** it produces:

- **similar recurring annual savings**
- **high implementation certainty**
- **fast enough realization**
- **limited hidden leakage** from underutilized retained sites
- **a risk-adjusted NPV close to the closure option**

If it achieves the same headline savings but with lower certainty or slower timing, it is **not equivalent**.

---

## Practical threshold framework

Let:

- **S₁** = annual recurring savings under closure
- **C₁** = one-time closure charges
- **P₁** = probability of full implementation
- **T₁** = timing to realize savings

For the alternative:

- **S₂** = annual recurring savings under non-closure / hybrid
- **C₂** = one-time charges
- **P₂** = implementation probability
- **T₂** = timing

A simple comparison is:

**Risk-adjusted NPV ≈ P × PV(savings stream) − one-time charges**

So the alternative is economically equivalent only if:

1. **S₂ is close to S₁**, or higher if certainty is lower
2. **P₂ is high enough** to offset lower structural permanence
3. **T₂ is not materially slower**
4. **C₂ does not create a hidden cost burden later**

---

## Rule of thumb
A non-closure package typically needs to close **roughly 80–90% of the structural gap at minimum**, and more if:

- savings are temporary
- implementation is uncertain
- retained sites remain underutilized
- future bargaining reversal risk is high

If the package is mostly:
- wage moderation
- bonus restraint
- voluntary exits
- working-time flexibility

then it is often **insufficient on its own** unless paired with meaningful:
- line consolidation
- shift reduction
- product transfers
- overhead reduction
- site simplification

---

## Why closure usually scores better financially

Closure has three major financial advantages:

### 1) Permanent fixed-cost removal
A closed site usually eliminates:
- direct labor
- local overhead
- plant utilities
- maintenance
- some logistics and support costs

That makes the savings more durable.

### 2) Higher certainty
Closure is less dependent on:
- voluntary uptake
- later renegotiation
- partial implementation
- productivity slippage

### 3) Faster network simplification
If a site is truly redundant, closure can improve utilization at the remaining footprint, though that only works if the remaining plants can absorb volume without new bottlenecks or capex.

---

## When a non-closure or hybrid package can work
It can be equivalent if it contains a **real capacity reset**, not just labor concessions. For example:

- site or line consolidation
- shift elimination
- product transfer
- centralization of functions
- durable headcount reduction
- management and overhead cuts
- binding productivity commitments

The key is that the savings must be **structural, recurring, and enforceable**.

---

## What usually makes non-closure weaker
A non-closure package tends to underperform when it relies on:

- temporary wage cuts
- short-term labor concessions
- voluntary exits that may undershoot
- partial capacity reduction that leaves fixed overhead in place
- preserved complexity across too many plants

That leaves residual costs and future risk.

---

## My bounded judgment
Based on the scenario you described:

- **Closure-based restructuring** is the cleanest route to certainty and durable savings.
- A **hybrid package** is the most plausible alternative if it materially reduces capacity and overhead.
- A **pure non-closure / labor-concession package** is unlikely to be equivalent unless the quantified savings are unusually deep and implementation is very secure.

---

## What the site-by-site table should include
To assess equivalence properly, for each site or major function you need:

- fixed-cost base
- utilization
- break-even volume
- annual savings under each scenario
- one-time restructuring charges
- timing of savings
- headcount impact
- required capex
- execution risk
- risk-adjusted NPV
