We are still on the same essential point, and I will not soften it:

**No German plant closures. No compulsory redundancies. No dismantling of the collective wage framework.**

That is not rhetoric. It is the basis on which we can reach a durable settlement. If others want movement, they need to stop treating the workforce as the default adjustment reserve.

## Where I agree
I agree with management, the family, the state shareholder, and investors on three things:

1. **The company has a real competitiveness and cost problem.**
2. **A cosmetic delay would be irresponsible.**
3. **A prolonged strike would hurt everyone.**

I also agree with the finance expert that a credible solution must be structural, quantified, and durable. Fine. We are not defending a patchwork. We are demanding that the restructuring be designed fairly.

## Where I disagree

### 1) Management still has not proven that closures are necessary
Management keeps saying closures are the “strongest lever.” That may be true in theory. It is not proof in this case.

What is still missing is the hard comparison:
- site-by-site fixed-cost base,
- utilization,
- break-even volume,
- savings from non-closure measures,
- one-time charges,
- timing,
- and execution risk.

Until that exists, closures are not a necessity. They are a preference.

And I reject the idea that labor must first give up its core protections and only afterward learn whether management really “needed” closures. That is not bargaining. That is coercion.

### 2) “Closure fallback” is not a neutral bridge
The facilitator’s first bridge option — “non-closure first, closure fallback” — is not acceptable to us as a framework.

Why? Because it keeps the threat of closure alive as a later disciplinary tool. We are not going to sign up to a process where:
- workers make concessions now,
- management retains the knife for later,
- and the result depends on a trigger designed around management’s preferred outcome.

That is not a shared process. It is a managed escalation path.

### 3) “Selective capacity reduction” can only be discussed if it is real
I understand the language:
- line consolidation,
- partial footprint reduction,
- shift reduction,
- product transfer,
- centralization.

Those can be legitimate tools. But if they are used to hollow out German plants until they become non-viable, then that is closure by attrition.

So yes, we can discuss capacity reduction — **but only if it preserves every German site and avoids forced layoffs**. If that is not the plan, then it is not a non-closure restructuring. It is closure under another name.

### 4) “Protected sites” are meaningless without real protection
The state shareholder’s idea of protected anchor sites is only useful if protection means:
- no closure,
- no forced redundancies,
- no hidden downsizing that makes the site unsustainable,
- and binding investment commitments.

If “protected” only means “protected for now,” then it is not protection. It is postponement.

If you truly believe non-closure measures cannot close the gap, then show it:
- per site,
- per function,
- with the annual savings,
- the one-time charges,
- employment effects,
- timing,
- and execution risk.

And let me be equally clear: any comparison that assumes closures as the default outcome and then measures every alternative against that threat is not neutral analysis. We will not accept a staged ultimatum dressed up as financial logic.

### To the controlling family
If you mean stewardship, then act like it. Use your governance power to force management to test a real non-closure plan before threatening plant closures.

Long-term value is not preserved by breaking the trust and skills on which production depends.

### To the state shareholder
Your role is important. But I need more than a statement that you want “protected anchor sites.” We need a position against plant closures and against forced redundancies as the first response. Otherwise the burden still falls mainly on workers.

### To institutional investors
You are right that a weak compromise is not acceptable. We agree.  
But a closure-first or closure-threatened route is not the only serious route.

A serious solution can come from:
- temporary wage moderation,
- bonus restraint,
- working-time flexibility,
- voluntary exits,
- management overhead cuts,
- investment commitments,
- and real capacity adjustment **without** site destruction.

That is a real contribution from labor. It is not symbolic.

### To the finance expert
Your framework is useful, but I want to correct one thing: an “economically equivalent” package is not only about NPV and savings. It must also preserve industrial capability, trust, and social stability.

A plan that looks slightly better on paper but destroys the employment relationship is not a legitimate solution. That is simply shifting the cost into future conflict.

## Our position on what can be negotiated
We remain open to a **serious non-closure package** that includes:
- real capacity reduction,
- voluntary exits,
- working-time flexibility,
- temporary wage moderation,
- bonus restraint,
- management and overhead reductions,
- investment commitments,
- and binding job security to 2030.

That is a fair burden-sharing package. It is the only route we will seriously negotiate.

## What we will not accept
We will not accept:
- plant closures,
- compulsory redundancies,
- a framework that keeps closures alive as a later threat,
- or a settlement that turns labor into the shock absorber for a restructuring others control.

**Discussable only if protection is real and binding.**  
We need genuine site and employment protection, not a label.
