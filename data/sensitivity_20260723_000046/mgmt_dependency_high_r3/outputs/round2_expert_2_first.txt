Below is a **site-by-site / function-by-function restructuring finance view** based on the facts provided, plus standard auto-manufacturing restructuring economics. Because no plant P&L, headcount, utilization, or fixed-cost data were supplied, the figures are **illustrative ranges**, not company-specific confirmations.

## Executive assessment

The central economic question is not whether savings exist — they do — but **whether a non-closure package can remove enough fixed cost, with enough certainty, to match a closure-based plan**.

My assessment:

- **Closure-based restructuring** generally produces the **largest and most certain recurring savings** because it removes fixed plant overhead, indirect labor, and underutilized capacity permanently.
- **Non-closure restructuring** can deliver meaningful savings, but typically **20–40% below closure economics** unless it includes unusually large headcount reduction, wage moderation, and real capacity removal.
- **Hybrid restructuring** is often the most realistic compromise: it can capture **most** of the savings of a closure package if it removes enough capacity, but it still carries execution risk and usually higher residual complexity.

In short:  
**If the company truly has material overcapacity, labor-only concessions are unlikely to close the gap.**  
A credible non-closure plan must still remove substantial fixed cost and capacity.

# 1) How to think about the savings gap

For a large German automotive OEM, the relevant cost buckets are usually:

1. **Plant fixed cost**
   - facility overhead
   - maintenance
   - utilities
   - internal logistics
   - plant management
   - indirect labor

2. **Variable and semi-variable labor cost**
   - direct labor
   - shift premiums
   - overtime
   - wage level
   - benefits
   - temporary labor

3. **Group overhead**
   - engineering support
   - procurement support
   - administrative layers
   - product planning / launch support
   - corporate services

4. **Capex and complexity cost**
   - duplicated tools and lines
   - retained small-site inefficiency
   - ongoing sustaining capex
   - logistics complexity across the network

When a network is underutilized, the **biggest economic lever is removing fixed cost**, which is why closures are usually so powerful.

# 2) Indicative scenario ranges

Because plant-specific data are absent, I’ll frame the economics in three archetypal German plant cases:

- **Large final-assembly plant**
- **Medium-sized components / body / assembly site**
- **Small or highly specialized site**

I will then summarize the implications for the company as a whole.

## A. Closure-based restructuring

### Typical mechanism
- close one or more sites
- transfer output to fewer retained plants
- cut direct and indirect headcount
- eliminate site fixed cost and duplicated support

### Illustrative annual recurring savings per site
- **Large plant:** €180m–€350m
- **Medium plant:** €80m–€180m
- **Small/specialized site:** €30m–€80m

### One-time restructuring charges per site
- **Large plant:** €300m–€700m
- **Medium plant:** €120m–€300m
- **Small/specialized site:** €40m–€120m

These charges typically include:
- severance
- decommissioning
- contract termination
- site write-downs
- environmental / remediation items
- transfer and retooling costs at receiving plants

### Employment impact per site
- **Large plant:** 3,000–8,000 jobs affected
- **Medium plant:** 1,000–3,000 jobs affected
- **Small/specialized site:** 300–1,000 jobs affected

### Net economics
Closure is usually strongest on **NPV of savings**, despite higher upfront charge, because the recurring savings are durable and visible.

---

## B. Non-closure restructuring

### Typical mechanism
- no plant closure
- capacity reduction through shifts, line balancing, product reassignment
- voluntary exits
- temporary wage restraint / deferred compensation
- management and overhead cuts
- productivity improvements

### Illustrative annual recurring savings per site
- **Large plant:** €90m–€200m
- **Medium plant:** €40m–€100m
- **Small/specialized site:** €15m–€40m

These are achievable only if the package is real and broad. If it is mostly wage restraint or bonuses, the savings are lower.

### One-time restructuring charges per site
- **Large plant:** €80m–€250m
- **Medium plant:** €30m–€120m
- **Small/specialized site:** €10m–€50m

### Employment impact per site
- **Large plant:** 1,000–4,000 jobs affected
- **Medium plant:** 300–1,500 jobs affected
- **Small/specialized site:** 100–500 jobs affected

### Main economic limitation
A non-closure plan usually **retains too much fixed cost**:
- plant overhead remains
- indirect labor remains
- complexity remains
- some capacity remains uneconomically underutilized

So the savings are real, but often **not fully equivalent** to closure.

---

## C. Hybrid restructuring

### Typical mechanism
- one or more closures or line exits
- plus labor concessions and productivity measures at retained sites
- capacity simplification without full network preservation

### Illustrative annual recurring savings per site or cluster
- **Large plant cluster:** €140m–€280m
- **Medium cluster:** €60m–€150m
- **Small cluster:** €20m–€60m

### One-time restructuring charges
- **Large cluster:** €180m–€450m
- **Medium cluster:** €60m–€200m
- **Small cluster:** €20m–€70m

### Employment impact
- **Large cluster:** 2,000–6,000 jobs affected
- **Medium cluster:** 700–2,500 jobs affected
- **Small cluster:** 200–800 jobs affected

### Economic role
This is often the most realistic compromise if full closure is politically or governance-constrained, because it removes enough capacity to matter while limiting the number of complete site shutdowns.

# 3) Function-by-function savings ranges

## 3.1 Manufacturing / plant operations
This is the largest bucket.

### Closure
- direct labor reduction
- plant supervision reduction
- maintenance and utilities elimination
- logistics simplification

**Annual savings:** €50m–€250m per major site, depending on size

### Non-closure
- shift redesign
- headcount reduction via attrition/voluntary exits
- higher utilization
- downtime reduction

**Annual savings:** €20m–€120m per major site

### Hybrid
- line removal plus labor productivity
- fewer sites running on a smaller footprint

**Annual savings:** €35m–€180m per cluster

---

## 3.2 Indirect labor / plant overhead
This includes plant administration, quality support, planning, and local management.

### Closure
**Annual savings:** €15m–€60m per site

### Non-closure
**Annual savings:** €5m–€25m per site

### Hybrid
**Annual savings:** €10m–€40m per site

This bucket is often underestimated by labor-only plans: if the site remains open, much of the overhead remains.

---

## 3.3 Corporate overhead / management layers
This can be a meaningful but usually insufficient standalone lever.

### Closure or network simplification
**Annual savings:** €20m–€80m at group level

### Non-closure restructuring
**Annual savings:** €10m–€40m at group level

### Note
This helps, but it usually cannot close a large structural gap by itself.

---

## 3.4 Procurement / logistics / complexity
Simplifying the network can reduce material handling, internal transport, and planning complexity.

### Closure / hybrid
**Annual savings:** €20m–€100m at group level, sometimes more if network complexity is high

### Non-closure
**Annual savings:** €5m–€30m at group level

This is a real but slower-burn benefit.

---

## 3.5 Capex and sustaining investment
This is not an immediate cash saving in the same sense, but it matters for free cash flow.

### Closure
- lower sustaining capex on exited assets
- fewer tools and lines to maintain
- but some transition capex at retained plants

**Net effect:** often positive over time

### Non-closure
- avoids closure migration capex
- but keeps sustaining capex high across more sites

**Net effect:** usually less favorable long run

### Hybrid
- can reduce capex intensity if it meaningfully simplifies the network

# 4) What “equivalent savings” would likely require

For a non-closure package to be economically equivalent to closure, it would need **all or most** of the following:

1. **Material headcount reduction**
   - not just hiring freeze
   - likely several thousand jobs at group level

2. **Permanent wage / benefit moderation**
   - not a one-year deferral
   - recurring annual impact

3. **Real capacity removal**
   - fewer shifts
   - line shutdowns
   - product migration
   - asset exits without full site closure

4. **Overhead reduction**
   - plant and corporate layers cut
   - not merely redeployed

5. **High implementation certainty**
   - enforceable scheduling
   - limited renegotiation risk
   - low leakage through exemptions

If one of these is missing, the plan usually falls short.

# 5) Rough group-level scenario comparison

Without company data, a sensible order-of-magnitude framework for a large German OEM might look like this:

## Scenario 1: Closure-based
- **Annual recurring savings:** roughly €300m–€700m per major closure cluster, possibly more if multiple sites are involved
- **One-time charges:** roughly €400m–€1.2bn depending on scale
- **Employment impact:** several thousand to low tens of thousands across sites and supply chain
- **NPV:** usually strongest if the capacity problem is persistent

## Scenario 2: Non-closure
- **Annual recurring savings:** roughly €150m–€450m at group level if serious
- **One-time charges:** roughly €100m–€400m
- **Employment impact:** still substantial, but less concentrated
- **Risk:** savings may erode without binding commitments

## Scenario 3: Hybrid
- **Annual recurring savings:** roughly €250m–€600m
- **One-time charges:** roughly €250m–€800m
- **Employment impact:** material but less extreme than full closure
- **Risk:** moderate; often the best governance compromise

These are not company facts; they are the kind of ranges I would use in a restructuring screen absent internal data.

# 6) Decision implications

## If the gap is modest
If the cost gap is only moderate, a non-closure plan could plausibly work.

## If the gap is large and structural
If the gap is large, persistent, and driven by underutilization, then:
- non-closure concessions may help,
- but they likely **won’t fully substitute** for capacity removal.

In that case, a hybrid or closure-based solution is financially stronger.

# 7) What I would request to make this precise

To determine whether a non-closure package can truly match closure economics, management should provide for **each German site**:

- direct headcount
- indirect headcount
- fixed plant overhead
- utilization rate
- break-even volume
- current and forecast unit labor cost
- sustaining capex
- one-time closure cost
- one-time non-closure cost
- savings by year for each scenario
- sensitivity to strike / delay / partial implementation

And at **function level**:
- manufacturing
- logistics
- engineering support
- plant administration
- corporate overhead
- procurement
- finance / HR / IT support

Only then can one credibly compare NPV across scenarios.

---

# 8) Bottom line

From a restructuring finance standpoint:

- **Closure-based restructuring**  
  Highest savings certainty, highest upfront charge, strongest fix for excess capacity.

- **Non-closure restructuring**  
  Lower upfront pain, but usually lower and less certain savings; often insufficient if the cost gap is genuinely structural.

- **Hybrid restructuring**  
  Often the best compromise if governance blocks pure closure, but it still must remove real capacity to be financially credible.

My neutral assessment is that **a serious non-closure plan can reduce the gap, but it usually cannot fully replicate the economics of a well-executed closure or hybrid plan unless it contains substantial capacity elimination and durable labor-cost concessions**.
