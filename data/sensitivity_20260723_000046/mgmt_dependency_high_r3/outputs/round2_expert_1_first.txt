Below is a finance-and-cost assessment of the restructuring choice, limited to the economic and operating dimensions.

## Executive view

Based on the information in the record, the company appears to have a **real structural cost gap** in Germany, but the discussion so far does **not yet quantify** it enough to prove that plant closures are the only viable remedy.

From a restructuring finance perspective:

- **Closure-based scenarios** usually deliver the **largest and most certain fixed-cost reduction**, especially when the issue is excess installed capacity and duplicated overhead.
- **Non-closure scenarios** can close part of the gap through wage restraint, working-time changes, voluntary exits, and overhead cuts, but they often leave:
  - **less permanent savings**,
  - **more implementation risk**,
  - and **weaker certainty** that capacity is actually removed.

In practical terms, if the company needs a **large, durable reduction** in unit costs, a non-closure package typically has to be much broader than “no closures” in name only. It must include substantial capacity elimination, labor-cost concessions, and overhead rationalization. Without that, it is usually **economically inferior** to a closure-based restructuring.

## What can be said with confidence

### 1) The problem is likely both fixed-cost and variable-cost related
For a large German auto manufacturer, a competitiveness gap usually comes from a combination of:

- **underutilized plants**
- high labor cost per vehicle
- excess overhead and support functions
- complexity in manufacturing network and product allocation
- legacy commitments that limit flexibility

That means there are two distinct savings buckets:

1. **Structural/fixed savings**  
   Plant-level overhead, maintenance, utilities, logistics, indirect labor, and duplicated management cost.

2. **Workforce/unit-cost savings**  
   Wage moderation, working-time flexibility, productivity gains, and reduced headcount.

If the company’s issue is truly “structural overcapacity,” then labor concessions alone rarely solve it. Overcapacity has to come out of the system somewhere.

### 2) Closure generally produces the highest savings certainty
A closure removes:
- direct plant fixed costs,
- a share of indirect labor,
- maintenance and site overhead,
- internal logistics and some supply-chain complexity,
- and often some tooling and support duplication.

The economic advantage is not just the savings size; it is the **certainty** and **durability**:
- the cost disappears permanently,
- it is easy to verify,
- and it does not rely on recurring negotiations.

### 3) Non-closure packages can save money, but often with a gap
A non-closure restructuring can work if it includes:
- substantial workforce reduction through voluntary exits,
- sustained wage restraint or deferred pay,
- changes in working time and shift patterns,
- management overhead cuts,
- product and investment discipline,
- and genuine capacity reduction achieved without keeping idle assets alive.

But if no plant is closed, the company often keeps:
- some excess fixed cost,
- more management layers than needed,
- and more complexity than a closure scenario.

So the usual result is **partial savings**, not full equivalence.

## Scenario comparison

Because the underlying figures are not provided, I cannot state exact euro amounts. I can, however, compare the economics in a decision-useful way.

### Scenario A: Closure-based restructuring
**Mechanism**
- close one or more plants
- transfer or discontinue output
- reduce workforce materially
- cut fixed overhead and capacity

**Typical financial profile**
- **Savings:** highest
- **Timing:** slower in year 1 due to closure charges, then fast ramp-up in savings
- **Certainty:** high if legally and operationally executed
- **One-time charges:** high
- **Labor impact:** highest
- **Capex:** lower in the near term, because excess capacity can be removed; may require capex for consolidation and product migration

**Cost of execution**
- severance
- site remediation
- contract termination
- decommissioning and write-downs
- possible pension / restructuring provisions
- transitional logistics and retooling

**Economic strength**
- Best when the gap is large and persistent.
- Best when there is clear overcapacity and low likelihood of recovery in the underperforming sites.

### Scenario B: Non-closure restructuring
**Mechanism**
- no plant shutdown
- working-time changes
- voluntary exits
- temporary pay measures
- management overhead reductions
- productivity commitments
- possible redeployment across sites

**Typical financial profile**
- **Savings:** moderate to high, but usually below closure-based savings
- **Timing:** faster to announce, but often slower to fully realize
- **Certainty:** medium to low unless backed by enforceable commitments
- **One-time charges:** lower than closure, but still meaningful
- **Labor impact:** lower direct job destruction, but still substantial if headcount falls materially
- **Capex:** can remain elevated if the network stays more complex

**Main weakness**
- Fixed cost base remains partly intact if assets are preserved.
- Savings are more exposed to renegotiation and execution slippage.
- The company may still carry surplus capacity, which suppresses margins.

### Scenario C: Hybrid package
**Mechanism**
- partial capacity reduction
- one or more smaller site rationalizations, line eliminations, or asset exits
- combination of labor concessions and overhead cuts
- targeted investment commitments

**Typical financial profile**
- **Savings:** can be close to closure scenario if designed aggressively
- **Timing:** intermediate
- **Certainty:** better than pure non-closure, less than full closure
- **One-time charges:** medium to high
- **Labor impact:** medium to high
- **Capex:** can be optimized if the network is simplified enough

**Economic strength**
- Often the most realistic compromise if a full closure package is politically or governance-constrained.
- Can close much of the gap if the company is willing to remove enough capacity.

## The key issue: what “equivalent savings” actually means

For a non-closure plan to be economically equivalent, it must likely match closure economics on **three dimensions**, not just one:

1. **Magnitude of savings**
2. **Permanence of savings**
3. **Certainty of delivery**

In practice, many “no closure” packages can match **a portion** of the savings, but not all three dimensions at once.

### Why?
Because some savings are hard to replace without closing assets:
- building overhead does not vanish if the plant stays open
- logistics duplication remains
- maintenance and site support remain
- management attention remains fragmented
- small underutilized sites often stay expensive even after concessions

So unless the non-closure package includes real capacity elimination, it usually leaves a **savings gap**.

## One-time restructuring charges versus ongoing savings

This is central.

### Closure scenario
You usually get:
- **large upfront charges**
- followed by **high recurring savings**

The restructuring charge is the price paid to remove capacity.

### Non-closure scenario
You usually get:
- **lower upfront charges**
- but also **lower recurring savings**

That can look attractive in the short run, but if the recurring savings are insufficient, the company simply postpones the problem.

From a finance standpoint, the right test is not “which has the lower one-time cost?”  
It is “which delivers the higher net present value after all restructuring charges and execution risks?”

If closures eliminate a large fixed-cost base, they often win on NPV even with bigger upfront charges.

## Labor and employment implications

### Closure-based
- biggest workforce reduction
- highest social cost
- highest litigation, strike, and execution risk in operational terms
- strongest immediate effect on unit labor cost

### Non-closure-based
- can still reduce headcount materially through voluntary exits and attrition
- lower immediate social disruption
- but often requires ongoing wage or flexibility concessions
- risk that the workforce cost benefit erodes over time

### Hybrid
- can deliver meaningful workforce reduction while preserving some sites
- may be the best balance if closures are not governable
- but still needs sufficient scale to matter financially

## Capex implications

A restructuring can free or consume capex depending on design.

### Closure scenario
Potentially:
- lower sustaining capex in the closed network
- lower duplication
- but higher transition capex for migration and retooling at retained sites

### Non-closure scenario
Potentially:
- avoids closure-related transition capex
- but may require more ongoing capex to keep multiple sites viable
- may not reduce complexity enough to improve capex efficiency

### Practical implication
If the company is trying to restore margins while funding EV transition, a restructuring that leaves too much plant complexity in place can crowd out strategic capex.

## My bounded estimate of likely savings relationship

Without company data, I would frame the savings relationship as follows:

- A **serious non-closure package** may achieve roughly **part** of the savings of a closure-based plan, but usually not all of it.
- If designed aggressively, it might approach closure economics only if it includes:
  - significant headcount reduction,
  - meaningful wage or benefit moderation,
  - removal of substantial capacity,
  - and hard governance commitments that prevent backsliding.
- If it relies mainly on temporary labor concessions, the savings gap will likely remain **material**.

In plain terms:  
**“No closure” is not automatically impossible, but it must prove it can remove enough cost base to be economically credible.**  
If it cannot, then management’s case for structural closure remains financially stronger.

## Decision-relevant conclusion

From a restructuring finance perspective, the company should compare the options on **net present value of savings net of restructuring charges**, not on headline job counts or political acceptability.

My assessment is:

- **Closure-based restructuring**: highest savings certainty, highest upfront charge, strongest economic logic if the gap is large.
- **Non-closure restructuring**: lower upfront pain, but often leaves a savings shortfall and implementation risk.
- **Hybrid package**: often the most realistic path if governance constraints block full closure, but it must still remove real capacity.

## What I would ask management for next
To determine whether a non-closure plan can truly match the economics, I would want:

1. baseline cost gap by site and by function
2. plant-by-plant fixed cost
3. utilization rates and break-even volumes
4. headcount, indirect labor, and overhead by site
5. one-time closure charges versus one-time non-closure charges
6. expected annual savings and timing by scenario
7. sensitivity to strike, delay, and partial implementation

Without that, no one can credibly say the non-closure route is fully equivalent.
