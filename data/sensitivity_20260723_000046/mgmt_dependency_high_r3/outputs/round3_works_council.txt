## Where I agree

I agree with management, the family, the state shareholder, and investors on one point: **the company needs a durable solution, not a cosmetic delay**.  
I also agree that a prolonged strike would hurt everyone, including the workforce. That is precisely why management should stop pushing a confrontation that creates it.

I also take seriously the finance expert’s point that:
- overcapacity and fixed-cost burden are real,
- non-closure measures must be structural to matter,
- and a weak compromise would simply postpone the problem.

That does **not** change our red lines. It only means the burden of designing a serious alternative is now even clearer on management.

## Where I disagree

### 1) Management’s closure logic is not proven
Management keeps saying closures remain necessary if alternatives fall short. That is circular unless you can show the actual gap site by site.

So far, what we have is:
- a general claim of overcapacity,
- a general claim of high German costs,
- and a general claim that closures are the cleanest answer.

That is **not yet proof** that closures are unavoidable.

The analyst has said exactly what matters: a non-closure solution must be judged by **magnitude, permanence, and certainty**. Fine. Then show the figures.  
Until then, closures are not a necessity; they are a management preference.

### 2) The “hybrid” idea is too vague to support
Everyone is suddenly talking about a hybrid package. But hybrid can mean almost anything:
- a little closure by another name,
- a little labor concession,
- a little overhead cut,
- and then declaring victory.

That is not acceptable.

If “hybrid” means:
- one plant goes,
- forced redundancies become possible,
- and the workforce is then told to accept it because at least it is not worse,

then that is not compromise. That is a staged erosion of our rights.

### 3) Investors and owners are still underestimating social and industrial damage
You all keep focusing on margins and certainty. We focus on the real production system:
- skills,
- apprenticeships,
- supplier networks,
- local industrial ecosystems,
- and trust.

Closing a German plant is not a line item. It destroys capabilities that cannot be bought back later at a lower price.

If you think that is acceptable because the net present value looks cleaner, then you are ignoring long-term industrial value.

## On the expert’s ranges

The finance ranges are useful, but they do **not** justify your preferred conclusion.

Yes, closures can generate larger recurring savings.  
Yes, non-closure packages may leave a gap if they are weak.  
But the analyst also said clearly that the gap depends on the design.

That means the correct response is **not** to jump to closures. It is to design a serious non-closure restructuring that actually removes cost:
- working-time changes,
- voluntary exits,
- temporary pay moderation,
- management overhead reduction,
- product and investment commitments,
- and real capacity adjustment without site destruction.

If management wants to claim that this still cannot match closures, then management must provide the plant-by-plant figures and prove it.

- temporary,
- proportionate,
- reversible where possible,

- working-time flexibility
- voluntary exits

- management and overhead reductions

What we will **not** consider:
- closures
- forced redundancies
- dismantling of the job-security framework
- using labour as the only shock absorber
