I will be equally direct.

## My position

I agree that the company needs a **durable** restructuring and that a purely symbolic compromise would be irresponsible. I also agree that the cost problem is real and cannot be denied.

But I do **not** accept that the only serious route is plant closures and compulsory redundancies. As the regional state shareholder, I cannot support a solution that destroys jobs and industrial capacity in Germany while claiming to protect the company’s future.

What I can support is a restructuring that:
- preserves German sites as far as economically possible,
- avoids compulsory redundancies,
- shares the burden fairly,
- and restores competitiveness without gutting the regional economic base.

## Where I agree with the other parties

### To management
You are right that the company needs real savings, not rhetoric. The finance assessment confirms that a superficial package would just postpone the problem. I take that seriously.

### To the controlling family
You are right that value preservation matters and that endless confrontation would be destructive. A strike spiral and continued erosion of margins help no one.

### To the union and works council
You are right that the workforce did not create this crisis and should not bear the burden alone. Site trust, skills, apprenticeships, and regional spillovers are real economic assets, not sentimental extras.

## Where I disagree

### 1) Plant closures in Germany cannot be treated as the default
Management keeps saying closures remain on the table if needed. I understand the logic, but I reject any approach that starts from closure-first thinking.

The finance expert made one point very clearly: closures may yield the highest certainty, but that does **not** mean they are automatically the right answer. They also carry major one-time costs, social damage, and long-term erosion of the company’s embedded capabilities.

A plant closure in Germany is not a neutral efficiency measure. It is a structural break with consequences for:
- employment,
- suppliers,
- apprenticeships,
- regional purchasing power,
- and the company’s legitimacy as an industrial anchor.

### 2) Compulsory redundancies are not acceptable as a starting point
I do not accept forced layoffs as a first-line instrument. If the company has not exhausted alternatives, then compulsory redundancies are not justified.

The burden of proof is still on management to show that:
- capacity reductions without closures,
- working-time solutions,
- voluntary exits,
- overhead reductions,
- and owner/management contributions

cannot achieve the necessary savings.

### 3) “No guarantees, because competitiveness” is too one-sided
Management says blanket job-security guarantees to 2030 are incompatible with restructuring. That may be true in absolute form. But the inverse is also true: a restructuring that gives the workforce no real security is politically and socially unstable, and therefore not durable.

So I do not support unconditional guarantees. But I **do** support binding protection where feasible:
- site commitments,
- phased workforce adjustment,
- voluntary instruments first,
- no forced redundancies unless a truly unavoidable last resort is proven,
- and real investment commitments attached to any labor concession.

## On the scenarios

### Scenario A: Closure-based restructuring
I do not support this as the preferred path.

Why?
- It is the most destructive socially.
- It risks provoking a major labor conflict.
- It weakens the company’s regional anchor role.
- It may create lasting governance damage that outweighs the savings.

I am not saying closures can never be discussed in any circumstance, but they are not the solution I can endorse at this stage.

### Scenario B: Non-closure restructuring
This is the only path that is even remotely acceptable from the state’s perspective, but only if it is real and not cosmetic.

That means:
- meaningful capacity reduction,
- measurable cost savings,
- management and overhead cuts,
- voluntary workforce instruments,
- and a contribution from owners and management.

What I will not support is a “non-closure” package that simply preserves the existing structure and shifts costs onto the workforce.

### Scenario C: Hybrid package
This is the only scenario that might be workable if the numbers genuinely require deeper change.

But “hybrid” must not become a euphemism for closures by stealth. If a hybrid package is proposed, then it must be judged by two tests:
1. Does it preserve as much German industrial capacity and employment as possible?
2. Does it actually close the savings gap with sufficient certainty?

If the answer to either is no, then it is not a serious settlement.

  1. closure,

Without that, we are being asked to choose between slogans.

## My bottom line

I am **open to a serious non-closure or hybrid restructuring** if it truly closes the gap and protects the company’s long-term health.

I am **not open** to:
- a closure-first package presented as unavoidable before alternatives are proven,
- compulsory redundancies as an early or default tool,
- or a settlement that sacrifices German sites and jobs while asking the state to bless the result.

The regional state will use its rights to prevent a fait accompli. If management wants our support, it must bring a solution that is economically credible **and** socially sustainable. If it cannot, then it should not expect approval for destructive measures in Germany.
