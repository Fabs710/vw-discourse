We are now past the point where broad principles can substitute for economics.

Let me respond directly.

## Where I agree

I agree with all parties on three points:

1. **The company needs a durable solution, not a short postponement.**
2. **A prolonged strike would damage everyone’s interests.**
3. **A cosmetic deal that leaves the German cost base structurally uncompetitive is unacceptable.**

So yes: I am not asking for a symbolic exercise. I am asking for a package that actually restores competitiveness and frees the money we need to invest in products and technology through 2030.

## Where I disagree — sharply

### 1) “No closures” is not a serious starting position
The Works Council and Union keep repeating that German plant closures are off the table.

I reject that as a blanket rule.

If a network is structurally overbuilt, then preserving every site regardless of economics is not a restructuring. It is managed decline. You cannot tell management to restore margins, fund future products, and reduce overcapacity while ruling out the most powerful structural lever before the numbers are even tested.

The finance assessment made the key point: closures usually deliver the **largest and most certain fixed-cost reduction**. That is not ideology; that is how industrial restructuring works.

### 2) “No compulsory redundancies” cannot be a hard veto
I prefer to avoid forced redundancies. But I will not accept a guarantee that the workforce is insulated from all adjustment regardless of whether the alternative measures are sufficient.

If labor wants to keep every site open and every current protection intact, then it must show how the cost gap closes fully and durably. So far, that has not been shown.

### 3) Binding job and site guarantees through 2030 are incompatible with the scale of change
I understand the desire for security. But blanket guarantees through 2030 would lock in the current cost problem through 2030.

That is not social sustainability. That is postponement.

## On the expert assessment
The analyst confirmed something important: a non-closure package can save money, but usually **not enough** unless it includes real capacity removal, labor-cost concessions, overhead cuts, and strong implementation certainty.

That means the burden is now on the employee side to show a **fully quantified alternative** that matches closure economics on:
- magnitude,
- permanence,
- certainty.

Until then, “no closures” is not an economic answer. It is a refusal to confront the scale of the problem.

## On the role of the family, the state, and investors

### To the controlling family
I agree that we should avoid blunt destruction for its own sake. But I do not accept the idea that value preservation means preserving every current structure.

Value is destroyed by strikes, yes. But it is also destroyed by an overcosted network that keeps draining cash and leaves the brand unable to invest. Stewardship means supporting a package that is economically meaningful, not one that merely sounds socially balanced.

### To the state shareholder
I recognize your political responsibility. But using the blocking position to prevent any meaningful restructuring would not protect the regional base; it would weaken the company and, over time, the region with it.

### To institutional investors
You are right to insist on durable savings and implementation certainty. I agree. But do not mistake a closure-free rhetorical package for seriousness. If the non-closure route does not close the gap, then it is not a solution.

## What I am prepared to accept
I am not here to insist on closures as an ideology. I am prepared to accept **any package that meets the economics**.

That means I am open to:
- capacity reductions,
- voluntary exits,
- working-time changes,
- temporary employee contributions,
- overhead reductions,
- management restraint,
- and a hybrid package if it truly removes enough cost base.

But let me be equally clear: the package must be:
- **structural**
- **durable**
- **quantified**
- **credible on implementation**

If it does not deliver that, then it is not good enough.

## What I will not accept
I will not accept:
- a non-closure plan that leaves the company still overcosted,
- a blanket exclusion of closures and compulsory redundancies,
- or a settlement that makes management and owners absorb only a cosmetic share while the structural problem remains.

That would simply postpone the crisis to the next round, with less cash and less trust.

## The immediate next step
We need to stop arguing in abstractions and move to a **site-by-site comparison**.

I want management to produce, for each German site and major function:
- fixed cost base,
- utilization,
- break-even volume,
- annual savings under:
  1. closure,
  2. non-closure restructuring,
  3. hybrid restructuring,
- one-time restructuring charges,
- employment impact,
- timing of savings,
- and implementation risk.

I also want the analyst to stress-test whether a non-closure or hybrid package can actually close the margin gap **after** charges, delays, and execution risk.

## Bottom line
Here is the position, plainly:

- I agree the solution must be durable.
- I agree it must not be cosmetic.
- I agree the burden must be shared.

But I do **not** accept the employee side’s attempt to rule out closures and compulsory redundancies in advance and then call that a solution.

A non-closure package is acceptable **only if it is economically equivalent**. If it is not, then we need to be honest about that instead of pretending slogans can fix the cost base.

This restructuring is about restoring competitiveness, hitting return-on-sales, and funding the future. If the proposal does not do that, it will not get my support.
