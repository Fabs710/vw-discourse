We are at the point where the real choice is being clarified, and I will be equally direct.

## Our position
We remain prepared to negotiate a serious restructuring package, but **not** one built on:
- German plant closures,
- compulsory redundancies,
- or the dismantling of the collective wage framework.

Those are not acceptable starting points, and they do not become acceptable just because management keeps repeating “structural” and “durable.”

## Where we agree
We agree with management, the owner side, the state shareholder, and the investors on one essential point: **this company needs a durable solution, not a temporary patch**.  
We also agree that a prolonged strike would damage the company, the workforce, and the industrial base. That is exactly why management should stop moving toward confrontation and start treating a non-closure settlement seriously.

We also take seriously the finance assessment:
- there is likely a real structural cost gap,
- non-closure measures must be meaningful to matter,
- and a cosmetic compromise would be pointless.

That does **not** mean closures or forced layoffs are justified. It means the package must be designed properly and shared fairly.

## Where we disagree

### 1) Management has not proven that closures are unavoidable
You keep saying closures remain on the table if alternatives fall short. But you still have not shown the site-by-site numbers that prove that point.

So far we have:
- a general claim of overcapacity,
- a general claim of high German costs,
- and a general claim that closures are the cleanest answer.

That is not proof. It is a preference.

If you want to argue that non-closure measures cannot work, then bring the numbers:
- plant-by-plant fixed cost,
- utilization,
- break-even volume,
- savings from non-closure measures,
- one-time charges,
- and execution risk.

Until then, closures are not “necessary.” They are a management choice.

### 2) The “hybrid” language is too vague
I agree with the works council on this point: “hybrid” can become a euphemism for doing closures by another name and calling it compromise.

That would not be a settlement we support.

If hybrid means:
- some sites preserved,
- real capacity reduction,
- voluntary workforce measures,
- overhead and management cuts,
- and investment commitments,
then it is worth examining.

If hybrid means:
- one plant closed,
- forced redundancies normalized,
- and labor told to accept it because it is not worse,
then no. That is not shared sacrifice. That is one-sided pressure.

### 3) Owners and investors still overstate the case for closure
Yes, closures can produce strong recurring savings. Nobody disputes that.

But you are treating savings as if they exist outside the social and industrial system that creates them. They do not.

A closure also creates:
- loss of skilled labor,
- loss of apprenticeship pipelines,
- supplier disruption,
- trust damage,
- and higher strike risk.

So the question is not “Can closure save money?”  
The question is: **Can it save enough, net of risk and damage, to justify the rupture?**

That has not been demonstrated.

## What we are willing to discuss
We are not saying “do nothing.” We are saying: **a serious restructuring can be done without closures and without compulsory redundancies if management is willing to share the burden.**

We are prepared to discuss:
- temporary wage restraint or deferred wage components,
- suspension or reduction of bonuses,
- working-time flexibility,
- voluntary exit programs,
- productivity improvements,
- management overhead cuts,
- investment commitments for German sites,
- and capacity adjustments short of site closures.

Those are real contributions. They are not symbolic.

## What we will not accept
We will not accept:
- German plant closures,
- compulsory redundancies,
- unilateral changes to the collective framework,
- or a package that makes the workforce the shock absorber while management and owners stay protected.

That is not burden sharing. That is a transfer of the crisis onto employees.

## To management
If you want a real negotiation, then put forward a **fully quantified non-closure plan**.

Not a headline. Not a threat. Not a “last offer.”  
A real plan that shows:
1. the cost gap by site and function,
2. the savings from non-closure measures,
3. the timing of those savings,
4. the one-time charges,
5. the employment effects,
6. and the management/owner contributions.

And I will be explicit: the plan must exclude:
- plant closures,
- compulsory redundancies,
- and the destruction of the collective wage framework.

If you cannot do that, then you are not restructuring responsibly. You are demanding that labour carry the burden for management’s failure to design a better solution.

## To the controlling family
You say you want value preservation. Then do not back a strategy that burns trust, triggers conflict, and destroys productive capability in the name of short-term margin repair.

If you want stewardship, use your influence to force management to present a real alternative based on:
- site preservation,
- fair burden sharing,
- and durable competitiveness without social rupture.

## To the state shareholder
Your blocking position matters. Use it to prevent a fait accompli. Do not settle for vague statements about “social sustainability.” Demand it in the actual structure of the package:
- no closures,
- no forced layoffs,
- and a binding employment framework.

## To institutional investors
You are right that a weak compromise is not acceptable. We agree.

But a closure-driven approach is not the only credible route to durability. A non-closure plan can be credible if it removes enough cost, enough capacity, and enough complexity — and if management and owners also contribute.

If you want certainty, support a deal that preserves industrial capability and avoids industrial war. A strike-driven closure strategy is not certainty; it is delayed damage.

## Bottom line
We remain open to a **serious non-closure restructuring** that includes:
- real capacity reduction,
- temporary and fair employee contributions,
- management overhead cuts,
- owner contribution,
- and binding site and employment protection.

We are **not** open to:
- plant closures,
- compulsory redundancies,
- or a settlement that treats workers as the default absorber of the crisis.

If management wants movement, it must come with a quantified non-closure plan. Otherwise, it should expect resistance, not consent.
