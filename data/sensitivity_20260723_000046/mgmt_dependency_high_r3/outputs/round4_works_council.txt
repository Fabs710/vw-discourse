We are not moving off the core red lines.

The facilitator’s “bridge options” may be useful as procedural scaffolding, but they do **not** change the substance:  
**no German plant closures, no compulsory redundancies, and no dismantling of the job-security framework.**

## Where I agree
I agree with management, the family, the state shareholder, and investors on one thing: **we need numbers, not slogans**.  
I also agree that a weak, cosmetic compromise would be worse than no deal at all.

And I agree with the finance expert’s basic logic:
- a durable solution must be structural,
- it must reduce fixed costs,
- and it must be credible on implementation.

That is exactly why management must stop using closure threats as a shortcut.

## Where I disagree

### 1) “Closure fallback” is not neutral
The facilitator’s “non-closure first, closure fallback” sounds balanced, but it is not balanced from our side. It bakes in a threat that one party can later use to force concessions.

We do **not** accept a package where:
- the workforce first proves what can be saved,
- and then management keeps the right to close plants anyway if the results are not to its liking.

That is not a bridge. That is a staged ultimatum.

### 2) The burden is still being pushed onto labor
Management, investors, and the family all keep talking about “structural reduction,” “certainty,” and “durability.” Fine. But in practice the pressure always lands on:
- jobs,
- sites,
- collective protections,
- and wages.

Meanwhile, management wants to keep the option of closures and forced redundancies in reserve. That is not shared sacrifice. That is asymmetric risk allocation.

### 3) “Selective capacity reduction” must not become closure by another name
I heard the language carefully:
- line consolidation,
- partial footprint reduction,
- shift reduction,
- product transfer,
- centralization.

Let me be equally clear: if those measures are used to hollow out sites until they are no longer viable, then this is simply closure through attrition.

We will not accept a plan that dismantles plants indirectly while pretending the word “closure” was never used.

### 4) Protected sites are only meaningful if they are truly protected
The state shareholder’s idea of protected anchor sites is only useful if it comes with **real** guarantees:
- no closure,
- no forced redundancies,
- no hidden restructuring by gradual depletion,
- and binding investment commitments.

If “protected” only means protected until the next budget cycle, then it is not protection.

## On the other stakeholders

### To management
You still have **not proven** that closures are unavoidable.  
You keep saying closures are the strongest lever. That may be true in theory. It is **not** proof that they are necessary in this case.

If you want to claim necessity, then bring the site-by-site comparison. Show:
- fixed-cost base,
- utilization,
- break-even volume,
- savings under non-closure measures,
- one-time charges,
- employment effects,
- and execution risk.

Until then, the closure case remains an assertion, not a demonstrated need.

And I will say this plainly: any “objective trigger” that ultimately leads to a closure decision is not acceptable if the trigger is designed around management’s preferred outcome. We will not sign away jobs and sites today on the promise that the knife may be used later.

### To the controlling family
If you truly want stewardship, then stop treating closure pressure as a legitimate bargaining tool from the start.  
Use your governance power to enforce a **real** non-closure comparison, not a managed march toward shutdowns.

You cannot claim to defend long-term value while normalizing threats against the workforce that actually creates that value.

### To the state shareholder
I appreciate the idea of protecting anchor sites. But I need to be blunt: a deal that preserves only a subset while allowing others to be hollowed out is not good enough if it sacrifices the job-security framework across the group.

Our position is not “save the politically convenient sites.”  
Our position is: **no German plant closures and no forced redundancies anywhere in the domestic footprint.**

### To institutional investors
You are right that a weak settlement is not acceptable. We agree.  
But you are wrong if you think the only serious answer is to keep closure on the table as an ever-present disciplinary threat.

A serious restructuring can be built from:
- temporary employee contributions,
- working-time flexibility,
- voluntary exits,
- management overhead cuts,
- product and investment commitments,
- and capacity adjustment **without** site destruction.

If that still does not close the gap, then management should first look at its own cost base and strategic choices, not immediately at closing plants and breaking guarantees.

### To the finance expert
You are useful when you insist on the right metrics. But I want to be equally clear: an “economically equivalent” package is not one that merely matches closure on paper while destroying trust and institutional stability in practice.

The threshold must include:
- site preservation,
- no forced layoffs,
- enforceable co-determination,
- and long-term industrial capability.

If a model ignores those, it is too narrow to be the basis for a legitimate decision.

We are **not** prepared to consider:

## Direct answer to the facilitator’s bridge options

**Rejected.**  
Because it keeps closures alive as a later instrument of pressure. We do not accept that framework.

### Option 2: “Selective capacity reduction without full plant closure”
**Conditionally discussable only if it truly preserves every German plant.**  
If “selective” means line changes, shift changes, and product reallocation **without** plant closures and without forced layoffs, then fine — bring the numbers.  
If it means shutting sites indirectly, then no.

### Option 3: “Protected sites plus structural burden sharing”
**Discussable only if protection is real and group-wide.**  
Protected sites must be protected in substance, not just in name. And burden sharing must not be a euphemism for workforce concessions while management keeps the decisive tools.
