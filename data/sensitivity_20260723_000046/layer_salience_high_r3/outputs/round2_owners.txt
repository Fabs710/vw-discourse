I appreciate the clarity in the round 2 positions. I will be equally direct.

As controlling shareholder, my test is simple: **does the package restore competitiveness materially and durably, and can it be executed without tearing the company apart?** If not, it is not a solution.

## Where I agree
I agree with management, the state, and the investors on one central point: **this cannot be a cosmetic compromise**. The company has a structural cost problem, and if we do not reset it, we will keep eroding margins, investment capacity, and long-term value.

I also agree with the workforce side on another point: **a prolonged conflict would be value-destructive**. Industrial peace matters. A deal that triggers a long strike, permanent mistrust, or governance paralysis is not acceptable either.

## Where I disagree with the workforce side
I do **not** accept “no plant closures and no compulsory redundancies” as an absolute starting line if it prevents a real restructuring.

I understand the social and political logic behind that line. But from an ownership and governance perspective, it is not enough to say “protect every site” if the result is an uncompetitive cost base and a company that underperforms for years. That would be a slow destruction of value, not protection of value.

At the same time, I also do **not** support a blunt closure-led strategy if it creates a conflict spiral or undermines the company’s ability to execute. So I am not backing management to simply force through pain for its own sake.

## To management
You are right to insist on structural savings. But I want to be precise: **“all options remain open” is only credible if you can show a disciplined path, not if it becomes a bargaining weapon without a workable end state**.

I need to see:
- quantified savings,
- a clear timing path,
- and a settlement that restores margin without leaving the company in an endless confrontation.

If closures are truly necessary, they must be justified as the last-resort instrument, not the rhetorical starting point. If alternatives can deliver the same economic effect, I want to see them.

## To the works council and union
I hear your insistence on no closures and no compulsory redundancies. I also hear your call for burden-sharing.

But I need to be blunt: **time-limited concessions alone do not solve a structural competitiveness problem**. If you want to avoid closures and forced layoffs, then the alternative package must be economically equivalent in effect, not just more comfortable in presentation.

That means:
- real and durable labor-cost reduction,
- measurable capacity reduction,
- credible flexibility on deployment and production structure,
- and a package that survives beyond the next bargaining round.

I will not support a deal that preserves every guarantee, every site, and every legacy arrangement while leaving the company structurally too expensive.

## To the regional state
I respect the state’s role and the need to preserve industrial continuity. But I also need to be clear: **social stability cannot be used to freeze the business model**.

Yes, closures are serious. Yes, they are politically sensitive. But if every difficult adjustment is treated as unacceptable, then the company will pay later in lower investment, weaker competitiveness, and eventually fewer jobs overall.

Your support is valuable if it helps shape a settlement that is both economically real and socially sustainable. It is not helpful if it simply blocks every hard decision.

## What would move me
I am prepared to support a negotiated alternative to closures and compulsory redundancies if it contains all of the following:
- a **material, durable cost reset**,
- **credible capacity reduction**,
- a **meaningful employee contribution**,
- **executability without prolonged industrial conflict**,
- and **protection of the company’s long-term investment capacity**.

I am open on the method:
- capacity realignment,
- working-time solutions,
- internal redeployment,
- qualification,
- voluntary instruments,
- and balanced employee contributions.

I am **not** open to a package that merely preserves the status quo in a softer form.

## My bottom line
I will support a tough restructuring if it is:
1. economically real,
2. durable,
3. executable,
4. and value-preserving over the long term.

I will not support denial of the problem.  
I will also not support a conflict spiral that damages the enterprise’s value.

So the next step must be concrete: bring forward a package that closes the gap materially. If you cannot do that, then management must keep its structural options open, including the hard ones.
