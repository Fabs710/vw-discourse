Management says the company needs a durable solution. On that point, I agree.

Where I fundamentally disagree is the conclusion that **plant closures and compulsory redundancies must remain on the table** as if they were just ordinary tools. They are not ordinary tools from our perspective; they are a direct attack on the workforce, on trust, and on the entire co-determination framework that has allowed this company to succeed for decades.

### To management
You are asking the workforce to pay for strategic errors it did not make. That is unacceptable.

If you want real support for restructuring, then present a package that proves you can achieve the savings **without** closures and **without** forced layoffs. We are not here to rubber-stamp a fait accompli. We are here to co-determine the future of this company, and that means alternatives must be seriously pursued, not used as bargaining theater.

What we can discuss:
- capacity reductions through planning and shift adjustments,
- working-time measures,
- internal redeployment across plants,
- qualification and training,
- voluntary instruments,
- temporary or negotiated employee contributions,
- and a binding investment program for German sites.

What we will not discuss as acceptable starting points:
- shutting German plants,
- compulsory redundancies,
- removing job-security protections as a shortcut,
- or threatening the workforce into concessions.

### To the family holding and investors
You both keep saying “economically real” and “durable.” Fine. Then prove that your preferred route is the only one.

Right now, what I hear is a demand for structural savings with the workforce carrying the full downside. That is not burden-sharing; that is shifting risk downward. If you want a value-preserving solution, then you must accept that preserving industrial capability in Germany is part of value, not a sentimental extra.

I also reject the logic that says: if plant closures are not accepted, then the workforce is somehow blocking reality. Reality also includes:
- co-determination,
- the legal veto over closures,
- the company’s social responsibility,
- and the damage a prolonged industrial conflict would do to output, reputation, and execution.

### To the regional state
I appreciate that you reject closures as a default. But “only if alternatives fail” is still too close to normalizing closure as an option. We need a stronger line from all governance actors: **the priority must be to keep every German plant open** and to build the restructuring around that assumption.

### On what would move us
Let me be precise: the works council can move on methods, not on core guarantees.

We can engage constructively on:
- a serious capacity plan,
- redeployment across the German network,
- training and qualification,
- working-time flexibility,
- and a negotiated employee contribution.

But we would only consider a settlement if it includes:
1. **No plant closures in Germany**
2. **No compulsory redundancies**
3. **Binding site and job guarantees through 2030**
4. **A real co-determination role in shaping the restructuring and implementation**
5. **Visible contributions from management and ownership, not just from employees**

If these conditions are not on the table, then there is no basis for a fair settlement.

### Bottom line
We will not be pushed into accepting closures or forced layoffs because management calls them “structural.” A strategy that destroys jobs to solve a cost problem is not a sustainable strategy.

If the company wants peace, it must offer security. If it wants sacrifice, it must share it. If it wants cooperation, it must respect co-determination.

Otherwise, we remain in resistance.
