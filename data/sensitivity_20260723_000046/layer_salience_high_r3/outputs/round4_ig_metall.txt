## Where I disagree strongly

### 1) The “no-closure test phase” is not acceptable if it keeps closures alive as a standing threat
Moderator: you are trying to make the closure threat look like a neutral review mechanism. It is not.

If the workforce is asked to deliver concessions under a phase where closure remains an exit option, then every promise we make is made under coercion. That is precisely what we will not accept.

A meaningful negotiation requires **a real no-closure basis from the start**, not a timed ultimatum.

### 2) Compulsory redundancies are not a legitimate “failure mechanism”
I reject the idea that forced layoffs can be kept as a narrowly defined backstop. Once you insert compulsory redundancies as a contingency, you have already weakened the guarantee and opened the door to pressure tactics.

If the company needs headcount adjustment, then use:
- voluntary exits,
- early retirement,
- vacancy management,
- internal redeployment,
- training,
- reduced hours,
- and temporary contributions.

Not coercion.

### 3) “Economic equivalence” cannot mean that workers carry the structural pain while others stay insulated
Management, family owners, and investors keep saying the same thing in slightly different forms: the alternative must be economically equivalent.

That is fair in principle. But in practice, “equivalent” cannot mean:
- employees give up wages and security,
- sites remain under threat,
- owners contribute little,
- and management keeps the option to cut deeper later.

If you want a no-closure settlement, then the burden must be shared visibly:
- **management compensation restraint**
- **distribution restraint**
- **owner-side capital commitment**
- **investment commitments**
- **real industrial continuity**

Without that, “equivalence” is just a polite term for shifting the pain downward.
