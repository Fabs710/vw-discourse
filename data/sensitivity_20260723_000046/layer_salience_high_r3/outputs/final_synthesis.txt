## 1. Decision context
The company faces a structural restructuring crisis: overcapacity, high German cost levels, weak market conditions, and pressure to restore margins without losing future-investment capacity. Governance is unusually constraining: co-determination gives labor an effective veto on plant closure, while the controlling family, the regional state, and investors also hold blocking influence. The decision is therefore not simply whether to cut costs, but whether the company can achieve a durable reset through a negotiated package that is financially credible, legally feasible, and socially governable.

## 2. Stakeholder assembly
Weighted by standing, the most consequential actors are: Group & Brand Management, the Controlling Family Holding, the Regional State Government, the Works Council, the Industrial Union, and Institutional Investors. Management, family, and state carry high governance weight; the works council and union carry high legitimacy and operational leverage through co-determination and strikes; investors carry financial legitimacy and support value-based constraints but are less decisive individually.

## 3. Final positions
### Group & Brand Management
Management accepts the need for durable restructuring and supports voluntary exits, redeployment, working-time measures, productivity gains, and investment commitments. It rejects a blanket no-closure/no-forced-redundancy rule and insists closures and compulsory redundancies must remain available if the alternative fails to close the savings gap.

### Works Council
The works council holds firm: no plant closures in Germany, no compulsory redundancies, binding job and site guarantees through 2030, real co-determination in implementation, and visible burden-sharing from management and owners. It is willing to discuss voluntary exits, hours reductions, redeployment, and productivity improvements, but only inside a durable no-closure framework.

### Industrial Union
The union aligns closely with the works council: no closures, no forced redundancies, binding security, and fair burden-sharing. It is prepared to negotiate wage restraint, bonus moderation, reduced hours, voluntary exits, and qualification, but rejects using closures or layoffs as bargaining leverage.

### Regional State Government
The state supports industrial continuity, no closures as the baseline, no compulsory redundancies, and stronger burden-sharing from capital and management. It is not defending stagnation, but it wants a binding, executable industrial settlement that protects regional employment and avoids normalizing site closures.

### Controlling Family Holding
The family supports a durable competitiveness reset and rejects symbolic compromises that preserve the cost base. It agrees that alternatives may replace a large share of closure savings, but insists closures and compulsory redundancies must remain available unless a no-closure package is economically equivalent and enforceable.

### Institutional Investors
Investors want margin restoration, structural cost reduction, and a credible, durable solution. They are open to voluntary exits, redeployment, working-time flexibility, and owner/management burden-sharing, but will not support a settlement that freezes overcapacity or removes hard restructuring tools entirely.

### Expert: plant-by-plant restructuring economist
The economist’s position is decisive for feasibility: a no-closure package can work, but only if it captures roughly 70–80% of the recurring savings of a closure-led scenario and achieves real capacity reduction. Alternatives can meaningfully reduce labor cost and part of overhead, but they usually do not fully eliminate plant-level fixed burdens.

## 4. Genuine conflicts (structurally incompatible positions)
The central incompatibility is between employee-side insistence on a permanent no-closure/no-forced-redundancy settlement and management/shareholder insistence on keeping hard measures available if equivalence is not reached. A second structural conflict is over what counts as “equivalent”: labor sees durable protections plus concessions as sufficient if jobs and sites are preserved, while capital and management define equivalence primarily in recurring savings, capacity reduction, and margin recovery. A third conflict concerns sequencing: labor demands alternatives first without closure pressure, while management wants fallback options retained from the outset.

## 5. Areas of convergence
All sides accept the need for a durable restructuring rather than a short-term patch. All sides accept that voluntary exits, redeployment, qualification, working-time solutions, and some employee contribution belong in the package. All sides also recognize the need for investment in future products and transformation through 2030, and none favors open-ended conflict if a negotiated settlement is possible.

## 6. Key trade-offs for the decision-maker
The decision-maker must trade off job and site security against the company’s ability to remove structural overcapacity quickly and credibly. A no-closure deal improves industrial peace and preserves skills, but may leave residual fixed costs and risk a later crisis if savings are insufficient. A closure-led solution maximizes cost reset but carries high conflict, legal, political, and execution risk, plus severe social cost.

## 7. Integrative pathways
1. **Time-limited no-closure pact with quantified savings test**: no closures and no compulsory redundancies for a defined period, with plant-by-plant targets, voluntary exits, hours reductions, and an agreed review mechanism.  
2. **No closures, but durable capacity reduction**: all German sites remain open, while shift reductions, product consolidation, indirect-function cuts, and internal footprint shrinking deliver real capacity loss.  
3. **Hybrid settlement with conditional hard-measure trigger**: immediate moratorium on closures/forced layoffs, followed by a strict, pre-agreed trigger if savings or utilization targets are materially missed, with further negotiations required before any hard action.  
4. **Burden-sharing package**: employee contributions paired with management compensation restraint, owner-side distribution restraint, and binding investment commitments to make the deal politically and financially credible.

## 8. Open questions
- What is the exact residual savings gap after voluntary tools, working-time measures, and redeployment?
- Which plants or functions carry the highest fixed-cost burden and how much can be reduced without closure?
- What level of owner and management contribution is visible enough to sustain labor acceptance?
- What enforcement mechanism would prevent a “voluntary” program from becoming disguised forced downsizing?
- What threshold of recurring savings would the board, family, and state each consider sufficient?

## 9. Recommended next step
Commission a plant-by-plant financial and capacity model that compares three cases: closure-led restructuring, strong no-closure restructuring, and a hybrid compromise. The model should quantify recurring annual savings, one-time transition costs, capacity reductions, and 2030 job-security outcomes, then map those results to the governance constraints of co-determination and shareholder vetoes. This is the necessary basis for a real bargaining round, because the interaction revealed that the decisive dispute is not principle but residual-gap arithmetic.

## 10. Emergent insights that only appeared through multi-turn interaction
The discussion revealed that the true bargaining unit is not “close vs. don’t close,” but the size of the remaining gap after voluntary and productivity measures. It also became clear that labor’s red line is not simply about preserving every job; it is about preventing closure and forced redundancy from remaining active pressure tools during negotiation. The expert input sharpened the debate by setting an implicit equivalence threshold: a no-closure package needs roughly 70–80% of closure-case recurring savings to be economically credible, which makes partial compromise possible but rules out symbolic packages.

A further emergent insight is that burden-sharing is not just distributive fairness; it is a credibility mechanism. Without visible management and owner sacrifice, employee concessions are likely to be seen as coercive rather than negotiated, reducing the chance of implementation. Finally, the conversation showed that the state’s role is not merely political support but governance leverage: it can help define whether industrial continuity is enforced through binding commitments or allowed to dissolve into future re-litigation of the same crisis.
