We agree on the **objective**, not on the **test**.

The objective is clear: restore competitive margins, reduce structural cost, and stop value erosion. On that, we are aligned with management, the family, and even the labor side in principle. But the only question that matters to us as shareholders is whether the package is **credible, durable, and economically sufficient**.

## Where we agree
- The company needs a **durable restructuring**, not a cosmetic truce.
- The current German cost base appears **too high**.
- **Voluntary exits, redeployment, working-time flexibility, and productivity-linked investment** should all be part of the solution.
- A prolonged strike or open-ended conflict would be **value-destructive**.
- Management and owners should show **visible burden-sharing** if they want labor concessions.

## Where we disagree

### 1) “No closures” cannot be an absolute starting point
To the works council, union, and state: your position is understandable politically, but as capital providers we cannot back a package that rules out the main structural tool before the economics are tested.

If overcapacity is real and fixed-cost heavy, then plant closures must remain available **unless and until** a quantified alternative closes the gap.  
Otherwise, this becomes a guarantee of **continued margin impairment**, which we will not support.

### 2) “No compulsory redundancies” is not acceptable as a hard veto
We prefer voluntary tools. We want them used first. But we do not accept a permanent prohibition on compulsory redundancies if the company cannot otherwise achieve the required savings.

A restructuring cannot be held hostage to an absolute employment veto if that means shareholders are left with an uncompetitive business.

### 3) Guarantees through 2030 are only acceptable if the economics are truly covered
Binding job and site guarantees are not a problem in themselves. The problem is when they freeze a cost structure that the market no longer supports.

If you want no closures and no forced layoffs, then show:
- quantified recurring savings,
- credible capacity reduction,
- durable labor-cost reduction,
- and a clear path back to target margins.

Without that, guarantees are just a transfer of risk from labor to capital and future earnings.

## On the specialist input
The expert’s analysis is helpful and, frankly, consistent with our view:

- Alternatives can replace a **substantial share** of closure savings.
- But they often do **not fully replicate** the fixed-cost reset of a true closure if overcapacity is structural.
- Working-time measures are a bridge, not a complete solution.
- Redeployment preserves employment, but does not by itself eliminate excess cost.

That means the burden of proof is on the employee side: if you want closures and redundancies off the table, the alternative must be **economically equivalent in effect**, not merely more acceptable socially.

## What would move us
We could support a no-closure settlement only if it contains all of the following:
1. **Quantified annual savings** that materially restore margin
2. **Real capacity reduction**, not just softer staffing language
3. **Durable labor-cost contribution**
4. **Owner and management burden-sharing**
5. **Investment commitments that improve productivity and future earnings**
6. **Strong enforcement and review mechanisms**

We are open on the method:
- voluntary exits,
- early retirement bridges,
- redeployment,
- shift consolidation,
- temporary wage moderation,
- bonus restraint,
- and targeted investment.

But we are **not** open to a settlement that preserves every site and every protection while the company remains structurally too expensive.

## To management
You are right to demand a real reset. But “closures remain on the table” is only credible if you can show the residual gap after alternatives. Give us the numbers.  
We want a side-by-side comparison of:
- closure-led restructuring,
- no-closure alternative,
- transition costs,
- timing,
- and risk of underdelivery.

If the no-closure package genuinely closes the gap, fine. If it doesn’t, then hard options must remain available.

## To the works council and union
We are not asking for a cosmetic sacrifice. We are asking for a package that **restores competitiveness**. If you want us to support a no-closure outcome, then it must be more than a political promise.

What we will not back:
- a deal that keeps all German sites effectively insulated,
- a permanent ban on hard restructuring tools,
- or a settlement that delays the same crisis to 2027 or 2028.

## To the regional state
Your role is to push for industrial continuity. That is legitimate. But continuity without competitiveness is not value-preserving.

Use your leverage to help shape a quantified, executable package. Do not use it to shield the company from necessary adjustment.

## Bottom line
Our support depends on one thing:

**Does the package credibly and durably restore competitive margins?**

If yes, we can support a negotiated alternative to closures and forced redundancies.  
If no, we will oppose a cosmetic settlement and insist that structural options remain open.

If the parties want a specialist estimate of how much a no-closure package must save to be economically equivalent, we should summon one now.
