I’ll respond directly.

## Where I agree
I agree that:
- the company needs a **durable restructuring**, not a cosmetic delay;
- **voluntary exits, redeployment, working-time flexibility, qualification, and investment-linked productivity gains** all belong in the toolbox;
- **management and ownership must contribute visibly** if we want a deal that can hold;
- a drawn-out conflict would damage output, reputation, and value.

I also take seriously the specialist input: alternatives can replace a **large part** of closure-related savings. That is useful. It is not, however, enough to settle this debate in the workforce’s favor.

## Where I disagree — and sharply
### 1) “No closures” cannot be a hard precondition
To the Works Council and Union: I respect the industrial logic of your argument, but I do **not** accept a blanket no-closure rule as a starting point.

Why? Because our problem is not symbolic. It is structural:
- German factory costs are far above target,
- the European market is shrinking,
- competition is intensifying,
- and the brand is missing its return-on-sales target.

If we rule out closures and compulsory redundancies outright, before testing whether alternatives actually close the gap, then we are not restructuring the business — we are preserving an uncompetitive cost base and hoping sentiment will substitute for economics.

That is not acceptable.

### 2) “No compulsory redundancies” cannot be an absolute veto either
I hear the concern about coercion. But if the alternative package does not deliver enough durable savings, then the company cannot be left with no credible enforcement mechanism.

Compulsory redundancies are not my preferred instrument. They are not the goal. But I will not agree to a framework that makes the company structurally unable to act if voluntary tools fall short.

### 3) Guarantees through 2030 cannot freeze the business
To all employee-side actors: binding job and site guarantees sound attractive, but if they lock in the current footprint regardless of economics, then they become a straightjacket.

We need protection for transition — yes. We do **not** need a legal shell that preserves every site and every legacy cost structure through 2030 while the market keeps moving against us.

## On the specialist’s assessment
The economist confirmed what I already know from the numbers:
- voluntary exits can save a lot;
- redeployment helps;
- working-time measures help;
- investment improves productivity;
- but closures produce the strongest fixed-cost reduction.

That matters. Because our issue is not only labor cost. It is also:
- underused capacity,
- plant overhead,
- complexity,
- and the drag of a footprint that is too large for the volume outlook.

So yes, alternatives can deliver substantial savings. But “substantial” is not the same as “sufficient.”

If the remaining gap is material, then management cannot be asked to pretend it does not exist.

## To the Works Council
You say we are turning your red lines into “parameters for testing.” No. We are insisting on economic reality.

I do **not** dispute the value of co-determination, skills, or industrial know-how. But co-determination does not mean we are obliged to maintain every site regardless of cost.

You also say closures are destruction, not adjustment. That is emotionally understandable, but it does not solve the financing problem. The company needs margin, not sentiment.

If you want to avoid closures, then the alternative must do more than protect jobs in name. It must deliver:
- durable labor-cost reduction,
- measurable capacity reduction,
- and a cost base that actually works.

That is the test. Not slogans, not moral appeals.

## To the Union
I appreciate that you are defending against disguised downsizing. That is legitimate. But you are still asking us to accept a no-closure framework without proving that it can restore competitiveness.

A temporary wage restraint, bonus moderation, and reduced hours are not enough on their own if the structural gap remains. You know that as well as I do.

So here is the point: if you want to avoid hard measures, then bring an alternative that is economically equivalent in the only sense that matters — recurring savings, capacity reduction, and margin improvement.

## To the Regional State
I understand your political constraints and your role as an anchor shareholder. But “no closures as a starting point” is not the same as “no closures ever.” We need to be careful not to turn political discomfort into industrial policy.

If the state wants to help preserve the industrial base, then it should support a package that is:
- durable,
- quantified,
- and executable.

It should not support a settlement that delays the hard choices until the company is weaker and the eventual correction is harsher.

## To the Controlling Family and Investors
I agree with you on the central point: a cosmetic compromise would destroy value.

But I also expect you to recognize that the workforce is not the only source of burden-sharing. If you want a negotiated alternative to closures, then management and ownership must show real restraint and commitment, not just ask labor to absorb the adjustment.

That said, burden-sharing does not change the basic arithmetic. If alternatives do not close enough of the gap, we cannot declare victory because the optics are better.

## On the proposed “no-closure test phase”
I am open to a **time-limited restructuring phase** only if it is real and enforceable.

That means:
- specific savings targets,
- specific capacity-reduction measures,
- specific dates,
- and a clear review point.

But I reject the idea that a test phase can become a permanent shield against hard decisions. If the phase fails, consequences must be real. Otherwise it is just a postponement device.

## What management can accept
I can work with:
- voluntary exit programs,
- early retirement bridges,
- redeployment,
- working-time flexibility,
- temporary labor-cost contributions,
- productivity measures,
- and investment commitments through 2030.

I can even work with a strong no-compulsory-redundancy preference if the package genuinely delivers.

What I will **not** accept is:
- a blanket no-closure taboo,
- full site guarantees regardless of economics,
- or a deal that leaves the cost base structurally uncompetitive.

## What I need to see next
Before anyone asks me to retreat from closures or compulsory redundancies, I want three things on the table:

1. **A quantified savings gap**
   - by year,
   - by cost line,
   - by site.

2. **A quantified alternative package**
   - what voluntary exits, hours, redeployment, and productivity measures actually deliver.

3. **A residual gap analysis**
   - what remains uncovered,
   - and why.

If the alternative truly closes the gap, then fine — it will be supported.  
If it does not, then all structural options must remain open.

## Bottom line
I am not here to impose closures for their own sake.  
I am here to restore competitiveness.

That means:
- **no cosmetic compromise**,
- **no permanent overcapacity**,
- **no cost structure that misses the market again**,
- and **no agreement that looks fair but fails economically**.

If the employee side can bring a package that is truly equivalent in effect, I will consider it seriously. If not, then closures and compulsory redundancies cannot be ruled out as instruments of restructuring.
