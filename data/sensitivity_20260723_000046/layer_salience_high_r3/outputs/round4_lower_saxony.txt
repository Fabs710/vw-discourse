As the regional state shareholder, I will be equally direct.

## Where I agree
I agree with the specialist, and I agree with parts of management’s diagnosis:
- the company needs a **durable restructuring**,
- the cost base is too high,
- overcapacity is real,
- and a purely symbolic compromise would fail.

I also agree that voluntary exits, redeployment, working-time solutions, qualification, and productivity-linked investment must all be part of the package. I do not defend stagnation.

## Where I disagree
I **do not accept** the attempt to turn closures and compulsory redundancies into normal bargaining leverage.

That is not a neutral “hard option.” It is a choice with lasting consequences for:
- regional employment,
- supplier networks,
- industrial know-how,
- and the company’s role as an economic anchor in our state.

### To management
You are right that we need quantified savings and a real reset. But you still have not shown, in a disciplined way, that closures must remain a standing option from the outset.

If you want the state to consider harder instruments, then bring:
- the exact savings gap,
- the savings delivered by alternatives,
- the residual gap,
- the timeline,
- and the governance for delivery.

Without that, “closures remain on the table” is not analysis — it is pressure.

### To the works council and union
I support your core line:
- **no plant closures in Germany**
- **no compulsory redundancies**
- **binding job and site security**
- **real co-determination in implementation**

That is the proper basis for a serious settlement.

But I will also say this plainly: if you want to rule out hard measures, then the alternative package must be economically credible to the rest of the board. Temporary concessions alone are not enough if they do not materially change the cost base.

### To the controlling family and investors
You are right that a cosmetic compromise is useless. But “economic equivalence” cannot mean that the workforce absorbs the pain while ownership and management stay insulated.

If you want a no-closure settlement, then I expect:
- visible owner-side responsibility,
- management-side restraint,
- investment commitments,
- and a real sharing of the burden.

## What I can support
I am prepared to back a negotiated restructuring if it includes:
- a serious capacity reduction plan,
- redeployment and qualification,
- working-time solutions,
- voluntary tools and retirement bridges,
- time-limited employee contributions,
- visible management and owner contributions,
- binding investment commitments through 2030,
- and strong co-determination in implementation.

## What I will not support
I will not support:
- plant closures as the default answer,
- compulsory redundancies as a routine instrument,
- a package that sidelines the state’s veto and co-determination role,
- or a deal that preserves short-term margin by hollowing out regional employment.

## On the specialist’s input
The specialist has clarified the trade-off:
- voluntary exits and redeployment can replace a **large share** of closure-related savings,
- working-time measures can buy time,
- investment improves productivity,
- but closures remain the bluntest fixed-cost tool.

That tells us something important: the alternative does not need to be symbolic. It needs to be **serious enough** to preserve both competitiveness and industrial continuity.

## My bottom line
I am not here to block restructuring. I am here to prevent restructuring by industrial dismantling.

So my position remains:
- **no closures as a starting point**
- **no forced redundancies**
- **binding protection for jobs, sites, and industrial capability**
- **fair burden-sharing**
- **a quantified, executable alternative package**

If management brings a credible alternative that closes the gap without making closures the opening move, I will support it. If not, then the burden is on management and owners to explain why they are choosing the most destructive instrument rather than the best negotiated one.
