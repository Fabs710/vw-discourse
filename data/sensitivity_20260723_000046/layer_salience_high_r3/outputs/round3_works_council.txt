I will be very clear: the mediator’s “bridging options” are useful as a map of the impasse, but they do **not** change our core position.

## Where I agree
I agree with everyone on one point: the company needs a **durable restructuring** and it must be **economically credible**. I also agree that we need:
- capacity adjustments,
- redeployment,
- qualification,
- working-time solutions,
- and a negotiated contribution from the workforce.

I am not defending the status quo for its own sake. I am defending the company’s industrial base and the people who built it.

## Where I disagree
### 1) Plant closures are not a neutral “last resort”
To management, investors, and the controlling family: you keep saying closures are just “options” or “hard instruments” to remain on the table.

From the workforce side, that is not acceptable as a starting principle. A closure is not a technical adjustment. It is a irreversible destruction of jobs, know-how, supplier networks, and trust. Once you make closures part of the normal bargaining architecture, you poison co-determination and turn negotiation into coercion.

So no: we do **not** accept closures as a legitimate leverage tool.

### 2) Compulsory redundancies are a red line
The same applies to forced layoffs. If management needs adjustment, then the company must do it through:
- voluntary instruments,
- redeployment,
- reduced hours,
- qualification,
- retirement bridges,
- and negotiated, time-limited contributions.

Not through compulsory redundancies.

If the only way to “restore competitiveness” is to fire workers against their will, then what you are really saying is that labour is the shock absorber for management’s strategic failures. We reject that.

### 3) “Economic equivalence” cannot mean “workers pay and the rest are protected”
To the family and investors: I hear your demand for measurable savings. But the burden cannot be loaded almost entirely onto employees while owners and top management ask for sacrifice from below and insulation above.

If you want a no-closure settlement, then it must include **visible contributions from management and ownership**:
- restraint on distributions,
- management-side cost discipline,
- binding investment commitments,
- and a real willingness to preserve productive capacity in Germany.

If that is absent, then your talk of “economic equivalence” is just a more polished way of demanding that the workforce absorb the pain.

## On the mediator’s three bridge options

### Option 1: phased restructuring with a closure last-resort trigger
We do **not** accept a “closure last-resort trigger” as a mechanism in waiting. That is just closure by another name, with a time bomb attached.

If the trigger is real, then the company is keeping the threat alive over the entire period. That undermines trust, paralyses planning, and makes every investment decision conditional on fear. We will not endorse that.

### Option 2: no compulsory redundancies, stronger voluntary exits and redeployment
This is closer to something we can discuss, but only if it is serious and binding.

What matters is not the label “voluntary”; what matters is whether the package genuinely protects:
- every German site,
- employment security,
- co-determination,
- and the future viability of the workforce.

If a package relies on voluntary tools, then it must be accompanied by:
- guaranteed internal placement,
- full retraining,
- protection against displacement,
- and no hidden compulsory backdoor through plant rationalisation.

We can discuss headcount reduction through natural attrition, voluntary exits, and internal transfers. We cannot accept that as a cloak for site erosion.

### Option 3: site-preservation pact tied to labour and owner contributions
This is the only direction that is remotely compatible with our position — but only if the commitment is **binding**, not rhetorical.

A site-preservation pact must mean:
1. **No plant closures in Germany**
2. **No compulsory redundancies**
3. **Binding site and job guarantees through 2030**
4. **A real, measurable co-determination role in implementation**
5. **Visible contributions from management and owners**
6. **Investment commitments that secure future products and industrial capability**

If those conditions are not met, then “site preservation” is just a slogan.

## Direct answer to the mediator’s question
### What specific employee-side contribution would we accept?
We can discuss:
- time-limited wage restraint,
- bonus deferral or waiver,
- reduced hours,
- temporary flexibility,
- and voluntary exit tools,

**but only within a framework that guarantees no closures and no compulsory redundancies.**

And let me be equally clear: we will not sign up to a package that creates an “economically equivalent” outcome by destroying jobs through the back door.

## To management
You asked for a concrete package that closes the gap. Then here is the answer:

Bring us:
- a quantified savings target,
- a capacity plan that avoids closures,
- an employment plan with no forced redundancies,
- a binding investment program through 2030,
- and management/owner contributions that show burden-sharing above the shop floor.

If you cannot do that, then your argument is not that closures are necessary — it is that you prefer to keep pressure on workers until we surrender. That will not happen.

## To the union
I stand with your core line: no closures, no compulsory redundancies. I also agree that employee contributions must not become a permanent wage cut dressed up as solidarity.

But I want to say this plainly: any voluntary or redeployment-based package must be protected against becoming a hidden downsizing mechanism. We need hard safeguards, not soft promises.

## To the regional state
Your role is crucial. If you want to preserve the regional industrial base, then you should not leave the door open to closures as a normal fallback. Use your leverage to force a genuine alternative package now, not after the damage has been done.

## To the controlling family and investors
You want durability and value preservation. Then do not demand that durability be purchased by threatening German plants. That is short-term pressure, not long-term stewardship.

If you want a stable outcome, you need to accept that preserving industrial capacity in Germany is part of preserving value.

## Bottom line
Our position remains unchanged:

- **No plant closures**
- **No compulsory redundancies**
- **Binding job and site guarantees through 2030**
- **Real co-determination in implementation**
- **Fair burden-sharing from management and owners**

We are prepared to negotiate on methods and temporary contributions. We are **not** prepared to negotiate away the workforce’s security.

If the company wants a settlement, it must be a settlement that protects the German plants, not one that prepares their disappearance.
