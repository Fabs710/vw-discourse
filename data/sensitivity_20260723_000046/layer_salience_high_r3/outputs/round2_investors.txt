We agree with the central diagnosis, and that matters: **this company needs a durable restructuring that materially restores competitiveness.** If the result is merely a softer headline with the same cost base and the same margin problem, we will not support it.

## Where we agree
- **Management is right** that the current German cost base and overcapacity are unsustainable.
- **The family is right** that prolonged conflict and a weak compromise would destroy value.
- **The state is right** that a settlement that tears apart the company’s social license can also destroy value.
- **The workforce side is right** that a cosmetic deal is not acceptable.

So far, so good.

## Where we disagree
We do **not** accept the current employee-side line as a sufficient solution:
- **“No plant closures”**
- **“No compulsory redundancies”**
- **“Binding guarantees through 2030”**

on their own do **not** answer the economics. If those conditions block a real cost reset, then they are not a responsible defense of long-term value; they are a way of preserving structural underperformance.

We also do **not** accept management’s rhetoric unless it is backed by numbers. Saying “closures are on the table” is not yet a plan. If closures are being used mainly as bargaining pressure without a disciplined execution path, that is also value-destructive.

## Our position
As capital-market shareholders, our test is simple:

**Does the package credibly restore competitive margins on a durable basis?**

If yes, we can support it.  
If no, we will oppose it, regardless of how politically elegant it looks.

That means we need:
- **measurable, durable labor-cost reduction**
- **credible and permanent capacity reduction**
- **clear execution timing**
- **deal certainty**
- **protection of investment capacity for future products**

## What we can support
We are open to a negotiated alternative to closures and forced redundancies **only if it is economically equivalent in effect**. That means:
- real fixed-cost reduction, not just temporary concessions,
- real capacity adjustment, not just shifting volumes around,
- productivity improvements that are enforceable,
- and a package that survives beyond the next bargaining round.

If the workforce side wants to avoid hard measures, it needs to bring something that closes the gap materially. Time-limited concessions alone are not enough.

## What we cannot support
We will not support:
- a deal that leaves the company structurally too expensive,
- a compromise that preserves all legacy guarantees while margins remain weak,
- or a prolonged stand-off that delays the adjustment and damages earnings.

We also will not support a “closure-first” outcome if it is simply a blunt instrument that triggers a strike spiral, damages execution, and destroys value without a better end state.

## To management
You need to be more concrete. Bring:
- the size of the required savings,
- the timing,
- the operational changes,
- and the governance of delivery.

If closures are genuinely necessary, show why alternatives fail on economics, not just on management preference.

## To the works council and union
We understand your red lines, but they do not by themselves create a viable business model. If you want us to back a no-closure outcome, it must be **financially equivalent** to the hard measures. Not politically equivalent. Financially equivalent.

## Bottom line
We are prepared to back a negotiated settlement **only if** it restores competitive margins, reduces structural costs, and protects long-term enterprise value.

Anything less is a cosmetic settlement, and we will not endorse it.
