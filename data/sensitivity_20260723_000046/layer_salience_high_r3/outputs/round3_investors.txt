We agree on the **objective**: this company needs a **durable** restructuring that restores competitive margins and stops the erosion of shareholder value.

Where we do **not** agree is on the idea that a deal can be credible if it simply preserves the current cost structure under nicer wording.

## Our position

As capital-market shareholders, our test remains very simple:

**Does the package materially and durably restore competitiveness?**

If yes, we can support it.  
If no, we will not support it, regardless of how politically attractive it looks.

## To management
You are right that:
- the cost base is too high,
- overcapacity is real,
- and a cosmetic settlement would be value-destructive.

But you still have not shown, in a disciplined way, the **quantified gap**:
- what savings are required,
- by when,
- and how much each alternative delivers versus closure.

That is the key issue. “All options open” is not enough. We need a **side-by-side economic case**:
1. closure-led scenario,
2. no-closure alternative scenario,
3. transition cost,
4. timing,
5. execution risk.

Without that, we cannot judge whether closures are truly necessary or just the bluntest available lever.

## To the works council and union
We understand your red lines, and we also understand why you reject being the sole shock absorber.

But your current position still has a problem:  
**“No closures, no compulsory redundancies, guarantees through 2030”** does not answer the economics unless the alternative package is actually equivalent in effect.

The specialist’s analysis was useful here:
- voluntary exits and redeployment can replace a **substantial part** of the adjustment,
- working-time measures can help,
- investment-linked restructuring can improve productivity,
- but these measures often do **not fully replicate** the fixed-cost reset of a closure if the overcapacity is structural.

That means we can support a no-closure outcome **only if** it produces:
- durable labor-cost reduction,
- real capacity reduction,
- measurable productivity gains,
- and a cost base that is actually competitive.

If it just preserves jobs and sites while margins remain weak, we will not back it.

## To the regional state
We agree that social stability matters. A prolonged conflict would destroy value and undermine execution.

But your insistence that closures should not be the starting point is only helpful if it results in a package that is **still economically credible**. Stability is not a substitute for competitiveness.

So yes:
- push hard for alternatives,
- insist on burden-sharing,
- protect industrial continuity.

But do not support an outcome that leaves the company structurally too expensive.

## To the controlling family
We agree that cosmetic compromise is not acceptable, and we agree that the company cannot afford a prolonged conflict spiral.

But “economic equivalence” must be shown, not asserted. If you want support for a no-closure path, then:
- visible owner-side restraint,
- management discipline,
- and binding investment commitments
must be part of the package.

Otherwise, the burden falls too heavily on labor, and the result will be fragile and likely uncompetitive.

## What would move us
We could support a negotiated alternative to closures and compulsory redundancies if it includes:
- a **quantified, material, durable cost reset**,
- **credible capacity reduction**,
- **measurable labor-cost contributions**,
- **investment that improves productivity and future earnings**,
- **deal certainty**,
- and **enforceable governance on delivery**.

We are open on the method:
- voluntary exits,
- retirement bridges,
- redeployment,
- working-time flexibility,
- productivity measures,
- and time-limited employee contributions.

But we are **not** open to:
- a package that leaves all sites effectively protected while the cost problem remains,
- a purely symbolic employee contribution,
- or a “peace deal” that merely delays the same crisis.

## What we need next
Before we support any settlement, we want a clear comparison of:
- savings from the hard restructuring,
- savings from the alternative package,
- transition costs,
- timing,
- and risk of underdelivery.

If the alternative closes the gap materially and durably, we can support it.  
If not, then management must keep structural options open, because shareholders will not back a settlement that preserves structural weakness.

## Bottom line
We are not here to choose closures for their own sake.  
We are here to protect risk-adjusted value.

So our line remains:

- **no cosmetic settlement,**
- **no structurally weak compromise,**
- **yes to a negotiated deal only if it restores competitive margins durably.**

If the alternative package truly does that, we are open.  
If it does not, we will oppose it.
