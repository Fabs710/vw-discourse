As the regional state shareholder, I will respond directly.

I agree with management, the family, and the investors on one core point: this situation requires a **durable** restructuring, not delay, and not a cosmetic compromise. The company does need a credible path back to competitiveness.

But I do **not** accept the implicit conclusion that plant closures and compulsory redundancies must stay on the table as normal instruments from the outset. For the state, that is not a neutral technical question. It is a decision about regional employment, supplier networks, industrial continuity, and the company’s role as an economic anchor.

## Where I agree
- The company has a real competitiveness problem.
- The cost base and overcapacity cannot simply be ignored.
- A negotiated solution is preferable to prolonged conflict.
- Capacity adjustments, redeployment, working-time solutions, qualification, and employee contributions are all legitimate parts of the discussion.
- The package must be economically real and durable.

## Where I disagree
### 1) Closures are not the starting point
Management keeps saying closures are “one instrument among others.” Politically and economically, that is too casual. A closure is not just a savings line; it is a lasting structural break with consequences that extend far beyond this bargaining round.

If closures are treated as the default leverage, then the whole discussion starts from the wrong place. The burden of proof is not on the workforce alone. It is on management to show that all serious alternatives have been exhausted and that any closure would be truly unavoidable.

### 2) “Equivalent in effect” cannot mean “employees absorb everything”
I also want to respond to the family and investors. I understand the insistence on material savings. But “economic equivalence” cannot be a code word for shifting the entire adjustment burden onto employees while leaving ownership, governance, and management contributions vague.

If a deal is to be socially and politically sustainable, then burden-sharing must be visible on all sides:
- management discipline,
- owner responsibility,
- investment commitments,
- and employee contributions that are time-limited and balanced.

### 3) A blunt cost reset can destroy long-term value
The capital-market side says that preserving jobs and sites at all cost is not value-preserving. That is true. But the reverse is also true: a restructuring that destroys trust, triggers industrial conflict, weakens the regional industrial base, and erodes the company’s social license can destroy value very quickly.

So I reject the framing that says only closures are “real” and everything else is merely political. In a company of this importance, social stability is not decoration; it is part of the asset base.

## What I would support
I am prepared to support a negotiated restructuring if it contains all of the following:
- **core German sites preserved wherever possible**
- **no compulsory redundancies**
- **binding redeployment and qualification measures**
- **credible capacity reduction through production realignment and working-time instruments**
- **a fair, time-limited employee contribution**
- **visible management and owner-side contribution**
- **investment commitments for future products and technologies through 2030**
- **a strong co-determination role in implementation**

That is the kind of package that can restore competitiveness **without** gutting the regional industrial base.

## What I will not support
I will not support:
- plant closures as a first-line solution,
- compulsory redundancies as a routine bargaining instrument,
- a package that removes the state’s veto and co-determination role in practice,
- or a “solution” that preserves financial optics while hollowing out employment in the region.

## To management
If you want my support, come back with a concrete alternative package that is serious on savings but does not make closure the default answer. “All options remain open” is not a strategy; it is a pressure tactic.

## To the works council and union
I hear your red lines, and I understand them. But I also need to be honest: if we want to protect sites and jobs, then we need a package that is credible to the other side on economics. A blanket refusal to discuss any structural adjustment will not be enough to secure a settlement.

## Bottom line
My position remains:

**Yes to a tough restructuring. No to a closure-led approach unless every serious alternative has been demonstrated to fail.**  
And even then, any such step would need the strongest possible justification and a socially balanced solution.

I am prepared to broker a compromise that protects the company’s long-term health and the region’s industrial base. I am **not** prepared to endorse an outcome that guts regional employment or sidelines the state’s co-determination role.
