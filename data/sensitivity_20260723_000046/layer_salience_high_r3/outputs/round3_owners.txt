I’ll be direct.

The specialist’s analysis confirms what we already knew: **alternatives can replace a substantial part of the savings, but not automatically the full economic effect of closures if the overcapacity is real and structural**. That matters. It means the employee side cannot simply declare “no closures, no redundancies” and expect the economics to disappear.

## My position

As controlling shareholder, I will support a **durable restructuring** only if it materially restores margins, reduces structural cost, and remains executable without tearing the company apart. I do **not** support a cosmetic compromise. I also do **not** support a conflict spiral that destroys value.

So my answer is:

- **Yes** to a serious negotiated alternative if it truly closes the gap.
- **No** to an arrangement that preserves every protection while leaving the company uncompetitive.
- **No** to prolonged confrontation that damages the enterprise, the brand, and governance.

## To the Works Council
I respect your role in co-determination, and I agree that trust, skills, and industrial capability matter. But I do **not** accept your position that closures and compulsory redundancies must be taken completely off the table from the outset.

Your argument is morally understandable, but it is not enough economically.

The specialist’s point is decisive here: voluntary exits, redeployment, and working-time measures can often cover **part** of the gap, sometimes a large part. But if the footprint is structurally too large, they may not cover **all** of it. So I will not bind the company to an absolute no-closure doctrine unless you can show a package that is truly equivalent in savings and durability.

That said, I also do **not** want closures used as lazy leverage. If management wants me to back hard instruments, it must show why alternatives are insufficient on numbers, not just preference.

## To the Trade Union
I agree with you that the burden cannot fall only on employees. A fair settlement needs visible contribution from management and ownership. That is legitimate.

But I do **not** accept the idea that time-limited concessions plus guarantees are enough if they do not materially reset the cost base. A temporary wage measure does not solve a structural competitiveness problem by itself.

If you want a no-closure outcome, then you need to bring something more than rhetorical protection:
- durable labor-cost reduction,
- real productivity change,
- enforceable redeployment,
- and measurable capacity reduction.

Also: no hidden backdoor. “Voluntary” cannot become forced downsizing under another label.

## To Management
You are right that the company needs structural change, and the specialist confirms that alternatives alone may not fully replicate closure savings. But you must also be careful not to overstate the case.

“Closures remain on the table” is not a strategy. It is only credible if you can prove:
- the exact savings gap,
- what the alternatives deliver,
- what remains uncovered,
- and why closure is truly necessary rather than simply easier for management.

I want quantified scenarios, not general pressure. If the numbers show that a no-closure package can close most of the gap and preserve execution, then that is preferable. If not, then we need to be honest that some hard measures may be unavoidable.

## To the Regional State
I understand your political and economic constraints. I also agree that social stability is part of value. But social stability cannot become a veto over reality.

Your position is strongest if you push for a genuinely quantified alternative package now. That means:
- credible savings,
- credible investment,
- credible transition support,
- and a clear failure test.

What I do **not** want is a settlement that delays the hard decision until the company is weaker and the eventual adjustment becomes harsher.

## On the specialist’s findings
The specialist’s assessment is useful because it clarifies the real trade-off:

- **Voluntary exits and redeployment** can save a lot, but they are usually more expensive per job removed.
- **Working-time measures** help, but mostly as a bridge.
- **Investment-linked restructuring** is good for long-term productivity, but not an immediate cost fix.
- **Closures** give the strongest fixed-cost reduction, but at high social and execution cost.

That means there is no free lunch. If the workforce wants no closures and no forced redundancies, then the alternative must be serious enough to be economically equivalent in practice, not just more attractive politically.

## My governance line
I will support a settlement if it meets these conditions:

1. **Material, durable cost reset**
2. **Credible capacity reduction**
3. **Executability without prolonged conflict**
4. **Protection of future investment capacity**
5. **Visible burden-sharing from management and owners**
6. **A quantified savings plan, not slogans**

I am open on the **method**:
- voluntary exits,
- retirement bridges,
- redeployment,
- working-time flexibility,
- productivity measures,
- targeted investment,
- and time-limited employee contributions.

But I am **not** open to a package that simply locks in the current footprint and calls that stability.

## What I want next
Before anyone asks me to rule out hard measures, I want a side-by-side comparison of:
- closure-led savings,
- no-closure alternative savings,
- transition costs,
- timing,
- and risk of execution failure.

If the alternatives are genuinely close enough, then I will support them. If they are not, then the company cannot be asked to carry permanent overcapacity just to avoid difficult decisions.

## Bottom line
I am not here to force conflict. I am here to protect long-term value.

That means:
- **no cosmetic deal,**
- **no endless confrontation,**
- **no automatic closure taboo,**
- and **no restructuring that preserves structural weakness**.

Bring a quantified, durable package that materially resets the business. If you can do that without closures and forced redundancies, I will consider it seriously. If you cannot, then all structural options must remain open.
