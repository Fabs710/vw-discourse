I’ll respond directly, because the company does not have the luxury of vague formulas.

## First: where I agree
I agree with everyone on one point: **this restructuring must be durable**. A cosmetic deal, a temporary pause, or a solution that merely hides the cost problem for another year is not acceptable.

I also agree that:
- the company needs investment in future products and technology through 2030,
- we need a negotiated settlement if we want execution certainty,
- and capacity, productivity, working time, redeployment, and qualification are all relevant tools.

## Where I disagree — clearly
What I cannot accept is the workforce-side claim that:
- **no plant closures**
- **no compulsory redundancies**
- **binding job guarantees through 2030**
- and at the same time a meaningful structural cost reset

can all be combined **without** a very large, very credible, and durable employee contribution.

That is not realism. That is a demand that management absorb the full adjustment burden while the company remains structurally too expensive.

### To the Works Council
You say the workforce did not cause the strategic errors. That may be true in a moral sense, but it does not solve the economic problem.

What matters now is this:
- German factory costs are far above target.
- The European market is shrinking.
- Competitors are leaner and faster.
- Our brand is missing its return-on-sales target.

If we preserve every site, preserve every guarantee, and avoid any hard restructuring instrument, then we will not restore competitiveness. We will postpone pain and deepen the problem.

You say closures are an attack on trust. I say an uncompetitive company is an attack on the future of the workforce. If we fail to act structurally now, we will end up with a weaker company, less investment, and ultimately fewer secure jobs overall.

### To the union
I hear your point about shared sacrifice. But I need to be blunt: **time-limited concessions are not enough if the structural gap remains**.

A one-off wage contribution, bonus deferral, or temporary working-time adjustment does not solve a cost base problem that is persistent. I am not asking for symbolism. I am asking for a package that materially changes the economics of the business.

So if you want to rule out plant closures and compulsory redundancies entirely, then you must bring something that is economically equivalent in effect:
- durable labor-cost reduction,
- real capacity reduction,
- sustained productivity improvement,
- and binding flexibility on deployment and production allocation.

Not aspirations. Not promises. Measurable savings.

### To the regional state
I appreciate that you do not want closures as the default. Neither do I. But I will not accept a position that effectively says: “start from preserving everything, and only then maybe consider hard measures if all else fails.”

We are already in a crisis. This is not a theoretical exercise. If the political framework blocks the only instruments that can deliver scale, then it becomes part of the problem.

A social license matters. So does the company’s survival. And the company’s survival requires a restructuring that actually closes the gap.

### To the controlling family and investors
I agree with you on one essential point: **we need real, durable savings**. I also agree that a prolonged conflict would destroy value. But we should not confuse a tough bargain with a weak one.

The market will not reward a “peace” that leaves us uncompetitive. Nor will it reward a strike spiral. That is why management is insisting on an outcome that is both economically meaningful and executable.

## My position, plainly
Here is what management requires:

1. **Structural reduction of overcapacity**
   - Not just shift changes or temporary volume adjustments.
   - We need a permanent reduction in the cost base.

2. **Meaningful labor-cost contribution**
   - Durable, not merely symbolic.
   - If you want to avoid closures, the labor-side savings must be substantial.

3. **Flexibility on workforce deployment**
   - Internal redeployment alone is not enough unless it actually lowers fixed costs.

4. **A settlement that restores margin and frees investment capacity**
   - We need room to fund future products and technology to 2030.

## What I am prepared to discuss
I remain open on the **method**:
- capacity reductions,
- production realignment,
- working-time solutions,
- redeployment,
- qualification,
- voluntary tools,
- and a negotiated employee contribution.

I am also open to avoiding closures **if** the alternative package is strong enough.

But I am **not** open to:
- a package that keeps all German plants effectively insulated,
- a blanket ban on compulsory redundancies regardless of economic necessity,
- a promise of full job security through 2030 with no equivalent cost relief,
- or a deal that looks stable but leaves us uncompetitive.

## The hard truth
If the employee side is saying “we will only negotiate if closures and redundancies are off the table,” then I have to say clearly: **that is not a viable basis for restructuring**.

If the governance side is saying “we will support change, but only if it delivers real structural savings,” then I agree — because that is the only responsible position.

So let’s stop pretending that we can preserve every existing protection and still restore competitiveness by willpower alone. We need a package that changes the economics.

If you want management to move away from closures, bring a package that is genuinely equivalent in effect. Otherwise, I will continue to argue that all options must remain open.
