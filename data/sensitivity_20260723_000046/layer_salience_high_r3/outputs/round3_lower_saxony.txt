As the regional state shareholder, I will be direct.

I **agree** with all sides on one point: this company needs a **durable restructuring**. Cosmetic compromise will not work, and delay would only deepen the crisis. I also agree that we need real savings, not slogans, and that investment in future products, technology, and transformation through 2030 is essential.

But I **do not accept** the premise that plant closures and compulsory redundancies must remain normal, standing tools from the outset. For the state, that is not a neutral technical option. It is a decision with lasting consequences for regional employment, supplier networks, and the company’s role as an industrial anchor.

### To management
You keep saying closures and forced redundancies must remain available if alternatives fail. I understand the pressure. But I am not willing to treat closure as a routine bargaining instrument while we are still inside the negotiation. That is not responsible restructuring; it is pressure escalation.

If you want the state to support a tougher package, then show us a **specific, quantified alternative** that proves why closures are truly unavoidable. Not a general statement, not “all options open,” but:
- the exact savings gap,
- the timing,
- the operational measures,
- and the governance for delivery.

Without that, you are asking for political cover for an outcome that may not be necessary.

### To the works council and union
I agree with you on the essential red lines:
- **no plant closures in Germany as a starting point**
- **no compulsory redundancies**
- **binding protection for jobs, sites, and industrial capability**

I also agree that the burden cannot fall only on employees. If the company wants a social settlement, then management and ownership must contribute visibly as well.

Where I would press you is this: if you insist on no closures and no forced layoffs, then the alternative must be **credible to the rest of the board on economics**. Time-limited concessions, while important, are not enough by themselves if they do not materially change the cost base. We need a package that is not only fair, but executable and durable.

### To the controlling family and investors
You are right that a purely symbolic compromise is useless. But “economic equivalence” cannot mean that employees absorb the pain while ownership and management stay protected.

If you want a no-closure outcome, then I expect:
- visible owner-side responsibility,
- management-side restraint,
- investment commitments,
- and a real sharing of the burden.

A settlement that preserves returns by hollowing out regional employment is not value-preserving in the long run. It destroys the social license the company needs.

## What I can support
I am prepared to back a negotiated restructuring if it includes:
- a **serious capacity reduction plan**
- **redeployment and qualification**
- **working-time solutions**
- **voluntary tools and retirement bridges**
- **time-limited employee contributions**
- **visible management and owner contributions**
- **binding investment commitments through 2030**
- **strong co-determination in implementation**

That is the kind of package that can protect the company’s long-term health while preserving its role in the region.

## What I will not support
I will not support:
- plant closures as the default answer,
- compulsory redundancies as a routine instrument,
- a package that sidelines the state’s veto and co-determination role,
- or a “solution” that looks efficient on paper but guts regional employment.

## My bottom line
The state will help broker a tough settlement, but not one that treats German plants as disposable. We are prepared for restructuring. We are not prepared for regional industrial dismantling.

If management wants my support, bring a concrete alternative that delivers real savings **without making closures the starting point**. If such an alternative genuinely fails, then we can discuss the next step. But that burden has not yet been met.
