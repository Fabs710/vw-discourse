I will respond plainly.

## My position
As controlling shareholder, I support a **durable restructuring that materially restores competitiveness and margins**. I do **not** support a settlement that simply preserves the existing cost structure and calls that “peace.” Nor do I support a prolonged conflict that destroys value, damages the brand, or weakens the company’s governance capacity.

The specialist input confirms the basic reality: **alternatives can replace a substantial share of the savings, but not necessarily the full fixed-cost effect of closures if overcapacity is structural**. That is the core issue. It means no one gets to pretend economics are optional.

---

## Where I agree

### With management
I agree that:
- the company needs **real structural change**,
- the current German cost base is too high,
- overcapacity cannot be carried indefinitely,
- and a purely symbolic compromise would be value-destructive.

I also agree that **closures and compulsory redundancies cannot be removed from the universe of possible outcomes unless an alternative package is truly equivalent in effect**. That is not ideology; it is governance reality.

### With the specialist
I agree with the specialist’s conclusion:
- voluntary exits, redeployment, working-time reductions, and investment-linked restructuring can deliver **substantial** savings,
- but they often do **not fully replicate** the fixed-cost reset of a closure-heavy plan.

That matters because it means the workforce side cannot simply declare a permanent no-closure doctrine and assume the business case disappears.

### With the state
I agree that stability, industrial continuity, and co-determination matter. A prolonged conflict would destroy value for everyone. The company needs a solution that is executable, not just politically convenient.

---

## Where I disagree

### To the works council
I respect the role of co-determination and I understand the social and industrial arguments. But I do **not** accept your absolute position of:
- no plant closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- binding guarantees through 2030,
as hard preconditions.

Why? Because that would effectively remove the management and ownership ability to fix a structural cost problem if the alternatives do not fully close the gap. That is not stewardship; that is freezing an uncompetitive structure.

You say closures are irreversible destruction. That is true. But so is long-term value erosion if the business remains structurally too expensive. I am not prepared to trade one form of destruction for another.

### To the union
I agree that workers should not be the sole shock absorber. Management and ownership must contribute visibly. But I do **not** accept the argument that time-limited concessions plus guarantees are enough if the economic gap remains material.

A temporary wage or bonus measure is not a substitute for a durable operating reset.

### To management
I support the need for a hard-nosed restructuring, but I do **not** support using closure threats as an end in themselves or as lazy leverage. If you want shareholder backing for harder instruments, you need to show:
- the quantified savings gap,
- what the alternatives deliver,
- and what remains uncovered.

“Closures remain on the table” is not a plan. It is only justified if the numbers prove the alternatives are insufficient.

### To the regional state
I understand your political constraints, but I do **not** accept the idea that closures should be pushed off the table as a matter of principle. The state cannot ask shareholders to underwrite indefinite overcapacity for the sake of comfort.

If the state wants to preserve the industrial base, it must push for a package that is economically real, not just socially preferred.

---

## What I am prepared to support

I would support a negotiated package if it contains all of the following:

1. **A quantified savings target**
   - by site,
   - by year,
   - and by cost line.

2. **A real capacity reduction plan**
   - not just temporary volume smoothing.

3. **Voluntary headcount reduction tools**
   - early retirement,
   - voluntary exits,
   - redeployment,
   - vacancy management.

4. **Working-time and productivity measures**
   - but only as part of a durable structural package.

5. **Visible burden-sharing**
   - management restraint,
   - owner-side restraint,
   - and investment commitments.

6. **A failure test**
   - if the alternative package misses the savings target materially, hard measures must remain available.

That is the only credible basis for a settlement that protects long-term value.

---

## What I do not accept

I do **not** accept:
- a blanket no-closure doctrine,
- a permanent no-forced-redundancy commitment regardless of economic reality,
- or a deal that locks in current sites and current protections while leaving the company uncompetitive.

I also do **not** accept a hidden downgrade where “voluntary” becomes forced downsizing by another name. If there is to be a no-closure path, it must be real, quantified, and executable.

## My read of the specialist analysis
The economist has clarified the trade-off well:

- **Closures**: strongest fixed-cost relief, highest disruption.
- **Voluntary exits/redeployment**: substantial labor-cost relief, but less complete on fixed overhead.
- **Working-time reductions**: useful bridge, not a full structural fix.
- **Investment-linked restructuring**: strong long-term, weak immediate fix.

So the question is not whether alternatives exist. They do.  
The question is whether they are **sufficiently durable and economically equivalent**.

Until that is shown, I will not support ruling out hard structural options.

---

## On the proposed “no-closure test phase”
I would only support such a phase if it is:
- tightly time-bound,
- quantified,
- monitored at plant level,
- and includes a real failure mechanism.

I am **not** interested in a soft postponement that simply delays the same issue. If the phase fails, consequences must be real. Otherwise we are just buying time at the expense of value.

---

## Bottom line
My line is simple:

- **Yes** to a serious negotiated alternative if it truly closes the gap.
- **No** to a settlement that preserves every protection while leaving the company structurally weak.
- **No** to a prolonged conflict that destroys value.
- **No** to an automatic closure taboo.

If the employee side can bring a package that is economically equivalent in effect and durable in execution, I will consider it. If not, then all structural options must remain open.
