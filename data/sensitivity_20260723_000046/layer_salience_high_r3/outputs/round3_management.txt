I’ll respond directly.

## Where I agree
I agree with everyone on one basic point: **this company needs a durable restructuring**. We do not have time for a cosmetic compromise, a temporary postponement, or a package that merely shifts the problem into 2027 or 2028.

I also agree that:
- investment in future products and technology through 2030 is essential,
- industrial peace is valuable,
- capacity, redeployment, training, working-time flexibility, and voluntary tools all belong in the discussion,
- and a negotiated outcome is preferable to a drawn-out conflict.

## Where I disagree — and strongly
What I do **not** accept is the claim that we can secure a meaningful structural reset while making:
- **no plant closures**
- **no compulsory redundancies**
- **binding job and site guarantees through 2030**
- and still expect a cost base that is competitive

That combination does not solve the problem. It preserves the very structure that is too expensive.

### To the Works Council
You keep saying closures and forced redundancies are an attack on trust. I do not dismiss that. But trust cannot be the only metric here.

The reality is:
- German factory costs are far above target.
- The European market is shrinking.
- Our competitors are leaner.
- The brand is missing its return-on-sales target.

If we preserve every site and every legacy protection, we will not restore competitiveness. We will simply delay the adjustment and make the eventual correction harsher.

You say the workforce did not cause the strategic errors. That may be true, but it does not change the economic gap. Management is responsible for fixing that gap, and that means structural change — not just symbolic measures.

### To the Trade Union
I agree that time-limited concessions alone are not enough. That is precisely why I am not asking for symbolic gestures.

But I do **not** accept your conclusion that because temporary measures are insufficient, closures and compulsory redundancies must be ruled out from the start. That is backwards.

If you want to rule those tools out, then you must show a package that is **economically equivalent in effect**, not merely more comfortable in form. That means:
- durable labor-cost reduction,
- measurable capacity reduction,
- productivity gains,
- flexibility in deployment and production allocation,
- and a deal that survives beyond the next bargaining round.

A one-off contribution with full guarantees layered on top does not solve a structural cost problem.

### To the Regional State
I understand your role and your concerns. But I have to be blunt: “closures only if all alternatives fail” is not a strategy, and it is not enough to restore competitiveness.

If every hard measure is treated as politically untouchable, then the company is effectively being asked to carry its overcapacity indefinitely. That is not sustainable.

Social stability matters, yes. But social stability does not preserve jobs if the company remains uncompetitive. Long-term industrial health does.

### To the Controlling Family
I agree with you on one critical point: this cannot be a cosmetic compromise. We need a settlement that materially changes the economics.

Where I differ is that I will not support an outcome that freezes the current structure and calls that “stability.” Stability without competitiveness is just decline at a slower pace.

### To Institutional Investors
You are right to insist on durable margin improvement and measurable savings. That is exactly my position. But you should also be clear: if we are denied the instruments that can produce structural change, then the company will not reach the return profile the market expects.

## What management requires
Let me state this plainly:

### We need:
1. **A structural reduction of overcapacity**
   - not just shift tweaks or temporary volume changes.

2. **A material labor-cost contribution**
   - durable, not symbolic.

3. **A real reduction in fixed cost**
   - through capacity realignment, redeployment, and where necessary hard measures.

4. **A settlement that restores margin and frees investment capacity**
   - because future products and technology must be funded.

## What I am open to
I remain open on the **method**:
- capacity reductions,
- production realignment,
- working-time solutions,
- redeployment,
- qualification,
- voluntary exits,
- retirement bridges,
- and negotiated employee contributions.

If you can deliver equivalent savings through those means, I will look at them seriously.

## What I am not open to
I am **not** open to:
- a package that keeps every German plant effectively insulated,
- a blanket ban on compulsory redundancies,
- a promise of full job and site security through 2030 regardless of economic reality,
- or a “shared sacrifice” deal in which management is expected to fix a structural cost problem without structural tools.

## On the mediator’s bridge options
### 1) Phased restructuring with a closure last-resort trigger
I understand why this was proposed, but I do not regard it as sufficient if the trigger is so remote or so constrained that it becomes meaningless.

If the alternative package fails to close the gap, then consequences must be real. Otherwise we are just postponing the same dispute.

### 2) No compulsory redundancies, stronger voluntary exits and redeployment
This can be part of a solution, but only if it delivers the scale needed. It cannot be a disguise for keeping the cost base intact.

### 3) Site-preservation pact tied to measurable contributions
This is the most constructive of the three, but it must include **durable** employee-side cost contribution and operational restructuring, not only guarantees.

## My position on the employee-side red lines
I am hearing:
- no plant closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- binding guarantees through 2030.

I need to be equally clear: those are **not acceptable as hard preconditions** if they prevent a meaningful restructuring.

They may be aspirations. They are not a viable starting point for a company under acute cost pressure.

## What a viable settlement would require
A viable package would need to contain:
- a **material, durable cost reset**,
- **measurable capacity reduction**,
- **real workforce contribution**,
- **strong redeployment and qualification measures**,
- and **sufficient flexibility** to restore competitiveness.

If the employee side wants to avoid closures and forced layoffs, then the savings must be genuinely equivalent — not politically equivalent.

## Bottom line
I will not endorse a deal that preserves the current cost structure and calls it compromise.

I am prepared to negotiate hard on the **path** to savings.  
I am **not** prepared to dilute the savings requirement itself.

If you bring a package that truly closes the gap and is executable, I will consider avoiding closures and forced redundancies. If not, then management must keep all structural options open, because the company’s competitiveness comes first.
