Management keeps asking for “real structural adjustment,” but you are still refusing to answer the central question: **why must that adjustment be paid for by closing plants and threatening jobs instead of by using the many alternatives that have already been named?**

Let me be equally direct.

### Where I agree
I agree with almost everyone here on one point: **cosmetic measures are not enough**. We do need a durable package. We do need measurable savings. We do need a serious path back to competitiveness.

But that does **not** mean plant closures or compulsory redundancies are legitimate starting points. It means the company must finally present a restructuring that is **economically credible and socially responsible**.

### Where I disagree
I reject the logic that says:
- capacity must be reduced, therefore a German plant must close;
- labour costs must come down, therefore jobs must be destroyed;
- competitiveness must improve, therefore workers must surrender the job-security framework.

That is not the only path. It is just the easiest path for management because it shifts the burden downward.

### What is missing from your arguments
So far, management and investors have repeated “real savings” and “implementation certainty,” but you have **not** shown that closures are the only way to get them.

If you claim equivalence, then put numbers on the table:
- How much savings are required?
- Over what time horizon?
- From which cost blocks?
- How much can be delivered through working-time reductions, redeployment, voluntary exits, productivity improvements, procurement, overhead, management compensation, and ownership contributions?
- What exactly is the gap that supposedly cannot be closed without closures?

Until that is answered, your closure threat is just leverage, not necessity.

### Our position remains unchanged
We are prepared to discuss:
- capacity reductions **without** site closures,
- temporary working-time measures,
- negotiated employee contributions,
- internal redeployment,
- voluntary programs,
- productivity and organization improvements,
- management restraint,
- and owner contributions.

But we will **not** discuss:
- compulsory redundancies as an acceptable instrument,
- closing German plants,
- or dismantling the job-security architecture that has protected this workforce for years.

### On burden sharing
I note that everyone wants workers to contribute. Fine — but then let’s be honest: **management, owners, and capital must contribute in a way that is visible, binding, and proportionate**.

That means, at minimum:
- management compensation and bonus restraint,
- overhead cuts at the top,
- a real capital contribution from owners,
- and a commitment to invest in German sites rather than drain them.

If those contributions are real, then we can discuss limited employee measures. But not as a pretext for job destruction.

### On co-determination
The state government is right on one thing: co-determination is not decorative. And the family owners are right that short-term conflict destroys value. That is precisely why management should stop treating the workforce as an obstacle and start treating it as a partner with a veto.

Because let’s be clear: any closure-led strategy will face our resistance in the supervisory board, in the works councils, and in the plants. You do not have a free hand here.

### My test for any package
If you want our support, bring a package that includes all of the following:
1. **Binding guarantees for every German site**
2. **No compulsory redundancies**
3. **Security of employment through 2030**
4. **A measurable management and owner contribution**
5. **A negotiated industrial plan with the workforce involved in shaping implementation**

If you cannot accept that framework, then don’t ask us to call closures “inevitable.” They are not inevitable — they are a choice. And we will oppose that choice.
