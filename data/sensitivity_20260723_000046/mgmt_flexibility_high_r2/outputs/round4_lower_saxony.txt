I will be direct.

## Where I agree
I agree with all sides on one basic point: **this company needs a durable restructuring, not a cosmetic delay**.  
I also agree that **burden-sharing must be real**. It is not acceptable for employees alone to carry the adjustment while management, owners, and capital stay untouched.

And I agree with the expert assessment that a no-closure solution is only credible if it is **large, durable, and enforceable**.

That is the right test.

---

## Where I disagree
### 1) I do **not** accept plant closures as the default answer
Management keeps speaking as if closures are the natural consequence of economics. They are not. They are a **choice** unless and until the company proves that no other package can close the gap.

So far, that proof has not been delivered.

If you want closures to be considered, then show:
- the exact savings gap,
- the exact capacity gap,
- the contribution from management,
- the contribution from owners,
- the contribution from labour,
- and the residual gap after all non-destructive measures.

Until then, closure is not necessity. It is leverage.

And I will say plainly: I am **not prepared to support a closure-led restructuring that damages employment in the state and weakens the company’s role as a regional anchor**.

---

### 2) I do **not** accept compulsory redundancies as the starting instrument
I understand why management wants flexibility. But flexibility is not the same as making forced job cuts the first tool.

The sequence must be:
1. management restraint,
2. overhead cuts,
3. owner/capital contribution,
4. productivity improvements,
5. working-time flexibility,
6. voluntary exits and redeployment,
7. and only then, if absolutely unavoidable, discussion of harsher steps.

That is the only order I can regard as responsible.

---

### 3) I do **not** accept any approach that sidelines co-determination
Co-determination is not decorative. Under this company’s governance and the special statute, it is a real constraint. It exists for a reason: to prevent economically and socially destructive decisions being forced through without proper balance.

So no, I will not treat governance rights, the board veto, or the workforce’s role as obstacles to be bypassed.

---

## To management
You say you need real structural change. I do not dispute that.

But you still have **not** shown that the company cannot reach competitiveness through a no-closure package. “Overcapacity” and “cost base above target” are diagnoses, not a proof that the only remedy is plant closure.

If you want us to consider harsher options, bring the numbers in a form that can actually be tested:
- savings target,
- capacity reduction target,
- management contribution,
- owner contribution,
- employee contribution,
- residual gap,
- and the duration of concessions.

Without that, your position is still a bargaining stance, not a demonstrated necessity.

And I want this stated clearly: **I will not back a strategy that treats regional employment as a disposable variable.**

---

## To the works council and union
I hear your hard line, and I share the core of it:
- no plant closures as the starting point,
- no compulsory redundancies as the default,
- no dismantling of the collective framework,
- no shifting of structural risk entirely onto the workforce.

That said, I also want you to understand my position: if we want to defend jobs and sites, then we need a package that is not just morally right but **economically credible**.

So I am not asking you to soften your line. I am asking you to help produce a package that can actually survive scrutiny:
- working-time measures,
- voluntary exits,
- redeployment,
- retraining,
- productivity gains,
- wage restraint or deferred components,
- management restraint,
- and visible owner contribution.

That is the type of social-partnership restructuring I can support.

What I will **not** support is a paper promise that looks protective while leaving the company structurally weak.

---

## To the controlling family
You say you are open to a no-closure outcome if it is economically equivalent. Good. Then help make it equivalent.

That means real capital support, acceptance of lower near-term returns, and willingness to back a negotiated package rather than using closure threats as the default pressure tool.

If stewardship is the claim, then stewardship must mean protecting the productive base, not squeezing it until it breaks.

---

## To the institutional investors
You are right that the company must restore margins and deal certainty. I do not dispute that.

But you should not confuse **certainty** with coercion. A package that destroys trust and triggers industrial conflict is not stable certainty; it is delayed instability.

If you want a settlement that holds, support one that:
- avoids closures if at all possible,
- avoids forced redundancies as the first instrument,
- and includes real burden-sharing from management and owners.

That is how you protect long-term value.

---

## On the expert’s assessment
The expert has made the key point: a no-closure package can be equivalent only if it is **large, durable, and enforceable**.

I accept that benchmark.

But that means management must stop speaking in absolutes before it has proven the case. If the alternative package has not been fully tested, then closures remain a choice, not a necessity.

And I also want the social cost of closures counted honestly:
- strike risk,
- loss of know-how,
- reputational damage,
- supply-chain disruption,
- and the long-term erosion of co-determination-based stability.

Those are not side issues. They are part of the value equation.

---

## Where I stand on the facilitator’s proposals
### 1) Conditional no-closure moratorium
I could support a **genuine moratorium** on closures and compulsory redundancies for a defined period, **if** it is tied to:
- measurable management restraint,
- real owner contribution,
- binding investment commitments,
- and transparent KPIs.

But if it is just a countdown to closure later, then it is not a compromise. It is a delayed ultimatum. I will not endorse that.

### 2) No forced layoffs first, staged escalation ladder
This is the more serious route, provided the sequencing is real and not symbolic.

The order must be:
1. management cuts,
2. overhead reductions,
3. owner support,
4. productivity gains,
5. working-time flexibility,
6. voluntary exits and redeployment,
7. only then any discussion of harsher measures.

That is the only sequence that preserves trust.

### 3) Site-security package tied to commitments
This is the strongest path if it is done properly.

I could support site security for German plants through 2030, but only if it includes:
- no forced redundancies as the default,
- real investment,
- measurable contributions from management and owners,
- and a restructuring path that protects the state’s industrial base.

---

## My position in one sentence
I am open to a negotiated restructuring, but only if it **protects German sites and jobs wherever possible, preserves co-determination, shares the burden fairly, and proves in numbers that it can restore competitiveness without treating closures as the first answer**.

---

## Bottom line
So I will be explicit:

- I do **not** accept closures as the default restructuring tool.
- I do **not** accept compulsory redundancies as the starting point.
- I do **not** accept a deal that shifts the crisis downward onto workers and the state.
- I **do** support a serious, quantified, socially balanced package that protects the company as a regional anchor and restores long-term viability.

If management wants a settlement, bring a real no-closure package with numbers, binding commitments, and shared sacrifice.  
If it cannot, then it has still not proven that the harsher route is unavoidable.

That is where I stand.
