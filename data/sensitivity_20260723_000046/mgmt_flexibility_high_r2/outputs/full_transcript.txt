[Works Council (Betriebsrat) (Plant-level employee representation under co-determination) — Round 1]

- **No plant closures in Germany.**
- **No compulsory redundancies.**
- **No unilateral dismantling of the historic job-security framework.**

- capacity adjustments,
- temporary working-time reductions,

2. **Job security through 2030**
3. **A real co-determination role for the workforce in shaping the restructuring**
4. **A fair burden-sharing approach that does not put the whole adjustment on employees**

We are ready to negotiate seriously. But we will not negotiate away the existence of plants or the jobs of the people who built this company.

[Expert: Corporate restructuring / turnaround fin — Round 3]
## Expert assessment: can a no-closure package be economically equivalent to closure-based restructuring?

Yes — **sometimes**, but only under fairly strict conditions. In large industrial restructurings, a **no-closure / no-forced-layoff package can be economically equivalent in annual savings** if the company can combine enough of the following:

- labor-cost concessions,
- working-time reduction / flexibility,
- voluntary exits and natural attrition,
- capacity backloading or line underutilization reduction,
- procurement and overhead cuts,
- management compensation restraint,
- and capital/owner support.

However, in a **high-fixed-cost automotive manufacturing network**, a no-closure solution is usually **harder to make durable** than a closure-based one, because:
- plant closures remove fixed cost directly and permanently,
- while concessions and flexibility measures can erode over time,
- and they often depend on labor peace and continued governance cooperation.

So the key question is not “can savings be found elsewhere?” but:
**can the alternative package match the closure case on three tests: cash savings, structural permanence, and implementation certainty?**

---

## 1) Benchmark framework: what “equivalent” should mean

For a restructuring to be truly equivalent to closures, it should match the closure case on:

### A. Annual EBITDA impact
- Same or near-same recurring annual cost reduction.
- Same or near-same margin uplift.

### B. Cash-flow impact
- Upfront restructuring cash may be lower in no-closure cases.
- But recurring operating cash flow must not be materially weaker.

### C. Structural permanence
- Savings should not expire in 2–3 years when temporary concessions roll off.

### D. Execution certainty
- Deal must be implementable without repeated renegotiation or strike risk.

### E. Capacity alignment
- If overcapacity is real, there must be a real capacity reduction, even if not via closure.

---

## 2) Typical economics: closure vs no-closure

### Closure-based restructuring
Usually delivers:
- direct fixed-cost removal,
- lower labor, utilities, maintenance, logistics, and SG&A overhead,
- better asset utilization across the remaining network,
- and cleaner long-term margin improvement.

Typical economic profile:
- **higher one-time cash costs**
  - severance,
  - decommissioning,
  - site remediation,
  - transfer costs,
  - pension or contractual charges.
- **higher recurring savings**
  - often the cleanest route to durable savings.

### No-closure restructuring
Can deliver:
- wage restraint / deferred compensation,
- reduced weekly hours,
- temporary short-time work models,
- voluntary exits,
- redeployment,
- productivity gains,
- procurement and overhead cuts,
- management pay restraint,
- capacity smoothing,
- and sometimes capex deferral.

Typical economic profile:
- **lower one-time cash costs**
- but recurring savings are often:
  - smaller,
  - slower to realize,
  - and less certain to persist.

---

## 3) What size of alternative package is usually needed?

I can’t verify the company-specific numbers from the information provided, so I will use scenario logic.

For a large German OEM passenger-car operation with structurally high labor and fixed costs, a credible no-closure package usually needs to cover:

### 1. Labor-cost concessions
Often the largest lever outside closures.

Possible annual impact:
- wage restraint,
- bonus reductions,
- removal or suspension of special payments,
- working-time concessions.

Indicative range:
- **mid-single-digit to low-double-digit percentage of direct labor cost**
- but only if negotiated durably.

### 2. Capacity rationalization without closure
This is the hard part.

Options:
- remove shifts,
- consolidate variants,
- shorten line hours,
- move some volume to other plants,
- outsource non-core components,
- reduce complexity.

Indicative annual impact:
- can be material,
- but usually less than full site closure unless actual production volume is removed.

### 3. Voluntary workforce reduction
Through:
- retirement bridges,
- severance-linked exits,
- natural attrition,
- internal transfer.

This can help structurally, but:
- it takes time,
- can be expensive upfront,
- and may not match the speed of plant closure.

### 4. Management and overhead cuts
Useful and important for credibility, but usually not enough on their own.

Typical contribution:
- meaningful,
- but rarely a substitute for network rationalization.

### 5. Procurement and industrial efficiency
Can be material if there is genuine complexity reduction.

But:
- these savings are often already partially planned,
- and they can be harder to “bank” than site closure savings.

## 4) My benchmark view: when no-closure can work

A no-closure package is credible only if it satisfies most of these conditions:

### Condition 1: the savings gap is not too large
If the required restructuring is modest to moderate, no-closure may work.

If the company needs a very large structural correction — for example, major fixed-cost removal across a mature network — then no-closure becomes harder.

### Condition 2: concessions are durable
Temporary wage freezes or short-term working-time cuts are **not enough** unless there is a credible multi-year framework.

### Condition 3: capacity reduction is real, not just cosmetic
If factories keep most of the same cost base but produce a little less, savings are often insufficient.

### Condition 4: the package is enforceable
You need:
- clear KPIs,
- triggers,
- monitoring,
- and consequences if targets are missed.

### Condition 5: governance support is stable
In a co-determined environment, a package that depends on recurring veto-risk is less durable than closure-based rationalization.

## 5) Why closures often outperform in structural turnarounds

From a turnaround finance perspective, closures are attractive because they:
- permanently remove fixed cost,
- simplify the network,
- improve capacity utilization at remaining sites,
- reduce management complexity,
- and make forecast cash flows cleaner.

If the business has:
- persistent overcapacity,
- weak pricing power,
- and a cost base materially above peers,

then closures may produce a better EV and margin outcome than an extended negotiated concession package.

That said, closures also create:
- labor conflict risk,
- political risk,
- execution delay,
- reputational damage,
- and possible supply-chain disruption.

So the right question is not “which is better in theory?” but:
**which has the higher risk-adjusted NPV?**

## 6) Liquidity effects: no-closure vs closure

### No-closure package
Usually better for near-term liquidity because:
- fewer severance and site exit cash costs,
- less decommissioning spend,
- less working-capital disturbance,
- and fewer disruption costs if labor peace holds.

But if the savings are shallow or temporary:
- liquidity relief is deferred,
- and cash burn may continue.

### Closure-based package
Usually worse upfront for liquidity because of:
- severance,
- closure costs,
- redundancy charges,
- site exit costs,
- and possible strike or ramp-up disruption.

But if implemented well:
- it can improve medium-term operating cash flow much more.

So:
- **No-closure = better immediate liquidity, weaker certainty of long-term cash improvement**
- **Closure = worse immediate liquidity, stronger structural cash improvement**

## 7) Margin impact: what matters most

If management is missing return-on-sales target, the relevant issue is recurring operating margin.

A package improves margin only if it reduces:
- direct labor cost,
- indirect labor cost,
- overhead,
- and fixed manufacturing burden,
faster than it reduces volume or efficiency.

### No-closure package margin risks
- concessions may be partially offset by lower morale or productivity slippage,
- remaining plants may still carry excess fixed costs,
- negotiated savings may expire,
- and complexity remains high.

### Closure package margin benefits
- clearer fixed-cost absorption improvement,
- better network efficiency,
- and stronger pricing discipline if volume is concentrated.

Thus, if the company’s margin gap is large, closures often have a stronger and more reliable margin effect.

[Expert: Corporate restructuring / turnaround fin — Round 4]
## Evidence-based restructuring assessment

I’ll stay in my lane: corporate finance, restructuring economics, plant/network rationalization, and turnaround feasibility.

### 1) Core conclusion
A **credible no-closure / no-forced-layoff package can exist only if it delivers materially similar economics** to a closure-based plan on:
- **annual cash savings**
- **capacity reduction**
- **operating margin uplift**
- **implementation certainty**

In large automotive restructurings, the key issue is usually not whether savings can be found somewhere, but whether a **non-closure package can reach the same durable cost base**. In many cases it cannot, because:
- wage/working-time measures are often **temporary or partially reversible**
- voluntary exits are **slower and more expensive**
- productivity gains are **real but limited**
- overhead cuts are usually **not enough** to offset plant-level fixed cost leakage
- maintaining excess site/network capacity preserves a large portion of the cost burden

So the question is not ideological. It is arithmetic.

---

## 2) How to compare the options

### Closure-based restructuring
Typically delivers:
- permanent fixed-cost removal
- full elimination of site-level overhead and local indirect costs
- structural capacity reduction
- stronger margin uplift if utilization improves at remaining plants

Costs:
- redundancy, severance, shutdown, decommissioning, transfer, and legal/industrial-relations costs
- higher execution risk
- strike risk
- reputational damage
- potential disruption to supply chain and launch programs

### No-closure alternative
Typically relies on a mix of:
- working-time reductions
- wage restraint / bonus waivers / deferred compensation
- voluntary exits
- redeployment
- productivity initiatives
- procurement and overhead cuts
- capital/owner support

Benefits:
- lower immediate social disruption
- lower industrial conflict if negotiated well
- preserves know-how and political stability

Limitations:
- savings may be **less durable**
- savings are often **smaller per job displaced**
- capacity reduction is usually **weaker**
- may require **ongoing concessions** to hold the line

---

## 3) What would make a no-closure package “equivalent”?
For a major OEM, equivalence would usually mean:

1. **Savings gap closed on a recurring basis**  
   Not just one-time cost relief. The package must reduce the annual run-rate cost base by enough to restore target margin.

2. **Capacity cut in the right places**  
   If demand is structurally lower, keeping all sites open while trimming shifts may not fully solve overcapacity.

3. **Labor flexibility with durability**  
   Temporary wage restraint or short-time measures are not equivalent to permanent structural reduction.

4. **Owner/management contribution is real**  
   Investors will not accept an employee-only solution; employees will not accept a labor-only sacrifice. Both sides need material contributions.

5. **Enforcement mechanism**  
   A moratorium or review trigger only works if KPIs are explicit and consequences are pre-agreed.

---

## 4) Practical scenario estimate
Without company-specific numbers, I can only give a scenario framework.

### If the restructuring gap is modest
If management needs, say, a **low-single-digit percentage of sales** improvement in margin and only **partial capacity alignment**, a no-closure package may be feasible via:
- headcount attrition
- working-time changes
- bonus restraint
- procurement savings
- overhead reduction
- limited voluntary exits

### If the gap is large
If the business needs a **multi-year structural reset** with significant excess capacity, then closures usually become the only tool that reliably delivers:
- permanent fixed-cost removal
- faster break-even improvement
- less ongoing dependence on labor concessions

In that case, a no-closure package might still reduce pain, but it is unlikely to be fully equivalent.

---

## 5) Margin and liquidity implications

### Closure-based package
**Margin:** Usually better long-term if utilization of remaining plants improves.  
**Liquidity:** Worse upfront due to cash restructuring costs.  
**Risk:** Higher labor and political conflict risk.  
**Time to benefit:** Can be slower if contested, but stronger once executed.

### No-closure package
**Margin:** Can improve meaningfully, but often not as much unless labor concessions are large and durable.  
**Liquidity:** Better near term because shutdown cash costs are avoided.  
**Risk:** Lower initial disruption, but higher risk of under-delivery if concessions fade or productivity gains disappoint.  
**Time to benefit:** Faster start, but potentially weaker end-state.

---

## 6) My judgment on feasibility
### A no-closure deal is feasible if:
- the required savings gap is **not too large**
- the business can absorb the remaining capacity through utilization gains, redeployment, and demand recovery
- employee concessions are **multi-year and enforceable**
- management and owners make **visible financial contributions**
- there is a real mechanism to convert temporary savings into durable structural savings

### A no-closure deal is not feasible if:
- the company has **deep structural overcapacity**
- German site fixed costs are the major problem
- demand recovery is uncertain
- labor concessions are mostly temporary
- management requires a permanent step-down in the cost base

---

## 7) What I would ask management to quantify
To determine whether no-closure is actually equivalent, management should disclose:
1. annual savings target
2. fixed vs variable cost split
3. cost per plant and per shift
4. required capacity reduction
5. cash restructuring cost of closures
6. cash savings from non-closure measures
7. margin bridge to target
8. sensitivity to wage, utilization, and volume scenarios

Without that, “closures are necessary” is not yet a proven statement; it is a negotiated position.

---

## 8) Bottom line
From a restructuring finance perspective:

- **Closure-based restructuring** is usually the more reliable path to a permanent cost reset when overcapacity is severe.
- **No-closure restructuring** can work, but only if it produces **durable, quantified, and enforceable savings** close to the closure case.
- If the no-closure package depends mainly on temporary labor concessions, it is **not economically equivalent**.
- If it includes substantial owner support, overhead cuts, productivity improvement, and structural capacity alignment through other means, it may be viable.
