I will respond plainly and directly.

## To the Works Council
I agree with your hard line on the essentials:
- **no plant closures**
- **no compulsory redundancies**
- **no dismantling of the collective wage framework**

That remains the union’s baseline as well. And I agree with you that management has not yet proven that destructive instruments are unavoidable.

Where I would sharpen the point is this: we should not let management turn a “review trigger” into a disguised closure mandate. Any review has to be **jointly governed, transparent, and conditional on management, owners, and capital first delivering their part**. If they miss targets, the answer is not automatically closure. The answer is: then they must do more on the non-destructive side.

## To Management
You still have not met the burden you claim to be under.

You say:
- structural overcapacity,
- a shrinking market,
- cost pressure,
- and the need for flexibility.

I do not deny the business pressure. I deny that you have shown closures and forced redundancies are the necessary response.

If you want the union to consider deeper labour-side measures, then put the numbers on the table in a way that can actually be tested:
1. exact annual savings target,
2. exact capacity reduction target,
3. exact management contribution,
4. exact owner/capital contribution,
5. exact employee contribution,
6. exact residual gap.

Until that exists, “closures remain on the table” is not a business case. It is leverage.

And let me be clear: I still do **not** accept compulsory redundancies as a default instrument, and I do **not** accept plant closures as the starting point.

## To the State Government
I agree with you that closures are not the preferred path and that this company matters for regional industrial stability. But you need to be more forceful than “we prefer no closures.”

If you want a no-closure settlement, then use your governance role to insist on:
- binding site and job protection,
- binding investment commitments,
- visible owner contribution,
- management restraint,
- and a real sequencing rule that makes closures the last resort only after every alternative has been exhausted and proven insufficient.

That is the minimum for credibility. Anything less just dresses up uncertainty as caution.

## To the Controlling Family
You say you are open to a no-closure solution if it is economically equivalent. Good. Then help make it equivalent in reality, not just in rhetoric.

That means:
- capital support,
- acceptance of lower near-term returns,
- investment in German sites,
- and a clear commitment not to use closure threats as a bargaining weapon against the workforce.

If owners want stewardship, they must back the productive base. They cannot ask labour to carry the full adjustment and call that governance.

## To Institutional Investors
I understand your focus on margin recovery, implementation certainty, and value preservation.

But I do not accept the way you keep framing social conflict as a secondary issue. It is not secondary. A package that destroys trust, triggers strikes, and weakens the collective framework can destroy more value than it creates.

So if you want certainty, support a package that is:
- economically credible,
- socially stable,
- and enforceable without recurring threats to sites and jobs.

If the only model you support is “closure first, labor pays most,” then you are not solving the company’s problem. You are shifting it into the future.

## On the expert assessment
The expert made one useful point: a no-closure package can work only if it is **large, durable, and enforceable**.

I agree. That is exactly why I reject half-measures and cosmetic deals.

But the expert also confirmed something management keeps trying to avoid: **the burden of proof is on the closure side** if closures are presented as unavoidable. Until the company shows that a no-closure package cannot close the gap, closure is still a choice, not a necessity.

And I also want to stress this: if you count only accounting savings and ignore the social cost of closure — strike escalation, loss of know-how, reputational damage, and the erosion of co-determination-based stability — then you are undercounting the real cost of your preferred solution.

## On the facilitator’s proposals

### 1) Conditional no-closure moratorium
This can be a serious path **only if it is genuine**.

I would support a moratorium on:
- plant closures
- compulsory redundancies

for a defined period, **provided** that management, owners, and capital make binding contributions and the package is monitored by transparent KPIs.

What I will not accept is a “moratorium” that is just a countdown timer to closure if workers do not accept an ultimatum later.

### 2) No forced layoffs first, staged escalation ladder
This is closer to a realistic approach.

But the ladder must be real:
1. management restraint,
2. overhead cuts,
3. owner/capital contribution,
4. productivity gains,
5. working-time flexibility,
6. voluntary exits and redeployment,
7. only then, after demonstrated exhaustion of the above, any discussion of harsher steps.

That sequencing matters. It ensures the workforce is not asked to sacrifice first and most.

### 3) Site-security package tied to commitments
This is the strongest route if the aim is a durable settlement.

But site security must be real, not selective PR protection. It has to include:
- no forced redundancies,
- binding investment in German plants,
- and a negotiated employee contribution matched by management and owners.

If that package is serious, we will engage constructively. If it is just a way to isolate one site while threatening others, then it is not a solution.

## What the union is prepared to discuss
Let me restate this clearly so nobody can pretend otherwise:

We are prepared to discuss:
- temporary working-time reductions,
- voluntary exits,
- redeployment,
- retraining,
- productivity improvements,
- bonus waivers,
- deferred compensation,
- and a negotiated labour-cost contribution.

But we are **not** prepared to accept:
- plant closures,
- compulsory redundancies,
- or the dismantling of the collective wage framework.

Those are not bargaining chips for us. They are the boundary of a fair deal.

## What would move us
A serious deal must show:
- management restraint,
- owner contribution,
- real investment in German sites,
- quantified employee contribution,
- and binding protection against closures and forced layoffs.

That is shared sacrifice.

Anything else is workers underwriting the restructuring while others keep their hands clean.

## Bottom line
We are not refusing restructuring. We are refusing a restructuring that socializes pain downward and calls it competitiveness.

So my position remains:
- **No plant closures**
- **No compulsory redundancies**
- **No weakening of the collective wage framework**
- **Yes to a negotiated, quantified, durable package with fair contributions from management, owners, and capital**

If management wants a settlement, bring a real no-closure package with numbers and binding commitments. If not, then the union will continue to resist a solution that destroys jobs to protect comfort at the top.
