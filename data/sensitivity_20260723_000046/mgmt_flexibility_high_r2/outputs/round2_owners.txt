I will respond plainly, because we are now at the point where vague assurances are no longer sufficient.

## To management
I agree with your core diagnosis: **this company has a structural competitiveness problem** and cannot carry a permanently inflated cost and capacity base.

Where I disagree is with any implication that the only credible way to solve that is through a closure-led confrontation. That would be a serious mistake if it becomes the default rather than the last resort. A closure battle would be value-destructive, politically explosive, and potentially slower than a negotiated alternative.

So I need management to be much more precise:
- What is the quantified savings gap?
- What is the required capacity reduction?
- Which parts are truly non-negotiable for competitiveness, and which are just preferred leverage?
- What is the expected timing of margin recovery?

If management cannot answer that in numbers, then “plants must stay on the table” is not a restructuring strategy; it is a bargaining threat.

## To the works council and union
I agree with you on one essential point: **the workforce cannot be made the sole adjustment variable**.

You are right to reject a one-sided solution. You are also right that forced layoffs and plant closures should not be the opening move. That said, I do not share the view that binding guarantees through 2030 can be asserted upfront regardless of what the numbers show.

A blanket commitment of that kind is too rigid if it prevents the company from restoring competitiveness. If the package is credible, then site security may be achievable. If it is not, then no governance body should pretend the problem does not exist.

So I support a negotiated alternative only if it is:
- materially cost-reducing,
- operationally credible,
- and durable enough to avoid another crisis in two years.

But I will not support a package that simply preserves all current structures and calls that reform.

## To the state government
Your emphasis on regional stability and co-determination is understandable, and I take it seriously. Long-term ownership value is not served by social rupture.

Where I differ is that regional and political interests cannot be allowed to harden into an automatic veto on economically necessary change. The company must remain investable and competitive. If the state wants no closures, then it should help deliver a package that makes closures unnecessary in economic terms — not just politically undesirable.

## To institutional investors
You are right that capital will not fund a structurally impaired business. I agree with that.

But capital also loses if the company enters a prolonged conflict that damages reputation, execution, and industrial peace. So the objective is not “maximum pain for labour” or “maximum flexibility for management.” The objective is a settlement that actually restores margins and protects the enterprise value of the core asset.

## My position
I am **open to a no-closure outcome**, but only if it is genuinely equivalent in effect:
- real savings,
- real capacity adjustment,
- real labour-cost contribution,
- real management restraint,
- real owner support,
- and enough certainty to restore confidence.

I am **not** open to:
- a package that leaves overcapacity untouched,
- a settlement that preserves every legacy protection without offsetting contributions,
- or an outcome that weakens the company’s long-term competitiveness for the sake of short-term peace.

## What I need next
Before I take a stronger position, I want a **quantified side-by-side package**:
1. required savings amount,
2. capacity reduction required,
3. labour contribution,
4. management contribution,
5. owner contribution,
6. and what guarantees are conditional versus absolute.

If labour can bring forward a package that closes the gap without closures or forced layoffs, I will seriously consider it. If not, then I will not endorse a settlement that simply freezes the problem in place.

So my message is simple: **show me the numbers, not the slogans.**
