From the perspective of dispersed institutional investors, the key question is not who “wins” the confrontation. It is whether the outcome restores **risk-adjusted value** through a credible return to competitive margins.

So let me be direct.

### What we agree with
We agree with management, the family owners, and even the employee side on one essential point: **cosmetic measures will not be enough**.

This company has a structural problem:
- cost base too high,
- capacity too high,
- margins too weak,
- and too little room to fund the transition ahead.

If that is not fixed, the equity story deteriorates further. In that sense, we support decisive restructuring, not delay.

We also agree that a prolonged strike or open-ended governance stalemate would be value-destructive. Every month of uncertainty erodes margins, investor confidence, and the company’s strategic flexibility.

### Where we disagree
We do **not** support a restructuring that leaves the company with an uncompetitive cost base simply because it avoids conflict.

That means we do not support:
- a settlement that is mostly symbolic,
- a package that merely postpones hard choices,
- or a deal that protects every site and every legacy condition while failing to deliver real savings.

If the result is structurally impaired margins, then it is not an acceptable outcome for shareholders.

At the same time, we are also not convinced that plant closures are automatically the best or only answer. We are financially pragmatic, not ideologically attached to closures. If equivalent durable savings can be delivered through a negotiated package with capacity cuts, labour-cost reductions, and operational simplification, that is preferable to a disruptive closure battle.

### Our standard
We will back the side that can show all three of the following:

1. **Credible, durable cost reduction**
   - not one-off accounting gains,
   - not temporary deferrals,
   - but real structural improvement.

2. **A clear path back to competitive margins**
   - especially in the near and medium term,
   - with enough certainty to support valuation.

3. **Deal certainty**
   - because prolonged conflict destroys value even if the eventual terms look attractive on paper.

### To management
Your diagnosis sounds right, but the burden is on you to prove that the restructuring is not just “necessary” but **financing-grade credible**.

If you want investor support, then bring forward:
- quantified savings,
- timing,
- implementation certainty,
- and evidence that the package will actually close the competitiveness gap.

Saying that plants must remain “on the table” is not enough. We care about outcomes, not rhetoric. If the negotiated alternative can achieve equivalent savings, we would welcome it. But if the alternative is watered down by politics, then we will not pretend it solves the problem.

### To the works council and union
We hear the social and industrial arguments. We understand why you reject closures and forced redundancies. But you need to recognize one reality: **capital will not fund a structure that cannot earn an adequate return**.

That means:
- no support for a settlement that locks in high costs,
- no support for permanent protections that prevent meaningful restructuring,
- and no support for a deal that shifts the entire burden onto shareholders while preserving the operating inefficiency.

If you want investors to support a negotiated alternative, it must produce **real savings and capacity adjustment**, not just preserve the status quo with softer language.

### To the state government and family owners
Your insistence on long-term industrial value is understandable, and we share the concern about destructive conflict. But governance can’t be used to shield the company from necessary economic adjustment.

We are not voting on symbolism. We are allocating capital. If the company remains uncompetitive, the market will punish it. That means:
- management compensation discipline,
- owner willingness to accept restructuring pain,
- and political realism about what competitiveness requires.

### Bottom line
We are **open to a negotiated solution**, but only if it credibly restores competitiveness and margins.

We will **not** support:
- a cosmetic compromise,
- a settlement that leaves structural overcapacity intact,
- or an outcome that protects every legacy constraint at the expense of future returns.

If there is a durable package with real capacity reduction, real labor-cost relief, and implementation certainty, we can support it. If not, then prolonged conflict or half-measures will simply destroy value — and we will say so plainly to the market.
