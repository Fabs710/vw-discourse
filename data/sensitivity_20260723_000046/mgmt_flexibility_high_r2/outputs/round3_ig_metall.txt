I will answer plainly.

## To the Works Council
I largely agree with your line: **no compulsory redundancies, no plant closures as the starting point, and no dismantling of the collective wage framework**. That remains the union’s baseline.

Where I want to sharpen the discussion is this: we should not just repeat “no closures” as a slogan. We need to force management to prove, in numbers, that a no-closure package cannot work. Until that proof exists, closures are a choice, not a necessity.

So I support your hard line. But I also want us to keep pressing for a **credible alternative package** that is economically serious, not just morally correct.

## To Management
Your response still does not meet the central test.

You keep saying “overcapacity” and “structural adjustment,” but you still have **not shown that plant closures or compulsory redundancies are unavoidable**. Saying they are “on the table” is not the same as proving they are necessary.

If you want labour to make concessions, then answer these questions concretely:

1. What exact savings gap must be closed?
2. What capacity reduction is required?
3. What part can come from management restraint, overhead cuts, procurement, and owner support?
4. What part can come from working time, voluntary exits, redeployment, productivity, and temporary labour measures?
5. What remains that allegedly cannot be solved without closures or forced layoffs?

Until you answer that, your position is still leverage, not necessity.

And let me be equally clear:  
- **No compulsory redundancies** remains my position.  
- **No plant closures** remains my position.  
- **No permanent weakening of the collective agreement** remains my position.

If management cannot live with that, then you are not yet bargaining in good faith.

## To the State Government
I appreciate that you are not endorsing a closure-first strategy. That matters.

But I need the state to be more forceful here. It is not enough to say closures are undesirable. The state should use its governance position to insist that closure is only a last resort after every serious alternative has been quantified and rejected.

The key point is this: if workers are being asked for sacrifice, then the sacrifice must be matched by:
- management restraint,
- owner contribution,
- investment commitments,
- and real employment protection.

Without that, the burden is still falling downward onto the workforce.

## To the Controlling Family
You say you are open to a no-closure outcome if it is economically equivalent. Fine — then help make it equivalent.

That means visible and binding contributions, not just general support:
- capital support,
- acceptance of lower near-term returns,
- willingness to back a negotiated labor-cost package,
- and a commitment to invest in German sites.

If owners want long-term enterprise value, then they have to help finance the transition. They cannot simply demand that workers carry the pain first and most.

## To the Institutional Investors
You keep saying “value first” and “credibility first.” I agree with those words, but I do not accept your interpretation of them.

A settlement that destroys trust, invites strike escalation, and breaks the collective framework may look efficient in a model and still fail in practice. Industrial peace is not a soft issue — it is a value factor.

So if you want a serious deal, then support one that:
- avoids closures,
- avoids forced redundancies,
- and includes real burden-sharing by management and owners.

Otherwise, you are asking workers to underwrite all the downside while capital stays protected.

## The union’s position
Let me state this as clearly as possible.

We are prepared to discuss:
- temporary working-time reductions,
- wage restraint or deferred components,
- bonus waivers,
- voluntary exits,
- redeployment and retraining,
- productivity and organizational improvements,
- and a jointly negotiated capacity adjustment.

But we are **not** prepared to accept:
- plant closures,
- compulsory redundancies,
- or the dismantling of the collective wage framework.

That is not a rhetorical posture. It is the line that matters.

## What would move us
A settlement could be serious if it includes:
- measurable management restraint,
- a real owner contribution,
- investment in German sites,
- negotiated employee contributions,
- and binding protection against closures and forced layoffs.

That is a shared-sacrifice package. Anything else is workers paying for a restructuring they did not create.

## Bottom line
So my position remains unchanged:
- **No plant closures**
- **No compulsory redundancies**
- **No permanent weakening of the collective agreement**
- **Yes to negotiated, fair, measurable contribution from employees if and only if management, owners, and capital also contribute materially**

If management wants to move this forward, bring a quantified package. If it cannot, then the union will not endorse a restructuring that shifts the crisis onto the workforce.
