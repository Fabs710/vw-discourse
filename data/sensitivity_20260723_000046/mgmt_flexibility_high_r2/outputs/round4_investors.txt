We are prepared to engage on a serious package, but not on a package that leaves the company structurally weak.

## Where we agree
We agree with management, the family, and the state on the basic diagnosis:
- the business has a **real structural cost and capacity problem**;
- cosmetic measures are not enough;
- and prolonged conflict would destroy value.

We also agree with labour and the state that burden-sharing cannot fall only on employees. Any credible settlement must include:
- management restraint,
- owner contribution,
- overhead cuts,
- and operational simplification.

That said, agreement on the diagnosis does **not** mean agreement on the remedy.

## Where we disagree

### 1) A blanket “no closures” line is not acceptable
We do **not** support preserving every site as a matter of principle if the result is still an uncompetitive cost base.

If a no-closure package can truly deliver:
- the required savings,
- real capacity reduction,
- and durable margin improvement,

then we would support it.

But if not, then “no closures” becomes a constraint that protects legacy structure at the expense of equity value. That is not acceptable to us.

### 2) A blanket “no compulsory redundancies” line is also not acceptable
We prefer voluntary, negotiated adjustment. Forced layoffs are disruptive and raise execution risk.

But we will not support a categorical ban if it prevents the company from reaching a competitive cost base. The question is not whether forced exits are pleasant; it is whether the final structure is viable.

### 3) Binding job-security guarantees through 2030 are only acceptable if the economics work
A hard guarantee with no real economic conditions would just defer the problem.

If employee protection is to be credible, it has to be tied to:
- measurable savings,
- capacity alignment,
- and a package that actually restores competitiveness.

## On the expert assessment
The expert made the key point correctly: a no-closure package can be equivalent only if it is **large, durable, and enforceable**.

That is exactly the standard we want applied.

But that standard cuts both ways:
- labour must prove that its alternative is financially equivalent;
- management must prove that closures are actually necessary, not merely convenient.

Right now, neither side has fully closed that proof gap.

## What we need to see next
We need the quantified side-by-side package the moderator asked for:

1. required annual savings  
2. required capacity reduction  
3. labour contribution  
4. management contribution  
5. owner/capital contribution  
6. timeline  
7. residual gap  
8. expected margin recovery  
9. implementation risk

Without that, this debate stays rhetorical.

## Our threshold
We will support whatever package credibly restores:
- competitive margins,
- capacity discipline,
- and deal certainty.

We will not support:
- a cosmetic compromise,
- a package that preserves structural overcapacity,
- or a politically comfortable deal that delays pain and destroys value later.

## To labour
If you want investors to support a no-closure path, it must be:
- quantified,
- durable,
- and materially cost-reducing.

Binding guarantees are not a substitute for economics.

## To management
You still need to show the actual gap:
- what savings are required,
- what can be achieved without closures,
- and what cannot.

If closures are truly necessary, prove that. If they are just leverage, that should be said plainly too.

## Bottom line
We remain open to a negotiated no-closure outcome **only if it is economically equivalent** to closure-based restructuring.

If it is not equivalent, we will not support it.

Bring the numbers. Until then, our position remains:
**value first, credibility first, and no tolerance for structural impairment disguised as compromise.**
