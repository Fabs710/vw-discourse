As the regional state government and anchor shareholder, I will respond plainly.

## Where I agree

I agree with management, the family owners, and the investors on one essential point: **this company needs a real, durable restructuring, not a cosmetic postponement**.

I also agree with the works council and the union that **the workforce cannot be made to carry the entire burden**. If the solution is just wage restraint from employees while management, owners, and capital remain insulated, that will not be acceptable and it will not hold politically or industrially.

So yes: we need a serious package, and yes: burden-sharing must be real.

## Where I disagree

### 1) I do not accept plant closures as the default answer
Management keeps saying closures cannot be ruled out “in principle.” I understand the logic of leverage, but I reject the idea that German sites should be treated as disposable adjustment mass.

A closure-led approach would:
- damage regional employment,
- weaken industrial capability,
- destroy trust in the company’s social partnership model,
- and create political and governance conflict that could itself undermine restructuring.

That is not a side issue. It is part of the economic calculation.

### 2) I do not accept forced redundancies as the first or preferred instrument
I can understand that management wants flexibility. But if the restructuring is designed around compulsory job cuts from the start, then it is not a negotiated solution — it is a transfer of pain downward.

Any serious package must first exhaust:
- working-time adjustments,
- redeployment,
- voluntary exits,
- temporary compensation measures,
- productivity improvements,
- overhead and management restraint,
- and capital contributions.

### 3) I do not accept the framing that co-determination is merely advisory
The special statute, our shareholding, and the board structure are not ornaments. They exist to ensure that decisions with major regional and social consequences are not imposed unilaterally.

So I am not prepared to support a strategy that treats the supervisory board and the state’s role as obstacles to be overcome.

## What I think is missing from management’s case

Management keeps asking for “real structural savings,” but it still has not answered the key question:

**What is the quantified gap that truly cannot be closed without closures?**

If management wants to insist that closures remain on the table, then it must show:
- the exact savings target,
- the exact capacity reduction target,
- what share can be achieved through non-closure measures,
- what labour-cost contribution is required,
- and what management/owner contribution is being offered.

Until that is on the table, the closure argument is not a necessity argument. It is a bargaining position.

## What I think the employee side still needs to answer

I also want to be candid with the works council and union.

You are right to reject a one-sided solution. But saying “no closures, no compulsory redundancies” is **not yet a full restructuring answer** unless you show how the required savings are achieved in practice.

So I need to see, in concrete terms:
- how much labour flexibility you are prepared to offer,
- what temporary concessions are possible,
- what productivity measures are realistic,
- and how much capacity reduction can be handled without site destruction.

If you want the state government to back a no-closure settlement, it must be economically credible, not just morally appealing.

## What I can support

I would support a package built on these principles:

1. **No plant closures as the starting point**
   - Closures should not be the opening move.

2. **No compulsory redundancies if avoidable**
   - Voluntary, negotiated, and socially cushioned instruments must come first.

3. **Real burden-sharing**
   - Management compensation restraint
   - Overhead cuts at the top
   - Owner willingness to contribute
   - Employee contribution through temporary and negotiated measures

4. **Binding investment commitments for German sites**
   - If the company asks for concessions, it must commit to future products, technologies, and industrial capacity in Germany.

5. **A credible restructuring plan with measurable outcomes**
   - Savings
   - Capacity alignment
   - Timeline
   - Implementation certainty

## What I cannot support

I cannot support:
- a restructuring that starts with plant closures,
- mass layoffs as the primary tool,
- or a deal that leaves the state and workforce with the social costs while capital is protected from adjustment.

I also cannot support a settlement that weakens co-determination or the state’s veto role under the special statute.

## My position to each side

### To management
Bring forward a quantified proposal that proves why closures are necessary, not merely possible. If you want state support, you need to show real alternatives were assessed fairly and that the package is economically necessary, not just convenient.

### To the works council and union
If you want the state to defend a no-closure settlement, then give us a package that is actually financeable and operationally credible. I will not defend symbolism. I will defend durable jobs and viable plants.

### To the family owners and investors
Long-term value is not served by social rupture. A deal that destroys trust and regional stability may look efficient in theory but will be costly in practice. If you want structural reform, you must also accept visible contributions and not demand that workers absorb everything.

## My bottom line

I am open to a negotiated restructuring.

But I will only support one that:
- **protects German sites wherever possible,**
- **avoids compulsory redundancies as the default,**
- **shares the burden fairly,**
- **preserves co-determination,**
- and **keeps the company as a regional industrial anchor**.

If management comes back with a closure-first logic and no serious alternative analysis, I will oppose it politically and through our governance rights. If labour comes back with no credible flexibility at all, then there is no solution either.

So the next step is not more rhetoric. It is a **quantified, side-by-side restructuring package** showing:
- required savings,
- capacity adjustment,
- employee contribution,
- management and owner contribution,
- and the safeguards for jobs, sites, and investment.

On that basis, we can negotiate seriously. Without it, we cannot.
