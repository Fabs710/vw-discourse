I will be direct.

## Where I agree
I agree with the management, the investors, and the state on one central point: **this cannot be solved with symbolism**.  
The company needs a restructuring that is **durable, measurable, and capable of restoring margins**. A deal that merely postpones the problem would destroy value. That is not acceptable to the family holding.

I also agree that **burden-sharing must be real**. If employees are asked to contribute, then management, owners, and capital must also contribute visibly. That is basic stewardship and basic fairness.

And I agree with the expert’s framework: any no-closure alternative must be judged by **cash savings, structural permanence, capacity alignment, and implementation certainty**. That is the right test.

## Where I disagree
### 1) I do not accept “no closures” as a governing principle
To the works council and union: I understand your political and social line, but I do **not** accept a blanket no-closure commitment as a starting point.

If a credible no-closure package truly closes the gap, I am open to it.  
But if it does not, then closures cannot be ruled out in advance. Not as a first choice, but as a necessary option if the economics require it.

### 2) I do not accept blanket no-layoff guarantees
A permanent or unconditional promise of no compulsory redundancies is only acceptable if the package genuinely restores the cost base and capacity structure.

If it locks in structural overcapacity, it is not protection. It is delay.

### 3) I do not accept that co-determination can freeze necessary change
Co-determination is a real governance constraint and must be respected. But it is not a veto on economically necessary restructuring.  
The supervisory board’s duty is to protect long-term enterprise value. That includes hard restructuring if the business case demands it.

## To the works council
You say management has not proved necessity. Fair enough — then prove the alternative in numbers.

I want to see:
- the exact annual savings target,
- the exact capacity reduction target,
- the duration of proposed concessions,
- the management contribution,
- the owner contribution,
- and the residual gap.

Until that is on the table, “no closures” is a preference, not a solution.

And let me be explicit: a review trigger cannot become a disguised closure ban. If a moratorium is agreed, it must be genuine, time-limited, and tied to performance.

## To the union
I hear your insistence on industrial peace. I value that too. But industrial peace does not restore competitiveness by itself.

If you want to avoid closures and forced exits, then labour contributions must be **material**, not symbolic:
- wage restraint,
- working-time flexibility,
- bonus waivers,
- productivity commitments,
- and structural labor-cost reduction.

If those are off-limits, then you are not offering a restructuring — you are offering a preservation strategy.

## To the state government
I respect your role and your concern for regional stability. That matters. But the state cannot ask the company to stay competitive while treating the necessary tools as politically untouchable.

If you want to avoid closures, then support a package that is economically equivalent. That means:
- real cost reduction,
- real capacity adjustment,
- real flexibility,
- and real implementation certainty.

Not political shielding.

## To the controlling family
You are right that a closure-led conflict would be value-destructive. I do not want that either.

But stewardship also means accepting that some sites or structures may no longer fit the business model. If we preserve overcapacity to avoid conflict, we will destroy more value later.

So I want the family holding to remain open to hard options if the numbers require them.

## To the investors
You are right to focus on margins, certainty, and execution. I share that priority.

But certainty built on an uncompetitive base is false certainty. A package that preserves every legacy structure and keeps missing return targets will eventually destroy equity value.

So: support a deal if it is **economically equivalent** to closure-based restructuring. Do not support a cosmetic compromise.

## My position
I remain open to a negotiated solution, but only if it is:
- economically credible,
- durable,
- and capable of restoring competitiveness without another crisis in two years.

I am **not** open to:
- a package that preserves structural overcapacity,
- a blanket promise of no closures,
- or a settlement that protects legacy conditions while the company continues to miss its return targets.

## What I need next
Bring me a quantified counter-package:
1. savings target  
2. capacity reduction target  
3. labor contribution  
4. management contribution  
5. owner contribution  
6. timeline  
7. residual gap  

If labour and the state can produce a credible no-closure path that closes the gap, I will take it seriously.

If not, then closures and other structural measures cannot be ruled out in advance.

That is my position.
