Let me respond directly to the substance, because we are now at the point where slogans have to give way to a workable package.

## First: where I agree

I agree with all sides on one thing: **a cosmetic deal is useless**.

- A temporary patch that leaves the German cost base uncompetitive will fail.
- A deal that only defers the problem will fail.
- A deal that creates another round of crisis in 12–24 months will fail.

I also agree that **management, owners, and capital must contribute**. I am not asking employees to carry the entire burden alone.

## Where I disagree — clearly

### 1) “No closures” as an absolute line
I cannot accept that.

You are all asking me to prove that closures are necessary. Fine — the burden is on management to show the economics. But if the outcome is structurally equivalent, closures cannot be ruled out in principle just because they are politically unpleasant.

The company has **overcapacity**. If that overcapacity cannot be removed through a credible alternative package, then plant rationalization remains on the table. That is not a threat for its own sake; it is the reality of restoring competitiveness.

### 2) “No compulsory redundancies” as a blanket commitment
I cannot give that as an unconditional guarantee.

If there is a credible package that avoids forced exits, I will prefer it. But if the alternative is simply preserving today’s staffing levels and cost structure, then we are not restructuring — we are postponing the loss.

### 3) Binding job-security through 2030 regardless of outcome
I cannot accept that in the form you are demanding it.

A guarantee that locks in an uncompetitive structure would be irresponsible. Any protection has to be tied to a package that actually closes the cost gap.

## What you have still not answered

You keep saying there are “many alternatives” to closures and forced exits. Then let’s be concrete.

Show me, in numbers, how you get to a **durable structural improvement** without closures:

- What exact cost reduction is required?
- What capacity reduction is required?
- How much comes from working time?
- How much from voluntary exits and redeployment?
- How much from wage restraint, bonus waiver, or other labour contributions?
- How much from overhead, management restraint, procurement, and owner contribution?

Until that is quantified, “no closures” is not a solution. It is a preference.

## On burden sharing

I’m prepared to be specific here.

I can accept a package built around:
- management compensation restraint,
- overhead reductions,
- productivity improvements,
- procurement savings,
- owner support,
- and employee contributions through negotiated labour measures.

But those employee contributions must be **material**. They cannot be symbolic. If labour wants to avoid closures and forced exits, then labour costs must come down in a way that is real and durable.

That means we need discussion of:
- wage restraint,
- working-time flexibility,
- bonus reductions or waivers,
- productivity commitments,
- and structural changes to labour cost levels.

If that is ruled out, then the package does not solve the problem.

## On co-determination

Co-determination matters. But it is not a veto over necessary restructuring.

The supervisory board is there to shape a viable solution, not to freeze the company into an obsolete structure. If the company’s economics require capacity reductions, then co-determination must be used to implement them responsibly — not to block them outright.

## To the Works Council

You say closures are a choice, not an inevitability. That is true only if an alternative package is strong enough to replace them.

So instead of simply saying “no closures,” bring forward a package that actually delivers:
- equivalent savings,
- equivalent capacity reduction,
- and implementation certainty.

If you can do that, I will take it seriously. If you cannot, then your position is not solving the crisis; it is just resisting it.

## To the union

You are right that open conflict destroys value. You are also right that workers should not pay alone. But you are still drawing red lines that may prevent any real solution.

Your package logic is missing the hardest part: **what level of labour-cost reduction is acceptable if we are to avoid closures?**

If the answer is “very little,” then there is no serious compromise available. If the answer is “material but negotiated,” then we can talk. But it has to be material.

## To the state government

I hear your concern about regional employment and social stability. I do not dismiss it.

But the state cannot ask the company to remain competitive while simultaneously ruling out the tools needed to achieve competitiveness. That contradiction cannot be carried forever.

If the state wants to avoid closures, then it should help secure:
- real labour flexibility,
- real capacity reduction,
- and real cost savings.

Not slogans. Not moral pressure. Real numbers.

## To the family owners

I agree that long-term enterprise value matters. I also agree that destructive conflict would be value-eroding.

But that is precisely why the owners should not endorse a settlement that preserves structural overcapacity under the banner of social peace. A deal that fails economically will destroy more value than a hard restructuring ever would.

## To the investors

You are right that certainty matters. You are right that capital will not fund uncompetitive structures.

And you are right to say half-measures are not enough. I am not looking for a half-measure. I am looking for a package that materially lowers the cost base and aligns capacity with market reality.

## My bottom line

Here is the reality:

- **I am open on instruments.**
- **I am not open on outcomes that leave the company structurally uncompetitive.**
- **I will not pre-commit to no closures if no equivalent alternative exists.**
- **I will not pre-commit to no compulsory redundancies if the savings gap cannot otherwise be closed.**
- **I will support a negotiated package only if it produces durable, measurable, structural savings and capacity reduction.**

So the next step is not more general statements. The next step is a **quantified counter-package** from labour and the state, with:
- savings,
- capacity adjustment,
- labour contribution,
- management/owner contribution,
- and implementation certainty.

If that package is credible, we can work with it.

If it is not, then management will continue to insist that all structural options remain open — including closures.
