I will be direct.

## Where I agree

I agree with all sides on one essential point: **cosmetic measures are not enough**.

This company needs a restructuring that is:
- durable,
- measurable,
- and capable of restoring margins and competitiveness.

I also agree that **burden-sharing must be real**. It would be unacceptable for employees alone to carry the adjustment while management, owners, and capital remain untouched. That would not be a stewardship solution; it would be value-destructive and likely unstable.

And I agree that we need to move from slogans to a **quantified package**.

---

## Where I disagree

### 1) I do not accept “no closures” as an absolute principle
The works council and union keep presenting no closures as a moral baseline. I understand the politics of that position. I do **not** accept it as a governing principle for the company.

If the economics can be solved without closures, excellent. I am open to that.

But if a credible package cannot close the structural gap otherwise, then closures must remain available. Not as a first choice. As a necessary option if the numbers require it.

### 2) I do not accept blanket no-layoff guarantees
A permanent or unconditional promise of no compulsory redundancies is only acceptable if the package actually restores the cost base and capacity structure.

If it locks in an uncompetitive setup, it merely postpones the crisis and makes the eventual adjustment worse.

### 3) I do not accept that co-determination can veto necessary change
Co-determination is a real governance constraint and must be respected. But it is not a right to freeze the enterprise in an obsolete structure.

The supervisory board’s duty is to preserve the long-term enterprise value of the core asset. That includes accepting difficult restructuring if the business case demands it.

---

## What I think the other parties still have not answered

You all keep asking management to prove necessity. That is fair. But the burden is not only on management. If you want to rule out closures and forced exits, then show the alternative in numbers.

I want a side-by-side comparison of:

1. **Required annual savings**
2. **Required capacity reduction**
3. **Employee contribution**
4. **Management contribution**
5. **Owner/capital contribution**
6. **Residual gap**
7. **Timeline to margin recovery**

Until that exists, “no closures” is not a solution. It is a preference.

---

## To the works council and union

You are right that the workforce should not pay alone. You are right that management restraint, owner support, and investment commitments matter.

But your position is still incomplete unless you can show how the required savings are achieved without destroying the enterprise’s competitiveness.

So I need more than a refusal. I need a package with substance:
- working-time flexibility,
- voluntary exits,
- redeployment and retraining,
- productivity improvements,
- wage restraint or bonus waivers,
- and a meaningful, durable labour-cost contribution.

If those measures are not material, then the package will not solve the problem.

And let me be clear: **I will not support a deal that simply preserves today’s structures and calls that restructuring.**

---

## To management

You are right to insist on structural change. But you still need to separate:
- what is truly necessary,
- from what is simply useful leverage.

If closures remain on the table, you need to explain quantitatively why:
- the savings gap cannot otherwise be closed,
- the capacity reduction cannot otherwise be achieved,
- and the margin recovery cannot otherwise be secured.

Do that clearly. Then the governance bodies can assess whether a no-closure path is genuinely equivalent.

Right now, the case is not yet sufficiently proven.

---

## To the state government

I respect the state’s role and its concern for regional stability. That concern matters.

But the state cannot ask the company to remain competitive while also implying that every difficult restructuring tool is politically off limits. If the state wants to avoid closures, it should help secure a package that is economically equivalent.

That means:
- real flexibility,
- real contribution,
- real cost reduction,
- and real implementation certainty.

Not symbolic protection.

---

## To institutional investors

You are right that the company cannot fund structural weakness indefinitely. I agree.

But you should also be careful not to treat social conflict as a side effect. Prolonged conflict can destroy more value than a negotiated restructuring, even a painful one.

Your threshold should therefore be:
- durable savings,
- credible capacity alignment,
- and industrial peace sufficient to implement the plan.

If a no-closure package delivers that, it is worth supporting. If it does not, then it is not credible.

---

## My position

I remain open to a negotiated solution. But only if it is:

- **economically equivalent** to a closure-based restructuring,
- **materially cost-reducing,**
- **durable,**
- **and capable of restoring competitiveness without another crisis in two years.**

I am **not** open to:
- a package that preserves structural overcapacity,
- a blanket promise of no closures,
- or a settlement that protects legacy conditions while the company continues to miss its return targets.

---

## What I want next

I support the moderator’s call for a quantified side-by-side package, but it must be real and not rhetorical.

Bring:
- the required savings,
- the required capacity adjustment,
- the labour contribution,
- management restraint,
- owner contribution,
- and the remaining gap.

If labour and the state can produce a credible no-closure path that closes the gap, I will take it seriously.

If not, then closures and other structural measures cannot be ruled out in advance.

That is my position.
