I appreciate the positions that have been laid out, and I want to respond clearly from the standpoint of the controlling owners and long-term stewards of this company.

### First: what I agree with
I agree with management on the basic diagnosis: **this company cannot keep carrying an uncompetitive cost and capacity structure** and still expect to fund the next generation of products, technology, and industrial capability. If we avoid structural change now, the long-term damage will be worse.

I also agree with the works council, the union, and the state government on one essential point: **a destructive conflict is value-eroding**. We do not want prolonged industrial warfare, reputational damage, or a settlement that breaks trust and weakens the enterprise’s future.

So yes: the goal must be a **durable, negotiated solution**.

### Where I disagree
I do **not** accept the idea that the company can be made competitive while preserving every current structure intact. That is not stewardship; it is denial.

Equally, I do **not** accept a restructuring logic that makes plant closures the default instrument. Once you start treating closures as the easy answer, you risk destroying industrial capability and social capital that are not easily rebuilt. That would be a serious error.

But I also need to be equally explicit with labour: **a blanket “no closures, no compulsory redundancies, no changes to the framework” position is not enough by itself**. If the savings and capacity reductions cannot be delivered in a credible way, then you are not offering a solution — you are just freezing the problem in place.

### What must be true for me to move
I can support a settlement if, and only if, it meets three conditions:

1. **It delivers real, durable competitiveness gains**
   - Not symbolic cuts.
   - Not short-lived deferrals.
   - Real improvement in margins, cost base, and capacity alignment.

2. **It preserves long-term enterprise value**
   - That means protecting the company’s industrial core, brand strength, and ability to invest.
   - A deal that “saves face” today but weakens the company for years is not acceptable.

3. **It avoids unnecessary instability**
   - Forced confrontation, open-ended strikes, or a governance crisis would all destroy value.
   - Any package must be robust enough to hold.

### On plant closures
My position is not that closures must happen. It is that **they cannot be ruled out in principle if no equivalent alternative exists**.

That is an important distinction.

If management can show that equivalent savings and capacity reductions can be achieved through:
- working-time arrangements,
- voluntary departures,
- redeployment,
- temporary labour contributions,
- productivity improvements,
- and other negotiated instruments,

then that is preferable to closures.

But if the alternative package is just a way to preserve everything unchanged while calling it reform, then it will not be enough. I will not support a settlement that merely postpones necessary decisions and continues structural overcapacity.

### On compulsory redundancies
I would strongly prefer to avoid them. They are socially costly and can damage the company’s standing and know-how base.

But I am not prepared to make an absolute commitment that would remove any credible restructuring leverage. If we want to avoid compulsory redundancies, then the alternative package has to be substantial enough to make that possible.

### On burden sharing
Here I agree with the union and the state government: **labour cannot be the only side asked to contribute**.

Management, overhead, procurement, and capital all need to be part of the burden-sharing logic. If employees are asked for concessions, then they must see visible contributions from leadership and owners as well.

But burden sharing does not mean symmetry in rhetoric. It means effectiveness in outcome. The question is not whether everyone suffers equally. The question is whether the package restores the company’s competitiveness without permanently impairing it.

### On co-determination and governance
I also want to be clear here: **our governance rights matter**, and they exist to protect the long-term integrity of the enterprise, not to rubber-stamp a one-sided restructuring.

That means:
- no attempt to bypass the supervisory board,
- no attempt to force through a closure-led strategy without a viable alternative review,
- and no attempt to use this crisis to weaken the ownership structure or regional safeguards.

At the same time, co-determination is not a shield against necessary adaptation. It is a mechanism for shaping a viable settlement. If the only answer is “no” to all material change, then governance cannot do its job.

### My bottom line
I am **not** taking a blank cheque position for management, and I am **not** endorsing a closure-first approach.

But I am also **not** willing to support a settlement that preserves jobs and sites on paper while leaving the company structurally uncompetitive.

So my message to all parties is simple:

- Bring forward a package that **genuinely** restores competitiveness.
- Show how it achieves **durable** savings and capacity alignment.
- Keep the solution **socially stable** enough to last.
- And do not assume the owners will back anything that permanently weakens the enterprise.

If a credible alternative to closures exists, I am open to it. If not, then I will not support a deal that substitutes slogans for restructuring.
