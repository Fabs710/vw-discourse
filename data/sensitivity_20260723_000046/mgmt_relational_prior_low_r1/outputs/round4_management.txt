I will be equally direct.

The workforce side keeps repeating “no closures, no forced redundancies” as if repetition could substitute for an economically sufficient solution. It cannot. We all now agree the problem is structural. The question is whether the response is structural enough. So far, I do **not** see that it is.

## Where I agree
I agree with the experts on one crucial point: a cosmetic compromise is useless. If we do not materially improve the cost base, the company will remain under pressure, investment will remain constrained, and we will simply postpone the next crisis.

I also agree that temporary gestures alone do not solve this. Any deal has to be durable through 2030 and must restore margins, not just industrial peace.

## Where I disagree
### With the Works Council and Union
Your position still amounts to: preserve all German sites, rule out compulsory redundancies, and then expect management to somehow close a major structural cost gap anyway. That is not a solution. It is a constraint package.

You say the burden should be shared. I agree. But “shared” cannot mean that management and owners absorb the economic reality while the operational footprint stays largely untouched. If the footprint is too expensive and overcapacity is real, then refusing closures in principle is not responsibility. It is wishful thinking.

You also keep portraying a closure fallback as bad faith. I reject that. A fallback is not a threat for its own sake; it is a credibility mechanism. If the non-closure measures deliver enough, the fallback will not be needed. If they do not, then the company must have a way to correct course. That is called governance.

### With the state
I respect the state’s role, but I do not accept the idea that your blocking position can be used to freeze structural adjustment indefinitely. You are right that a reckless move would destroy value. But so would an arrangement that preserves every site while leaving the company uncompetitive.

### With capital-side stakeholders
You are right to demand credibility and durability. But credibility does not come from the abstract promise that “something will improve.” It comes from a quantified path that closes the gap. That is why I keep asking for numbers. Not because I want to avoid the hard choices, but because I want to make sure we are not settling for a weak compromise.

## On the finance benchmark
The expert has been clear: a non-closure package is only credible if it closes roughly **70–80%** of the recurring structural gap. I accept that as a serious benchmark.

But that cuts both ways:
- If the package reaches that level, then we can discuss whether a hybrid solution is sufficient.
- If it does **not**, then the employee side’s “no closures” red line is simply not enough.

And here is the key point: if the remaining gap is still material, I am not prepared to pretend that capacity reduction without shutdown, voluntary exits, and wage restraint can magically absorb it all. That is not serious industrial policy.

## What I need to see
The discussion has now reached the point where slogans must give way to evidence.

I want a concrete, quantified answer to six questions:
1. What is the exact annual structural gap?
2. What does each non-closure measure contribute?
3. How much durable savings comes from labor-cost contribution?
4. How much comes from overhead and productivity?
5. How much comes from capacity reduction short of closure?
6. What remains if closures are excluded entirely?

If the remaining gap is small enough, then a hybrid package may be viable. If it is not, then the employee side needs to stop treating closure as illegitimate in principle and engage on the real trade-off.

## My position
I remain open to a **hybrid or staged package** if — and only if — it delivers a durable structural reset.

That means:
- measurable and recurring cost reductions,
- real capacity correction,
- a genuine employee contribution,
- management overhead cuts,
- and a settlement that restores competitiveness.

What I will **not** accept is:
- a deal that preserves all historical structures,
- a package that leaves the company materially above target on cost,
- or a “no-closure” settlement that is politically attractive but economically incomplete.

## Final point
Let me be blunt: if management is expected to preserve every site and every guarantee while also restoring margins, then someone has to explain where the structural savings come from. So far, I have not heard a credible answer that closes the gap fully without deeper structural action.

The burden of proof is not on management to prove a closure for its own sake. The burden is on the employee side to prove that a no-closure alternative is economically sufficient. Until then, closures remain a legitimate part of the restructuring toolkit, not a taboo.
