From the capital-markets side, the specialist’s framing is exactly right: this is not about defending one constituency’s preferences, it is about whether the package closes the structural gap fast enough and durably enough to restore margins and protect future investment.

## Where we agree
- The company clearly has a **real fixed-cost and capacity problem**.
- A **cosmetic settlement** would destroy value.
- The market will punish **prolonged conflict** just as surely as it will punish inaction.
- Investment capacity through 2030 matters, and cost action has to protect that.

## Where we disagree with the employee side
The Works Council and Union keep repeating “no closures, no compulsory redundancies” as if that were enough to solve the economics. It is not.

If those red lines are maintained, then the employee side has to show, in numbers, how the company gets to:
- a materially lower fixed-cost base,
- reduced overcapacity,
- and a margin trajectory that the market can believe.

So far, I have not seen that. “Working time,” “voluntary exits,” “redeployment,” and “productivity” may help, but unless they close most of the gap on a durable basis, they are not equivalent to structural capacity removal. If they only preserve the current footprint with slightly softer costs, that still leaves shareholders with an under-earning asset.

## Where we disagree with management
I also do not want management to treat plant closure as the automatic default if the non-closure package is merely imperfect. The specialist is right: closures carry heavy cash cost, execution risk, and industrial-relations damage.

So management needs to be precise, not rhetorical:
- What is the actual annual cost gap?
- How much can be closed without closures?
- What remains if closures are removed from the table?
- What is the NPV of each route?

If management cannot show that a non-closure package falls materially short, then it should not overstate the necessity of closures. But if the gap remains large, then the capital side will not support preserving an uncompetitive structure just to avoid difficult decisions.

## Where we stand
What would move us is a **quantified, staged package** that delivers most of the savings quickly and durably, with clear enforcement. That means:

- meaningful capacity reduction,
- real labor-cost improvement,
- overhead discipline,
- voluntary workforce reduction,
- and a trigger mechanism if delivery falls short.

A staged approach is acceptable **only if it is not a delay tactic**. It must have milestones, fallback actions, and credibility.

## Our bottom line
- We will not back a deal that leaves the cost base structurally uncompetitive.
- We will not back a prolonged industrial standoff that destroys value.
- We will support the most value-preserving route to durable margin recovery, whether that is achieved through closures, hybrid measures, or a non-closure package that is genuinely equivalent in effect.

Right now, the burden is on labor to show that its alternative is not just politically preferable, but financially sufficient. And the burden is on management to show the gap honestly rather than using closures as a negotiating club.
