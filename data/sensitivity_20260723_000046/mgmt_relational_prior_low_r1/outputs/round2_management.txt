Let me respond directly, because we are now at the point where vague principles are no longer enough.

I agree with one part of what the employee side is saying: this company does need a negotiated, durable transformation, and a cosmetic deal will not solve anything. I also agree that investment in future products and technology must remain protected. On that, we are not far apart.

But I do **not** agree with the core premise being repeated here: that German plant closures, compulsory redundancies, and meaningful labour-cost adjustment can simply be ruled out in advance.

That position is not a restructuring strategy. It is a veto on adaptation.

## On the Works Council’s position
You say the workforce is being treated as the adjustment variable for past strategic mistakes. I reject that framing. We are dealing with current, structural realities:
- German factory costs are too high,
- the European market is shrinking,
- competition is more intense than before,
- and our brand is not meeting its return-on-sales target.

If we keep the current footprint and cost structure largely intact, we do not “protect the future”; we weaken it. Skilled capacity is only an asset if it is economically sustainable. Preserving every site regardless of economics is not responsible stewardship.

You are asking for:
- binding site guarantees,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- protection of co-determination,
- and a future horizon to 2030.

I am telling you plainly: those demands are only acceptable if they are matched by a **real and durable** cost and capacity correction. Otherwise, they amount to freezing an uncompetitive structure and expecting management to call that a solution.

## On the Union’s position
I hear your willingness to discuss temporary wage moderation, bonuses, working-time flexibility, voluntary exits, redeployment, and qualification. That is the right direction in principle.

But I need to be equally clear: those measures alone do **not** automatically close the structural gap. If the outcome is merely:
- a bit of wage moderation,
- some working-time tinkering,
- some voluntary attrition,
- and a promise of productivity gains,

while the fundamental overcapacity and cost problem remains, then that is not enough.

A durable settlement must deliver:
- measurable capacity reduction,
- a genuine labour-cost contribution,
- lower fixed costs,
- and a credible path back to competitive margins.

And I will not accept a settlement that keeps the company structurally too expensive simply because it avoids hard choices.

## On the Regional State Government
I respect the state’s role and the special governance constraints. I also understand the importance of regional stability. But I need to say this clearly: “no closures unless alternatives are exhausted” is too open-ended if the practical effect is to make every serious alternative reversible and every closure politically impossible.

If you want to be constructive, then help deliver an alternative that is genuinely equivalent in economic effect. If not, then you are asking management to absorb the cost of inaction.

The state cannot simultaneously insist on:
- no closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- no meaningful change to guarantees,
- and a fully restored competitive cost base,

without explaining how those objectives are supposed to coexist.

## On the controlling family and investors
I agree with one point from the capital side: prolonged conflict destroys value. That is correct. A prolonged strike or endless uncertainty would be damaging, and management is not blind to that.

But I do **not** accept the implication that management should soften its position simply to avoid confrontation. Confrontation is not the goal; structural correction is. If pressure is needed to secure a durable settlement, then so be it. The company cannot be governed by the fear of conflict alone.

What I need from the capital side is not rhetorical support for “competitiveness,” but acceptance that competitiveness sometimes requires hard industrial decisions. If the employee side refuses every meaningful adjustment, then investors should not pretend that preserving value is compatible with preserving every historical guarantee.

## Where I draw the line
Let me be unambiguous:

- I will **not** accept a deal that leaves the company with the same structurally uncompetitive cost base.
- I will **not** accept a cosmetic compromise that avoids closures and layoffs in form, but preserves overcapacity in substance.
- I will **not** accept a settlement that blocks the funds needed for future products, software, and EV investment through 2030.

## What I would consider
I remain open to a negotiated path that avoids compulsory redundancies **if** it delivers equivalent structural effect. That means:
- real and quantified capacity reduction,
- meaningful labour-cost relief,
- voluntary and socially managed workforce reduction,
- productivity and process improvement,
- working-time flexibility,
- and a settlement that is durable enough to restore medium-term margins.

If the employee side can bring forward a package that genuinely closes the gap, I will engage seriously. If not, then we will continue to press for deeper structural measures, including plant-level consequences where necessary.

So my message is simple: I am prepared to negotiate a solution, but I am not prepared to negotiate away competitiveness.
