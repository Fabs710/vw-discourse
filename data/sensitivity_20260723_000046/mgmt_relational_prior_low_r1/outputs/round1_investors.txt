From the perspective of dispersed institutional investors, the issue is not whether restructuring is painful; it is whether it is credible enough to restore risk-adjusted value.

I agree with management, and partly with the controlling family, on the core diagnosis: if the company’s German cost base remains structurally uncompetitive, then margins will stay under pressure and equity value will continue to erode. A cosmetic settlement that preserves the current economics would be unacceptable. We have been watching the margin gap, and we do not want capital tied up in a business that cannot earn its cost of capital.

Where I disagree with the current tone from the employee side is on red lines that appear to rule out the very measures that would make the business viable. “No closures ever” and “no compulsory redundancies” are not, by themselves, a business plan. If those positions simply preserve excess capacity and keep fixed costs too high, then they leave shareholders with a structurally impaired asset. That is not something we can support.

At the same time, I do not support management treating closures as the first or only instrument. Prolonged conflict, strikes, and uncertainty destroy value too. If the company spends the next year in industrial confrontation, the market will punish that just as much as it would punish inaction. So the standard is not “be aggressive”; it is “be credible, durable, and implementable.”

What would move us is a package that clearly and durably closes the cost gap:
- quantified capacity reduction,
- meaningful labour-cost improvement,
- a credible reduction in fixed overhead,
- no open-ended guarantees that lock in an uncompetitive structure,
- and enough certainty that the market can believe the plan will actually be executed.

We would also expect the settlement to protect the company’s ability to invest in future products. Cutting costs without preserving investment capacity would just defer the next crisis.

So my position is:
- I am not aligned with a purely defensive approach that protects every site regardless of economics.
- I am not aligned with a management strategy that creates prolonged disruption without a clear path to completion.
- I am open to alternative measures to closures and layoffs only if they are economically equivalent in effect, durable, and credible to the market.

If the employee side can offer a genuine package of working-time flexibility, voluntary reductions, productivity gains, and labour-cost moderation that closes the gap in a measurable way, we would take that seriously. But if the result is merely to postpone hard decisions while keeping the cost base structurally high, then we will not back it. We can reallocate capital elsewhere, and we will.

In short: deliver a believable return to competitive margins, or expect investor pressure to intensify.
