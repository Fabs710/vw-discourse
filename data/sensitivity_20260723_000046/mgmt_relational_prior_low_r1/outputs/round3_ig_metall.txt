I will respond directly and without hiding behind generalities.

## Where I agree
I agree with the Works Council, the state shareholder, and even parts of management on one point: **this cannot be a cosmetic deal**. The company has a real competitiveness problem, and a durable settlement must be quantified, enforceable, and investment-capable through 2030.

I also agree that a weak arrangement which merely postpones the problem would be bad for everyone, including the workforce.

## Where I disagree
I do **not** accept the conclusion that this therefore justifies:
- German plant closures,
- compulsory redundancies,
- or turning workers into the default shock absorber for strategic and structural failures.

That remains my red line.

### To management
You keep saying the gap is structural. Fine. But you still have not shown that **closures are necessary** rather than preferred. That is the key difference.

You say a non-closure deal must close most of the gap. I agree with that standard in principle. But then prove, with numbers, that you have exhausted the serious non-closure instruments:
- capacity reduction without shutdown,
- working-time adjustments,
- voluntary exits,
- redeployment,
- qualification,
- management overhead cuts,
- and a fair but bounded employee contribution.

Until that is done, closure is not “responsible,” it is just the hardest option presented as inevitability.

### To the controlling family and investors
You are right that value destruction is real. But you are underestimating the value destruction of a confrontation that breaks the collective framework and detonates trust.

A settlement that preserves short-term accounting comfort at the expense of long-term industrial peace is not value creation. It is deferred damage.

### To the Regional State Government
You are right to reject a closure-first reflex. But I need to be equally clear: a vague “fallback later” model is not enough if it becomes a corridor to closures in disguise.

If the state is serious about protecting the industrial base, then it must help lock in:
- no compulsory redundancies,
- no unilateral closures,
- and binding implementation control, not just rhetorical support.

## Our position: still clear
### Non-negotiable red lines
1. **No German plant closures.**
2. **No compulsory redundancies.**
3. **No dismantling of the collective wage framework.**
4. **No open-ended insecurity imposed on workers.**

I am not moving off that because management repeats the word “structural.”

## What we are prepared to discuss
We remain open to a **serious shared-sacrifice package**, but only if it is actually shared.

That means:
- temporary and limited wage moderation,
- reduced bonuses and special payments,
- working-time flexibility,
- voluntary exits and retirement windows,
- redeployment and training,
- productivity improvements,
- and limited employee contributions.

But this must be matched by:
- management overhead cuts,
- owner willingness to accept lower distributions or stronger contribution,
- binding investment commitments,
- and site security.

## On the expert’s numbers
The specialist’s point matters: a non-closure package must close most of the gap to be credible. I do not dispute that.

What I dispute is the assumption that only closures can achieve that. That is still unproven.

A **hybrid package** is the only route worth discussing further — but only if “hybrid” does **not** mean “closure later by another name.” If that is what is being smuggled in, then it is not a compromise, it is a delay tactic.

## What we would require for a real deal
If management wants a settlement, then it needs to table a package with these elements:

- a transparent quantified cost-gap analysis,
- savings by measure,
- **all German sites kept open**,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- a binding investment and product roadmap through 2030,
- management and ownership contributions,
- and a genuine role for the workforce in implementation.

## My answer to the mediator’s bridge proposals
### Proposal 1: Trigger-based escalation
Not acceptable if the trigger can lead to closures or forced layoffs. That is not security; it is conditional threat.

### Proposal 2: Hybrid package with non-core capacity adjustments
Potentially discussable, but only if it preserves every German site and avoids forced redundancies.

### Proposal 3: Temporary labor concessions for binding commitments
Potentially discussable, but only if the commitments are legally binding and include:
- no closures,
- no forced redundancies,
- no unilateral management escape hatch,
- and no dismantling of the wage framework.

## Bottom line
We are prepared to contribute fairly. We are **not** prepared to finance restructuring by surrendering jobs, plants, or collective standards.

If management wants a durable settlement, it must come back with a package that proves it can close the gap **without** making the workforce carry all the pain.

If it cannot do that, then it should not expect industrial peace.
