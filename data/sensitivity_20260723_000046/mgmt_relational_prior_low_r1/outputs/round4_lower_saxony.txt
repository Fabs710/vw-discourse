I will be equally direct.

We now have enough clarity to separate serious restructuring from a closure-driven bargaining strategy. I accept the need for a **durable, quantified correction**. I do **not** accept the move to treat German plant closures as the default or the residual option if labor does not voluntarily surrender enough.

## Where I agree

### With the specialists
The finance benchmark is useful: a solution must be **durable through 2030**, not cosmetic, and it must close most of the gap. I accept that a weak or temporary package would be irresponsible.

### With the state
You are right that a reckless restructuring would damage legitimacy, provoke conflict, and undermine the company’s role as a regional anchor. I also agree that the company needs a real margin recovery, not symbolism.

### With labor and the works council
You are right that the burden cannot fall on workers alone. Any serious deal must include contributions from management and ownership, plus binding investment commitments.

## Where I disagree

### On keeping closures as a fallback
I do **not** accept a permanent closure corridor as an acceptable “credibility mechanism.” That is not neutral governance; it is a standing threat against jobs and sites in our state.

If workers are asked to make sacrifices now, then the basic settlement must be that those sacrifices are exchanged for **real site security**. A deal that says “give concessions first, and if management later decides it still wants closures, we’ll revisit” is not a fair compromise. It is conditional insecurity dressed up as prudence.

That is fundamentally unacceptable to us.

### To management
You keep saying the footprint may be too expensive. Fine — but you still have not proven that closures are unavoidable. “Preferred” is not the same as “necessary.” Before any closure logic is even discussed, I need to see:

- the actual annual cost gap,
- what non-closure measures close,
- what management contributes,
- what ownership contributes,
- and what remains after serious internal restructuring.

Until that is transparent, closure remains an assertion, not a justified conclusion.

### To the controlling family
I understand the value-preservation argument. But value is not preserved by detonating industrial trust and leaving a permanent scar on the regional economy. If you want long-term value, then support a package that keeps German sites open and avoids forced redundancies.

### To institutional investors
You are right to demand credible economics. But credibility cannot be built on a workforce-side threat. If you want a durable settlement, it must rest on:
- binding savings,
- binding investment,
- binding management discipline,
- and binding job and site security.

Not on keeping closure pressure alive indefinitely.

## My position
I remain open to a **serious hybrid settlement** — but only if it truly avoids closures and forced redundancies, and only if it is strong enough to be durable.

That means I can discuss:
- voluntary exits,
- working-time flexibility,
- temporary and limited labor-cost contributions,
- productivity and complexity reduction,
- redeployment and qualification,
- management overhead cuts,
- and owner contributions.

But I will not move on:
1. **no German plant closures,**
2. **no compulsory redundancies,**
3. **no fallback mechanism that reopens closure threats later,**
4. **no erosion of co-determination or job-security guarantees.**

- what savings come from voluntary exits,
- what from shift and working-time changes,
- what from overhead and management cuts,
- what from productivity and complexity reduction,
- what from capacity reduction without shutdown,
- and what remains if closures are fully excluded.

If the answer is that a no-closure package can genuinely close most of the gap, then that is the route I will support. If the answer is that it cannot, then management must explain why it is still insisting that the workforce carry the final burden rather than owners, overhead, strategy, and investment discipline.

## Bottom line
We are prepared to support a tough, fair, and binding restructuring. We are **not** prepared to support a package that preserves the company on paper while sacrificing plants, jobs, and regional trust in reality.

So my message to all sides is simple:
- **No closure fallback as a bargaining instrument.**
- **No compulsory redundancies.**
- **No weakening of the state’s role.**
- **Yes to a quantified, durable, shared-sacrifice package that protects the industrial base.**

If the numbers prove that a no-closure settlement can work, I will support it.  
If not, then the burden is on management and capital to justify why they are asking the state and workforce to accept a path that damages our regional anchor.
