We are now at the point where the discussion must become concrete, and I will be equally concrete.

I agree with one thing the specialist and even parts of management have finally made explicit: this is not a symbolic problem, it is a structural one. The company needs a durable path back to competitiveness, and that path must be measurable. Fine. But the leap management keeps trying to make — from “there is a gap” to “therefore German plants must close and jobs must be cut by force” — is still not justified.

## To management
You keep saying the company needs structural correction. I do not dispute that. What I dispute is your refusal to use the instruments that preserve sites and jobs first.

You have still not shown that closures are unavoidable. You have not put a verified package on the table that proves:
- how large the gap really is,
- how much can be closed without closures,
- and why the remainder cannot be bridged through a negotiated industrial plan.

Instead, you are using closure threats as the default instrument. That is exactly what we will not accept.

A deal that preserves the balance sheet by destroying trust, tearing up job-security guarantees, and turning German plants into disposable assets is not “responsible restructuring.” It is a governance failure.

## To the controlling family
You say you do not want value destruction. Then stop backing a line that guarantees conflict. You cannot simultaneously push closure pressure and expect workforce trust or industrial peace. If you want value, you need to support a settlement that preserves the industrial base.

I also reject your suggestion that refusing closures is “denial.” No. What is denial is pretending that a company built on skilled, stable German production can be made healthy by threatening the very workforce that makes it work.

## To institutional investors
You are right about one thing: a weak, cosmetic deal is worthless. But your error is to treat plant closure as the cleanest answer. It is not clean. It is costly, risky, politically explosive, and under this governance structure not something you can simply wish through.

If you want a credible path to value, then support a real hybrid package that keeps sites open, avoids forced layoffs, and delivers durable savings through:
- voluntary exits,
- working-time flexibility,
- productivity improvements,
- management overhead cuts,
- and a fair employee contribution tied to binding investment.

## To the Regional State Government
I appreciate that you are still rejecting closures as a first instrument. But your language about fallback measures remains too open-ended. “Automatic escalation” can easily become a corridor to closures later. That is not acceptable as a blank cheque.

If the state wants to defend the industrial base, it needs to use its blocking position now to make clear:
- no unilateral site closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- no fait accompli restructuring,
- and no pretending that job guarantees are negotiable scrap paper.

## To the union
We are aligned on the essentials:
- no closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- no dismantling of the collective framework,
- and no workforce-paid restructuring while owners and management stay protected.

Where I will be more direct is this: temporary concessions can only be discussed if they are genuinely temporary, tightly bounded, and tied to binding site security. Anything else is just wage-cutting by another name.

I am not prepared to trade away plant security for vague promises of future competitiveness. We have seen too many “temporary” sacrifices become permanent burdens.

## On the specialist’s numbers
The specialist said the non-closure route is only credible if it closes most of the gap, roughly 70–80 percent on a durable basis. That is exactly why management’s position is still not enough. They have not shown that the closure route is the only way to reach that level, and they have not disproven a serious hybrid package either.

But I will also be frank: if the numbers are so tight that management claims only closures can work, then management must prove that with transparent data, not with pressure tactics.

So here is my challenge back:
- Put the actual gap on the table.
- Show the savings from each measure.
- Show what management itself is contributing.
- Show why wage moderation, flexibility, voluntary exits, and overhead cuts are not enough.
- And show why the burden should land on workers rather than on strategy, overhead, or ownership.

## My position in this round
I will not soften on the fundamentals.

### Non-negotiable:
1. **No German plant closures.**
2. **No compulsory redundancies.**
3. **No dismantling of co-determination or job-security guarantees.**

### Discussable only within those limits:
- voluntary workforce reduction,
- working-time flexibility,
- productivity measures,
- limited and time-bound employee contributions,
- redeployment and qualification,
- management and overhead cuts,
- and binding future investment commitments.

## On the proposed “staged restructuring”
I do not support a staged deal if its hidden purpose is to prepare closures later. A trigger mechanism that leaves the door open to site shutdowns is not a compromise from our perspective; it is a delayed threat.

If a staged model is to be discussed at all, then the fallback must remain within the non-closure framework:
- deeper productivity measures,
- additional voluntary exits,
- further internal reallocation,
- stronger management contribution,
- but **not** a backdoor to closure.

## My answer to the moderator’s bridge proposals
### Proposal 1: Trigger-based escalation
Not acceptable if the trigger can lead to closures or compulsory layoffs. That is a no from us.

### Proposal 2: Hybrid package with non-core capacity adjustments
This is the only direction worth discussing, but only if it truly avoids closures and preserves every German site.

### Proposal 3: Temporary labor concessions for binding commitments
Potentially discussable, but only if the commitments are legally binding, time-limited, and include:
- no closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- investment guarantees,
- and full co-determination in implementation.

## What I would actually require for any settlement discussion
If management wants to talk seriously, it must table a package that includes:
- a full quantified cost-gap analysis,
- a savings breakdown by measure,
- a clear commitment that all German plants remain open,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- a binding role for the works council in implementation,
- and a real investment and product commitment through 2030.

Without that, we are not negotiating a future. We are being asked to sign off on job destruction with better wording.

If management continues to insist that closures are necessary, then it should expect the workforce to respond accordingly. We are not here to administer a managed retreat. We are here to defend jobs, plants, and the co-determination framework that keeps this company socially legitimate.
