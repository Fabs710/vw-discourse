I will be equally clear.

We have now had enough rounds to distinguish between a **serious restructuring discussion** and a **closure-threat strategy dressed up as realism**. I accept the first. I reject the second.

## Where I agree

### With the specialists
The finance experts have confirmed what matters: a settlement must be **durable, quantified, and sufficient in run-rate terms through 2030**. I agree. A symbolic package or a one-year patch would be irresponsible.

I also accept the benchmark that a non-closure package must close **most** of the structural gap. That is a fair test. If the company has a real problem, then the solution has to be real too.

### With the state
I agree that a reckless move would damage legitimacy, industrial peace, and ultimately value. The state is right to insist that this company is not just a balance-sheet object; it is a major industrial anchor with social and regional responsibilities.

### With the works council
I agree on the core direction: workers should not carry all the pain, and any contribution must be matched by management and ownership. That is not ideology; that is basic fairness.

## Where I disagree

### On keeping closures as a fallback
I do **not** accept the idea that plant closures must remain available as a residual threat if the package underdelivers.

That is not a neutral safeguard. It is a bargaining weapon aimed at the workforce. It makes every “binding” commitment conditional on a future management judgment, while workers are asked to give real concessions now. That is not shared sacrifice; that is one-sided insecurity.

If a package is to be negotiated in good faith, then the outcome must not contain a hidden closure corridor. Otherwise, workers are being asked to finance a process that still leaves their sites and jobs hanging by a thread.

### On management’s framing
Management keeps repeating that closure is the cleanest or most credible option. I reject that framing.

A closure may be easier to announce in a spreadsheet, but it is not automatically the best industrial decision. It carries:
- severe conflict risk,
- execution risk,
- legal and co-determination barriers,
- production disruption,
- and long-term trust destruction.

So no, closure is not the default answer simply because it is the harshest one.

### To the controlling family and investors
You both keep asking for credibility. Fine — but credibility from the workforce side comes from **binding guarantees**, not from permanent threats.

If you want employees to make concessions, then you need to accept that those concessions must be exchanged for real security:
- no forced redundancies,
- no plant closures,
- no open-ended insecurity,
- no erosion of the collective wage framework.

Without that, the union cannot responsibly ask members to give more.

## What is actually on the table from our side
I am still prepared to discuss a **shared-sacrifice package**, but only if it is genuinely balanced and does not hide a closure option behind technical language.

That means we can discuss:
- temporary wage moderation,
- reduced bonuses and special payments,
- working-time flexibility,
- voluntary exits and retirement windows,
- redeployment,
- training and qualification,
- productivity improvements,
- and a fair employee contribution.

But only if this is matched by:
- management overhead cuts,
- owner contribution,
- binding investment commitments,
- and a firm, explicit no-closure and no-forced-layoff commitment.

## What I reject outright
1. **German plant closures**
2. **Compulsory redundancies**
3. **A fallback mechanism that can reopen closure pressure later**
4. **A deal where workers pay first and others contribute later or not at all**
5. **Any weakening of the collective wage framework as a condition of survival**

## On the remaining gap
Here is the central point: if management says the non-closure package still leaves a gap, then the answer is not automatically “close a plant.” The answer is to keep working the remaining gap through other means that are still within the socially acceptable framework:
- further productivity measures,
- stronger overhead cuts,
- more complexity reduction,
- voluntary exits,
- internal reallocation,
- management and owner contribution,
- and investment discipline.

If those measures are not enough, then management must explain why it expects workers to absorb the final shortfall alone. That explanation does not exist yet.

## To management
You still have not answered the basic question in a way that justifies your course:

**What non-closure measures have actually been exhausted, and why are they insufficient?**

Not what you prefer. Not what is cleaner for governance. What has been exhausted and proven inadequate.

Until I see that, I will continue to regard closures as an asserted threat, not a demonstrated necessity.

## To the state
You asked for quantified conditions under which a staged restructuring would be acceptable. My answer is simple: if a staged mechanism contains a closure fallback, it is not acceptable. The state should not help build a corridor to shutdowns in disguise.

If the state wants to protect the industrial base, it should support a settlement with:
- no closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- binding investment,
- and binding implementation control.

## To the specialist
I want the breakdown, but with one important filter: I am not asking whether closures would make the finance model prettier. I am asking whether the company can reach a durable solution **without** them.

So I want the plant-by-plant and function-by-function analysis of:
- voluntary exits,
- shift and working-time changes,
- overhead and management cuts,
- productivity gains,
- complexity reduction,
- and the remaining gap if closures and forced layoffs are excluded.

And I want the answer to be framed around **what is still feasible within a no-closure settlement**, not around how to reintroduce closure by the back door.

## My position, plainly

### Non-negotiable:
- No German plant closures.
- No compulsory redundancies.
- No erosion of the collective wage framework.
- No fallback mechanism that keeps closure pressure alive.

### Discussable:
- Time-limited wage restraint.
- Bonus restraint.
- Working-time flexibility.
- Voluntary exits.
- Redeployment and retraining.
- Productivity and complexity improvements.
- Management and owner contributions.
- Binding investment and product commitments through 2030.

## Final line for this round
We are prepared to contribute fairly to a durable settlement. We are **not** prepared to finance restructuring by accepting a permanent threat to plants and jobs.

If management and capital want a deal, they must stop treating closure as the default enforcement mechanism and come back with a genuinely binding no-closure package that closes the gap through shared sacrifice. If they cannot do that, then they should not expect the workforce to make the first and largest concession.
