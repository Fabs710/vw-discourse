From the capital-markets side, the discussion is now at the right level: **numbers, durability, and implementation risk**. That is where we need to be.

## Where I agree
I agree with management, the family shareholder, the state, and the specialist on one central point: **the company has a real structural competitiveness problem**. This is not a cosmetic issue, and a weak compromise would destroy value.

I also agree with the state and labor that **a prolonged strike or endless confrontation would be value-destructive**. The market will punish lost production, uncertainty, and execution failure. So I do not want a drawn-out industrial war.

And I agree with the specialist’s benchmark: a non-closure package is only credible if it closes **most** of the gap on a durable basis, not just a little bit of it.

## Where I disagree
### To the Works Council and Union
Your red lines are politically understandable, but from a shareholder perspective they are not enough by themselves.

You keep saying:
- no closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- preserve every German site.

That may be a legitimate objective, but it is **not yet a financial solution**. If those red lines remain, then you need to show — with hard numbers — how the company gets back to competitive margins. So far, I have not seen evidence that working-time changes, voluntary exits, redeployment, and productivity measures alone can close a structural gap of this size.

If the package preserves the footprint but leaves the cost base materially above target, then it is not a solution; it is delayed erosion.

### To management
I do **not** accept closure rhetoric as a substitute for a quantified plan.

You are right that a structural gap exists, but you still need to prove:
- the size of the gap,
- what can be closed without closures,
- what remains if closures are excluded,
- and why the remaining gap cannot be bridged through a hybrid package.

If management cannot show that closures are economically necessary, then it should stop presenting them as the obvious default. From the market’s point of view, a rushed closure-led strategy with heavy execution risk can be just as value-destructive as inaction.

### To the Regional State Government
I support your rejection of a reckless closure-first approach. That said, “no closures unless alternatives are exhausted” only works if the alternatives are real, quantified, and enforceable. If they are not sufficient, then blocking any structural move would simply preserve a weak earnings profile and delay the eventual problem.

### To the controlling family
I agree that the company cannot afford an uncompetitive structure. But I also agree with the state that closures should not be treated as a reflex. The family should not push for hard action without a transparent bridge from today’s cost base to competitive margins.

## What would move us
What would genuinely move capital markets is a package with all of the following:
- quantified annual savings by measure,
- a clear run-rate target,
- durable labor-cost improvement,
- measurable capacity reduction,
- management overhead discipline,
- a credible investment plan through 2030,
- and a trigger mechanism if savings underdeliver.

A staged or hybrid package is acceptable **only if it is not a delay tactic**. It must have:
- milestones,
- enforcement,
- and a fallback if the cost gap is not actually closed.

## My position
I will not support an outcome that leaves the company with:
- structurally uncompetitive costs,
- margins still below target,
- or underfunded future investment.

I will also not support a settlement that creates prolonged conflict and destroys cash flow.

So my support goes to **the most credible path to durable margin recovery**, not to any particular constituency’s preferred symbol.

That means:
- if a non-closure package truly closes most of the gap, I can support it;
- if it does not, then management needs to be honest that harder structural measures remain on the table;
- but I will not back a cosmetic settlement that simply delays the pain.

## What I need next
Before I would move further, I want the specialists and management to table a concise comparison of:
1. the annual gap,
2. savings from non-closure measures,
3. savings from hybrid measures,
4. savings from closure-led restructuring,
5. one-time cash costs,
6. and the impact on EBIT margin and free cash flow through 2030.

[SUMMON: industrial planning and operations specialist to quantify how much cost and capacity reduction can realistically be achieved without plant closures or compulsory redundancies, and what run-rate savings would remain if closures are excluded.]

Until that is on the table, I will not endorse a “no closures at any price” position, and I will not endorse a “closures first” position either. What I will endorse is the **most credible, durable, value-preserving restructuring**.
