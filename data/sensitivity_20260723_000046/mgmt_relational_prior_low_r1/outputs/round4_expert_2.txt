## Finance view: what is financially sufficient?

The expert benchmark is the right one:

- **A non-closure package is financially sufficient only if it closes most of the recurring structural gap on a durable basis.**
- For a large German automotive footprint, a practical threshold is **about 70–80% of the recurring gap** closed through durable measures.
- If the residual gap is still **material** after non-closure measures, then the package is likely **deferring** the restructuring problem rather than solving it.

That said, the key test is not the closure label itself. It is whether the package restores:

1. **EBIT/ROS margin**
2. **Free cash flow**
3. **Liquidity headroom**
4. **Investment capacity through 2030**

---

## Decision framework

A package is financially credible if it meets all three:

### 1) Gap coverage
It closes roughly **70–80%** of the recurring cost/capacity gap via durable actions:
- voluntary exits
- working-time/shift changes
- wage restraint / bonus moderation
- overhead reductions
- productivity and complexity improvements
- capacity reduction without full closures

### 2) Residual gap is absorbable
The remaining **20–30%** must be small enough to be covered by:
- normal cycle recovery
- mix improvement
- procurement savings
- further productivity gains

If not, the company remains structurally under-earning.

### 3) Cash-flow durability
The package must improve free cash flow quickly enough to fund:
- capex
- EV/software transition
- working capital
- and liquidity buffers

If savings are back-ended or one-off, the package is weaker.

---

## Side-by-side comparison

### A) No-closure package
**Typical levers**
- voluntary exits
- working-time flexibility
- wage moderation / bonus restraint
- overhead cuts
- productivity/complexity reductions

**Financial profile**
- **Annual recurring savings:** moderate to significant, but usually limited if no major footprint change
- **One-time costs:** lower than closures
- **Cash timing:** faster, if negotiated quickly
- **Risk:** may not eliminate enough fixed cost

**Verdict**
- Viable only if the gap is already modest or if labor-cost and productivity measures are unusually deep.

---

### B) Hybrid package
**Typical levers**
- capacity reduction
- line conversion / product reallocation
- partial site downsizing
- voluntary workforce reduction
- some overhead and shift optimization

**Financial profile**
- **Annual recurring savings:** usually higher than no-closure
- **One-time costs:** moderate
- **Cash timing:** medium
- **Risk:** depends on how much fixed cost is actually removed

**Verdict**
- Often the best economic compromise if it materially reduces fixed cost without full plant closures.

---

### C) Closure-led restructuring
**Typical levers**
- full plant closure
- deeper job cuts
- elimination of site fixed costs

**Financial profile**
- **Annual recurring savings:** highest
- **One-time costs:** highest
- **Cash timing:** slower near term because of severance, write-downs, transition costs
- **Risk:** execution, industrial conflict, disruption

**Verdict**
- Financially justified when the residual gap after non-closure measures remains too large to absorb.

---

## Practical threshold for sufficiency

My judgment, as a restructuring finance baseline:

- **If non-closure measures close ~70–80% of the recurring gap**, the package can be sufficient.
- **If they close materially less than that**, it is likely incomplete.
- **If the remaining gap is still in the hundreds of millions or low billions of euros annually**, closures or equivalent capacity exits become difficult to avoid on financial grounds.

---

## What matters more than the label “closure” or “no closure”

The economically relevant question is:

> Does the package remove enough **fixed cost** and **structural overcapacity** to restore target margins and free cash flow through 2030?

That can happen through:
- full closures,
- hybrid downsizing,
- or, in rare cases, a very deep no-closure package.

But if fixed costs remain largely intact, the company likely preserves the problem.

---

## One-time cost versus recurring savings

A closure-led plan may maximize recurring savings, but it also brings:
- severance and social plan costs
- write-downs
- tooling and relocation costs
- transition disruption
- supply-chain friction

So the correct comparison is:

**Recurring annual savings – one-time restructuring cost – transition friction**

A no-closure package may look weaker on savings, but can be stronger on near-term cash flow and execution certainty.

---

## Bottom line

- **Financially sufficient non-closure threshold:** roughly **70–80% of the recurring gap** closed durably.
- **Hybrid package:** often the most practical solution if it truly removes fixed cost.
- **Closure-led restructuring:** justified when the residual gap remains too large after all non-closure measures.
