I will respond directly, because the gap between the sides is still very real.

First, where I agree:
- The company does need a durable competitiveness solution.
- A cosmetic deal that only postpones the crisis is not acceptable.
- Investment in future products and technologies has to be protected.
- A negotiated settlement is far better than a prolonged industrial conflict.

But I need to be equally clear on where I do **not** agree:
- **No German plant closures.**
- **No compulsory redundancies.**
- **No restructuring where the workforce carries the main burden while management, owners, and investors escape meaningful contribution.**
- **No dismantling of the collective wage framework.**

The Works Council is right to insist that closures and forced layoffs would destroy trust and make any settlement unstable. Management keeps saying that closures are “necessary” because overcapacity exists. That is an assertion, not a conclusion. It has not yet been shown that the same savings cannot be achieved through a combination of:
- capacity reductions short of closure,
- working-time adjustments,
- voluntary exits and retirement windows,
- redeployment and qualification,
- productivity gains,
- and a negotiated employee contribution.

That is the route we remain prepared to pursue.

To management: if you want a serious labour-side contribution, then stop demanding blank-cheque concessions. Put forward the actual numbers and show the burden-sharing. So far, the signal from management has been: workers should accept permanent insecurity so the balance sheet looks cleaner. That is not a fair settlement, and it will not hold.

To the controlling family and investors: you are right that value destruction through drift or prolonged conflict is real. But value destruction also comes from forcing through a restructuring that tears up the social and industrial compact that made this company strong. If you want a durable solution, then you need to accept that the owners and leadership must also contribute, not just demand sacrifices downstream.

To the Regional State Government: I appreciate the clear rejection of closures as a first instrument and your insistence on a negotiated path. But I want to be very explicit: “no closures unless alternatives are exhausted” is too loose if it becomes a path to closure later. We need a stronger political and governance commitment that the German sites are preserved through a real transformation package, not kept on a conditional knife-edge.

So here is the union position in this round:

### Non-negotiable red lines
1. No plant closures in Germany.
2. No compulsory redundancies.
3. No dismantling of the collective wage agreement.
4. No open-ended insecurity imposed on workers.

### What we are prepared to discuss
- Temporary or limited wage moderation, **but only in a balanced package**.
- Reduced bonuses and special payments.
- Working-time flexibility.
- Voluntary workforce reduction measures.
- Redeployment, training, and qualification.
- Capacity adjustments short of closure.
- A negotiated employee contribution tied to binding site and investment commitments.

### What would make a deal possible
A package must include all of the following:
- binding site security,
- no forced layoffs,
- fair burden-sharing from management and ownership,
- credible investment commitments,
- and a measurable path to restoring competitiveness without sacrificing the workforce as the default adjustment variable.

If management wants something economically equivalent to closures, then it needs to prove equivalence in savings without destroying jobs and sites. That is the standard. If it cannot do that, then it is not entitled to call closures the only “responsible” option.

And let me say this plainly: if the answer remains “accept closures or accept chaos,” then the conflict will intensify. We have already shown that the workforce is organized, and we will not hesitate to escalate again if necessary. We are still willing to negotiate. But we will not be pushed into agreeing to a restructuring that violates our core mandate.
