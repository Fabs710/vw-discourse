Management, let me respond directly: you are still treating the workforce as the adjustment variable for strategic mistakes that were made elsewhere. I do not accept that framing.

You say the company needs a durable cost reduction. We agree that the company needs a future. But your conclusion — closures, forced job cuts, and dismantling guarantees — is exactly the line we reject. A “durable solution” that destroys trust, removes skilled capacity, and blows up the co-determination settlement is not durable; it is a conflict trigger with a short shelf life.

To the other capital-side stakeholders:
- **Yes**, the company must regain competitiveness.
- **No**, that does not automatically justify plant closures in Germany.
- **No**, it does not justify compulsory redundancies.
- **And no**, it does not justify turning long-standing job-security commitments into disposable paper.

You all keep saying we must offer an economically equivalent alternative. That is the right question, but the answer is not “accept closures because management prefers them.” The answer is a negotiated industrial plan that uses every instrument short of closure and forced layoffs:
- capacity reductions through internal redistribution,
- shorter working time and flexibility arrangements,
- voluntary exit and retirement pathways,
- qualification and redeployment,
- productivity improvements,
- temporary and limited employee contributions,
- and binding future product and investment commitments.

That is the framework we are prepared to work on. But it must be matched by **binding guarantees**:
- all German sites remain open,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- protection of co-determination,
- and a credible horizon to 2030.

Without that, there is no trust, and without trust there is no settlement.

To the Regional State Government: I appreciate that you are rejecting a unilateral closure course. But I am not satisfied with “unless alternatives are exhausted” language if it becomes a back door to plant closures later. We need a **clear commitment now** that the company will not use closures as a restructuring shortcut. The state should use its blocking position to defend the industrial base, not to create ambiguity.

To the controlling family and investors: you are right about one thing — prolonged conflict destroys value. Precisely. That is why management should stop escalating with threats and come back with a package that the workforce can actually support. If you want stability, then you must stop demanding the one thing that makes stability impossible: plant closures and compulsory redundancies.

Let me be equally clear about what we are **not** prepared to trade away:
- We will not barter away a plant for a temporary financial gain.
- We will not accept forced redundancies dressed up as “socially managed reduction.”
- We will not accept open-ended “competitiveness” arguments that lock in permanent insecurity for workers while the company keeps strategic optionality for everyone else.

If management is serious, then put numbers on the table:
- What exactly is the cost gap?
- How much can be closed through working-time and productivity measures?
- How much through voluntary measures?
- What investment is committed in return?
- And what legally binding protection is offered for each site?

Until that is done, your case for closures is just an assertion.

So my position in this round is simple:
1. **No German plant closures.**
2. **No compulsory redundancies.**
3. **No dismantling of job-security guarantees as the price of restructuring.**
4. **Yes to a negotiated transformation package** if — and only if — it protects every site, protects jobs, and gives the workforce a real role in shaping the future.

If management keeps pushing a closure-led plan, it should expect organized resistance, not compliance.
