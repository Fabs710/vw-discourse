I will be blunt and keep this at the governance level.

I agree with management on one central point: the current cost and capacity structure is not sustainable if it leaves the company below target margins and unable to invest through the cycle. If the business remains structurally uncompetitive, then value erodes for everyone — workers, the region, and the owners. I am not prepared to defend a status quo that slowly destroys the franchise.

That said, I do **not** support a simplistic “close plants and cut jobs” approach as the default answer. A restructuring that permanently damages industrial capability, triggers prolonged conflict, or weakens the company’s long-term reputation would also be value-destructive. In a company with this governance structure, any solution has to be durable, not just aggressive.

So my position is:

- I **support** a serious competitiveness program.
- I **support** meaningful structural cost reduction.
- I **support** a solution that preserves the company’s ability to invest in future products.
- I **do not support** a path that creates unnecessary instability or treats plant closures as the first instrument rather than the last resort.

To the Works Council and the Union: I understand the red lines you are drawing, and I do not dismiss them lightly. But saying “no closures ever” and “no compulsory redundancies” without offering an economically credible equivalent is not enough. If the alternative package does not close the cost gap in a durable way, then it is not a solution; it is a delay. The owners will not accept permanent value erosion, and neither should the workforce if it expects a viable future.

To management: you have made the case for urgency, and I take that seriously. But you will not get a blank cheque for permanent social disruption. If you want governance support for a tough settlement, then it must be demonstrably better than the status quo and better than a protracted conflict. That means:
- a credible, quantified cost-down plan,
- clear evidence that the burden is shared,
- a realistic path to implementation,
- and protection of the company’s long-term industrial base.

To the Regional State Government: I appreciate the emphasis on regional stability and the special governance constraints. Those constraints matter. But they cannot become a veto on necessary adaptation if no workable alternative is brought forward. Blocking change without offering an equivalent economic path only postpones the problem and increases the eventual cost.

My bottom line as controlling shareholder is this:

1. I will not support a restructuring that permanently impairs enterprise value.
2. I will not support a prolonged conflict that weakens the company and the brand.
3. I will support a tough but balanced package if it genuinely restores competitiveness and is durable in operational terms.

So the question for the next round is not whether change is needed. It is whether the employee side can present a package that closes the structural gap without forcing management to choose between competitiveness and chaos. If not, then the pressure for deeper structural measures will remain, and I will not shield the company from necessary decisions simply because they are uncomfortable.
