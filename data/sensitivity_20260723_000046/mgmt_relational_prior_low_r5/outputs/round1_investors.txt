From the capital markets perspective, the discussion so far has one clear positive: **everyone seems to accept that inaction is not an option**. On that point, I agree.

But I need to be equally direct: **I will not back a settlement that preserves a structurally uncompetitive cost base while dressing it up as “socially responsible restructuring.”** If the result is merely a cosmetic compromise that leaves margins impaired, the equity story remains broken and capital will continue to re-rate the business down.

### Where I agree
I agree with management, and partly with the family holding, that:
- the German cost base is too high,
- overcapacity must be reduced,
- and the company needs a durable path back to competitive returns.

I also agree that:
- voluntary instruments should come first where they can deliver real savings,
- training and redeployment matter,
- and all sides should contribute, including management overhead and top-layer costs.

### Where I disagree
I do **not** share the view that the solution must be found only in a way that avoids any plant closure, any compulsory redundancy, or any material change to legacy protections.

If those lines are absolute, then the result is very likely:
- insufficient savings,
- continued margin pressure,
- delayed restructuring,
- and another round of conflict later.

That is value-destructive. As shareholders, we can tolerate some friction; we cannot tolerate a settlement that simply defers the adjustment and keeps returns below the cost of capital.

### To the Works Council and Union
I respect the social logic of your position, but the financial logic is incomplete if it ignores competitiveness. A firm can preserve every site in the short term and still destroy shareholder value through persistent underperformance.

So I would say this plainly:
- **No, I cannot support a package that rules out closures in advance if the economics do not add up.**
- **No, I cannot support a package that protects every job regardless of productivity and demand.**

If you want investor support, you need to show that the alternative package is not just gentler, but **equally effective** in reducing the cost base and restoring margins. “Shared sacrifice” is not enough unless the numbers work.

### To management
You are right to insist on durability. But durability is not the same as maximal confrontation. If the company pushes too hard for a blunt settlement and triggers prolonged strikes, litigation, and governance deadlock, that also destroys value.

So management needs to present:
- a **credible quantified savings target**,
- a **clear timeline**,
- and a **package that the governance structure can actually pass**.

If the plan depends on a theoretical right to close plants but cannot secure the necessary majority, then it is not credible. Capital markets will discount it.

### To the Regional State Government
I understand the political constraints, but I would caution against using the state’s blocking position to freeze an uncompetitive structure. That may protect jobs temporarily, but it risks a weaker company, weaker future investment, and eventually a worse outcome for the region and the shareholders.

If the state wants to be constructive, it should focus on:
- securing a durable industrial plan,
- supporting productivity improvement,
- and accepting some structural adjustment where it is economically necessary.

### To the Family Holding
I appreciate the emphasis on value discipline. But value discipline cuts both ways: you do not protect long-term enterprise value by preserving short-term industrial peace at the expense of margins. The market will judge this package by whether it creates a credible earnings bridge back to acceptable returns.

### Our bottom line
We would support a restructuring only if it credibly delivers:
1. **durable structural cost reduction,**
2. **measurable capacity adjustment,**
3. **a clear return to competitive margins,**
4. **deal certainty with limited risk of prolonged disruption.**

What we will not support:
- a cosmetic compromise,
- an outcome that leaves the cost base uncompetitive,
- or a settlement that buys peace today by creating a bigger profitability problem tomorrow.

If there is a negotiated package that achieves the economics through a mix of labour contributions, capacity reductions, and management cost action, we are open. But if the core response is “no closures, no real concessions, preserve everything,” then from an investor standpoint that is not a solution — it is capital destruction.
