We agree on the diagnosis: **the company needs a durable restructuring, not a cosmetic compromise**. That much is clear.

Where we differ is on the instrument. From a shareholder perspective, I am **not attached to closures as ideology**, but I also will not support a package that leaves the cost base structurally uncompetitive.

## To management
I agree that the current structure likely has excess fixed cost and that a real 2030 plan must produce **permanent** savings. I also agree that management and overhead must share the burden.

What I do **not** accept is pushing closures as the first serious answer without a hard comparison against the alternatives. If you want capital-market support, show us the numbers:
- site-by-site economics,
- annual savings,
- permanent capacity removed,
- implementation timing,
- conflict/disruption risk,
- and the margin path to 2030.

If the no-closure route cannot close the gap, then say so plainly. But until that is demonstrated, I will not treat closures as the default benchmark of seriousness.

## To the works council and union
I respect the refusal to let workers carry the full burden. A one-sided settlement would be value-destructive because it would trigger conflict, delay, and execution risk.

But your position is still too absolute for me:
- “every German plant stays open”
- “no compulsory redundancies”
- “binding guarantees through 2030”

Those are only acceptable if they come with **equivalent, durable structural savings**. Otherwise, they preserve overcapacity and simply postpone the problem. That is not a solution shareholders can back.

I am open to:
- voluntary exits,
- redeployment,
- working-time flexibility,
- temporary wage/bonus moderation,
- qualification,
- and management/owner contribution.

But I will not support a deal that protects every legacy structure while margins remain structurally impaired.

## To the regional state government
I agree that legitimacy and regional stability matter. A strike-heavy, socially explosive solution can destroy value.

But I do not accept using veto power to freeze the footprint regardless of economics. If the state wants to block closures, it should help produce a package that still achieves:
- real capacity reduction,
- durable cost reduction,
- and a credible return to competitive margins.

## To the controlling family holding
I agree with the insistence on long-term enterprise value and discipline. I also agree that a bad closure program can destroy value if it triggers prolonged conflict.

Where I disagree is any assumption that the burden of proof is only on management to justify hard measures. The burden is also on the employee side to show that a no-closure package can deliver the same structural effect. So far, I have not seen that proof.

## My position
The acceptable outcome is one that:
1. restores competitive margins,
2. permanently reduces fixed costs and overcapacity,
3. limits industrial conflict,
4. and is credible enough to hold through 2030.

That means I can support:
- a strong no-closure package **if** it is truly equivalent in savings and durability,
- or a mixed package if it is the best balance of economics and execution risk.

What I will not support:
- a cosmetic compromise,
- a deal that protects every site while leaving the company expensive,
- or a settlement that keeps structural margin impairment in place.
