Below is a neutral industrial and workforce impact assessment of the **three restructuring archetypes** requested. I will stay within audit scope: plant-level operations, employment, capacity, logistics, implementation risk, supplier effects, and transition durability. I cannot responsibly judge political legitimacy or bargaining strategy.

## 1) Bottom-line assessment

From an industrial-audit perspective, the three packages differ mainly in **how fast they remove fixed cost and excess capacity** and in **how much operational disruption they create**.

- A **no-closure / no-forced-layoff package** can reduce cost, but only if the company can obtain:
  - very high voluntary exit volumes,
  - meaningful working-time and pay moderation,
  - strong overhead cuts,
  - product/volume reallocations,
  - and sustained management contribution.
  It is usually the **lowest social destruction** option, but also the **most execution-sensitive** and often the weakest on permanent fixed-cost removal.

- A **limited-closure package** is the clearest route to durable structural capacity reduction. It usually gives the **strongest fixed-cost relief per euro saved**, but it also has the highest local employment loss, supplier damage, and conflict risk.

- A **mixed package** is often the most realistic industrial compromise if the company needs both:
  - a durable cost reset, and
  - avoidance of the largest social and political shock.
  In practice, this means combining one or two site reductions or footprint changes with major voluntary exits, shift reductions, product reassignment, and labor-cost concessions elsewhere.

## 2) What can be said with confidence, and what cannot

### High-confidence facts in industrial terms
- If the company has **structural overcapacity**, then savings must come from one or more of:
  - lower headcount,
  - lower shifts/utilization,
  - lower overhead,
  - lower labor cost per vehicle,
  - and/or relocated production.
- **Permanent fixed-cost reduction** is generally more reliable when it includes:
  - site consolidation,
  - shutdown of redundant lines,
  - or elimination of a complete production footprint.
- A purely voluntary package can work only if there is enough:
  - attrition,
  - early retirement eligibility,
  - redeployment capacity,
  - and available demand at remaining sites.

### Uncertainties I cannot resolve from the record
- Exact plant-by-plant cost curves.
- Exact capacity utilization by site.
- Which models are assigned to which plants in the baseline.
- Voluntary exit uptake and timing.
- True cost of strikes, supply disruption, and lost output.

So any site ranking beyond the general industrial logic would be speculative unless you provide plant data.

## 3) Comparative industrial effects

### A. No-closure / no-forced-layoff package

#### Typical instruments
- voluntary severance/early retirement,
- working-time reductions,
- wage moderation or bonus restraint,
- internal redeployment,
- shift reductions,
- temporary line stoppages,
- overhead cuts,
- supplier and purchasing savings,
- management contribution.

#### Industrial impact
- **Fixed-cost savings:** moderate unless voluntary exits are very large.
- **Capacity reduction:** indirect; mostly through fewer shifts, lower utilization, and product migration rather than site removal.
- **Headcount effect:** can be significant, but usually slower and less certain than closure-based reduction.
- **Durability:** weaker unless accompanied by hard product allocation and permanent overhead restructuring.

#### Main risks
- Savings may be too small relative to the overcapacity problem.
- Redeployment capacity can saturate quickly.
- Voluntary exit programs often bring the wrong mix of departures: more cost than volume can be lost.
- If the demand environment worsens, the company may face a second restructuring later.

#### Worker and site impact
- Lowest immediate displacement.
- Higher chance of preserving site identity and supplier continuity.
- But if it underdelivers, it can create a delayed crisis and more uncertainty.

### B. Limited-closure package

#### Typical instruments
- closing one plant or one unit,
- shutting one redundant production line,
- concentrating volume into fewer sites,
- voluntary exits plus compulsory measures if needed,
- stronger overhead and procurement cuts.

#### Industrial impact
- **Fixed-cost savings:** highest and most immediate.
- **Capacity reduction:** direct and measurable.
- **Headcount effect:** large at the affected site, plus some indirect reduction elsewhere.
- **Durability:** strongest if the closed footprint is truly redundant and volume is transferred without bottlenecks.

#### Main risks
- Loss of know-how and ramp-up friction at receiving sites.
- Logistics strain if transferred volume travels farther or through constrained corridors.
- Supplier disruption, especially for locally embedded suppliers.
- Industrial conflict risk is highest here.

#### Worker and site impact
- Severe local job loss.
- Strong impact on regional employment and subcontractors.
- Potential morale effects across the network if workers fear a precedent.

### C. Mixed package

#### Typical instruments
- one limited closure or partial footprint reduction,
- strong voluntary exits elsewhere,
- reduced shifts,
- product migration,
- labor-cost concessions,
- management/owner contribution,
- targeted investment commitments.

#### Industrial impact
- **Fixed-cost savings:** usually intermediate to high.
- **Capacity reduction:** substantial, but less abrupt than full closure-heavy restructuring.
- **Headcount effect:** spread across more sites, reducing shock concentration.
- **Durability:** better than a pure voluntary plan, less disruptive than a broad closure program.

#### Main risks
- Complexity of execution.
- The package can become too diluted if the closure element is too small and the other concessions are too modest.
- Requires careful sequencing to avoid losing volume before receiving sites are ready.

#### Worker and site impact
- Mixed local outcomes.
- Some sites protected, some materially downsized.
- Often the most plausible path to preserve a core industrial base while still achieving meaningful capacity correction.

## 4) Site-by-site impact logic

Without named plant data, I can only state the **site types** that matter:

### Sites most at risk in any restructuring
- plants with **low utilization**,
- plants with **single-model dependence** and weak future allocation,
- plants with **high logistics cost** or poor network integration,
- sites with **aging fixed assets** requiring high reinvestment,
- plants whose output can be absorbed elsewhere with manageable transport cost.

### Sites more resilient
- plants with:
  - multi-model flexibility,
  - EV or future-platform allocation,
  - strong logistics access,
  - lower unit labor cost,
  - and recent capital upgrades.

### Audit rule
A plant should be considered a realistic closure or major downsizing candidate only if it meets several of these:
1. persistent underutilization,
2. no credible future product,
3. higher-than-network cost per unit,
4. high capex required to stay competitive,
5. feasible transfer of output to other sites.

If a site has a future platform, strong logistics, and strategic model importance, closure is normally a **last-resort** industrial decision, not an obvious first choice.

## 5) Workforce consequences by package

### No-closure / no-forced-layoff
- Best for employment continuity.
- Requires large voluntary mobility and retraining.
- Risk of hidden pressure: if volumes keep falling, people may be retained in name but underutilized in practice.
- Can preserve skill base if training is tied to real model allocation.

### Limited-closure
- Highest displacement at the affected site.
- Needs robust redeployment and qualification if social damage is to be contained.
- Risk of “hollowing out” if closing a plant removes critical know-how that is not transferred in time.

### Mixed
- More balanced workforce adjustment.
- More manageable for redeployment planning.
- Usually the most feasible for preserving a broader skills base while still reducing overall headcount.

## 6) Supplier and logistics effects

### No-closure / no-forced-layoff
- Lowest supplier shock.
- Preserves current inbound logistics patterns.
- But if only labor cost is adjusted while product structure stays unchanged, the supply base may still face uncertainty.

### Limited-closure
- Highest local supplier damage.
- Transport distances can increase if production is consolidated.
- One-time logistics redesign may create temporary inefficiency and startup losses at receiving plants.

### Mixed
- Moderate supplier disruption.
- Allows phased migration and more controlled logistics redesign.
- Best chance of keeping critical suppliers engaged if transition timing is clear.

## 7) Implementation time

### No-closure / no-forced-layoff
- Fast to announce, slow to realize.
- Savings often spread over multiple years.
- Highly dependent on negotiation and take-up rates.

### Limited-closure
- Long lead time if industrial shutdown is orderly, because you need:
  - customer transfer,
  - tooling relocation,
  - supply-chain revalidation,
  - and workforce transition.
- But once executed, the cost base changes permanently.

### Mixed
- Typically the longest to design, but often the most workable.
- Phased implementation is essential.

## 8) Long-term site viability

From an industrial audit standpoint, “site viability” depends on whether the plant can survive with:
- competitive unit labor cost,
- credible future products,
- sufficient utilization,
- and manageable fixed overhead.

### No-closure package
- Preserves viability only if it includes a real product and volume plan.
- If it relies mainly on temporary labor concessions, site viability remains fragile.

### Limited-closure package
- Concentrates volume into fewer, stronger sites.
- Viable for remaining plants if execution is disciplined.
- Weakens the closed region permanently.

### Mixed package
- Best balance for network resilience if the company can preserve a core German production base while removing excess capacity.

## 9) Practical conclusion

If the objective is **maximum social continuity**, the no-closure package is best — but it is the riskiest on whether the company truly fixes structural overcapacity.

If the objective is **maximum permanent cost correction**, limited closures are most effective — but they carry the greatest workforce, supplier, and conflict costs.

If the objective is **durable restructuring with less total value destruction than a pure closure strategy**, the mixed package is usually the strongest industrial option.
