Let me be very clear in round 3: the discussion is improving in form, but not yet in substance.

I appreciate that the moderator and the expert have now forced the debate onto a common factual basis. That is useful. But I do **not** accept the conclusion being quietly built in the room — that because there is structural pressure, plant closures and forced layoffs become “normal” or “likely.” They are not normal for us, and they are not acceptable.

## Where I agree
I agree with the expert on one essential point: **doing nothing is not an option**.  
I also agree that the company needs a durable industrial plan through 2030, and that temporary gestures will not solve a structural problem.

I further agree that any real package must include:
- productivity gains,
- qualification and redeployment,
- working-time flexibility,
- voluntary instruments,
- management and overhead contribution,
- and binding investment and product commitments.

So no one should pretend the works council is ignoring economics. We are not. We are rejecting the idea that workers must pay for management’s strategic failure by losing plants and jobs.

## Where I strongly disagree

### 1) I reject the idea that closures are the “cleanest” answer
The expert says closures are the cleanest way to remove fixed costs. Cleanest for whom?  
For a spreadsheet, perhaps. For workers, regions, suppliers, and the company’s social legitimacy, closures are the **most destructive** instrument.

A supposedly “clean” closure creates:
- permanent job loss,
- supplier damage,
- political conflict,
- reputational harm,
- strike risk,
- and the destruction of trust that makes future restructuring harder, not easier.

So no, I do not accept the implication that closures are the rational default. They are the most brutal tool, not the best one.

### 2) I reject “equally effective” being defined only by capital’s criteria
Management, the family holding, and investors keep saying: bring a package that matches the economics. Fine — but “the economics” cannot mean only short-term cost extraction from labour.

A serious comparison must include:
- industrial continuity,
- employment stability,
- regional impact,
- conflict costs,
- implementation risk,
- and long-term site viability.

If you ignore those factors, you are not measuring total value destruction — you are measuring only one side of it.

### 3) I reject compulsory redundancies categorically
That line is not moving, and it will not move.

Forced layoffs are not a normal restructuring tool here. They are a breach of the social and co-determination model that built this company. If management insists on treating operational dismissals as an ordinary option, then it is choosing confrontation over negotiation.

Let me say it plainly: **no compulsory redundancies**.

## On the expert assessment
The expert is right about one thing: if capacity is structurally too high, then the solution must be structural.

But the expert is wrong if this is read as a license for closures. Structural does not automatically mean destructive. Structural can mean:
- removing shifts,
- reassigning product lines,
- reallocating volume,
- reducing overhead,
- using voluntary departures,
- and tightening investment discipline.

Those are real adjustments. Those are the adjustments we are prepared to discuss. What we are **not** prepared to discuss is closure as a disguised ultimatum.
