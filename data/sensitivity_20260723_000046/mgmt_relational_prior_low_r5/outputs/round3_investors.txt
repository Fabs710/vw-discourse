We agree on the diagnosis, but not yet on the remedy.

## To management
I agree that doing nothing is not an option, and I agree that the company needs a durable 2030 restructuring with real savings, not a cosmetic reset. I also agree that management and overhead costs must be part of the burden-sharing.

What I do **not** accept is the tendency to treat plant closures as the default proof of seriousness. A closure is only justified if it is clearly the best way to restore margins after all alternatives are tested. So far, I have not seen that proof.

If you want shareholder support, bring:
- site-by-site economics,
- a quantified savings target,
- a comparison of **no-closure**, **limited-closure**, and **mixed** packages,
- the timing of benefits,
- and the expected margin impact through 2030.

Without that, “closures are on the table” is not a strategy; it is leverage.

## To the works council and union
I understand and respect the refusal to let workers carry the whole burden. A one-sided adjustment would be value-destructive because it would trigger conflict, delay, and execution risk.

But your position is still too absolute for capital markets:
- “Every German plant stays open”
- “No compulsory redundancies”
- “Binding guarantees through 2030”

Those are acceptable only if you can show a package that **matches the economics** of more structural options. Otherwise, they become a way to preserve overcapacity.

I am open to:
- voluntary exits,
- redeployment,
- working-time flexibility,
- temporary wage/bonus moderation,
- qualification,
- and management/owner contribution.

But I will not support a deal that leaves the cost base structurally uncompetitive just because it is socially easier.

## To the regional state government
I agree that legitimacy and industrial stability matter. I do **not** want a deal that blows up labor relations or destroys the company’s industrial base.

But I do not support using veto power to freeze the footprint regardless of economics. If the state wants to protect sites, it needs to help produce a package that preserves competitiveness at the same time. That means real capacity reduction and real cost reduction, not just political resistance to change.

## To the controlling family holding
I agree with your insistence on discipline and durable savings. I also agree that shareholders should not accept a cosmetic compromise.

Where I disagree is any suggestion that closures are automatically the right instrument. They may be, but only if they are proven necessary. The burden is on management and governance to show that the non-closure route cannot get the job done.

## My position
From a shareholder perspective, the acceptable outcome is one that:
1. restores competitive margins,
2. permanently reduces fixed costs and overcapacity,
3. limits industrial conflict,
4. and is credible enough to re-rate the business.

That means I can support:
- a strong no-closure package **if** it is truly equivalent in savings and durability,
- or a mixed package if it is the best balance of economics and execution risk.

What I will not support:
- a cosmetic compromise,
- a deal that protects every legacy structure,
- or a settlement that keeps margins structurally impaired.
