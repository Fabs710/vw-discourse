Yes. Based on the scenario, the key issue is not whether restructuring is needed — all parties now accept that it is — but whether a **no-closure / no-forced-layoff package** can plausibly deliver the same **durable cost and capacity correction** as a package that includes plant closures or deeper structural reductions.

## My expert assessment

### 1) The economic problem is real
For a large German automotive plant network, the combination of:
- declining European volumes,
- EV transition costs,
- elevated German labour and energy costs,
- high fixed manufacturing overhead,
- and excess installed capacity

usually creates a **fixed-cost absorption problem**, not just a wage problem.

That matters because:
- **variable cost measures** like temporary wage moderation or bonus restraint help,
- but **structural overcapacity** is mainly a fixed-cost issue.

If demand is permanently below the network’s designed capacity, then savings from:
- wage moderation,
- working-time flexibility,
- voluntary exits,
- and management cuts

may be **material** but often are **not sufficient on their own** unless they are very large and accompanied by genuine volume consolidation.

### 2) The decisive question is capacity utilization
In automotive manufacturing, the big swing factor is usually **plant loading**.

A rough rule of thumb:
- if a plant network is operating near or above **80–85% utilization**, it can often be stabilized with productivity measures and some labor-cost correction;
- if it is materially below that, fixed costs per vehicle rise sharply and the network becomes structurally uncompetitive.

In the situation described, management’s argument that there is **structural overcapacity** is economically credible. That does not automatically prove closures are the only answer, but it does mean any alternative must show:
- enough capacity removal,
- enough headcount reduction,
- and enough fixed-cost elimination.

### 3) No-closure packages can work only if they are genuinely structural
A no-closure package is not impossible, but it must do more than “save jobs.” It must remove cost permanently.

For example, a credible non-closure package would need some combination of:
- significant **volume reallocation** away from weak sites,
- conversion of some sites to lower-overhead functions,
- permanent elimination of shifts or lines,
- large-scale voluntary exits and redeployment,
- long-duration labor cost concessions,
- management and overhead reductions,
- and probably **product allocation commitments** to keep the remaining sites efficiently loaded.

What would **not** be enough:
- temporary wage freezes,
- one-off bonuses forgone for a year or two,
- vague promises of productivity,
- or soft capacity “adjustments” that leave the same plant footprint in place.

### 4) Closures are not the only way to cut capacity, but they are the cleanest way
From a pure manufacturing economics standpoint:
- closing a plant is the fastest way to remove fixed overhead,
- eliminate duplicate support functions,
- and simplify the network.

Alternative mechanisms can approximate the result, but usually with:
- more complexity,
- more governance risk,
- slower savings realization,
- and weaker certainty that the cost base really comes down.

So the economic hierarchy is usually:

1. **Plant closure / line shutdown**
2. **Site conversion plus permanent capacity reduction**
3. **Shift elimination / footprint reduction**
4. **Voluntary attrition / redeployment**
5. **Temporary labor concessions**

The lower you go on that list, the harder it is to achieve the same durable savings.

## What a financially credible package must show

The investor request is the right one: compare at least two packages on the same basis.

### Package A: No closures
This package would need to show:
- how many units of capacity are permanently removed,
- how many jobs are eliminated through voluntary means,
- how much annual fixed-cost saving results,
- what labor cost reduction is locked in through 2030,
- and whether margins recover to target.

### Package B: Limited closures / deeper structural cuts
This package would show:
- which plants or lines are closed,
- how much overhead is removed,
- how much capex burden is avoided,
- and how quickly profitability improves.

## Indicative economics: what usually drives the answer
I cannot validate exact numbers without site-level data, but in a German OEM network the major cost components are typically:

- **direct labour**
- **site overhead and maintenance**
- **logistics**
- **utilities / energy**
- **depreciation / capital employed**
- **engineering and support functions**
- **pension / restructuring charges**

For restructuring comparison, the most important metrics are:

1. **Annual fixed-cost savings**
2. **Per-vehicle fixed-cost reduction**
3. **Capacity removed**
4. **Headcount reduced**
5. **Time to cash benefit**
6. **Restructuring charge / payback period**

A package can look socially attractive but still fail if:
- the annual savings are too small,
- the payback is too long,
- or the remaining network is still underloaded.

## My judgment on the positions in the debate

### Management
Management is economically justified in saying that:
- the current structure is likely too costly,
- and capacity reduction is needed.

But it still has to prove, site by site, that closures are necessary rather than preferable.

### Works council and union
Their insistence on:
- no plant closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- and binding guarantees

is understandable, but from a restructuring standpoint it is only viable if they can produce a **quantified alternative** that closes the cost gap. Otherwise, it is a bargaining position, not an economic solution.

### Family holding and investors
They are right to demand:
- durable savings,
- structural correction,
- and a demonstrably competitive cost base.

But they should not assume that a closure-heavy package is automatically superior unless the operating model and industrial relations risk have been fully weighed.

### State government
Its role can slow or block change, but it cannot change the economics. If it insists on no closures, it should also be prepared to support a package that delivers equivalent structural savings.

## Bottom line
### My expert conclusion:
- **Doing nothing is not viable.**
- **A meaningful reduction in German capacity is likely required.**
- **A no-closure package is possible only if it produces equivalent fixed-cost and capacity reduction.**
- **If the no-closure package relies mainly on temporary labor concessions, it will probably be insufficient.**
- **Closures are not the only instrument, but they are often the most reliable one for restoring competitiveness when utilization is structurally too low.**
