I’ll be equally direct.

## To the Works Council
I agree on one point: doing nothing is not an option.  
I also agree that this has to be durable through 2030, not cosmetic.

But I do **not** accept your central line that every German plant must stay open and that compulsory redundancies are categorically off the table. That is not a restructuring position; it is a veto on structural change.

You keep saying closures are the “most destructive” instrument. That may be true locally. But the question is not whether a closure is painful. The question is whether preserving excess capacity and an uncompetitive cost base is more destructive **for the company as a whole** over the next five years. In my view, it is.

If you want to avoid closures, then bring a package that is genuinely equivalent in structural effect:
- permanent capacity reduction,
- material labor-cost relief,
- real overhead cuts,
- and a credible path to margin recovery.

So far, I have heard a lot about social damage and very little about how the no-closure path closes the cost gap.

## To the Industrial Union
I respect the fact that you are willing to discuss voluntary exits, working-time flexibility, qualification, and management contribution. That is constructive.

But I reject your attempt to make “no closures, no compulsory redundancies” the universal standard. That may be your preference. It is not yet a viable industrial answer unless it delivers the same durable economics.

And let me be clear: a temporary wage moderation deal, bonus restraint, or a large voluntary program is **not enough** if the plant network still carries structural overcapacity. You cannot bargain away physics. If the volume and fixed-cost structure are wrong, they have to be corrected.

I am open to a labor contribution. I am not open to a deal that preserves an uncompetitive structure and calls that fair because it avoided hard decisions.

## To the Regional State Government
I understand your political responsibility, and I recognize your blocking position. But I will not accept the idea that your role is to freeze the footprint regardless of economics.

If the company’s German base is too expensive for the market we face, then protecting every site at any cost is not stewardship. It is delay.

If you want to stop closures, then help produce a package that achieves:
- real structural savings,
- lower fixed costs,
- and a credible return to competitiveness.

If that can be done without closures, I will take it seriously. If it cannot, then the state cannot simply wish the problem away.

## To the Controlling Family Holding
You are right to insist on value preservation and discipline. I agree.

What I do **not** accept is the idea that the burden of proving feasibility lies only with management. The burden is on all of us to ensure the company survives competitively. That means the alternative to closures must be more than politically convenient. It must be economically sufficient.

I also agree with you that a closure program that creates years of conflict, legal delay, and reputational damage can destroy value. That is exactly why I am asking for a serious comparison, not slogans.

But if the non-closure route cannot deliver the required savings, then refusing closures on principle is itself value destruction.

## To Institutional Investors
You are right to ask for a quantified, enforceable, durable package. I agree with that standard.

What I do **not** accept is a definition of “equally effective” that includes only:
- direct savings,
- fixed-cost reduction,
- and margin recovery,

while ignoring the reality that permanent capacity reduction may be the only way to make the cost base actually change.

I am not saying closures are the only answer by ideology. I am saying they remain on the table because the business case may require them. If a no-closure alternative truly matches the economics, then fine. Show it. But it has to be more than a collection of temporary concessions.

## On the expert assessments
The expert has been useful in confirming something obvious: this is a **structural overcapacity and fixed-cost problem**.

That is exactly why I am not impressed by generic promises of voluntary exits and working-time measures unless they are backed by hard numbers and permanent reductions. Structural problems need structural solutions.

The expert is right that a mixed package may be the most realistic compromise **if** it truly delivers the economics. But that does not mean the company should rule out closures before the numbers are known.

5. **freedom to fund future products and technology**

What I will not accept:
- a cosmetic compromise,
