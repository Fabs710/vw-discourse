I will be straightforward.

## Where I agree
I agree with everyone on the basic diagnosis: **doing nothing is not an option**. The company needs a durable restructuring path through 2030, and it needs to restore competitiveness.

I also agree that this cannot be a cosmetic exercise. It must include:
- productivity improvements,
- qualification and redeployment,
- working-time flexibility,
- voluntary instruments,
- management and overhead contribution,
- and a binding product/investment perspective.

And I agree that management, owners, employees, and the state all have to contribute in some way.

## Where I disagree
What I do **not** accept is the way management, the family holding, and investors keep presenting plant closures as the “serious” or “clean” answer.

For me, closures are not a neutral technical instrument. They are the most destructive option for:
- regional employment,
- suppliers,
- industrial trust,
- and the company’s legitimacy as a German anchor employer.

So I am not prepared to treat closures as the default or starting point.

I also do **not** accept compulsory redundancies as an ordinary restructuring tool. That would cross a line for us politically, socially, and in governance terms.

## To management
You keep saying the non-closure route has not yet been proven equivalent. Fair enough — but then prove it properly.

I want a real comparison, not a rhetorical one:
- site-by-site economics,
- capacity utilization,
- future product allocation,
- permanent savings,
- conflict costs,
- and the margin effect through 2030.

If a no-closure package truly cannot work, that should emerge from the numbers. But until then, closures are not justified simply because they are easier to model on paper.

I also want to be clear: I will not support a package that destroys German sites and then calls itself a long-term strategy.

## To the Works Council and the Union
I share your insistence that workers must not be made the sole adjustment variable. That is correct.

I also support your demand that any restructuring must be socially responsible, with:
- no unilateral burden shifting,
- real management and owner contribution,
- qualification and redeployment,
- and binding investment commitments.

Where I am more cautious is in absolute formulations. I understand why you reject closures outright. But as shareholder and public actor, I need a package that is not only morally defensible, but economically durable.

So I am not asking you to give up your red lines today. I am asking you to help produce a credible alternative that can survive scrutiny on the numbers.

## To the controlling family holding
I hear your concern for long-term enterprise value. I share the concern for long-term viability.

But long-term value is not protected by tearing up the company’s social foundation. If a settlement breaks trust, damages industrial peace, and weakens the company’s role in the region, that is value destruction too.

So I do not accept a logic where the only meaningful criterion is permanent cost reduction. The full picture has to include:
- regional stability,
- supplier effects,
- implementation risk,
- and the long-term health of co-determination.

## To institutional investors
You are right to demand seriousness and durability. I do not object to that standard.

What I do object to is letting “equally effective” mean only “equally painful for labour.”  
A serious comparison must count the full cost:
- strike risk,
- conflict duration,
- supplier disruption,
- ramp-up loss,
- and legitimacy damage.

If a non-closure package can achieve real savings and structural correction with less total destruction, then that is the better outcome.

## My position as state shareholder
Let me be explicit about what I can support:

### I can support:
- a serious restructuring through 2030,
- material management and owner contribution,
- voluntary exits,
- internal redeployment,
- qualification and retraining,
- working-time flexibility,
- temporary and fairly shared labor contributions,
- and a binding investment/product path for German sites.

### I cannot support:
- a restructuring that guts regional employment,
- plant closures as the default solution,
- forced redundancies,
- or any outcome that sidelines the state’s co-determination and veto role.

That is not a tactical posture. It is a core boundary.

## What would move me
The only thing that would move me is a **credible, quantified, site-by-site restructuring model** showing:
1. a no-closure package,
2. a limited-closure package,
3. and a mixed package,

with:
- annual savings,
- permanent capacity reduction,
- job effects,
- implementation timing,
- strike/disruption risk,
- supplier impact,
- and margin effect through 2030.

[SUMMON: independent site-by-site restructuring auditor with plant-level data to compare the three packages on savings, capacity, employment, logistics, and execution risk]

That is the basis on which I would be prepared to judge whether a non-closure route is truly viable.

## Bottom line
My line is simple:
- I accept restructuring.
- I accept shared contribution.
- I do **not** accept closures as the default answer.
- I do **not** accept compulsory redundancies.
- I do **not** accept any settlement that weakens the company’s role as a regional industrial anchor.

If the numbers show that a negotiated package can preserve sites and jobs while restoring competitiveness, I will support it.  
If not, then I will use the leverage I have to block an outcome that would damage the region and the company’s long-term future.
