I’ll respond plainly.

## Where I agree
I agree with the core factual point repeated by several parties: **doing nothing is not an option**.  
I also agree that the company needs a **durable restructuring through 2030**, not a cosmetic fix.

And yes, I accept that a real package must include some mix of:
- productivity improvements,
- qualification and redeployment,
- working-time flexibility,
- voluntary instruments,
- management and overhead contribution,
- and investment/product commitments.

So no one should misread the union position as denial of the problem. We are not denying it. We are rejecting a solution that makes workers carry the full burden.

## Where I strongly disagree

### 1) Closures are not the “responsible default”
To management, the family holding, and investors: you keep framing plant closures as the clean or serious instrument. I reject that.

A closure is not simply an efficient line item. It is:
- permanent job destruction,
- loss of know-how,
- supplier damage,
- strike escalation,
- reputational damage,
- and a long-term blow to trust in the company’s labor relations.

So no: “cleanest on the spreadsheet” is not enough. If you want to argue closure, you must show that no other combination can achieve the required savings **with materially less total value destruction**.

### 2) “Equally effective” cannot mean “equally painful for labour only”
I agree with the moderator and expert that the comparison must be real and quantified. Fine.

But I reject any definition of effectiveness that counts only:
- direct savings,
- margin recovery,
- and fixed-cost removal,

while ignoring:
- conflict cost,
- strike risk,
- implementation delay,
- supplier disruption,
- and the long-term damage to production reliability and social legitimacy.

If you exclude those, you are not comparing viable restructuring paths. You are just optimizing for the easiest burden transfer.

### 3) Compulsory redundancies are not acceptable
I’ll say it again clearly: **no compulsory redundancies**.

Not as a first resort, not as a fallback, not as a bargaining chip. If management insists on forced layoffs, then it is choosing confrontation over settlement.

## To management
You say non-closure options have not yet been shown to close the gap. That may be true — but that is not the same as proving closure is unavoidable.

If you want the union to engage seriously on a no-closure path, then bring the actual model:
- site-by-site cost gap,
- current and future product allocation,
- required capacity reduction,
- realistic voluntary exit volume,
- wage and bonus measures,
- overhead cuts,
- management contribution,
- and the impact on margin through 2030.

Not a slogan. Not a threat. A credible industrial model.

If the no-closure package truly cannot work, that has to be shown against the full cost of conflict and implementation risk, not just on accounting savings.

## To the works council
I share your refusal to make workers the shock absorber. On the central point, we are aligned: **employees must not bear all the pain**.

Where I would be a little less absolute is on the tactical level. A durable settlement may still require some hard decisions on volume, shifts, and footprint — but only if they are achieved through negotiated means, with no forced redundancies and with real job protection.

So I am not asking you to surrender. I am asking you to keep the bargaining space open for a fair package that protects employment and the collective framework.

## To the regional state government
You are right to insist that legitimacy matters. But legitimacy won’t be preserved by vague resistance alone.

If the state shareholder wants to block destructive outcomes, it should help force a package with:
- no compulsory redundancies,
- binding site and employment safeguards,
- real owner and management contribution,
- and a credible industrial plan.

If management cannot deliver that, then yes, use your leverage. But don’t let this drift into a polite stalemate where the workforce is eventually asked to absorb the same pain through indirect means.

## To the controlling family holding
You keep saying you want long-term value preservation. Then show it.

Long-term value is not protected by:
- breaking the wage framework,
- weakening trust,
- or treating German production as disposable.

If you want our cooperation, then the package must include:
- material management and owner contribution,
- no forced redundancies,
- binding job and site security,
- and a genuine burden-sharing arrangement.

We are prepared to contribute. But we will not sign up to a one-sided “contribution” where employees give up income and security while capital preserves freedom of action.

## To institutional investors
You are right to demand a durable solution. But durable does not mean socially explosive.

A settlement that triggers strikes, demoralization, and implementation drag can destroy as much value as a blunt closure plan. So if you want the business to recover through 2030, support a package that:
- protects employment,
- preserves industrial peace,
- and still delivers real savings.

That means you should not treat the absence of closures as a weakness if the alternative can achieve the same structural effect through smarter means.

## On the expert assessment
The expert has been useful in one respect: it confirms that this is a **structural** problem, not just a wage issue.

But I reject any reading of that analysis that makes closures the implied default. Structural can also mean:
- shift reduction,
- product reassignment,
- footprint trimming without shutdown,
- voluntary exits,
- internal transfer,
- and overhead compression.

That is where we are prepared to negotiate.

## What we can accept
We are prepared to discuss:
- voluntary exits,
- early retirement,
- internal transfers,
- qualification and redeployment,
- temporary working-time measures,
- bonus restraint or limited wage moderation,
- productivity measures,
- management overhead reduction,
- and owner contribution.

We can also discuss a **temporary employee contribution** if — and only if — it is matched by genuine management and owner sacrifice and tied to binding site and job security.

## What we cannot accept
We cannot accept:
- plant closures as the starting point,
- compulsory redundancies,
- a hollowing out of the collective wage framework,
- or a restructuring that shifts the full adjustment burden onto the workforce.

## [SUMMON: independent industrial cost and conflict-risk modeller]
We need a neutral model that compares:
1. a **no-closure / no-forced-layoff** package,
2. a **limited-closure** package,
3. and a **mixed** package,

including:
- annual savings,
- permanent capacity reduction,
- headcount effects,
- strike and disruption risk,
- supplier impact,
- ramp-up risk,
- and margin effect through 2030.

Without that, the debate remains ideological.

## Final word
We are not here to defend the status quo at any cost. We are here to insist that restructuring be **fair, durable, and shared**.

So my position remains:
- no compulsory redundancies,
- no one-sided burden shifting,
- real management and owner contribution,
- and a negotiated solution that protects jobs, sites, and the collective wage framework.

If management wants a deal, it must stop treating closures as the opening move and start proving that a serious alternative can work. If it cannot, then it must be honest about who it expects to pay.
