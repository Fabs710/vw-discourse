Let me respond directly.

### To the Works Council
I understand your insistence on protecting jobs and sites, but I do **not** accept the idea that every German plant must stay open regardless of economics. That is not a restructuring strategy; it is a refusal to confront the structural overcapacity that is already hurting the company.

You keep asking for “transparent numbers.” Fair enough — and we should work with the site data. But the basic direction is already clear:
- German factory costs are too high,
- capacity exceeds demand,
- and the brand is missing its return target.

If you want to avoid closures, then you need to bring an alternative that delivers the **same structural savings**, not just softer language. That means:
- durable capacity reduction,
- materially lower labour costs,
- and a clear, enforceable path to improved margins.

I am open to voluntary instruments, internal redeployment, working-time adjustments, and qualification. But I will not accept a package that keeps the full cost base in place and calls it transformation.

### To the Industrial Union
I agree with you on one point: the burden cannot fall on employees alone. Management will contribute, and top-layer costs must be part of the package. But let’s be honest — management cost cuts alone will not solve this.

You say “no closures, no compulsory redundancies.” I cannot accept those as absolute red lines. If demand and economics do not support the current footprint, then some form of structural adjustment is unavoidable. The question is whether we do it through closures or through an alternative package that actually achieves the same effect.

And on “shared sacrifice”: it must be **material**. Wage moderation, bonus restraint, working-time changes, and other labour-cost measures need to be meaningful enough to restore competitiveness. Symbolic gestures will not get us to 2030 in a stronger position.

### To the Regional State Government
I hear your concern for the region and the industrial base. I share the goal of keeping strong manufacturing in Germany. But preserving industrial value does **not** mean freezing every site in place.

If the state uses its leverage to block any structural adjustment, then it risks protecting short-term employment while weakening the company’s future. That helps no one in the long run — not the workforce, not suppliers, and not the region.

So yes, we can discuss ways to preserve German production and investment. But I will not agree to a solution that rules out closures or major capacity reductions before the economics are tested properly.

### To the Controlling Family Holding
You are right that doing nothing is not an option. You are also right that the company needs value discipline. Where I disagree is on the idea that any resistance to closures is somehow a realistic governing position.

A company cannot keep a structurally too-large footprint and expect to remain competitive in a shrinking market. If we preserve overcapacity, we preserve the problem.

I support a serious, disciplined restructuring. But that means the governance side must also be prepared to back a tough package — not just urge “discipline” while expecting management to absorb all the reputational risk.

### To the Institutional Investors
You are correct to insist on durable structural savings and a credible return to competitive margins. I agree with that.

Where I push back is on the assumption that the only credible route is plant closure. It may be one route, but it is not the only route. I am open to alternatives **if** they are equally effective and enforceable.

But I will not support a settlement that simply avoids hard decisions and leaves the company underperforming for another cycle. That would indeed be capital destruction. So I’m not asking for a cosmetic compromise; I am asking for a **real** one — real in savings, real in capacity adjustment, real in labour cost.

### What I need from the employee side
If you want to avoid closures, then bring a package that can credibly close the gap:
- quantified labour-cost contribution,
- measurable capacity reduction,
- clear productivity improvements,
- voluntary exits and redeployment at scale,
- and a binding framework that actually changes the economics.

If that package is strong enough, I will seriously consider it. If not, then I will continue to argue that closures remain necessary.

### My position remains unchanged on the essentials
1. **We need deep, durable cost cuts.**
2. **We need structural capacity reduction.**
3. **We need a material labour-cost contribution.**
4. **We need a settlement that restores competitiveness through 2030.**

What I will not accept:
- a cosmetic deal,
- a promise to protect every site regardless of economics,
- or a package that leaves the company with the same uncompetitive cost base.

I am prepared to negotiate seriously. But the benchmark is not social comfort; it is whether the company becomes competitive again.
