I will be direct.

The discussion has become more useful, but we are still mixing two different questions:
1. **Is restructuring necessary?** Yes.
2. **Can that restructuring be done without any closures or compulsory redundancies?** Possibly, but only if someone can prove it delivers the same permanent economic effect.

So far, that proof is still missing.

## To management
I agree with the diagnosis that the cost base and capacity are misaligned with the market. I also agree that we cannot preserve value by doing nothing.

Where I am not yet convinced is the leap from “structural adjustment is needed” to “closures are therefore the right instrument.”  
That may be true in some sites, but not by assumption. I want the site-by-site case, and I want it tested against a serious non-closure alternative.

What I do **not** support is a restructuring that creates permanent industrial damage through conflict, implementation failure, or reputational fallout. If a closure program saves money on paper but destabilizes the company for years, that is not value creation.

So: show me the numbers, the timing, the payback, and the sensitivity to strike and disruption risk.

## To the works council and the union
I understand why you reject closures and forced redundancies. From a labor and social perspective, that is consistent. But from an ownership and governance perspective, an absolute “every plant stays open, no compulsory redundancies ever” line is **not yet a viable answer** unless it can be matched by equivalent structural savings.

I agree with you on several points:
- employees should not carry the burden alone,
- management and owners must contribute materially,
- voluntary instruments, redeployment, working-time measures, and qualification are all necessary,
- and a cosmetic compromise would be a mistake.

Where I disagree is your attempt to make the social cost of closures the only relevant cost. It is not. The company can also be damaged by preserving overcapacity, weak margins, and delayed decisions.

So if you want a no-closure settlement, bring a package that is:
- quantified,
- enforceable,
- durable,
- and large enough to restore competitiveness through 2030.

If that package exists, I will support it. If it does not, then insisting on it becomes a delay tactic, not a solution.

## To the regional state government
I respect the state’s role and understand its political responsibility. I also agree that legitimacy matters and that the company should remain a strong industrial anchor in Germany.

But I do **not** accept using the blocking position to freeze the footprint regardless of economics. That would protect the appearance of stability while quietly eroding the company’s future.

If the state wants to avoid closures, it should help produce a genuine alternative with real capacity reduction and real cost relief. If it cannot, then it is not serving the long-term interest of the company or the region by pretending closure risk does not exist.

## To the institutional investors
I agree with your core point: the package must be economically real, not symbolic. I also agree that if the no-closure route cannot restore margins, it is not acceptable.

Where I would caution is against treating closures as the automatic benchmark for seriousness. The right benchmark is:
- permanent fixed-cost reduction,
- durable capacity correction,
- and a credible return to target margins.

If a mixed or non-closure package can achieve that, then that is preferable from a value-and-stability perspective. If not, then we cannot rule out deeper structural measures.

## My current position
The family holding will support a solution that:
1. restores competitiveness and margins,
2. permanently reduces structural overcapacity,
3. preserves long-term enterprise value,
4. and avoids value-destructive instability.

That means I am open to a negotiated package with:
- voluntary exits,
- redeployment,
- working-time flexibility,
- wage moderation or bonus restraint,
- management and overhead reductions,
- and investment commitments.

I am also open to avoiding closures **if** the alternative is truly equal in effect.

What I am **not** open to:
- a cosmetic deal,
- a structure that leaves the company uncompetitive,
- or a package that substitutes political comfort for economic reality.

## What I need next
Before anyone asks governance to take a hard position, I want one clear comparison:
- **no-closure package**
- **limited-closure package**
- **mixed package**

Each should show:
- annual savings,
- permanent capacity reduction,
- job impact,
- implementation timeline,
- conflict risk,
- and margin effect through 2030.

If the no-closure option truly closes the gap, I will listen carefully.  
If it does not, then the answer cannot be “nothing painful must ever happen.”
