## To management
I agree with you on one essential point: **doing nothing is not an option**. The company needs a durable restructuring path through 2030, and it needs to be economically credible.

I also agree that:
- productivity has to improve,
- there must be real savings,
- management and owners must contribute,

Where I **do not** agree is with your continuing framing that plant closures in Germany must remain a normal option. For me, that is not a technical detail. It is a line that would damage:
- regional employment,
- the supplier base,
- trust in co-determination,
- and the company’s role as an industrial anchor.

If you want my support, then you need to show a package that preserves German production capacity **without making closure the starting assumption**. “Closures are cleaner” is not a sufficient argument. Cleaner for balance sheets is not the same as better for the company’s long-term social and industrial stability.

## To the Works Council and the Union
I support your insistence that the workforce must not be made the sole adjustment variable. That is correct.

I also agree that:
- management and owners must share the burden,
- voluntary instruments should come first,
- redeployment and qualification matter,
- working-time flexibility can help,
- and a negotiated transformation is preferable to confrontation.

Where I am more cautious is on the absolute wording. I understand why you say “every German plant stays open,” because you are defending the sites and the workforce. I share the aim of protecting sites and jobs in the state. But to keep that position credible in governance terms, we need a package that is not just morally right, but economically durable.

So I am not asking you to surrender. I am asking you to help produce a restructuring that is strong enough to remove the structural problem **without** tearing out the company’s German base.

That means I need to see:
- site-by-site numbers,
- real capacity reduction,
- management-side savings,
- owner contribution,
- and a binding product and investment plan.

If that can be achieved without closures and forced redundancies, I will back it. If not, then we are not yet at a solution.

## To the controlling family holding
I accept your central point that value destruction must be avoided and that the cost gap is real.

But I do **not** accept the implication that the only credible way to restore value is to make closures and deeper cuts the default governing logic. In a company of this importance, a “pure” financial logic can destroy more value than it saves if it breaks industrial legitimacy and triggers prolonged conflict.

So yes:
- the economics must be proven,
- alternatives must be tested,
- and structural change must be real.

But I expect the same seriousness about the **social and regional consequences**. A settlement that weakens the company’s anchor function is not good stewardship. It is a short route to long-term damage.

## To institutional investors
You are right to ask for a package that is quantified, enforceable, and durable.

I agree with that standard. What I do **not** accept is letting “equally effective” become a narrow test that ignores the wider cost of disruption, conflict, and regional damage. Value is not only the next quarter’s margin.

If a no-closure package truly restores competitiveness through 2030, preserves the industrial base, and is enforceable, then that is the better outcome. If it does not, then it is not enough. But the burden is not only on labour; it is also on management and owners to show that they are not using structural pressure as a pretext for excessive downsizing.

- footprint reduction without abandoning sites,
- product reallocation,
- shift changes,
- overhead reduction,

- redeployment,

That is the path I want tested before anyone treats plant closure as unavoidable.
