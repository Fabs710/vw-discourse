I will respond directly, because the stakes here are too high for vague formulae.

## To management
I agree with you on one point: **doing nothing is not an option**.  
I also agree that the company needs a credible industrial plan through 2030, real productivity gains, and a settlement that is durable rather than cosmetic.

But I **do not** accept your framing that plant closures in Germany are a necessary baseline. As the regional state shareholder, I cannot support a restructuring that treats German sites as disposable cost units. That would damage:
- regional employment,
- supplier networks,
- the company’s identity as an industrial anchor,
- and the long-term legitimacy of the restructuring itself.

If you want our support, then show us a package that is actually negotiable and economically robust **without defaulting to closures**. That means:
- transparent site-by-site numbers,
- a real savings target,
- management-side savings that are material,
- voluntary instruments and redeployment at scale,
- and binding investment commitments for future products in Germany.

If your case is simply “closures are easier,” that is not enough. Easier is not the same as better.

## To the Works Council
I support your core line: **no compulsory redundancies** and no dismantling of co-determination or the job-security framework by stealth.  
I also share your view that the workforce should not be made the sole adjustment variable.

Where I would be careful is in how absolute the “every German plant stays open” line becomes. I understand why you are drawing it, and I am not prepared to abandon site protection lightly. But the credible way to defend sites is to put forward a serious alternative that restores competitiveness while preserving industrial capacity in the region.

So yes:
- no forced layoffs,
- no unilateral closures,
- no fait accompli.

But we need a negotiated package that can withstand the economic test. If we simply refuse all structural adjustment, we risk losing the argument with the wider public and with parts of the governance structure.

## To the Industrial Union
I agree that a fair solution requires **shared sacrifice**.  
I also agree that management and owners must contribute, and that top-layer costs should not be exempt.

What I do **not** agree with is the suggestion that any structural adjustment is unacceptable if it affects plant footprints or labor costs. The company does need deep savings. If those savings cannot be achieved through productivity, working-time, voluntary measures, and management contributions alone, then we have to examine alternatives carefully and transparently.

But “shared sacrifice” must be more than a slogan. If you want us to oppose closures, then the alternative package must be **credible in scale and binding in effect**. I am open to that. I am not open to a package that merely postpones the problem.

## To the controlling family holding
You are right that preserving an uncompetitive structure is not a strategy. I agree with you that the company needs discipline and that value destruction must be avoided.

Where I disagree is with any approach that treats plant closures as the natural first resort. In a company of this regional importance, governance discipline also means understanding the wider consequences of industrial decisions. A solution that wounds the company’s social base can destroy value just as surely as weak margins.

So: yes to structural reform, yes to competitiveness, yes to material contributions from labor and management. But no to a logic that says regional employment is merely collateral damage.

## To institutional investors
I hear your insistence on durability and return to competitive margins. That is legitimate. But your benchmark cannot be reduced to “whatever gets the cost base down fastest.”

If the restructuring:
- triggers prolonged conflict,
- weakens industrial legitimacy,
- or hollowes out German production,
then you may get a short-term accounting improvement and a long-term strategic loss.

I am open to a package that is genuinely effective. I am not open to a package that is only financially neat on paper while politically and industrially unstable in reality.

## The state shareholder’s line
My position is this:

1. **German sites are not bargaining chips.**
2. **No compulsory redundancies.**
3. **No outcome that sidelines the state’s co-determination and veto role.**
4. **No restructuring that guts regional employment.**
5. **Yes to a negotiated package that secures competitiveness while preserving the company as a regional economic anchor.**

## What I can support
I can support a serious package that includes:
- voluntary exits instead of forced layoffs,
- internal transfers and qualification,
- working-time flexibility,
- temporary or targeted wage moderation,
- management and overhead contribution,
- investment commitments in Germany,
- and measurable productivity and capacity adjustments **without plant closures**.

## What I cannot support
I cannot support:
- plant closures in Germany as the default answer,
- compulsory redundancies,
- a settlement that weakens co-determination,
- or a restructuring that leaves the state and workforce with the damage while other stakeholders claim necessity.

If management wants our backing, bring a real alternative that preserves sites and jobs while restoring competitiveness. If you do that, we can be constructive. If not, we will use the leverage the statute and governance give us.
