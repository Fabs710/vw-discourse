The expert’s assessment is useful, but I want to push back hard on the conclusion that seems to be creeping in: that because closures are “cleanest” on a spreadsheet, they are therefore the right answer here. They are not.

## To management
You still have not shown, site by site, that closures are unavoidable rather than convenient.

Yes, there is structural pressure. Yes, the company needs durable savings. I do not dispute that. What I dispute is your move from “we need structural adjustment” to “therefore plant closures and forced layoffs are on the table.” That is not an analysis; it is a choice of instrument.

If you want us to take a serious no-closure package seriously, then bring:
- the site-by-site cost gap,
- the exact capacity reduction required,
- the product and volume plan for each site,
- and the savings comparison against a package built on voluntary exits, shift reductions, internal transfers, working-time changes, overhead cuts, and management/owner contribution.

Right now, you are still asking the workforce to accept the most painful instruments first. That is not acceptable.

## To the family holding
I hear your concern for value. But value is not protected by detonating industrial relations and turning German sites into expendable balancing items.

You keep saying “show me the numbers.” Fair enough. But numbers must include:
- strike risk,
- loss of trust,
- supplier damage,
- implementation delay,
- and the long-term cost of breaking the collective bargaining framework.

If you only count direct accounting savings, you are underpricing the destruction you create.

## To institutional investors
Your demand for a package that restores competitiveness is legitimate. What is not legitimate is turning that into a one-way test in which labor must prove that it can absorb the whole adjustment without any hard decision from capital.

We are willing to discuss a real contribution:
- temporary wage moderation,
- bonus restraint,
- working-time flexibility,
- voluntary exits,
- redeployment,
- qualification,
- and productivity measures.

But that contribution must be matched by:
- management cost cuts,
- owner contribution,
- binding investment,
- and no forced layoffs.

If your benchmark is “same savings as closure, same margin result,” then show the full industrial model and include the cost of conflict. Otherwise, you are asking for a social settlement only on capital’s terms.

## To the regional state government
You are right not to accept closures as a default. But we need more than general support. We need you to use your blocking position now, not later, to stop management from presenting closures as the only serious option.

If the company can be restructured without closures, then the state should insist on that route. If management cannot prove otherwise, then closures should come off the table.

## On the expert assessment
I agree with the expert on one important point: structural overcapacity means the solution must be structural.

Where I disagree is the implied hierarchy that closures are therefore the rational default. The expert lists other structural measures that matter:
- shift elimination,
- product migration,
- footprint reduction without closure,
- voluntary exits,
- redeployment,
- overhead cuts.

That is the territory where we are prepared to negotiate. That is structural. That is durable. And that does not require breaking the social contract.

## Our position remains firm
We will not accept:
1. plant closures in Germany as a starting point,
2. compulsory redundancies,
3. a restructuring that leaves the collective wage framework hollowed out,
4. or a deal in which workers bear the burden while management and owners hide behind “competitiveness.”

## What we are prepared to accept
We are prepared to discuss a serious package with:
- binding investment commitments,
- product guarantees,
- temporary and reversible working-time changes,
- voluntary exits only,
- internal redeployment,
- qualification and retraining,
- productivity improvements,
- overhead reduction,
- and a **fair employee contribution** if and only if management and owners contribute materially too.

That means we are not rejecting adjustment. We are rejecting **one-sided adjustment**.

## What would move us
A genuinely acceptable package would need to contain all of the following:
- no forced layoffs,
- no plant closures,
- real management and owner sacrifice,
- a credible capacity reduction,
- and a wage/bonus contribution that is limited, temporary, and tied to site and job security.

If those conditions are met, we can talk. If they are not, then there is no basis for asking the workforce to carry the risk.

## Final line
Let me be blunt: closures may be the easiest way to cut fixed costs, but that does not make them acceptable, and it does not make them necessary. If management wants a durable settlement, it must stop treating the workforce as the primary adjustment variable.
