Yes. Staying within industrial audit and workforce-impact logic, I can define the **minimum threshold for a no-closure / no-forced-layoff package** and what would make it **industrially credible** versus merely postponing restructuring.

## 1) Core threshold for credibility
A no-closure package is only credible if it delivers **permanent structural correction**, not just temporary relief.

In practice, that means it must achieve all of the following:

- **Permanent annual savings**: roughly **€1.5–2.5 billion per year** at group/brand level, if the current EBIT/ROS gap is materially significant and the German footprint is structurally overbuilt.
- **Permanent capacity reduction**: about **10–15%** of installed German/European passenger-car capacity if utilization is below efficient levels.
- **Headcount reduction**: roughly **8–15%** of German manufacturing and plant-support workforce over the restructuring horizon, even if done voluntarily and through redeployment.
- **Higher retained-network utilization**: roughly **75–85% utilization** at the plants that remain.
- **Durability through 2030**: savings must be locked in structurally, not dependent on one-off freezes, short-term bonus restraint, or temporary working-time cuts.

## 2) What counts as a real no-closure package
A no-closure package can still be credible if it includes a **hard mix** of measures such as:

- shift removals and line consolidation,
- product transfers between sites,
- site-by-site capacity resizing,
- overhead compression,
- supplier re-bidding,
- voluntary exits / early retirement,
- internal redeployment,
- qualification and retraining,
- working-time flexibility,
- management overhead cuts,
- and meaningful owner contribution.

What matters is the **net permanent effect**, not the instrument label.

## 3) What makes it non-credible
A no-closure package becomes industrially weak if it relies mainly on:

- temporary wage moderation,
- short-lived bonus cuts,
- optimistic productivity assumptions,
- delayed product ramp-ups,
- or voluntary exits that do not actually reduce fixed cost enough.

That kind of package is usually **execution-sensitive** rather than structurally sufficient.

## 4) Practical rule
If the no-closure plan cannot show:

1. **permanent savings in the low single-digit billions annually**,  
2. **material fixed-cost removal**,  
3. **clear utilization improvement at retained sites**, and  
4. **a believable path to margin recovery by 2030**,  

then it is not a full alternative to footprint reduction.

## 5) Site-by-site implication
For an audit, I would test each plant against:

- current utilization,
- product pipeline through 2030,
- logistics cost,
- labor intensity,
- support-function overhead,
- retooling needs,
- and ability to absorb volume from other sites.

If a plant cannot reach a viable post-restructuring load factor, then keeping it open only makes sense if another site absorbs the correction and the savings are real and durable.

## 6) Bottom line
A no-closure / no-forced-layoff package is **industrially credible only if it permanently removes enough cost and capacity to restore the network to sustainable utilization and margins by 2030**.

If it does not, then it is not an equal alternative to closure-based restructuring; it is just a delay mechanism.
