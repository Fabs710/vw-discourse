Yes. Staying strictly within automotive manufacturing economics and plant-network restructuring, here is the threshold view I would use.

## Bottom line
A **no-closure / no-forced-layoff** package stops being industrially credible when it **fails to deliver enough permanent fixed-cost and capacity reduction to restore margin durability by 2030**.

For a large European OEM with evident structural overcapacity, I would treat the package as **not credible** if it does not achieve roughly all of the following:

- **Permanent cost reduction:** about **€1.5–2.5 billion per year** at group/brand level for the German passenger-car footprint, depending on starting EBIT gap and volume outlook.
- **Permanent capacity reduction:** about **10–15% of European/German installed passenger-car capacity** if utilization is materially below efficient levels.
- **Headcount reduction:** roughly **8–15% of the German manufacturing and plant-support workforce** over the restructuring horizon, if that reflects true overcapacity and not just temporary underutilization.
- **Sustained utilization:** recovery to at least **~75–85% utilization** on the retained network; below that, fixed-cost absorption remains weak and the network usually still carries excess structure.
- **Durability:** savings must be locked in through **2030 and beyond**, not dependent on one-off wage freezes, short-term bonus cuts, or temporary working-time arrangements.

## How I would interpret that threshold
A no-closure package can still work if it achieves the same **economic effect** through:
- shift removal,
- line consolidation,
- internal volume transfers,
- product rationalization,
- supplier re-bidding,
- overhead compression,
- voluntary exits,
- working-time flexibility,
- and material management/owner contribution.

But if the package relies mainly on:
- temporary labor concessions,
- one-off payments,
- short-lived wage moderation,
- or optimistic productivity claims,

then it is **execution-sensitive rather than structurally credible**.

## Practical rule of thumb
If the alternative package cannot show:
1. **permanent annual savings** at least in the low single-digit billions,
2. **material fixed-cost elimination** rather than just labor-cost smoothing,
3. **a clear utilization step-up** at the retained plants,
4. and a **margin path that closes the current gap by 2030**,

then closure-based restructuring remains the more credible industrial solution.

## Key sensitivity
The exact threshold depends on:
- the company’s current revenue base,
- the depth of the EBIT/ROS gap,
- EV transition volume assumptions,
- and how much of the cost gap is truly fixed versus variable.

So I would not claim a single universal number. But in a network already judged to be structurally overcapacity, a no-closure package that cannot remove **at least high hundreds of millions to low billions annually** is usually too small to be credible.

## In one sentence
**If the no-closure plan does not permanently remove enough fixed cost to lift utilization and margin to a sustainable level by 2030, it is not a real alternative to footprint reduction—it is only a delay mechanism.**
