I will be direct.

I agree with everyone on one point: **doing nothing is not an option**. The company needs a durable restructuring path through 2030, and it must restore competitiveness and margins. I also agree that management, owners, and employees all have to contribute. That part is no longer in dispute.

Where I **do not** agree is with the repeated attempt to turn “no closures” and “no compulsory redundancies” into absolute premises. From a governance and ownership perspective, that is not yet a credible answer unless it is backed by a package that clearly and permanently closes the cost and capacity gap.

## To the Works Council
I understand why you reject closures and forced redundancies. I also agree that closures are not “clean” for the workforce, regions, or suppliers. But I do **not** accept your claim that preserving every site is automatically the less destructive choice.

If the company keeps excess fixed cost and structural overcapacity, it destroys value more slowly, but it still destroys value. That matters to the family holding, to the business, and ultimately to employment security in the long run.

So I can accept your argument **only if** a no-closure package is truly:
- quantified,
- enforceable,
- durable,
- and materially equivalent in economic effect.

At present, that proof is still missing.

## To the Industrial Union
I agree with you that workers should not carry the whole burden, and I accept that any serious package needs:
- voluntary exits,
- working-time flexibility,
- qualification and redeployment,
- productivity measures,
- and real management/owner contribution.

But I do **not** accept the categorical rejection of all plant closures and all compulsory redundancies as a governing principle. That is not a restructuring solution; it is a red line that may or may not be compatible with the economics.

If you want a no-closure route, then show a package that permanently reduces fixed cost and capacity enough to restore margins through 2030. Temporary concessions are not enough.

## To Management
I agree with the diagnosis that the cost base and capacity are misaligned with the market. I do **not** agree that you can simply assert closures are necessary without proving, site by site, that the alternatives fail.

You need to do better than a general closure thesis. Bring the actual comparison:
- no-closure package,
- limited-closure package,
- mixed package,

with:
- annual savings,
- permanent capacity reduction,
- headcount effect,
- implementation timing,
- strike/disruption risk,
- and margin impact through 2030.

If the non-closure option cannot close the gap, then say so clearly. But if you want governance support for hard measures, I need evidence, not just leverage.

## To the Regional State Government
I respect your blocking role and your concern for the region. I agree that industrial legitimacy matters and that the company should remain a strong German anchor.

But I do **not** accept a governance posture that freezes the footprint regardless of economics. That would protect appearances while eroding the company’s future.

If you want to avoid closures, help produce a package that truly restores competitiveness. If that cannot be done, then blocking every hard measure is not stewardship either.

## To the Institutional Investors
I agree with your demand for a serious, durable, value-restoring package. That is the right standard.

What I do **not** accept is any narrow test that counts only direct savings and ignores implementation risk, conflict cost, and long-term legitimacy. Those matter. But they do not eliminate the need for structural correction.

So the question is not whether the company can avoid pain. It is whether it can avoid **value-destructive** pain while still removing enough fixed cost and capacity.

## My position
The family holding will support a solution that:
1. restores competitiveness and margins,
2. permanently reduces structural overcapacity,
3. protects long-term enterprise value,
4. and avoids value-destructive instability.

That means I am open to:
- voluntary exits,
- redeployment,
- working-time changes,
- wage moderation or bonus restraint,
- management overhead reductions,
- owner contribution,
- and binding investment commitments.

I am also open to a non-closure package **if** it is genuinely equivalent in effect.

What I am **not** open to is:
- a cosmetic compromise,
- a settlement that leaves the company structurally uncompetitive,
- or a deal that preserves all legacy structures while postponing the hard decision.

## Final word
I will not endorse an arrangement that protects every site in principle but leaves the company uncompetitive in practice. The long-term value of the family holding’s core asset depends on restoring margins and competitiveness now, not preserving every structure at any cost.

If the employee side can show a real alternative, I will listen. If it cannot, then we have to be honest that structural restructuring sometimes requires hard footprint decisions.
