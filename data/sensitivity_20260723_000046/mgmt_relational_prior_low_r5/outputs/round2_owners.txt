I will respond plainly.

## To management
I agree with the core diagnosis: **doing nothing is not an option**. The company’s German cost base is too high, capacity is too large for current demand, and margins are not where they need to be. If we avoid structural change, we will destroy value more slowly but just as surely.

Where I do **not** agree is with any impression that a blunt closure program is automatically the right answer. A closure may be justified site by site, but only if management can show that:
- the economics truly require it,
- the alternatives have been tested seriously,
- and the implementation can be done without triggering a prolonged industrial breakdown that erodes more value than it saves.

So I want the numbers, not the rhetoric:
- site-by-site contribution to the cost gap,
- savings by measure,
- timeline to benefit,
- and the impact on future investment capacity.

If management cannot prove that a specific closure or capacity cut is necessary, then it should not assume the governance side will simply rubber-stamp it.

## To the Works Council
I respect the insistence on social stability and the company’s co-determination model. I also agree that trust matters, and that a restructuring which breaks labor relations can damage the enterprise.

But I do **not** accept the absolute line that every German plant must stay open and that compulsory redundancies are categorically impossible in all circumstances. That may be a defensible labor position, but it is not yet a viable answer to the structural problem.

If the business case shows permanent overcapacity, then some form of capacity reduction is unavoidable. The question is whether it happens through:
- voluntary exits,
- redeployment,
- working-time measures,
- product reallocations,
- or, where unavoidable, closures and job reductions.

I am open to alternatives, but they must be **credible in scale**. A package that preserves all sites, all guarantees, and the full cost structure is not a solution.

## To the Industrial Union
I agree that management and owners must contribute. That is proper stewardship. Top-layer savings, overhead discipline, and visible management restraint should absolutely be part of the package.

Where I disagree is your insistence that any structural adjustment is unacceptable if it affects sites or allows compulsory redundancies in principle. That is too absolute for the current situation.

You are right that labor should not carry the burden alone. But you have not yet shown an alternative package that achieves the same savings with equal certainty. If you want the family holding to back a no-closure route, then it has to be:
- quantified,
- enforceable,
- durable,
- and large enough to restore margins.

Otherwise, it is just a demand to delay the adjustment and hope the problem goes away.

## To the Regional State Government
I appreciate your concern for the industrial base and the region. I also understand the political constraints you face.

But I have to be equally clear: the state’s role is to safeguard long-term industrial value, not to freeze every structure in place. If the company’s footprint is permanently above what the market can support, then blocking all adjustment risks a worse outcome later — weaker margins, less investment, and potentially a smaller footprint anyway.

So I disagree with any suggestion that the state can or should veto structural change as a matter of principle. It should use its leverage to secure:
- a durable industrial plan,
- investment in future products,
- and a socially responsible restructuring.

But it should not assume that “no closures ever” is a sustainable governance position if the numbers do not support it.

## My position
The family holding will support a solution that:
1. restores competitiveness and margins,
2. reduces structural overcapacity,
3. protects the long-term value of the enterprise,
4. and does so through a negotiated, governable process.

What we will not support:
- a cosmetic compromise,
- a settlement that preserves an uncompetitive cost base,
- or a package that protects every legacy structure regardless of economics.

## What I can support
I am prepared to back:
- voluntary exit programs,
- internal transfers and qualification,
- working-time flexibility,
- temporary or targeted wage moderation,
- management overhead and executive cost reductions,
- and investment commitments tied to the restructuring.

I am also open to **avoiding closures where a credible alternative can deliver the same structural savings**. But “credible” means real numbers, real enforcement, and real durability — not aspirations.

## What I need next
Before anyone asks the governance side to block or endorse a path, I want three things on the table:
1. **A quantified target for cost and capacity reduction**
2. **A comparison of at least two alternatives**: one with closures, one without
3. **A binding package of contributions from labor, management, and owners**

If the no-closure route can genuinely deliver the economics, I will listen. If not, then we should stop pretending that principle alone can substitute for restructuring.

So yes: I am open to a negotiated settlement. But no: I will not support a deal that preserves value destruction in the name of avoiding hard decisions.
