[Works Council (Betriebsrat) (Plant-level employee representation under co-determination) — Round 1]
As Works Council, I will be very clear from the outset:

We will not accept plant closures in Germany and we will not accept compulsory redundancies. That is the line.

The workforce has already carried a heavy burden in this transformation, and it is not acceptable that management now tries to solve strategic mistakes by destroying jobs, breaking trust, and abandoning entire sites. A company of this size has alternatives: capacity adjustments, smarter working-time solutions, voluntary instruments, internal transfers, training and qualification, investment commitments, and a fair negotiated contribution from all sides.

What we will argue for is a restructuring that:
- keeps every German plant open,
- secures jobs in a binding way,
- preserves co-determination and the existing job-security framework,
- and includes a credible industrial plan through 2030, not just short-term cost cutting.

If management wants a solution, it must put a real, binding package on the table:
- site guarantees for all German plants,
- job guarantees through 2030,
- no forced layoffs,
- no unilateral breaking of existing protections,
- and genuine employee participation in shaping the transformation.

We are prepared to discuss changes that are fair, reversible, and socially responsible. We are not prepared to discuss closures, mass dismissals, or a fait accompli disguised as restructuring. If that is the direction being pursued, we will resist it with all means available to us, including industrial action and use of our co-determination rights.

## My expert assessment

### 1) The economic problem is real
For a large German automotive plant network, the combination of:
- declining European volumes,
- EV transition costs,
- elevated German labour and energy costs,
- high fixed manufacturing overhead,
- and excess installed capacity

usually creates a **fixed-cost absorption problem**, not just a wage problem.

That matters because:
- **variable cost measures** like temporary wage moderation or bonus restraint help,
- but **structural overcapacity** is mainly a fixed-cost issue.

If demand is permanently below the network’s designed capacity, then savings from:
- wage moderation,
- working-time flexibility,
- voluntary exits,
- and management cuts

may be **material** but often are **not sufficient on their own** unless they are very large and accompanied by genuine volume consolidation.

### 2) The decisive question is capacity utilization
In automotive manufacturing, the big swing factor is usually **plant loading**.

A rough rule of thumb:
- if a plant network is operating near or above **80–85% utilization**, it can often be stabilized with productivity measures and some labor-cost correction;
- if it is materially below that, fixed costs per vehicle rise sharply and the network becomes structurally uncompetitive.

In the situation described, management’s argument that there is **structural overcapacity** is economically credible. That does not automatically prove closures are the only answer, but it does mean any alternative must show:
- enough capacity removal,
- enough headcount reduction,
- and enough fixed-cost elimination.

### 3) No-closure packages can work only if they are genuinely structural
A no-closure package is not impossible, but it must do more than “save jobs.” It must remove cost permanently.

For example, a credible non-closure package would need some combination of:
- significant **volume reallocation** away from weak sites,
- conversion of some sites to lower-overhead functions,
- permanent elimination of shifts or lines,
- large-scale voluntary exits and redeployment,
- long-duration labor cost concessions,
- management and overhead reductions,
- and probably **product allocation commitments** to keep the remaining sites efficiently loaded.

What would **not** be enough:
- temporary wage freezes,
- one-off bonuses forgone for a year or two,
- vague promises of productivity,
- or soft capacity “adjustments” that leave the same plant footprint in place.

### 4) Closures are not the only way to cut capacity, but they are the cleanest way
From a pure manufacturing economics standpoint:
- closing a plant is the fastest way to remove fixed overhead,
- eliminate duplicate support functions,
- and simplify the network.

Alternative mechanisms can approximate the result, but usually with:
- more complexity,
- more governance risk,
- slower savings realization,
- and weaker certainty that the cost base really comes down.

So the economic hierarchy is usually:

1. **Plant closure / line shutdown**
2. **Site conversion plus permanent capacity reduction**
3. **Shift elimination / footprint reduction**
4. **Voluntary attrition / redeployment**
5. **Temporary labor concessions**

The lower you go on that list, the harder it is to achieve the same durable savings.

- supplier and purchasing savings,
- management contribution.

#### Industrial impact
- **Fixed-cost savings:** moderate unless voluntary exits are very large.
- **Capacity reduction:** indirect; mostly through fewer shifts, lower utilization, and product migration rather than site removal.
- **Headcount effect:** can be significant, but usually slower and less certain than closure-based reduction.
- **Durability:** weaker unless accompanied by hard product allocation and permanent overhead restructuring.

#### Main risks
- Savings may be too small relative to the overcapacity problem.
- Redeployment capacity can saturate quickly.
- Voluntary exit programs often bring the wrong mix of departures: more cost than volume can be lost.
- If the demand environment worsens, the company may face a second restructuring later.

#### Worker and site impact
- Lowest immediate displacement.
- Higher chance of preserving site identity and supplier continuity.
- But if it underdelivers, it can create a delayed crisis and more uncertainty.

### B. Limited-closure package

#### Typical instruments
- closing one plant or one unit,
- shutting one redundant production line,
- concentrating volume into fewer sites,
- voluntary exits plus compulsory measures if needed,
- stronger overhead and procurement cuts.

#### Industrial impact
- **Fixed-cost savings:** highest and most immediate.
- **Capacity reduction:** direct and measurable.
- **Headcount effect:** large at the affected site, plus some indirect reduction elsewhere.
- **Durability:** strongest if the closed footprint is truly redundant and volume is transferred without bottlenecks.

#### Main risks
- Loss of know-how and ramp-up friction at receiving sites.
- Logistics strain if transferred volume travels farther or through constrained corridors.
- Supplier disruption, especially for locally embedded suppliers.
- Industrial conflict risk is highest here.

#### Worker and site impact
- Severe local job loss.
- Strong impact on regional employment and subcontractors.
- Potential morale effects across the network if workers fear a precedent.

### C. Mixed package

#### Typical instruments
- one limited closure or partial footprint reduction,
- strong voluntary exits elsewhere,
- reduced shifts,
- product migration,
- labor-cost concessions,
- management/owner contribution,
- targeted investment commitments.

#### Industrial impact
- **Fixed-cost savings:** usually intermediate to high.
- **Capacity reduction:** substantial, but less abrupt than full closure-heavy restructuring.
- **Headcount effect:** spread across more sites, reducing shock concentration.
- **Durability:** better than a pure voluntary plan, less disruptive than a broad closure program.

#### Main risks
- Complexity of execution.
- The package can become too diluted if the closure element is too small and the other concessions are too modest.
- Requires careful sequencing to avoid losing volume before receiving sites are ready.

#### Worker and site impact
- Mixed local outcomes.
- Some sites protected, some materially downsized.
- Often the most plausible path to preserve a core industrial base while still achieving meaningful capacity correction.

## 4) Site-by-site impact logic

Without named plant data, I can only state the **site types** that matter:

### Sites most at risk in any restructuring
- plants with **low utilization**,
- plants with **single-model dependence** and weak future allocation,
- plants with **high logistics cost** or poor network integration,
- sites with **aging fixed assets** requiring high reinvestment,
- plants whose output can be absorbed elsewhere with manageable transport cost.

### Sites more resilient
- plants with:
  - multi-model flexibility,
  - EV or future-platform allocation,
  - strong logistics access,
  - lower unit labor cost,
  - and recent capital upgrades.

### Audit rule
A plant should be considered a realistic closure or major downsizing candidate only if it meets several of these:
1. persistent underutilization,
2. no credible future product,
3. higher-than-network cost per unit,
4. high capex required to stay competitive,
5. feasible transfer of output to other sites.

If a site has a future platform, strong logistics, and strategic model importance, closure is normally a **last-resort** industrial decision, not an obvious first choice.

## 5) Workforce consequences by package

### No-closure / no-forced-layoff
- Best for employment continuity.
- Requires large voluntary mobility and retraining.
- Risk of hidden pressure: if volumes keep falling, people may be retained in name but underutilized in practice.
- Can preserve skill base if training is tied to real model allocation.

### Limited-closure
- Highest displacement at the affected site.
- Needs robust redeployment and qualification if social damage is to be contained.
- Risk of “hollowing out” if closing a plant removes critical know-how that is not transferred in time.

### Mixed
- More balanced workforce adjustment.
- More manageable for redeployment planning.
- Usually the most feasible for preserving a broader skills base while still reducing overall headcount.

## 6) Supplier and logistics effects

### No-closure / no-forced-layoff
- Lowest supplier shock.
- Preserves current inbound logistics patterns.
- But if only labor cost is adjusted while product structure stays unchanged, the supply base may still face uncertainty.

### Limited-closure
- Highest local supplier damage.
- Transport distances can increase if production is consolidated.
- One-time logistics redesign may create temporary inefficiency and startup losses at receiving plants.

### Mixed
- Moderate supplier disruption.
- Allows phased migration and more controlled logistics redesign.
- Best chance of keeping critical suppliers engaged if transition timing is clear.

## 7) Implementation time

### No-closure / no-forced-layoff
- Fast to announce, slow to realize.
- Savings often spread over multiple years.
- Highly dependent on negotiation and take-up rates.

### Limited-closure
- Long lead time if industrial shutdown is orderly, because you need:
  - customer transfer,
  - tooling relocation,
  - supply-chain revalidation,
  - and workforce transition.
- But once executed, the cost base changes permanently.

### Mixed
- Typically the longest to design, but often the most workable.
- Phased implementation is essential.

## 8) Long-term site viability

From an industrial audit standpoint, “site viability” depends on whether the plant can survive with:
- competitive unit labor cost,
- credible future products,
- sufficient utilization,
- and manageable fixed overhead.

### No-closure package
- Preserves viability only if it includes a real product and volume plan.
- If it relies mainly on temporary labor concessions, site viability remains fragile.

### Limited-closure package
- Concentrates volume into fewer, stronger sites.
- Viable for remaining plants if execution is disciplined.
- Weakens the closed region permanently.

### Mixed package
- Best balance for network resilience if the company can preserve a core German production base while removing excess capacity.

## 9) Practical conclusion

If the objective is **maximum social continuity**, the no-closure package is best — but it is the riskiest on whether the company truly fixes structural overcapacity.

If the objective is **maximum permanent cost correction**, limited closures are most effective — but they carry the greatest workforce, supplier, and conflict costs.

If the objective is **durable restructuring with less total value destruction than a pure closure strategy**, the mixed package is usually the strongest industrial option.

Below is a neutral, industrial-economics response to the **open question now being asked of my mandate**: a side-by-side comparison of the three restructuring archetypes, with emphasis on **site-level logic, capacity, savings durability, workforce impact, implementation risk, and margin effect**.

## 1) Core conclusion

If the company truly has **structural overcapacity** and a **material fixed-cost problem**, then:

- a **no-closure / no-forced-layoff package** is the **least destructive socially**, but usually the **weakest** on permanent fixed-cost removal and the **most fragile** on execution;
- a **limited-closure package** is the **strongest** on durable cost and capacity correction, but also the **most disruptive** and highest-risk industrially;

## 2) How to judge the packages

The right comparison is not “which is nicest?” but:

1. **How much fixed cost is removed permanently?**
2. **How much capacity is taken out of the system?**
3. **How much headcount reduction is real and durable?**
4. **What is the implementation risk?**
5. **What is the conflict/disruption risk?**
6. **What is the margin impact by 2030?**

A package that looks good in year 1 but leaves the network structurally overloaded is not durable.  
A package that cuts hard but causes years of disruption can also destroy value.

## 3) Comparative assessment of the three packages

### A. No-closure / no-forced-layoff package

#### What it typically contains
- voluntary exits / early retirement
- internal transfers and redeployment
- working-time reductions
- wage moderation / bonus restraint
- overhead cuts
- shift reductions
- product reallocations
- management contribution

#### Likely industrial outcome
- **Fixed-cost savings:** moderate, not usually enough unless voluntary exits are very large and management/overhead cuts are substantial
- **Capacity reduction:** indirect and partial
- **Headcount effect:** meaningful, but slower and uncertain
- **Durability:** moderate to weak unless accompanied by hard product and volume changes

#### Main strengths
- Lowest social shock
- Lowest regional and supplier damage
- Lower strike probability if negotiated well

#### Main weaknesses
- Savings may be too small relative to the problem
- Execution depends on voluntary take-up
- Redeployment capacity can saturate
- Risk of a “second restructuring” later if demand weakens further

#### Bottom line
A no-closure package can work **only if**:
- voluntary exit numbers are very high,
- labor-cost concessions are material,
- overhead is genuinely cut,
- and production is reallocated in a way that permanently lowers network cost.

If those conditions are not met, it is likely **insufficient**.

---

### B. Limited-closure package

#### What it typically contains
- closure of one plant or one production footprint
- transfer of volume to other sites
- voluntary exits plus some involuntary adjustment if permitted
- stronger overhead, logistics, and procurement savings
- labor-cost concessions elsewhere

#### Likely industrial outcome
- **Fixed-cost savings:** strongest and most durable
- **Capacity reduction:** direct and measurable
- **Headcount effect:** large and immediate at the affected site
- **Durability:** high if receiving sites can absorb volume without bottlenecks

#### Main strengths
- Permanent reduction in network fixed cost
- Clear capacity correction
- Strongest answer if a site is truly redundant

#### Main weaknesses
- Highest local employment loss
- Highest supplier shock in affected region
- Highest strike/conflict risk
- Possible ramp-up losses at receiving sites

#### Bottom line
If one or more plants are genuinely underutilized, high-cost, and have no credible future product assignment, this is the **cleanest economic solution**.  
But it also carries the greatest industrial and political cost.

---

### C. Mixed package

#### What it typically contains
- one closure or major footprint reduction, but not a broad shutdown program
- major voluntary exits across the rest of the network
- shift reductions and working-time changes
- product reassignment
- management/owner contribution
- targeted investment commitments

#### Likely industrial outcome
- **Fixed-cost savings:** substantial, often closer to what the company needs
- **Capacity reduction:** meaningful and durable
- **Headcount effect:** spread across sites rather than concentrated entirely in one place
- **Durability:** better than a pure voluntary package, less disruptive than a closure-heavy one

#### Main strengths
- Balances economic correction and social containment
- More likely to be implementable than a pure closure strategy
- Less likely than broad closures to trigger a company-wide legitimacy crisis

#### Main weaknesses
- More complex to negotiate and execute
- Can become too diluted if the closure element is too small
- Requires strong sequencing and governance discipline

#### Bottom line
This is often the **best industrial compromise** if management needs real structural savings but wants to avoid maximum value destruction.

### Pathway 1: No-closure first, with pre-agreed review trigger
Negotiate a fully quantified no-closure package built around voluntary exits, redeployment, working-time changes, overhead cuts, management contribution, and investment commitments. Set an independent review date and pre-agree that if milestones are missed, site-level fallback options re-open under a structured process.

### Pathway 2: Mixed package
Preserve more sites, but require deeper permanent reductions elsewhere: management overhead cuts, product concentration, shift elimination, supplier renegotiation, and larger voluntary exit volumes. This gives labor and the state more protection while still delivering real structural change.

### Pathway 3: Site-by-site protection with fallback
Protect sites that meet defined productivity, product, and utilization thresholds, while subjecting weaker sites to a jointly governed review before any closure decision. This avoids blanket closure logic but preserves management’s insistence on performance discipline.

## 8. Open questions
- What exact permanent annual savings are required to make a no-closure package credible?
- How much capacity reduction is needed to restore sustainable utilization?
- What headcount reduction can be achieved voluntarily and through redeployment?
- What level of management and owner contribution is material enough?
- Which site-specific product allocations can support viability through 2030?
- How should conflict risk and supplier disruption be quantified in the comparison?
- Is the state shareholder willing to support a conditional fallback if a no-closure package falls modestly short?
- Can the workforce accept temporary labor contributions if matched by binding site and job protections?

## 9. Recommended next step
Commission an **independent site-by-site restructuring model** comparing three packages on the same factual basis:
1. no-closure / no-forced-layoff,
2. limited-closure,
3. mixed package.

The model should quantify annual savings, permanent capacity reduction, headcount impact, utilization by site, implementation timing, strike/disruption risk, supplier effects, and margin impact through 2030. Only then should the parties be asked to choose between a negotiated no-closure solution and a constrained footprint reduction.

## 10. Emergent insights that only appeared through multi-turn interaction
- The true dispute is not “restructure or not,” but **what counts as a credible equivalent** to closures under co-determined governance.
- The employee side did not merely reject cuts; it progressively clarified that it accepts restructuring if it is **voluntary, reversible, and shared**, which is a more nuanced stance than a simple no-change position.
- The capital side also became more precise: it is not closure as ideology, but closure as the currently most credible way to remove **permanent fixed cost** if alternatives cannot match the economics.
- The state shareholder’s role emerged as a distinct constraint: it is not just a political ally of labor, but a **blocking governance actor** balancing legitimacy, regional employment, and value preservation.
- The expert threshold sharpened the decision materially: a no-closure plan that does not permanently remove roughly **low-single-digit billions** in annual cost and materially reduce capacity is likely not a true alternative, only a delay mechanism.
- The conversation revealed that **implementation risk itself is economically material**: conflict, strikes, supplier disruption, and legitimacy loss are now central to evaluating whether a “cheaper” plan is actually cheaper.
- A viable compromise is most likely to come from **sequencing**: test a no-closure package under strict measurable milestones, while pre-agreeing a fallback review rather than forcing immediate closure decisions.
