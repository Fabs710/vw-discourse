## To the Works Council
I agree with you on one thing: **doing nothing is not an option**. I also agree that this has to be a durable 2030 plan, not a cosmetic fix.

But I fundamentally disagree with your core premise that **every German plant must stay open** and that **no compulsory redundancies** can ever be part of the solution. That is not a restructuring principle; it is a veto on structural change.

You keep saying closures are “destructive” and therefore unacceptable. What you are not facing is that preserving all sites may be more destructive in the medium term if it leaves the company with:
- a permanently uncompetitive cost base,
- excess fixed capacity,
- and no room to fund future products.

A company can preserve every plant on paper and still lose the ability to invest, compete, and employ people in the future. That is not protection. That is delay.

## To the Industrial Union
I agree that employees should not carry the whole burden. I agree that management and owners must contribute. I agree that voluntary instruments, working-time flexibility, qualification, and redeployment belong in the package.

What I do **not** accept is your attempt to turn “no closures, no forced redundancies” into a universal rule. You have not yet shown a package that achieves the same structural effect as capacity reduction through closures or equivalent footprint reduction.

And that is the key point:  
**I am not demanding closures as ideology. I am demanding durable structural savings.**

If you can deliver them through another route, I will consider it. But the route must be:
- quantified,
- enforceable,
- durable,
- and large enough to restore competitiveness.

A temporary wage moderation deal or a large voluntary program alone will not be enough if the underlying capacity problem remains.

## To the Regional State Government
I respect your role, and I understand your concern for jobs and the region. But I have to be direct: the state cannot simply declare that every site is protected and expect the economics to disappear.

If the company’s footprint is bigger than what demand supports, then protecting every legacy structure is not responsible stewardship. It is political comfort at the expense of future competitiveness.

I am open to a restructuring that preserves German industrial capacity. I am **not** open to a settlement that makes structural adjustment impossible in principle. If the state wants to prevent closures, then it must help produce an alternative that actually removes enough cost and capacity to restore margins.

## To the Controlling Family Holding
I agree with your central message: value destruction must be avoided, and the company needs discipline. I also agree that management has to prove its case site by site.

Where I disagree is with the suggestion that closure is only one option among many and should almost be avoided by default. That underestimates how serious the cost gap is.

Let me be blunt: if the business case shows persistent overcapacity and structurally high costs, then **not** closing or consolidating parts of the footprint can itself be value destruction. The burden is not on management to prove that adjustment is painful. The burden is on all of us to ensure the company survives competitively.

I do welcome your point that alternatives must be tested. Fine. But tested against what standard? The standard is not whether they feel acceptable. The standard is whether they close the gap.

## To the Institutional Investors
You are right to insist on a package that restores margins and is not cosmetic. I support that test.

What I do **not** accept is the idea that a no-closure package is acceptable simply because it sounds socially preferable. It must be economically equivalent. So far, it has not been shown to be.

You asked for numbers. Here is the hard truth: unless the alternative package produces a **material and permanent** reduction in fixed cost and capacity, it will not be enough. That means:
- not just temporary concessions,
- not just voluntary exits with uncertain uptake,
- not just transfer promises,
- but real network simplification and lower labour cost.

If the no-closure route can do that, good. If it cannot, then insisting on it is not prudence — it is denial.

## On the expert assessments
I appreciate the expert input. It confirms what management has been saying:  
this is a **fixed-cost and capacity problem**, not just a wage problem.

I agree with the expert that:
- temporary concessions alone are insufficient,
- structural overcapacity must be removed,
- and a purely voluntary package is execution-sensitive.

Where I part company is on the implication that closure is just the “cleanest” option among many. In reality, when capacity is structurally too high, closure or equivalent footprint reduction is often the only instrument that guarantees the cost base actually changes.

That is the uncomfortable truth everyone else is trying to avoid.
