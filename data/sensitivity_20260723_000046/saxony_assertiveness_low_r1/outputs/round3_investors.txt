I’ll be direct.

## Where we agree
I agree with management, the family owners, and the state on the core point: **the current cost structure is not acceptable**.  
If the German footprint stays structurally too expensive, then margins stay weak, the return-on-sales target remains out of reach, and equity value keeps leaking away. That is not tolerable from a shareholder perspective.

I also agree with the labor side on one important issue: **employees should not be the only adjustment variable**. A settlement that simply shifts pain onto the workforce while leaving management, owners, and overhead untouched would be too weak and too fragile.

And I agree with the expert’s general direction: **closures should not be the automatic first move** if serious non-closure measures can deliver enough savings.

## Where I disagree
My disagreement is with any position that treats **“no closures, no redundancies, every plant open to 2030”** as an outcome that can be accepted regardless of economics.

That is not a solution if it leaves the company with:
- excess capacity,
- structurally impaired margins,
- and a cost base that still underperforms competitors.

From a capital markets perspective, that would just be a delayed crisis. I would not support it.

## On the Works Council and Union
You are right to insist that the workforce should not carry the burden alone. But your red lines are too rigid as stated.

- **No compulsory redundancies**: acceptable only if the numbers genuinely work without them.
- **No plant closures**: acceptable only if the alternative package really achieves competitive margins.
- **2030 job and site guarantees**: acceptable only if they are conditional, time-bound, and tied to measurable savings and investment.

If the guarantees freeze the footprint before we know the economics work, then they become value-destroying, not protective.

I do not accept a settlement that preserves industrial comfort but fails the financial test.

## On management
Management is right to insist that the issue is structural, not cosmetic. But I want to be equally clear: management must now do more than say “the gap is large.”

What I need is a **site-by-site and measure-by-measure quantified bridge**:
- how much comes from working-time changes,
- how much from bonus restraint and wage moderation,
- how much from redeployment and attrition,
- how much from productivity gains,
- how much from product and volume reallocation,
- and how much remains after all that.

Without that, “closures may be necessary” remains an assertion, not a case.

## On the state government
Your insistence that closures be a last resort is reasonable. I support a disciplined sequence. But “last resort” must be defined by numbers, not by politics.

If alternatives are truly tested and still leave a large structural gap, then some footprint reduction must remain possible. The state should not turn “alternatives first” into a permanent veto on adjustment.

## On the controlling family owners
You are right that plant closures are permanent and should not be used casually. I agree. But I also need clarity: if the quantified alternative package does **not** close the gap, will you support the hard decision that follows?

Ownership cannot want competitiveness restored while refusing the instruments that may be required to do it.

## What would move us
We would support a settlement if it delivers all of the following:

1. **A credible, quantified cost-gap diagnosis**
   - including plant-level economics.

2. **A serious non-closure package**
   - working-time flexibility,
   - bonus restraint,
   - temporary wage moderation,
   - redeployment,
   - retraining,
   - productivity improvements,
   - management-side restraint.

3. **A real capacity reduction if needed**
   - not necessarily full closures first,
   - but enough footprint change to restore competitiveness.

4. **Conditional employment/site protections**
   - only to the extent they do not block the restructuring.

5. **Deal certainty**
   - no endless renegotiation after concessions are made.

## What we will not support
We will not support:
- a cosmetic compromise,
- a deal that leaves structural overcapacity untouched,
- a package that misses the return-on-sales target again,
- or a prolonged conflict that burns cash and destroys confidence.

We also will not support a settlement that asks capital to absorb lower returns indefinitely while the company avoids the hard decisions.

## My position on the expert’s framework
The expert’s hierarchy is sensible:
1. non-closure restructuring,
2. partial footprint rationalization,
3. closure only as last resort.

I can support that sequence. But it only works if the first step is **quantified, durable, and large enough** to matter. If not, then the later steps must stay on the table.

## What I need next
The discussion should now move from principles to numbers.

Until that model exists, I am not prepared to endorse any “guarantee” that simply locks in the current cost base.

## Bottom line
Our test is simple:

**Does the package credibly restore competitive margins on a durable basis?**

- If yes, we can support it.
- If no, we won’t.

So:
- **Yes** to serious alternatives first.
- **Yes** to shared burden.
- **Yes** to conditional protections.
- **No** to cosmetic settlements.
- **No** to preserving overcapacity by default.
- **No** to anything that leaves shareholder value structurally impaired.
