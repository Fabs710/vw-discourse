Below is a restructuring-focused assessment, not a legal or political one.

## Executive view

Based on the information provided, **closures are not automatically necessary as the first move**. In a large German automotive restructuring, it is often possible to generate **material savings through non-closure measures**—working-time adjustments, temporary pay and bonus restraint, redeployment, process productivity, purchasing/logistics efficiencies, headcount attrition, and product allocation changes.

However, whether those alternatives are **sufficient** depends on one central missing input: **the size of the structural annual cost gap that management must close**. Without that number, nobody can honestly say whether the non-closure package can fully substitute for footprint reduction.

My expert judgment is:

- **Non-closure measures can plausibly deliver meaningful savings**, often enough to bridge a **moderate** cost gap.
- If the gap is **large and structural**—for example, a permanent overcapacity problem with underutilized plants, weak utilization, and a multi-hundred-million-euro annual burden—then **some footprint reduction may become necessary**, but that does **not automatically mean plant closures**.
- A proper last-resort test should compare:
  1. non-closure savings,
  2. partial footprint reduction short of closure,
  3. full closure scenarios,
  4. and the time path to realize each.

In most cases, the right sequence is **first exhaust non-closure measures**, then test **partial footprint rationalization**, and only then consider **closure** if the economics still do not work.

## What can realistically be saved without closures?

### 1) Working-time reduction / short-time-like measures
**Plausible annual savings:** medium, but not huge on its own.

If a plant or multiple sites reduce hours, shift patterns, overtime, and agency labor, savings come from:
- lower paid hours,
- reduced shift premiums,
- lower overtime,
- fewer temporary workers,
- better line balancing.

**Order of magnitude:** often in the range of **low double-digit millions to perhaps low hundreds of millions euros annually** across a large footprint, depending on scale.

**Limitation:** this only works if production volume falls and utilization can remain operationally stable. It does not eliminate fixed-site overhead.

### 2) Temporary wage restraint / bonus moderation / special payments
**Plausible annual savings:** medium.

If the workforce agrees to:
- suspend or reduce special payments,
- cap bonuses,
- moderate tariff-related supplements,
- or accept temporary wage concessions,

the effect can be significant in a large German OEM workforce.

**Order of magnitude:** for a major domestic workforce, this can easily be **tens of millions to several hundred million euros annually**, depending on what is included and how broad the contribution is.

**Limitation:** this is usually **temporary or politically fragile**. It helps bridge a crisis, but it is not by itself a structural footprint fix.

### 3) Redeployment and internal mobility
**Plausible savings:** medium, but mostly through avoided severance and avoided external hiring.

Redeployment does not always reduce wage cost immediately, but it avoids:
- compulsory layoffs,
- recruitment costs,
- social plan costs,
- knowledge loss,
- and the inefficiency of carrying understaffed growth units while paying for excess in shrinking units.

**Order of magnitude:** direct annual P&L savings are variable; the biggest effect is often **avoided restructuring cost** and better utilization of existing labor.

### 4) Training/requalification for EV, battery, software, logistics roles
**Plausible savings:** indirect, medium to high over time.

This is often not a “cost cut” in year one; it is a **cost avoidance** and productivity measure. It can:
- reduce external hiring,
- improve throughput,
- support product mix changes,
- and preserve employment while shifting capacity.

**Limitation:** it is not a substitute for a structurally oversized footprint unless new products and volumes actually materialize.

### 5) Productivity improvements
This is often the **largest non-closure lever**, but also the one most often overstated.

Examples:
- line efficiency,
- scrap reduction,
- logistics simplification,
- maintenance optimization,
- supplier consolidation,
- reducing internal complexity,
- platform sharing,
- better sequencing and capacity smoothing.

**Plausible annual savings:** potentially **high**, especially in a complex German OEM environment.

A mature automotive plant network can sometimes extract **1–3% of manufacturing cost base** per year in sustainable productivity, and more if complexity is excessive. Across a very large operation, that can become **high tens of millions to low hundreds of millions euros annually**.

**Limitation:** productivity gains do not fully offset major fixed-cost overcapacity. They buy time and lower the required footprint reduction, but they do not magically remove structural excess plant cost.

### 6) Product and volume reallocation
This is often the most important non-closure lever if the company has multiple plants and model families.

By shifting:
- higher-margin models,
- EV volumes,
- battery-adjacent operations,
- or export production

into existing German sites, you can improve utilization and spread fixed costs.

**Plausible savings:** potentially very large, but only if demand and product allocation support it.

**Limitation:** this depends on product pipeline, platform timing, and market demand. If there is not enough volume, this lever is constrained.

## Can non-closure measures match closure economics?

### Short answer
**Sometimes yes, sometimes no.**

It depends on:
- the annual structural gap,
- the degree of plant underutilization,
- whether the company can shift profitable volume into existing plants,
- and the durability of labor concessions.

### A practical rule of thumb
Non-closure measures can often cover:
- **a moderate gap** if the company is mainly burdened by high labor cost, complexity, and poor utilization,
- but they rarely fully solve:
  - a **severe fixed-cost overcapacity problem**,
  - especially if one or more plants are structurally underloaded.

If management is talking about a **permanent cost gap** that is large enough to threaten return-on-sales, then:
- labor concessions alone are usually insufficient,
- productivity alone is usually insufficient,
- and redeployment alone is usually insufficient.

In that case, **some capacity reduction** becomes likely.

But crucially: **capacity reduction does not necessarily mean plant closure**. It may mean:
- fewer shifts,
- fewer lines,
- reduced model allocation,
- consolidation of overlapping production,
- or running a plant below historic size but still open.

## What is the likely savings hierarchy?

From most to least plausible in the near term:

1. **Reduce overtime, agency labor, and shift premiums**
2. **Temporary wage/bonus restraint**
3. **Productivity and complexity reduction**
4. **Redeployment and attrition**
5. **Product/volume reallocation**
6. **Partial footprint rationalization**
7. **Full plant closure**

The last item is the most drastic and usually the most expensive socially and politically. It may also be economically rational in some cases, but it should be the **end of the analysis**, not the beginning.

## What minimum footprint is “necessary for competitiveness”?

You cannot define a minimum footprint without a full footprint model. But the key metrics are:

- **capacity utilization by plant**
- **fixed cost per vehicle**
- **labor cost per vehicle**
- **logistics cost**
- **product complexity**
- **investment intensity per site**
- **margin contribution by model/site**
- **ability to absorb EV transition volumes**

A plant can be retained if it has:
- adequate utilization,
- competitive labor productivity,
- future model allocation,
- and acceptable logistics.

A plant is at risk if it has:
- persistent low utilization,
- no future product,
- high fixed costs,
- and no realistic path to improvement.

So the “minimum footprint” should be derived from:
1. projected demand,
2. product allocation,
3. realistic utilization targets,
4. and competitive cost benchmarks.

If management has not shown those numbers, then “minimum footprint” is not yet an analytical conclusion; it is a negotiating position.

## What would I need to assess this properly?

To determine whether closures are truly necessary, I would need:

1. **Current plant-by-plant utilization**
2. **Fixed and variable cost per site**
3. **Labor cost per vehicle by plant and product**
4. **Capacity and demand forecast by model through 2030**
5. **Current and proposed headcount by function/site**
6. **Product allocation and timing of next-gen models**
7. **Expected savings from each alternative measure**
8. **Cost of closure versus cost of keeping a plant open under a leaner model**
9. **Likely severance/social plan/transfer costs**
10. **Investment requirements by site**

Without this, any claim that closures are necessary is only partially substantiated.

## My structured assessment of the options

### Option A: Deep non-closure restructuring
Includes:
- working-time measures,
- temporary wage restraint,
- bonus moderation,
- productivity programs,
- redeployment,
- qualification,
- management compensation restraint,
- product reallocation.

**Assessment:** very plausible to deliver **substantial savings** and should be tested first.  
**Risk:** may not fully close a large structural gap.  
**Best use:** bridge the company through a demand trough and buy time for product transition.

### Option B: Non-closure restructuring plus partial footprint reduction
Includes:
- shift reduction,
- line consolidation,
- site specialization,
- partial insourcing/outsourcing,
- reduction of surplus capacity without full closure.

**Assessment:** often the economically strongest compromise if non-closure savings are not enough.  
**Best use:** when overcapacity is real but full closure is unnecessarily destructive.

### Option C: Plant closure
Includes:
- full site shutdown,
- relocation of volume,
- workforce reduction.

**Assessment:** only justified if:
- utilization is structurally poor,
- no future product is allocated,
- alternatives fail to bridge the cost gap,
- and closure economics outperform every other option net of social and transition costs.

**Important:** closure is usually the **most irreversible** and often the **highest one-off cost** solution, even if it produces longer-term fixed-cost relief.

## Bottom line

My expert conclusion is:

- **Yes, substantial savings can be achieved without closures or compulsory redundancies.**
- **No, that does not mean closures are never needed.**
- **Closures should only be considered after a quantified comparison shows that non-closure measures and partial footprint rationalization cannot close the structural gap.**
- In a German automotive group with co-determination constraints, the most realistic path is often a **layered compromise**: significant labor and management contributions, productivity improvements, product reallocation, and only then—if still necessary—limited footprint reduction.
