I will respond clearly from the perspective of the regional state government and anchor shareholder.

## Where I agree
I agree with management, the family owners, and the investors on one point: **the current situation is not sustainable**. The company does need a durable competitiveness response, and the workforce cannot be asked to bear no adjustment at all.

I also agree with the union and works council that **employees are not the only stakeholders who must contribute**. Management, owners, and the wider corporate side must carry part of the burden too.

## Where I disagree
What I do **not** accept is the current framing that plant closures in Germany and compulsory redundancies are simply the natural or unavoidable starting point.

That is too blunt, too destructive, and too risky for the region and for the company’s long-term legitimacy.

A restructuring that damages the regional industrial base, breaks trust with the workforce, and sidelines co-determination will not be a stable solution. It may create short-term accounting relief, but it risks long-term political, social, and industrial damage.

## To management
You say the footprint has to shrink and that the alternatives must be “sufficient in scale.” I understand that logic. But I have not yet heard a convincing case that you have genuinely exhausted the full range of alternatives before turning to closures.

That matters.

If closures are treated as the default instrument, then the burden is being shifted too far onto workers and the region. I cannot support that.

What I would need to see is:
- a **time-bound restructuring plan**,
- a **real comparison of alternatives**,
- quantified savings from non-closure measures,
- and a clear explanation of why those measures are insufficient before any closure decision is even contemplated.

Right now, I hear a lot about necessity, but not enough about serious testing of other paths.

## To the works council and union
I support your insistence that this cannot become a one-sided cut-and-close program. That said, I also have to be honest: if every plant, every job, and every guarantee is treated as absolutely untouchable, we may end up blocking the very adaptation the company needs.

So I cannot endorse a position that simply says “nothing changes.” That would also be irresponsible.

What I can support is a negotiated package that:
- preserves sites **wherever possible**,
- avoids forced redundancies **as far as feasible**,
- uses internal redeployment, qualification, working-time adjustments, and productivity measures first,
- and ties any employee contribution to **binding investment and employment safeguards**.

That is the path I see as politically and economically defensible.

## On plant closures
Let me be explicit: **I do not support closures as the first or preferred answer**.

Once a plant is closed, the effects go far beyond that site:
- jobs are lost,
- suppliers are weakened,
- local purchasing power falls,
- and the region loses industrial capacity that is very difficult to rebuild.

That is why closures need to be a last resort, not a convenient lever.

If management wants to argue that one or more closures are unavoidable, then it must prove:
1. that alternatives were genuinely exhausted,
2. that the impact on the region is minimized,
3. that the company’s future footprint in Germany remains credible,
4. and that the burden-sharing is fair.

Without that, I will not support it.

## On compulsory redundancies
I strongly prefer to avoid them. They would undermine trust and create unnecessary social damage.

If workforce reduction is unavoidable, then it must be handled through the most protective instruments available:
- voluntary solutions first,
- redeployment,
- qualification,
- natural attrition,
- and negotiated social safeguards.

Compulsory redundancies should not be the easy fallback.

## On job and site guarantees
I understand why the workforce is demanding binding guarantees. They are not asking for a free lunch; they are asking for security before accepting sacrifice.

A rigid guarantee through 2030 may be too absolute in some formulations, but there must be a **credible, enforceable protection framework**. Otherwise, any concession from labour will be viewed — rightly — as a temporary surrender that can be broken later.

So I am open to:
- time-limited site protection,
- conditional employment commitments,
- and staged review points,

but only if the restructuring package really preserves the industrial base and provides a future for the German footprint.

## On burden-sharing
If employees contribute, then the other sides must also contribute visibly and measurably.

That means:
- management restraint,
- owner support,
- investment commitments,
- and a real industrial plan for future products in Germany.

I will not support a model where the workforce gives concessions while ownership and management ask for sacrifice without making their own contribution visible.

## My bottom line
I am prepared to support a negotiated restructuring if it meets these conditions:

1. **Alternatives to closures are genuinely exhausted first**  
2. **German sites are preserved wherever possible**  
3. **Forced redundancies are avoided as far as feasible**  
4. **Burden-sharing is fair across all stakeholder groups**  
5. **Co-determination remains intact and meaningful**  
6. **There is a credible investment and product strategy for Germany**

What I cannot support is a package that:
- makes closures the starting assumption,
- treats German plants as disposable,
- sidelines the workforce,
- or weakens the company’s role as a regional economic anchor.

So my position remains: **restructuring yes, value-destroying closures first no**. If management wants my support, it must bring a serious, quantified, negotiated alternative that protects the region and the company’s future.
