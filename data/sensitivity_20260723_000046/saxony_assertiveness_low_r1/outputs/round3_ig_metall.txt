I will respond directly.

## Where I agree
I agree with the works council, the state government, and the family owners on one central point: **closures and forced redundancies must not be the starting point**.  
If management wants labour peace, it has to stop treating the workforce as the default adjustment variable.

I also agree that the situation is serious and that a restructuring is necessary. We are not defending the status quo for its own sake. The company needs a durable solution, not another temporary patch.

And yes: **employees cannot be the only group contributing**. If labour is asked for sacrifices, then management, owners, and the broader corporate side must also make visible contributions.

## Where I disagree
I do **not** accept management’s framing that plant closures and compulsory redundancies remain casually “on the table” simply because they are convenient ways to close a cost gap.

That is not a neutral technical position. It is a choice to shift the entire burden onto workers and communities.

I also do **not** accept the investor line that “deal certainty” requires keeping closures available as a threat. Threat-based bargaining may satisfy a spreadsheet, but it destroys trust and makes a durable settlement harder, not easier.

## To management
You keep saying the footprint must shrink and the savings must be structural. Fine — show the structural savings **without** making closures and layoffs the default answer.

What I need from management is not another abstract insistence on “minimum footprint,” but a quantified package with real burden-sharing, for example:

- working-time reduction and flexibility,
- redeployment across sites,
- qualification and retraining,
- productivity and process improvements,
- bonus restraint and management-side sacrifice,
- product and volume reallocation,
- and a temporary employee contribution.

If those measures are insufficient, then we can discuss what else is required — but only after the non-destructive options have been seriously negotiated and implemented.

What I will not accept is this logic:
1. declare German labour costs too high,
2. insist on plant closures and redundancies as a structural tool,
3. and then ask the workforce to validate the outcome after the fact.

That is not bargaining. That is coercion.

## On “minimum footprint”
Management and investors keep using the phrase as if it were self-evident. It isn’t.

Who defines it?
On what basis?
And why should the workforce accept a footprint decision that has not been proven against alternatives?

If there is a genuine need to resize, then let’s see the numbers:
- what savings come from shorter working time,
- what savings come from redeployment,
- what savings come from temporary wage restraint,
- what savings come from management compensation restraint,
- and what savings come from productivity agreements.

Only then can anyone honestly claim that irreversible measures are necessary.

## To the works council
We are aligned on the essentials:
- no compulsory redundancies,
- no one-sided burden-sharing,
- no erosion of collective standards,
- and no fait accompli.

I support your insistence that any labour contribution must be tied to site and job security. That is the right bargaining logic. If employees concede, they must get something durable in return.

Where I would sharpen the point is this: **a temporary contribution is possible; a permanent weakening of the collective wage framework is not**.  
I am prepared to discuss:
- temporary wage restraint,
- bonus moderation,
- working-time measures,
- and flexibility,

but only if they are:
- time-limited,
- proportionate,
- and linked to enforceable protections.

## To the state government
Your “alternatives first” position is the right one — but it has to be real. We need a process that is:
- time-bound,
- measurable,
- and not designed to drift into closure by exhaustion.

The state is right to reject a one-sided cut-and-close program. But it must also press for a deal that produces actual savings and is not just a polite delay of the same conflict.

## To the family owners
You say you oppose value destruction and want alternatives tested first. Good. Then support a package that gives workers reason to trust that promise.

That means:
- backing a binding employment framework,
- supporting site security where feasible,
- accepting that labour cannot be the only contributor,
- and being prepared to sacrifice some near-term return to secure a stable long-term settlement.

If ownership wants employee restraint, ownership must also show restraint.

## To the investors
You are right about one thing: a settlement that leaves the company uncompetitive is not acceptable.  
But you are wrong if you think the only route to competitiveness is to keep closure and layoffs as leverage over the workforce.

A prolonged strike, lasting distrust, and the destruction of the collective wage framework would also damage long-term value. You do not get “certainty” by making labour feel permanently insecure.

If you want a durable outcome, back a package that combines:
- real cost reduction,
- meaningful management and owner contribution,
- protection against compulsory redundancies,
- and a structured, time-bound labor contribution.

## What the union is prepared to discuss
We are prepared to negotiate a serious package that includes:

- temporary working-time reductions,
- internal redeployment,
- retraining and qualification,
- productivity commitments,
- bonus restraint,
- and a temporary employee contribution.

But I want to be explicit about the conditions:

1. **No compulsory redundancies**
2. **No German plant closures as part of the negotiated package**
3. **Binding job protection for the restructuring period**
4. **Binding site protection**
5. **Preservation of the collective wage framework**
6. **Visible contributions from management and owners**

## What is not acceptable
These are our limits:

- A wage-cut-only solution is not acceptable.
- A restructuring based on threats of plant closure is not acceptable.
- A package that weakens collective bargaining for years is not acceptable.
- A deal where workers pay twice — through lower wages and job losses — is not acceptable.

## Bottom line
The union is ready for a fair shared-sacrifice settlement.  
But “shared sacrifice” means exactly that: **shared**.

If management and the capital side want our cooperation, they need to stop demanding that employees absorb the entire shock. Bring a quantified, time-bound alternative package with real contributions from management and owners, and with binding protection against forced redundancies. Then we can talk seriously.

If not, then I will say plainly: this will not be settled by pressure alone.
