I will be direct.

I agree with management on one core point: **the current cost and capacity structure is not sustainable**. If the company does not restore competitiveness and margins, we will all be dealing with a much worse outcome later — weaker investment capacity, lower resilience, and eventually a more disorderly restructuring. That is not in the owners’ interest, and it is not in the workforce’s interest either.

But I do **not** agree that the only credible route is to go straight to plant closures and forced redundancies. At this stage, that is not a self-evident necessity; it is a choice with very high value destruction risk. Once a plant is closed, the loss is permanent: industrial know-how, supplier relationships, regional trust, and corporate reputation do not come back easily. As a controlling owner, I cannot treat that as a routine cost-cutting measure.

So my position is:

### Where I agree
- **Status quo is not acceptable.**
- The company needs **durable structural savings**, not cosmetic adjustments.
- The burden cannot fall on employees alone.
- Any solution must protect the company’s ability to invest in future products and technology.

### Where I disagree
- I do **not** support a default strategy of German plant closures.
- I do **not** accept forced redundancies as a first-choice instrument.
- I do **not** support open-ended guarantees being rejected outright simply because they constrain management convenience.

### To the Works Council and the Union
You are right to insist that the workforce cannot be the only side paying for this. I also take seriously your demand for a credible industrial future in Germany. But I have to be equally clear: **a blanket “no closures, no compulsory redundancies, no structural change” line would be a mistake if it leaves the company uncompetitive**. Protecting every site at any cost is not stewardship; it is deferred damage.

What I can support is a package that preserves as much as possible **if** it delivers real and measurable competitiveness gains. That means:
- substantial capacity reduction,
- meaningful labour-cost contribution,
- internal redeployment and working-time flexibility,
- productivity improvements,
- and binding investment commitments tied to the restructuring.

If there is a credible alternative that achieves the necessary savings without closures, I would prefer that. But it must be real, not rhetorical, and it must close the margin gap. Symbolic sacrifices will not be enough.

### To the Regional State Government
I understand your concern about regional damage, and I share the view that a reckless closure-first approach would be short-sighted. Your insistence on exhausting alternatives is reasonable.

However, I would caution against turning the company into a permanently protected regional employment program. The business must remain competitive in the market, not just politically acceptable. If the company’s economics do not improve, then everyone loses — including the region.

So yes: exhaust alternatives first. But if alternatives do not produce a structurally viable footprint, then ownership cannot simply endorse indefinite overcapacity. That would be irresponsible.

### My governance position
As controlling shareholders, our duty is long-term value preservation. That means:
- **no value-destructive stalemate**,
- **no superficial compromise that postpones the hard decision**,
- but also **no unnecessary destruction of productive assets**.

I am therefore prepared to support a negotiated restructuring only if it includes:
1. **verifiable structural savings**,
2. **a credible path to lower German operating costs**,
3. **durable labour peace**,
4. **real investment commitments**, and
5. **a footprint decision based on necessity, not ideology**.

If management can show that these savings are achievable without closures, that is the preferable route. If it cannot, then we should not pretend that emotional opposition changes the economics. But before moving to plant closures, I would want to see that every serious alternative has genuinely been exhausted.

So my message is simple: **I support competitiveness, not self-inflicted damage; restructuring, not permanent impairment; and a negotiated solution, but only if it is economically credible.**
