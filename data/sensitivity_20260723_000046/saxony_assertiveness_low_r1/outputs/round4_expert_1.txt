## Executive assessment

Based on the positions laid out, **plant closures are not automatically necessary as the first move**. In a large German automotive footprint, there is usually substantial room to generate savings through non-closure measures first, especially when the current issue is a combination of:
- underutilized capacity,
- high fixed labour and overhead costs,
- expensive shift patterns,
- excess complexity,
- and weak product allocation.

However, whether closures become necessary depends on **the size of the structural gap**. If the company needs to remove, say, several hundred million euros of annual cost and the non-closure package can only reliably deliver a fraction of that, then some **permanent footprint reduction** may still be required. The key is to quantify the gap properly before treating closure as inevitable.

## 1) What non-closure measures can realistically save

For a large German carmaker, a serious non-closure package can often deliver savings in the following bands:

### A. Working-time measures / short-time-like capacity adjustment
- Temporary working-time reduction
- shift optimization
- reduced overtime
- lower agency labour

**Plausible annual savings:**  
- roughly **€50m–€200m** depending on scale and baseline shift premium structure

These measures are strongest where plants have persistent slack capacity and expensive shift systems.

### B. Wage moderation / bonus restraint / special-payment reductions
If negotiated, temporary or multi-year restraint on special payments, variable pay, or allowance structures can create meaningful savings.

**Plausible annual savings:**  
- roughly **€75m–€250m**

This is often underestimated in public debate because the savings are spread across many cost lines.

### C. Productivity and complexity reduction
Includes:
- line balancing
- lower variant complexity
- fewer indirect staff per vehicle
- material flow simplification
- lean improvements
- reduced internal logistics waste

**Plausible annual savings:**  
- roughly **€100m–€300m** over 1–3 years

This is one of the largest non-closure levers, but it requires disciplined execution and a real management commitment.

### D. Redeployment and attrition
If the issue is overstaffing rather than immediate demand collapse, natural attrition and internal redeployment can remove a substantial number of positions without forced redundancies.

**Plausible annual savings:**  
- roughly **€50m–€150m** initially, rising over time

This is slower than plant closure but often politically and operationally less destructive.

### E. Management-side contribution
Includes:
- executive pay restraint
- overhead cuts at HQ and brand functions
- travel, procurement, support-function rationalization
- reduction in duplicated governance layers

**Plausible annual savings:**  
- roughly **€20m–€100m**

Small relative to labour, but important symbolically and for burden-sharing credibility.

### F. Product allocation / volume reallocation
If German plants are underfilled, securing additional model or component volume can materially improve fixed-cost absorption.

**Plausible annual savings / margin impact:**  
- roughly **€100m–€400m equivalent**, depending on whether the incremental volume is truly additional or just shifted from elsewhere

This is often the single most valuable “closure-avoidance” lever if future product mandates are available.

## 2) Aggregate savings from non-closure measures

A credible package combining the above could plausibly deliver:

- **Low case:** €200m–€300m per year
- **Base case:** €350m–€600m per year
- **High case:** €600m–€900m per year

That range is wide because the actual outcome depends on:
- baseline overcapacity,
- union willingness to accept time-limited wage/working-time concessions,
- whether product allocation can be changed,
- and whether management can execute productivity improvements credibly.

## 3) What closures add

A plant closure, or equivalent permanent footprint removal, typically yields:
- elimination of fixed overhead,
- removal of underutilized asset base,
- lower indirect labour,
- simplification of logistics and planning,
- permanent reduction in maintenance and support costs.

But it also brings:
- one-time severance and restructuring costs,
- asset impairment,
- relocation costs,
- supplier disruption,
- and often a long implementation timeline.

### Plausible annual savings from closure
For a mid-size German plant in automotive manufacturing, depending on scale:
- **€150m–€400m+ annual savings** is plausible after ramp-down and normalization

### One-time costs
Very often substantial:
- **€200m–€800m+** per plant, sometimes more if assets are specialized or employee counts are large

### Payback
- often **2–5 years**, but can be longer if closure costs are very high or if lost volume has to be replaced elsewhere at higher cost

So closures can be economically powerful, but they are not “free savings.” They are a trade-off between durable fixed-cost reduction and high social/operational disruption.

## 4) Partial footprint reduction as the most likely compromise

In many German industrial restructurings, the most effective compromise is not “all alternatives” or “full closure,” but **partial footprint reduction**, such as:
- reducing one shift,
- converting a site to a narrower product set,
- consolidating functions,
- shifting part of production to another site,
- closing only a line or hall,
- or reducing headcount through attrition and targeted voluntary exits.

### Why this matters
Partial footprint reduction can capture much of the closure benefit while avoiding:
- full site shutdown,
- total loss of regional industrial presence,
- and the most severe governance conflict.

### Plausible economics
- **Annual savings:** often **€100m–€300m per site equivalent**
- **One-time costs:** lower than full closure, but still material
- **Implementation:** faster than full closure if the remaining site remains operational

This is often the economically rational middle path if the non-closure package closes most but not all of the gap.

## 5) Return on sales impact

If the brand is missing its return-on-sales target, the key question is how much operating margin improvement is needed.

As a rough analytical rule:
- every **€100m** of recurring operating savings adds roughly **0.1–0.2 percentage points** of ROS on a large revenue base, depending on turnover scale

So:
- a **€300m** recurring saving package might improve ROS by around **0.3–0.6 percentage points**
- a **€600m** package might improve ROS by **0.6–1.2 percentage points**

That is enough to matter materially, but if the structural gap is larger than this, then footprint changes become harder to avoid.

## 6) My view on whether closures are necessary

### Closures are **not necessarily necessary** if:
- the cost gap is moderate rather than severe,
- product allocation can be improved,
- temporary labour concessions are substantial,
- and productivity/complexity reduction is real rather than symbolic.

In that case, a negotiated non-closure package plus partial footprint adjustments may be sufficient.

### Closures become **more likely necessary** if:
- demand is structurally lower than installed capacity,
- the company has multiple underfilled sites,
- non-closure measures only deliver short-lived savings,
- and the residual gap remains several hundred million euros after all alternatives.

Then closure or equivalent permanent capacity removal is likely the only way to restore the cost base durably.

## 7) What is missing for a proper decision

To assess properly, I would need:
1. **Plant-by-plant fixed and variable cost data**
2. **Installed capacity vs. utilization by site**
3. **Labour cost per vehicle or per unit**
4. **Product allocation plan for the next 3–5 years**
5. **One-time restructuring costs by option**
6. **Demand forecast by model and region**
7. **Overhead duplication analysis**
8. **Sensitivity analysis for wage, energy, and volume assumptions**

Without that, any statement that closures are “required” is only a hypothesis, not a proven conclusion.

## Bottom line

From a restructuring perspective, the most plausible sequence is:

1. **Test non-closure measures seriously**
2. **Use partial footprint reduction if a gap remains**
3. **Reserve full closure for the true last resort**

In other words:  
**closures are not the default necessity; they are the fallback if quantified non-closure and partial-footprint measures fail to close the structural gap.**
