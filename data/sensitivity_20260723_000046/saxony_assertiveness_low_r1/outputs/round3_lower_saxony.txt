I will respond directly as the regional state government and anchor shareholder.

## Where I agree
I agree with management, the family owners, and investors on one thing: **the status quo is not sustainable**. The company needs a durable competitiveness response, not a temporary patch. I also agree that employees cannot be the only group asked to contribute.

And I agree with the works council and union on another point: **we cannot solve this by simply pushing the burden onto workers and the region**. A solution that destroys trust, social peace, and the industrial base in our state is not a stable solution.

## Where I disagree with management
Management keeps saying closures and compulsory redundancies remain on the table because the gap must be closed. I understand that argument — but I do **not** accept closures as a first-line instrument, and I do **not** accept compulsory redundancies as the default fallback.

What I have not yet seen is a convincing demonstration that all alternatives have truly been exhausted before moving to irreversible steps. That matters, because closures are not just an accounting measure. They mean:

- loss of jobs,
- damage to suppliers,
- loss of regional purchasing power,
- and permanent weakening of industrial capability in our state.

If management wants support for hard measures, it must first prove that non-closure alternatives cannot close the gap. Right now, I see assertion, but not yet sufficient proof.

## Where I disagree with the works council and union
I support your insistence that plant closures and forced redundancies must not be the starting point. That is correct.

But I cannot endorse a position that effectively says **every German site must remain open under all circumstances, with no room for adjustment at all**. That would also be irresponsible if it leaves the company structurally uncompetitive.

So my position is not “nothing changes.” My position is:

- restructuring yes,
- closures only as a last resort,
- compulsory redundancies only if absolutely unavoidable,
- and only after serious, quantified alternatives have been tested.

## On “exhausting alternatives”
This phrase must mean something concrete. For me, it means a **time-bound, measurable process**, not an open-ended delay and not a pretext for predetermined closures.

I want to see a package that quantifies savings from:

- working-time reductions,
- temporary flexibility,
- internal redeployment,
- qualification and retraining,
- productivity improvements,
- bonus restraint and management-side contribution,
- and product/volume reallocation.

If that package genuinely closes the gap, then closures become unnecessary. If it does not, then we can discuss what further step is unavoidable. But that discussion must come after the alternatives are proven insufficient, not before.

## On site and job guarantees
I understand why the workforce wants binding guarantees through 2030. They are not asking for a gift; they are asking for security before accepting sacrifice.

I cannot support an unconditional guarantee that freezes every site and every job regardless of economics. But I **can** support a credible, enforceable protection framework that is:

- time-limited,
- conditional,
- and tied to real investment and restructuring commitments.

In other words: not empty promises, but not a blank cheque for closure either.

## On burden-sharing
If labour is to contribute, then the other sides must contribute visibly too.

That means:
- management restraint,
- owner support,
- and concrete investment commitments into future products and technology in Germany.

I will not support a model where workers carry the burden while the corporate side merely observes and investors demand speed without sharing responsibility.

## To the family owners
You say alternatives should be tested first. I agree. But that has to be backed by willingness to support a negotiated settlement, not just to postpone hard decisions.

If the alternatives are sufficient, then support them.  
If they are not sufficient, then the owners must also accept that the company cannot be protected by words alone.

## To the investors
You are right that a cosmetic compromise would be value-destructive. But a settlement that damages industrial legitimacy and triggers lasting conflict is also value-destructive.

You want deal certainty; so do I. But certainty does not come from threatening the workforce with irreversible measures from the outset. It comes from a package that is economically credible **and** socially sustainable.

## My position
So my position is:

- **I support restructuring.**
- **I do not support closures as the first move.**
- **I do not support compulsory redundancies as the easy fallback.**
- **I support a serious, quantified alternative package.**
- **I support conditional protections for jobs and sites.**
- **I support burden-sharing from management, owners, and labour.**
- **I support investment commitments that keep Germany industrially relevant.**

If management can show, with numbers, that alternatives really are insufficient, then we can continue the discussion. But that burden of proof is on management — not on the workforce and not on the region.

## Bottom line
The state government will not support a restructuring that treats our region as disposable.  
We are willing to back a tough but fair settlement that protects the company’s long-term health **and** preserves its role as a regional economic anchor.

What we will not support is:
- closures pushed as a first reflex,
- forced redundancies as a bargaining tool,
- or a one-sided restructuring that leaves the region to absorb the damage.

If a specialist is needed to quantify the alternative savings and footprint scenarios, then I would strongly suggest we **summon an independent industrial restructuring expert** to compare the options on a transparent basis.
