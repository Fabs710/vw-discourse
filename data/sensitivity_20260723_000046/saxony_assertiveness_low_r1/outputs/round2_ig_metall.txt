I’ll be equally direct.

## Where I agree
I agree with the Works Council on the fundamentals:
- **no compulsory redundancies**,
- **no one-sided burden on employees**,
- and **a negotiated solution must protect jobs, sites, and collective standards**.

I also agree that management’s “status quo is impossible” line is not enough by itself. If they want deep concessions, they must show a credible industrial plan and real burden-sharing from owners and management.

## Where I disagree
What I do **not** accept is turning the demand for a serious restructuring into a blank cheque for closures, forced job cuts, or a permanent erosion of the collective wage framework.

The union’s position remains:
- **No plant closures in Germany** as a bargaining starting point.
- **No compulsory redundancies**.
- **No dismantling of collective agreements**.
- **No open-ended insecurity**.

If management wants labour to contribute, that contribution must be **limited, negotiated, and matched by contributions from the corporate side**. Workers are not the shock absorber for every strategic mistake.

## On management’s arguments
Management says capacity has to come out of the system and that savings must be structural. I don’t dispute the need for structural savings. I dispute the conclusion that closures and forced redundancies are the only credible route.

There are still serious alternatives that can deliver real savings:
- temporary working-time reductions,
- internal redeployment,
- qualification and reskilling,
- product and volume allocation,
- productivity agreements,
- bonus restraint and management-side concessions,
- and a negotiated employee contribution if jobs and sites are protected.

What I will not accept is a package where the workforce pays twice:
1. through lower wages or worse conditions, and
2. through job losses and plant insecurity.

That is not a partnership model. That is a transfer of risk onto employees.

## On the investors’ position
I hear the concern about margins and deal certainty. But investors need to understand something very clearly: **a settlement that provokes prolonged conflict, warning strikes, and lasting damage to morale is not “certainty.”** It is instability with a financial spreadsheet attached.

If the capital side wants a durable solution, it must accept that:
- labour peace has a price,
- job protection has value,
- and social stability is not a cosmetic add-on.

A purely financial reading that ignores industrial relations will produce a bad outcome for the company as a whole.

## On the family owners and the regional government
You both say alternatives should be exhausted first. Good. Then let’s define that honestly.

Exhausting alternatives means:
- no closure decision pushed through as a first move,
- no forced redundancies as a fallback if negotiations become difficult,
- no “temporary” concessions from workers that become permanent,
- and a real commitment from ownership to support a compromise package.

If the owners and state want to avoid closure, they must be prepared to back a package with:
- site security,
- job security,
- and investment commitments that give the plants a future.

## What we are prepared to discuss
The union is prepared to negotiate a **fair shared-sacrifice package** if it meets these conditions:

### 1) Job and site security
- No compulsory redundancies.
- No plant closures.
- A binding employment framework with strong protection through the restructuring period.

### 2) Labour contribution, but limited and fair
We can discuss:
- working-time flexibility,
- temporary wage restraint,
- bonus reductions or suspension,
- and other collective-cost contributions.

But those concessions must be:
- time-limited,
- proportionate,
- and explicitly linked to job and site guarantees.

### 3) Corporate-side contribution
We need visible contributions from:
- management compensation,
- owner support,
- and corporate investment commitments.

Employees cannot be the only side “making sacrifices” while every other lever is left untouched.

### 4) Future industrial plan
Any deal must include:
- clear product commitments,
- investment in future technologies,
- and a footprint strategy that keeps Germany industrially relevant.

## What is not acceptable
Let me be absolutely clear:
- **Plant closures are not an acceptable bargaining outcome.**
- **Forced layoffs are not an acceptable bargaining outcome.**
- **A wage-cut-only solution is not an acceptable bargaining outcome.**
- **A restructuring that weakens collective bargaining for years is not an acceptable bargaining outcome.**

If management insists that these are the only options, then it is not offering restructuring — it is demanding surrender. That will not get industrial peace.

## My bottom line
The union is ready for a serious settlement. But the settlement must be based on:
- shared sacrifice,
- protected jobs,
- protected sites,
- and preserved collective standards.

If those conditions are met, we can discuss meaningful employee contributions. If they are not, we will resist, including through industrial action.
