From the perspective of dispersed institutional investors, the core issue is not which stakeholder makes the strongest moral claim. It is whether the outcome will **credibly restore competitive margins and protect long-term equity value**.

So I will be blunt.

## What we agree with
We agree with management, the family owners, and even parts of the employee side on one important point: **the status quo is not acceptable**.

The company has a structural cost problem, and the market will not reward delay, symbolism, or half-measures. If German operations remain materially above competitor cost levels, then margins will stay impaired and capital will keep being misallocated. That is unacceptable from a shareholder perspective.

We also agree that the burden should not fall on employees alone. A durable solution needs burden-sharing and some form of negotiated settlement. Prolonged strike conflict would destroy value and should be avoided if possible.

## Where we disagree

### 1) A “no closures, no redundancies” line is not credible if it blocks competitiveness
To the Works Council and Union: we understand your red lines, but from our perspective they are only acceptable **if they still leave the company with a cost base that is actually competitive**.

If the package excludes plant closures and compulsory redundancies in a way that simply preserves overcapacity, then it is not a solution — it is delay. And delay is value-destructive.

We will not support an outcome that leaves:
- the cost base structurally uncompetitive,
- margins permanently impaired,
- or management unable to deliver the return-on-sales target.

### 2) Site guarantees are only acceptable if they do not become a substitute for real restructuring
We are open to transitional protections and a negotiated path. But broad guarantees through 2030 are only useful if they sit inside a package that produces **hard, durable savings**.

If guarantees become a political shield against necessary restructuring, then they will be priced by the market as future underperformance. That reduces the equity value of the business.

### 3) “Exhaust alternatives first” must mean real alternatives, not endless negotiations
To the Regional State Government and the family owners: we understand the preference for avoiding closures. But “exhaust alternatives” cannot mean indefinite review while the margin gap remains open.

Alternatives are fine if they are:
- measurable,
- durable,
- sufficient in scale,
- and implemented quickly.

If they do not close the competitiveness gap, then they are not alternatives. They are postponements.

## What would move us
We are prepared to support a negotiated restructuring if it delivers all of the following:

1. **A verifiable, durable cost reduction**
   - not temporary optics,
   - not accounting tweaks,
   - but a structural reduction in German operating costs.

2. **A clear capacity adjustment**
   - whether through closures, partial closures, product reallocation, or other real footprint reduction,
   - but it must be enough to match demand and restore returns.

3. **A credible labor contribution**
   - working-time flexibility, pay moderation, internal redeployment, and other measurable savings are acceptable,
   - but only if they are significant enough to matter.

4. **Deal certainty**
   - the market needs to know the restructuring will actually happen,
   - not remain hostage to future vetoes or renewed conflict.

5. **A path back to investment and margin recovery**
   - the restructuring has to free up cash and improve returns,
   - otherwise it is not a restructuring, just a delay of decline.

## What we will not support
We will not back:
- a cosmetic settlement,
- an agreement that preserves excess capacity,
- a package that leaves the return-on-sales target out of reach,
- or prolonged conflict that destroys earnings without fixing the footprint.

## Bottom line
We are not attached to closures as a matter of principle. If management, owners, and labor can produce an alternative that **really** restores competitiveness, we would support it.

But if the alternative is merely “keep all sites, avoid layoffs, and promise future savings,” then we do **not** support it.

The test is simple: **does the package credibly restore margins and protect long-term value?** If yes, we can support it. If not, we will not.
