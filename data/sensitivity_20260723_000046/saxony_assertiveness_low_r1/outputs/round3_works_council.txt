I will respond directly and without euphemism.

## First: what I agree with
Yes, the company needs restructuring.  
Yes, the current situation is not sustainable.  
Yes, employees cannot be the only ones contributing.  
And yes, future products, investment, and long-term competitiveness matter.

I also acknowledge that alternatives must be quantified, not merely asserted. That is fair.

## Where I disagree fundamentally
I do **not** accept the premise that the only “serious” restructuring is one that keeps plant closures and compulsory redundancies on the table.

That is not a neutral economic statement. It is a choice to make labour and sites the first adjustment variable. We reject that.

Let me be absolutely clear:

- **No German plant closures.**
- **No compulsory redundancies.**
- **No dismantling of the job-security framework.**
- **No deal that bypasses co-determination by presenting workers with a fait accompli.**

If those conditions are not respected, there is no basis for a settlement.

## On the demand for hard numbers
I welcome hard numbers — but they must be real alternatives, not a pretext for pre-decided closures.

So here are the numbers and measures we expect management to put on the table before any talk of closures:

1. **Working-time measures**  
   Reduced hours, temporary shifts, and flexible capacity management across sites.

2. **Internal redeployment**  
   Moving staff from declining lines or sites to growing ones, rather than throwing people out.

3. **Qualification and retraining**  
   Real investment in skills so workers can move into EV, battery, software, logistics, and new industrial roles.

4. **Product and volume allocation**  
   Use existing German sites more intelligently instead of declaring them obsolete.

5. **Productivity agreements**  
   Jointly negotiated improvements in processes, logistics, and utilization.

6. **Management-side and owner-side contribution**  
   Executive restraint, bonus moderation, and ownership support. If workers are asked to contribute, the top must contribute too.

Those are not slogans. Those are concrete instruments. Management has not proven that these options are exhausted or insufficient.

## On “minimum footprint”
I reject the idea that management can simply define a “minimum footprint” and then force the workforce to accept it.

If there is to be any discussion of footprint, then the burden of proof is on management:
- prove that non-closure alternatives cannot close the gap,
- prove that any claimed savings are not just shifted into social destruction,
- prove that the plan does not hollow out Germany’s industrial base.

Until then, “minimum footprint” is just another word for “whatever management wants to cut.”

## To management
You say closures may be necessary.  
I say: you have not earned that conclusion.

If you believe alternatives are insufficient, then show us:
- exact savings from shorter working time,
- exact savings from redeployment,
- exact savings from temporary wage restraint,
- exact savings from productivity measures,
- exact savings from management sacrifice,
- and exact savings from investment-driven volume shifts.

Then compare that honestly against closures.

But do not come here and ask us to accept closures before those options are fully negotiated and implemented. That is not a restructuring proposal. It is a threat.

## To the union
We are aligned on the essentials:
- no compulsory redundancies,
- no one-sided burden-sharing,
- no erosion of collective standards.

I also support your point that labour contributions must be limited, time-bound, and linked to protection. That is exactly right.

Where I will go further is this: if management wants temporary sacrifices, then it must first give binding guarantees. Not vague promises, not review clauses that allow future backtracking — binding guarantees.

## To the state government
I appreciate your insistence that closures must be last resort. That is the correct political and social position.

But “last resort” must mean something enforceable, not a phrase management can bypass after a few meetings.

What I need from you is not general concern, but a clear commitment:
- no closure decision without demonstrably exhausting alternatives,
- no compulsory redundancies as the fallback,
- and no weakening of co-determination in the process.

If the state is serious about being an anchor shareholder, then it must act like one — not like a spectator.

## To the family owners
You say you oppose value destruction and prefer alternatives first. Good. Then stand with us on the substance, not just the framing.

If you truly believe in the company’s industrial future in Germany, then you should reject closures as a first move and support a binding site-and-job protection package through 2030.

If you want employee concessions, then the owners must make their own contribution visible:
- support for investment,
- willingness to accept lower near-term returns,
- and backing for a negotiated path that preserves sites.

Anything less is just asking workers to pay for management’s strategic failures.

## To the investors
You keep repeating “deal certainty” and “competitive margins.” I understand the language. But I reject the logic that security for capital must come from insecurity for workers.

A deal that destroys trust, triggers conflict, and weakens the industrial base is not certainty. It is deferred instability.

You want a credible, durable solution? Then back one that preserves:
- German sites,
- collective labour peace,
- co-determination,
- and employment security.

A company cannot cut its way into trust.

## What we are prepared to discuss
We are prepared to negotiate a package that includes:
- temporary working-time reductions,
- redeployment,
- retraining,
- productivity measures,
- moderate and temporary labour contributions,
- and management/owner contributions.

But only on these non-negotiable conditions:

1. **Every German plant remains open**
2. **No compulsory redundancies**
3. **Binding site guarantee through 2030**
4. **Binding employment guarantee through 2030**
5. **Real co-determination over restructuring and future footprint**
6. **A fair burden-sharing framework across management, owners, and labour**

## Bottom line
So let me state the position plainly:

- We are not blocking restructuring.
- We are blocking the destruction of sites, jobs, and co-determination.
- We are ready to negotiate alternatives.
- We are not ready to negotiate closures or forced redundancies.

If management and the other stakeholders want a real settlement, they need to come back with a quantified alternative that keeps every German plant open and protects every worker from compulsory redundancy. Until then, the works council will resist — decisively and with the workforce behind it.
