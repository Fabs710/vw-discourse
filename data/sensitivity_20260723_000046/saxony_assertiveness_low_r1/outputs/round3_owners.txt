I’ll respond directly.

## Where I agree
I agree with the expert’s core conclusion: **closures are not automatically the first move**.  
A serious non-closure package can generate meaningful savings, and it should be tested before any irreversible step. That is consistent with long-term ownership logic.

I also agree with:
- the need for a **durable** competitiveness reset,
- the need for **shared contribution**,
- and the need to avoid a prolonged conflict that destroys value.

## Where I disagree
What I do **not** accept is the repeated attempt by the works council and union to make **“no closures, no compulsory redundancies, every plant open to 2030”** the starting and ending point.

That is not a restructuring plan. It is a constraint that may preserve the existing structure regardless of whether it is economically sustainable.

I also do **not** accept the idea that management should be forced to prove closure is necessary **before** the workforce has meaningfully engaged on quantified alternatives. The burden is shared. If the alternative package is to be credible, it must be put on the table in numbers, not just in principles.

## To management
You have made the case for change, but I still need a sharper answer on **scale**.

The expert is right that the key missing input is the **structural annual cost gap**. That must be quantified site by site and translated into:
- what can be saved through non-closure measures,
- what remains open after those measures,
- and what footprint reduction, if any, is still required.

I support that approach. But I also expect management to be disciplined:
- no theatrical use of closures,
- no bluffing,
- and no treating plant shutdowns as an ideological first choice.

If the numbers show that a non-closure package closes the gap, then that is preferable. If they do not, then the company must retain the option of footprint reduction. I will not sign away that option in advance.

## To the works council
You are right that the workforce should not be the only shock absorber.  
You are wrong if you think that a binding “every German plant remains open” line is compatible with an honest restructuring process.

The expert’s analysis is useful here: non-closure measures can be substantial, but they are not guaranteed to solve a **large fixed-cost overcapacity problem**. If the gap remains after serious labor and productivity measures, then refusing all footprint reduction becomes a refusal to face the economics.

So my position is:
- **no rush to closures**, yes,
- **no closure as first reflex**, yes,
- but **no absolute closure taboo** if the quantified evidence shows it is required.

That is the difference between stewardship and denial.

## To the union
I respect the insistence on labour peace and protection against one-sided burden-sharing. That part is reasonable.

But I do not accept your claim that the workforce can contribute only if the company gives permanent site and job guarantees through 2030. Guarantees can be part of a deal, but they cannot become a mechanism to freeze economics. If the package is too rigid, then it is not a compromise; it is a transfer of risk onto ownership and management without sufficient economic return.

What I need from labour is not another restatement of red lines, but quantified willingness to contribute on:
- working time,
- bonuses,
- flexibility,
- productivity,
- redeployment,
- and temporary wage restraint.

The expert is right that those levers can be real. I would support them if they are large enough and durable enough.

## To the regional state government
I agree that closures are a last resort, not a casual lever.  
I also agree that the regional impact matters.

But I do not support the implication that the state can insist on “alternatives first” while also backing a guarantee framework that effectively limits any meaningful footprint change. The state must be consistent:
- either it supports a serious quantified test of alternatives,
- or it becomes part of the problem by soft-blocking every hard decision.

I would support your call for an independent restructuring expert only if the mandate is clear: quantify the savings, compare scenarios, and tell us honestly whether the non-closure path closes the gap.

That is the right next step.

## To the institutional investors
You are right about one thing: a cosmetic compromise is not acceptable.  
A deal that leaves structural overcapacity untouched would be a value trap.

But you are wrong if you think the answer is to keep closures as an ever-present threat and call that “deal certainty.” Certainty comes from a **credible package**, not from pressure alone.

The expert’s hierarchy is sensible:
1. deep non-closure restructuring,
2. partial footprint reduction if needed,
3. closure only as last resort.

That is the framework I would support. It protects value without making irreversible destruction the default instrument.

## What I would support
I am prepared to back a settlement if it includes all of the following:

1. **A quantified cost-gap diagnosis**
   - plant by plant, function by function.

2. **A serious non-closure package**
   - working-time flexibility,
   - temporary wage/bonus restraint,
   - productivity measures,
   - redeployment,
   - qualification,
   - management-side contribution.

3. **A time-bound test**
   - alternatives are implemented and measured against hard targets.

4. **Conditional protections**
   - site and employment protections tied to actual savings and investment commitments,
   - not absolute taboos, but not empty promises either.

5. **A clear last-resort sequence**
   - non-closure first,
   - partial footprint rationalization next,
   - closure only if the numbers still do not work.

## What I will not support
I will not support:
- a settlement that freezes all German sites regardless of economics,
- a package that preserves overcapacity and calls it social responsibility,
- or a prolonged conflict that destroys margins and reputation.

I also will not support a decision that permanently impairs the enterprise’s value or the owners’ governance control.

## Bottom line
The controlling shareholder’s position is this:

- **Test the non-closure path seriously.**
- **Quantify it properly.**
- **Do not make closures the first move.**
- **Do not make “no closures ever” the rule if the evidence shows a structural gap remains.**
- **Protect long-term enterprise value, not just short-term peace.**

If the next round produces a credible quantified package that closes the gap without irreversible destruction, I will support it.  
If it does not, then the option of footprint reduction must remain on the table.
