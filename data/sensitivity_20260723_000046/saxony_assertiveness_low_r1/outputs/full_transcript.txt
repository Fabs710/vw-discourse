[Works Council (Betriebsrat) (Plant-level employee representation under co-determination) — Round 1]
As Works Council, I will be very clear from the start:

We will not accept plant closures in Germany and we will not accept compulsory redundancies. That is the line.

The workforce has already carried a heavy burden in this transformation, and management cannot now solve strategic mistakes by destroying jobs, breaking trust, and tearing up long-standing protections. A fait accompli will be met with firm resistance.

What we are prepared to discuss is a genuinely negotiated restructuring that secures:
- binding site guarantees for all German plants,
- job guarantees through 2030,
- no forced layoffs,
- and a real co-determination role for the workforce in shaping the future production footprint.

If savings are needed, then they must come from alternatives that preserve sites and employment: capacity adjustments, temporary working-time measures, internal redeployment, training, and a fair contribution from management and owners. But the burden cannot be placed one-sidedly on employees.

We are also demanding that any restructuring be tied to future investment, new products, and a credible industrial strategy for Germany. We will not trade away jobs today for vague promises tomorrow.

So my position is straightforward: no closures, no forced layoffs, no dismantling of co-determination. If management wants a solution, it must come to the table with a plan that protects the workforce and the German plants.

[Group & Brand Management (Executive management driving the restructuring) — Round 1]
I’ll be equally clear: **the status quo is not an option**.

We are facing a structural cost and capacity problem, not a temporary dip. German plant costs are far above target, European demand is shrinking, and our brand is still missing its return-on-sales target. If we do not make a durable correction now, we will simply burn through cash that should be going into future products, software, battery technology, and competitiveness to 2030.

So I need to challenge the red lines you have set.

I understand the political and social sensitivity, but **I do not accept a package that rules out closures in advance**. If the footprint is structurally too large, then capacity has to come out of the system. That may mean plant closure, partial closure, or another equivalent structural reduction. What I cannot accept is a cosmetic solution that keeps every site open while the cost base remains uncompetitive.

### On compulsory redundancies
I prefer negotiated solutions over forced layoffs, but I will not rule out workforce reductions as a consequence of restructuring if we cannot secure sufficient voluntary and structural measures. The company cannot carry excess capacity indefinitely.

A blanket job guarantee through 2030 is not compatible with the scale of change we need. We can discuss transitional protections, but only if they are tied to **real structural savings** and a credible path to lower costs. Guarantees without transformation would freeze the problem in place.

I do agree on one point: the burden cannot fall on employees alone. Management, the broader corporate side, and all stakeholders must contribute. But an employee contribution must be **material**, not symbolic. If we are to avoid harsher measures, then labour costs must come down in a way that is durable and measurable.

### On future investment
You are right that restructuring must be tied to future products and investment. That is exactly why I am pressing for this now. We need the capacity and margin to fund the next generation of vehicles and technology. Without a competitive base, there is no future industrial strategy to protect.

### Bottom line
If you are asking me to commit to **no closures, no forced redundancies, and full site guarantees**, then I have to say clearly: **that will not solve this crisis**.

What I am prepared to discuss is a package that delivers:
- a **substantial and durable reduction in capacity**,
- a **meaningful labour-cost contribution**,
- **organizational and working-time flexibility**,

If the employee side wants to avoid the most drastic outcomes, then it needs to engage on the scale of savings, not just on protecting every existing structure. Without that, we are postponing the inevitable and weakening the company further.

## Executive view

Based on the information provided, **closures are not automatically necessary as the first move**. In a large German automotive restructuring, it is often possible to generate **material savings through non-closure measures**—working-time adjustments, temporary pay and bonus restraint, redeployment, process productivity, purchasing/logistics efficiencies, headcount attrition, and product allocation changes.

My expert judgment is:

- **Non-closure measures can plausibly deliver meaningful savings**, often enough to bridge a **moderate** cost gap.
- If the gap is **large and structural**—for example, a permanent overcapacity problem with underutilized plants, weak utilization, and a multi-hundred-million-euro annual burden—then **some footprint reduction may become necessary**, but that does **not automatically mean plant closures**.
- A proper last-resort test should compare:
  1. non-closure savings,
  2. partial footprint reduction short of closure,
  3. full closure scenarios,
  4. and the time path to realize each.

In most cases, the right sequence is **first exhaust non-closure measures**, then test **partial footprint rationalization**, and only then consider **closure** if the economics still do not work.

---

## What can realistically be saved without closures?

### 1) Working-time reduction / short-time-like measures
**Plausible annual savings:** medium, but not huge on its own.

If a plant or multiple sites reduce hours, shift patterns, overtime, and agency labor, savings come from:
- lower paid hours,
- reduced shift premiums,
- lower overtime,
- fewer temporary workers,
- better line balancing.

**Order of magnitude:** often in the range of **low double-digit millions to perhaps low hundreds of millions euros annually** across a large footprint, depending on scale.

**Limitation:** this only works if production volume falls and utilization can remain operationally stable. It does not eliminate fixed-site overhead.

### 2) Temporary wage restraint / bonus moderation / special payments
**Plausible annual savings:** medium.

If the workforce agrees to:
- suspend or reduce special payments,
- cap bonuses,
- moderate tariff-related supplements,
- or accept temporary wage concessions,

the effect can be significant in a large German OEM workforce.

**Order of magnitude:** for a major domestic workforce, this can easily be **tens of millions to several hundred million euros annually**, depending on what is included and how broad the contribution is.

**Limitation:** this is usually **temporary or politically fragile**. It helps bridge a crisis, but it is not by itself a structural footprint fix.

### 3) Redeployment and internal mobility
**Plausible savings:** medium, but mostly through avoided severance and avoided external hiring.

Redeployment does not always reduce wage cost immediately, but it avoids:
- compulsory layoffs,
- recruitment costs,
- social plan costs,
- knowledge loss,
- and the inefficiency of carrying understaffed growth units while paying for excess in shrinking units.

**Order of magnitude:** direct annual P&L savings are variable; the biggest effect is often **avoided restructuring cost** and better utilization of existing labor.

### 4) Training/requalification for EV, battery, software, logistics roles
**Plausible savings:** indirect, medium to high over time.

This is often not a “cost cut” in year one; it is a **cost avoidance** and productivity measure. It can:
- reduce external hiring,
- improve throughput,
- support product mix changes,
- and preserve employment while shifting capacity.

**Limitation:** it is not a substitute for a structurally oversized footprint unless new products and volumes actually materialize.

### 5) Productivity improvements
This is often the **largest non-closure lever**, but also the one most often overstated.

Examples:
- line efficiency,
- scrap reduction,
- logistics simplification,
- maintenance optimization,
- supplier consolidation,
- reducing internal complexity,
- platform sharing,
- better sequencing and capacity smoothing.

**Plausible annual savings:** potentially **high**, especially in a complex German OEM environment.

A mature automotive plant network can sometimes extract **1–3% of manufacturing cost base** per year in sustainable productivity, and more if complexity is excessive. Across a very large operation, that can become **high tens of millions to low hundreds of millions euros annually**.

**Limitation:** productivity gains do not fully offset major fixed-cost overcapacity. They buy time and lower the required footprint reduction, but they do not magically remove structural excess plant cost.

### 6) Product and volume reallocation
This is often the most important non-closure lever if the company has multiple plants and model families.

By shifting:
- higher-margin models,
- EV volumes,
- battery-adjacent operations,
- or export production

into existing German sites, you can improve utilization and spread fixed costs.

**Plausible savings:** potentially very large, but only if demand and product allocation support it.

**Limitation:** this depends on product pipeline, platform timing, and market demand. If there is not enough volume, this lever is constrained.

### Short answer
**Sometimes yes, sometimes no.**

It depends on:
- the annual structural gap,
- the degree of plant underutilization,
- whether the company can shift profitable volume into existing plants,
- and the durability of labor concessions.

### A practical rule of thumb
Non-closure measures can often cover:
- **a moderate gap** if the company is mainly burdened by high labor cost, complexity, and poor utilization,

- fewer shifts,
- fewer lines,
- reduced model allocation,

# 2) Scenario model: plant-level logic

Because no site list is provided, I use a **representative three-plant structure** commonly seen in automotive footprints:

- **Plant B**: powertrain/component site

## Scenario A: Non-closure measures only
Measures:

## Scenario B: Partial footprint reduction
Measures:
- plant specialization
- line consolidation
- shift elimination at selected sites
- relocation of some volume
- closure of one line or one function within a plant
- reduction of non-core operations while keeping a site open

This is often the **best economic compromise** when management needs more than labor measures but wants to avoid full closures.

### Typical savings potential
- **Annual savings:** €700 million to €1.8 billion
- **One-time restructuring cost:** €500 million to €1.5 billion
- **Timing:** 12–30 months to full run-rate
- **ROS impact:** about **+0.6 to +1.5 percentage points**

### Plant-by-plant benchmark

| Network/logistics rationalization | €100m–€300m | €100m–€300m | supplier clustering, transport reduction, inventory efficiency |

### Assessment
Partial footprint reduction often delivers a **better savings-to-cost ratio** than full closure, because it preserves some fixed assets and avoids the highest social and transition costs while still removing meaningful capacity.

## Scenario C: Full plant closure
Measures:
- full shutdown of one or more German plants
- transfer of production elsewhere
- asset write-downs
- workforce reduction
- supplier network restructuring

### Typical savings potential
- **Annual savings:** €1.0 billion to €2.5 billion per major plant closure package
- **One-time restructuring cost:** €1.0 billion to €3.0 billion or more
- **Timing:** 18–48 months
- **ROS impact:** about **+0.8 to +2.0 percentage points**

### Plant-by-plant benchmark
| Plant type | One-time cost | Annual savings | Main mechanism |
|---|---:|---:|---|
| Large assembly plant closure | €500m–€1.5bn | €300m–€700m | elimination of fixed site cost and indirect labor |
| Powertrain/component plant closure | €300m–€1.0bn | €200m–€600m | fixed overhead removal, asset rationalization |
| Smaller/specialized site closure | €200m–€600m | €100m–€250m | site overhead, maintenance, staffing removal |
| Network/supplier transition costs | €200m–€700m | included above or additional | tooling transfers, logistics changes, launch risk |

### Important caveat
Closure savings are often **overstated in simple models** because they must net out:
- severance and social plan costs,
- parallel running during transfer,
- product launch disruption,
- supplier requalification,
- decommissioning,
- impairment charges,
- and sometimes higher logistics costs at the receiving site.

So while closure can produce the largest theoretical fixed-cost reduction, the **net present value** may be less attractive than it appears if execution is difficult or if the receiving plant is already near capacity.

# 3) Cash flow timing

## A. Non-closure measures
### Cash flow pattern
- **Year 0–1:** restructuring expense, then early savings begin
- **Year 1–2:** full run-rate savings often reached
- **Payback:** often within **6–18 months** if the measures are negotiated effectively

### Why it is attractive
- Low execution risk
- Lower social disruption
- Faster access to savings
- Often the best first-step solution

---

## B. Partial footprint reduction
### Cash flow pattern
- **Year 0–1:** planning costs, engineering, negotiation, line changes
- **Year 1–3:** gradual ramp of savings as capacity is removed
- **Payback:** often **2–4 years**

### Why it is attractive
- Preserves viable parts of the footprint
- Removes enough capacity to matter
- Usually less destructive than closure
- Often best for ROS recovery if the gap is structural but not extreme

---

## C. Full closure
### Cash flow pattern
- **Year 0–2:** heavy restructuring outflow, asset write-downs, dual-running costs
- **Year 2–4:** savings appear as volume transfers complete
- **Payback:** often **3–6 years** depending on transition complexity

### Why it may still be chosen
- If a plant is structurally uncompetitive
- If utilization is chronically weak
- If product allocation cannot sustain the site
- If the annual fixed-cost burden is too high to solve otherwise

# 5) What would make a closure economically necessary?

A closure becomes economically justified only if several conditions are met:

1. **Persistent underutilization**
   - low volumes with no credible recovery path

2. **No future product allocation**
   - no next-generation model or platform assigned

3. **High fixed cost per unit**
   - labor, overhead, utilities, and maintenance remain uncompetitive even after productivity work

4. **Alternatives insufficient**
   - non-closure and partial reduction do not close the cost gap

5. **Transition economics still positive**
   - after one-time costs, the present value of savings exceeds the investment required to close

If these are not all present, closure may be premature.

## Executive assessment

Based on the positions laid out, **plant closures are not automatically necessary as the first move**. In a large German automotive footprint, there is usually substantial room to generate savings through non-closure measures first, especially when the current issue is a combination of:

## 1) What non-closure measures can realistically save

For a large German carmaker, a serious non-closure package can often deliver savings in the following bands:

### A. Working-time measures / short-time-like capacity adjustment
- Temporary working-time reduction
- shift optimization
- reduced overtime
- lower agency labour

**Plausible annual savings:**  
- roughly **€50m–€200m** depending on scale and baseline shift premium structure

These measures are strongest where plants have persistent slack capacity and expensive shift systems.

### B. Wage moderation / bonus restraint / special-payment reductions
If negotiated, temporary or multi-year restraint on special payments, variable pay, or allowance structures can create meaningful savings.

**Plausible annual savings:**  
- roughly **€75m–€250m**

This is often underestimated in public debate because the savings are spread across many cost lines.

### C. Productivity and complexity reduction
Includes:
- line balancing
- lower variant complexity
- fewer indirect staff per vehicle
- material flow simplification
- lean improvements
- reduced internal logistics waste

**Plausible annual savings:**  
- roughly **€100m–€300m** over 1–3 years

This is one of the largest non-closure levers, but it requires disciplined execution and a real management commitment.

### D. Redeployment and attrition
If the issue is overstaffing rather than immediate demand collapse, natural attrition and internal redeployment can remove a substantial number of positions without forced redundancies.

**Plausible annual savings:**  
- roughly **€50m–€150m** initially, rising over time

This is slower than plant closure but often politically and operationally less destructive.

### E. Management-side contribution
Includes:
- executive pay restraint
- overhead cuts at HQ and brand functions
- travel, procurement, support-function rationalization
- reduction in duplicated governance layers

**Plausible annual savings:**  
- roughly **€20m–€100m**

Small relative to labour, but important symbolically and for burden-sharing credibility.

### F. Product allocation / volume reallocation
If German plants are underfilled, securing additional model or component volume can materially improve fixed-cost absorption.

**Plausible annual savings / margin impact:**  
- roughly **€100m–€400m equivalent**, depending on whether the incremental volume is truly additional or just shifted from elsewhere

# 7) Neutral conclusion

## My evidence-based assessment
- **Non-closure measures can plausibly deliver material savings**, but usually not enough to eliminate major overcapacity on their own.
- **Partial footprint reduction** is often the most economically balanced route when labour resistance to closures is high.
- **Full closure** yields the biggest recurring savings but also the largest one-time cost and greatest execution risk.

## Best-practice restructuring logic
1. Quantify plant economics site by site
2. Exhaust non-closure measures first
3. Use partial footprint reduction if the residual gap remains material
4. Reserve closure for the true last resort
