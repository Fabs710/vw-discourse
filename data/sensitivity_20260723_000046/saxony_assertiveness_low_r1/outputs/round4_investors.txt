I’ll be direct.

## Where I agree
I agree with management, the family owners, and the state on the core financial point: **the current cost base is still not acceptable**.

If the German footprint remains structurally too expensive, then:
- margins stay weak,
- return on sales misses target,
- cash generation stays constrained,
- and shareholder value keeps leaking.

That is not something capital markets can accept indefinitely.

I also agree with the expert and, to a degree, with labour on one important point: **closures should not be the first reflex** if serious non-closure measures can materially close the gap.

## Where I disagree
I do **not** accept the labour side’s attempt to make:
- no plant closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- and binding 2030 guarantees across all German sites

the baseline outcome.

That is not a credible restructuring if the numbers show structural overcapacity and an uncompetitive cost base. It would just lock in subscale economics and preserve margin impairment.

I also do **not** accept a cosmetic compromise that preserves every site while leaving the cost gap unresolved. That would be value-destructive, even if it looks peaceful in the short term.

## To the works council and union
You are right that employees should not be the only adjustment variable. I support that.

But your position is too rigid if it rules out any meaningful footprint change in advance. For shareholders, the test is simple:
**does the package restore competitive margins on a durable basis?**

If the answer is no, then “no closures ever” is not a solution — it is a delay.

The expert has already made clear that:
- non-closure measures can create material savings,
- but they may not be enough if the gap is large,
- and partial footprint reduction may be the better compromise before full closure.

That is the only labour-side argument I find economically serious: show that alternatives are large enough and durable enough to actually close the gap.

## To management
You are right to insist that the gap is structural, not cosmetic. But you still need to sharpen the case.

What I need from management is a **credible quantified bridge**, site by site and measure by measure:
- savings from working-time changes,
- savings from overtime and agency-labor reduction,
- savings from bonus restraint and wage moderation,
- savings from productivity and complexity reduction,
- savings from redeployment and attrition,
- savings from product and volume reallocation,
- and the residual gap after all that.

Without that, “closures may be necessary” is not yet a fully substantiated investment case.

I do support keeping closures and compulsory redundancies available **if** the numbers justify them. I will not sign away the company’s restructuring tools in advance.

## To the state government
Your insistence on closures as a last resort is reasonable. I support a time-bound, measurable alternatives test.

But that test must not become an open-ended veto. The process must end in one of two outcomes:
- either the non-closure package closes the gap,
- or the company moves to the next step.

What I will not support is a process that drifts, consumes time, and leaves the cost problem unresolved while conflict continues.

## To the controlling family owners
You are right that footprint reduction should not be used casually. I agree.

But if the quantified alternative package does **not** close the structural gap, then you must be prepared to back the hard decision. “Prefer alternatives first” is fine. “Never accept the consequences of insufficient alternatives” is not.

Ownership cannot demand competitiveness and then refuse the tools needed to achieve it.

## What would move us
We would support a settlement if it delivers all of the following:

1. **A quantified cost-gap diagnosis**
   - plant by plant, function by function.

2. **A serious non-closure package**
   - working-time flexibility,
   - temporary wage and bonus restraint,
   - productivity measures,
   - redeployment,
   - retraining,
   - management-side restraint.

3. **A time-bound test**
   - alternatives implemented and measured against hard targets.

4. **Conditional protections**
   - site and employment protections only to the extent they do not block the restructuring.

5. **A clear last-resort sequence**
   - non-closure first,
   - partial footprint rationalization next,
   - closure only if the numbers still do not work.

## What we will not support
We will not support:
- a cosmetic compromise,
- a deal that leaves overcapacity untouched,
- a settlement that structurally impairs margins,
- or a prolonged conflict that destroys cash and confidence.

We also will not support any arrangement that asks capital to accept permanently impaired returns while management avoids hard decisions.

## My position on the specialist assessment
The expert’s hierarchy is sensible:
1. non-closure restructuring,
2. partial footprint reduction if needed,
3. closure only as last resort.

That is the right framework. But it only works if step one is quantified, durable, and large enough to matter. If not, then the later steps must remain available.

So I support the independent restructuring analysis, but it must be operational, not rhetorical.

## Bottom line
Our test is simple:

**Does the package credibly restore competitive margins on a durable basis?**

- If yes, we can support it.
- If no, we won’t.

So:
- **Yes** to serious alternatives first.
- **Yes** to shared burden.
- **Yes** to conditional protections.
- **No** to cosmetic settlements.
- **No** to preserving overcapacity by default.
- **No** to anything that leaves shareholder value structurally impaired.
