I appreciate that management is being open about the scale of the problem. I do not dispute that the company needs to become more competitive and that the transition has to be financed sustainably. As an anchor shareholder and regional government, I also understand that this cannot be solved by wishful thinking.

But I have to be equally clear: **I cannot support a restructuring that relies on plant closures in Germany or on a one-sided destruction of jobs and co-determination.** That would be economically reckless for the region and politically unacceptable.

### Where I agree
- **The status quo is not acceptable.** We need a durable solution.
- **The company needs a stronger cost base** and more room for future investment.
- **All sides must contribute**, not just the workforce.

### Where I disagree
- I do **not** accept that closures are the necessary first answer.
- I do **not** accept that a blanket rejection of site guarantees is the only realistic position.
- I do **not** accept a restructuring that treats German plants as disposable and shifts the burden mainly onto employees and local communities.

Once a plant is closed, the damage is not reversible. Jobs are lost, suppliers suffer, regional value chains are weakened, and trust in the company’s role as an industrial anchor is damaged for years. That is not a minor trade-off; it is a structural loss.

### What I would consider
A negotiated restructuring can be supported if it meets three conditions:

1. **Sites and core employment are preserved wherever possible**
   - No rush into closures.
   - First exhaust alternatives: capacity adjustments, internal redeployment, working-time measures, product allocation, and operational efficiencies.

2. **The burden is shared fairly**
   - Employees can discuss a contribution if it is limited, temporary, and tied to clear guarantees.
   - Management and owners must also contribute visibly.
   - It cannot be “labour pays, everyone else watches.”

3. **There is a credible future for the German footprint**
   - Investment commitments must be real, not rhetorical.
   - New products and technology allocation must be part of the package.
   - The solution must stabilize the company long term, not simply produce a short-term accounting improvement.

### On compulsory redundancies
I would strongly prefer to avoid them. They would create unnecessary social and political damage, and I do not think they should be the starting point of negotiations. If the company insists that some workforce reduction is unavoidable, then it must at minimum be done through the most protective instruments available, with social safeguards and maximum voluntariness. But I am not prepared to endorse a strategy that normalizes forced layoffs as the easy route.

### On site and job guarantees
The works council and union are right to demand binding assurances. A complete guarantee through 2030 may be too rigid in the abstract, but a **credible, enforceable protection framework** is essential if employees are asked to make sacrifices. Without that, there is no trust and no settlement.

### On the role of co-determination
Let me also say this plainly: **the state will not stand aside if co-determination and regional interests are sidelined.** Our role is not symbolic. We are a legitimate stakeholder with a responsibility to the region, to jobs, and to the company’s long-term stability. Any outcome that tries to bypass that will face resistance.

### My message to management
If you want support for restructuring, bring a plan that:
- avoids closures unless every alternative has genuinely been exhausted,
- protects employment as far as possible,
- shares the burden fairly,
- and secures the company’s long-term industrial future in Germany.

If the plan is essentially “close plants and cut jobs to fix the cost base,” then I cannot support it. If it is a serious social-partnership restructuring that keeps the company strong and the region intact, then I am prepared to engage constructively.
