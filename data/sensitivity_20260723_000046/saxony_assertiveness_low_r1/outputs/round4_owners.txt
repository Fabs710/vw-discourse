I’ll respond directly.

## Where I agree

I agree with the expert, management, the state government, and even parts of the labor-side argument on one central point: **the status quo is not sustainable**. The company needs a real restructuring, and it needs to restore competitiveness and margins on a durable basis.

I also agree that:
- employees should not be the only ones contributing,
- non-closure measures must be tested seriously,
- and cosmetic fixes are not acceptable.

The expert’s framework is useful: **first test non-closure measures, then partial footprint reduction if needed, and only then closure as a last resort**. That sequence is sensible from an ownership standpoint as well.

## Where I disagree

I do **not** accept the works council’s and union’s attempt to turn:
- no plant closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- and binding 2030 guarantees across the board

into the starting and ending point of the process.

That is not a restructuring plan. It is a demand to preserve the current structure regardless of whether it is economically sustainable.

I also do **not** accept the idea that closures should be ruled out in advance. If the numbers show that non-closure measures do not close the structural gap, then footprint reduction must remain available. Otherwise we are not stewarding the company; we are postponing the problem.

## To the works council

I respect your insistence that workers should not bear the burden alone. That part is legitimate.  
But your line that every German plant must remain open and every job protected through 2030 is too rigid if it prevents meaningful restructuring.

The expert has already shown that non-closure measures can generate **material savings**, but also that they may not be enough if the gap is large. That is exactly why your absolute no-closure position is not convincing to me.

So my position is:
- **no rush to closures**, yes;
- **no closure as first reflex**, yes;
- but **no absolute closure taboo** if the quantified evidence shows it is required.

## To the union

I agree that labor contributions must be limited, time-bound, and matched by management and owner contributions. That is fair.

But I do **not** accept a settlement where labor can only contribute if the company gives permanent site and job guarantees through 2030. Guarantees can be part of the package, but they cannot freeze economics.

I am open to:
- temporary wage restraint,
- bonus moderation,
- working-time flexibility,
- redeployment,
- retraining,
- and productivity measures,

but only if they are sufficient and durable enough to matter.

If they are not sufficient, then we cannot pretend they solve the problem.

## To management

You are right to insist that the gap is structural and not merely cosmetic.  
But I want to be equally clear: you now need to quantify the gap far better than you have so far.

The expert was right to say the key missing input is the **plant-by-plant and function-by-function cost bridge**. I support that. We need to know:
- what can be saved through working-time changes,
- what through bonus moderation and wage restraint,
- what through redeployment and attrition,
- what through productivity and complexity reduction,
- what through product and volume allocation,
- and what remains after all that.

Without that, “closures may be necessary” remains an assertion, not a case.

I do support keeping closures and compulsory redundancies available **if** the numbers justify them. I will not sign away the company’s ability to take necessary structural decisions in advance.

## To the state government

Your call for a time-bound, measurable process is correct. I support it.  
But it must not become an open-ended veto on change.

I also agree that closures are not a casual instrument. They have regional and political costs, and they should not be used lightly. That said, the state cannot demand competitiveness and at the same time insist on a guarantee framework that effectively blocks adjustment.

So I support:
- restructuring yes,
- closures only as a last resort,
- compulsory redundancies only if absolutely unavoidable,
- and a serious quantified alternatives test before any irreversible step.

## To the institutional investors

You are right that a cosmetic compromise would destroy value. I agree with that.

But I do **not** accept the logic that “deal certainty” requires keeping closures and redundancies as permanent threats while labor is expected to accept the outcome. Certainty comes from a package that is economically credible and durable, not from pressure alone.

You need to accept that a deal that destroys trust, triggers conflict, and weakens industrial relations also destroys value. So the question is not just “how much can be cut,” but “what kind of settlement restores value without poisoning the company.”

## What I would support

I am prepared to back a settlement if it includes all of the following:

1. **A quantified diagnosis of the structural cost gap**
   - plant by plant, function by function.

2. **A serious non-closure package**
   - working-time flexibility,
   - temporary wage and bonus restraint,
   - productivity measures,
   - redeployment,
   - qualification,
   - management-side contribution.

3. **A time-bound test**
   - alternatives implemented and measured against hard targets.

4. **Conditional protections**
   - site and employment protections tied to actual savings and investment commitments,
   - not absolute taboos, but not empty promises either.

5. **A clear last-resort sequence**
   - non-closure first,
   - partial footprint rationalization next,
   - closure only if the numbers still do not work.

## What I will not support

I will not support:
- a settlement that freezes all German sites regardless of economics,
- a package that preserves overcapacity and calls it social responsibility,
- or a prolonged conflict that destroys margins and reputation.

I also will not support anything that permanently impairs the enterprise’s value or the owners’ governance control.

## Bottom line

The controlling shareholder’s position is this:

- **Test the non-closure path seriously.**
- **Quantify it properly.**
- **Do not make closures the first move.**
- **Do not make “no closures ever” the rule if the evidence shows a structural gap remains.**
- **Protect long-term enterprise value, not just short-term peace.**

If the next round produces a credible quantified package that closes the gap without irreversible destruction, I will support it. If it does not, then footprint reduction must remain on the table.
