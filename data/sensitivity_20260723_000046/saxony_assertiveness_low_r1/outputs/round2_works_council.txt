Management, investors, and even the family owners keep repeating the same thing in different words: “costs are too high, therefore the workforce must pay through closures, job cuts, or both.” I reject that logic.

Let me be precise.

## Where I agree
Yes, the company needs a credible future. Yes, the current situation is not sustainable. And yes, employees cannot be the only group contributing. We have said from the beginning that we are prepared to discuss a negotiated restructuring if it protects sites, jobs, and co-determination.

## Where I disagree
What I do **not** accept is treating plant closures and compulsory redundancies as the default answer.

- **No German plant closures.**
- **No compulsory redundancies.**
- **No dismantling of the job-security framework.**
- **No weakening of co-determination by forcing a fait accompli.**

That is not “emotion”; it is the minimum condition for any serious negotiation.

## On management’s argument
Management says capacity must come out of the system. Fine — then show us the alternatives that do that **without closing plants and without forced layoffs**.

There are alternatives that are real, not symbolic:
- working-time reduction,
- temporary capacity adjustments,
- internal redeployment across sites,
- qualification and retraining,
- product reallocation,
- productivity agreements,
- and a fair, temporary contribution from labour and management alike.

If management has already decided that German plants are disposable, then this is not a negotiation. It is an ultimatum. And that will be met accordingly.

## On the investors’ argument
Investors talk about margins and “deal certainty.” I understand that capital markets dislike uncertainty. But a settlement that destroys industrial capability, trust, and labour peace will create a much bigger problem later.

A so-called “credible” solution is not credible if it:
- removes productive sites,
- triggers social conflict,
- destroys the workforce’s trust,
- and weakens the company’s industrial base in Germany.

That is short-term financial thinking dressed up as discipline.

## On the family owners and the state government
You both say you prefer alternatives first. Then stand by that position.

Exhausting alternatives must mean more than a slogan. It means:
1. a binding commitment that no German plant will be closed,
2. a binding commitment that no employee will be forced out,
3. a jointly negotiated capacity and investment plan,
4. and real burden-sharing from management and owners.

If those conditions are not met, then “exhaust alternatives” becomes a pretext for eventual closures later. We will not accept that.

## What we are prepared to discuss
We can discuss a package that includes:
- temporary working-time reductions,
- agreed operational flexibility,
- internal transfers and redeployment,
- targeted qualification programs,
- a temporary employee contribution,
- and management-side contribution measures.

But only under one central condition: **every German site stays open and every job is protected against compulsory redundancy.**

We are also insisting on:
- a **binding site guarantee through 2030**,
- a **binding employment guarantee through 2030**,
- and a **real co-determination role** in shaping the restructuring and future footprint.

Without those guarantees, asking the workforce for concessions is not acceptable.

## On burden-sharing
If there is to be any contribution from employees, then it must be matched visibly:
- management compensation must be addressed,
- owners must contribute,
- investment commitments must be firm,
- and future product allocation must protect German sites.

The workforce will not finance a restructuring that others design, others control, and others profit from.

## Bottom line
We are ready to negotiate a **socially responsible restructuring**.

We are **not** ready to negotiate:
- plant closures,
- forced redundancies,
- or a one-sided cost-cutting program disguised as strategy.

If management wants a deal, bring a plan that keeps every German plant open, protects every worker from compulsory layoffs, and gives co-determination real power over the future. Otherwise, it will face resistance — in the works council, in the workforce, and on the shop floor.
