I’ll respond directly.

## First: what I agree with

I agree with the expert and, to a limited extent, with the state government and owners on one key point: **we need quantified options, not slogans**.

I also agree that:
- the current situation is not sustainable,
- employees should not be the only contributors,
- non-closure measures should be tested seriously,
- and a cosmetic compromise would be useless.

So yes: the discussion now has to be grounded in numbers and execution, not just red lines.

## Where I fundamentally disagree

I do **not** accept the works council’s and union’s central position that:
- no German plant closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- no meaningful footprint reduction,
- and binding 2030 guarantees across the board

can be made compatible with restoring competitiveness.

That is not a restructuring strategy. It is a demand to preserve the current cost base and footprint while asking management to somehow deliver a major margin turnaround anyway. That does not work.

## On the works council’s “no closures” line

I respect the fact that the works council wants to protect jobs and sites. But the line you are drawing is economically too rigid.

The expert has said clearly:
- non-closure measures can deliver **material savings**,
- but if the structural gap is large, **partial footprint reduction** or even closure may still be necessary.

That is the crucial point you are trying to ignore.

You keep saying closures are not “earned.” But if the numbers show persistent overcapacity and uncompetitive fixed costs, then refusing closures in advance is not prudence — it is denial.

## On compulsory redundancies

I prefer voluntary exits, redeployment, retraining, and attrition. That is my first choice.

But I will **not rule out compulsory redundancies in advance** if the package does not close the gap. Why? Because management must have tools that actually reduce the cost base. A restructuring that preserves headcount regardless of demand and cost reality is not serious.

That does not mean compulsory redundancies are the preferred path. It means I will not hand the workforce a veto over any effective adjustment.

## On job and site guarantees through 2030

A blanket guarantee through 2030 is too rigid if it freezes the footprint and prevents necessary adjustment.

I am open to:
- **conditional protections**,
- **time-limited guarantees**,
- **site- and role-specific security where future economics justify it**,
- and a structured process with clear milestones.

But I will not agree to a promise that locks in today’s footprint and leaves the company unable to respond if the cost gap remains. That would be irresponsible to the owners, to investors, and to the company’s future.

## On the union’s “shared sacrifice” argument

I do agree with you on one point: shared sacrifice must be real. Management, owners, and labour all need to contribute.

But your definition of shared sacrifice is too narrow if it excludes the structural levers the company actually needs.

Temporary wage restraint, bonus moderation, working-time flexibility, redeployment, and training are helpful. I welcome them. But they are **not enough on their own** if the company is structurally overextended.

If those measures fully closed the gap, great. But no one has shown that they do.

## On the expert’s scenario framework

The expert’s framework is useful, and I support it.

The hierarchy is:
1. non-closure restructuring,
2. partial footprint reduction,
3. closure only as last resort.

That is exactly the logic I am following.

But the union is trying to convert “last resort” into “never,” and that I reject.

So let me be explicit:
- I support serious testing of alternatives.
- I support a genuine quantified comparison.
- I do **not** support pre-committing to preserve every site.
- I do **not** support pre-committing to rule out compulsory redundancies.
- I do **not** support a process that produces a nice-looking compromise but leaves the cost base uncompetitive.

## What I need from labour

If you want management to rule out closures, then bring a package that actually closes the gap. Not a list of principles — a package.

That means hard numbers for:
- working-time reductions,
- temporary labor-cost restraint,
- bonus moderation,
- productivity gains,
- redeployment,
- attrition,
- and any volume reallocation you believe can be secured.

If that package is strong enough, then we can talk about footprint stability. If it is not, then closures must remain available.

## To the state government

I understand your concern about regional impacts. I take that seriously. But the state cannot ask us to solve a structural competitiveness crisis while also expecting us to rule out the instruments that may be needed to fix it.

Your call for an independent expert is right, and I welcome that.

But the mandate must be clear:
- quantify the savings from non-closure measures,
- compare them with partial footprint reduction,
- compare them with closure,
- and tell us honestly what actually closes the cost gap.

I will not support a process that turns “alternatives first” into a de facto veto on restructuring.

## To the controlling family owners

You are right that closures should not be used casually. I agree.

But you also need to be clear: if the numbers show that a durable competitiveness fix requires footprint reduction, will you support it? Because “prefer alternatives” is not the same as “back whatever is needed to restore value.”

The company needs a real mandate to act, not a symbolic preference for change without consequences.

## To the institutional investors

You are right to reject a cosmetic deal. A settlement that preserves too much overcapacity would simply postpone the problem and miss the ROS target again.

But I also reject the idea that “deal certainty” is achieved by keeping closures and redundancies as abstract threats while labor refuses to engage on the scale of the problem.

Deal certainty comes from a package that is:
- economically credible,
- durable,
- and sufficiently hard to restore margins.

So yes, I share your insistence on credibility. But credibility requires actual footprint and cost correction, not just discipline talk.

## What I am prepared to accept

I am prepared to consider a package that includes:
- working-time flexibility,
- temporary wage restraint,
- bonus moderation,
- redeployment,
- retraining,
- productivity improvements,
- management-side restraint,
- and investment commitments into future products and technology.

But only if it also delivers:
1. **real structural cost reduction**
2. **a substantial reduction in excess capacity**
3. **a meaningful labor-cost contribution**
4. **a path to restore return on sales by 2030**

If those conditions are not met, then the package is not sufficient.

## What I will not accept

I will not accept:
- a cosmetic compromise,
- a deal that protects every site regardless of economics,
- a package that leaves overcapacity intact,
- or a settlement that makes the company uncompetitive and starves future investment.

I also will not accept a situation in which management is expected to pretend the margin problem does not exist.

## My position on next steps

I support the expert’s quantified scenario comparison, but it must now become operational.

[SUMMON: independent industrial restructuring specialist to produce a plant-by-plant savings and footprint model, including non-closure savings, partial footprint reduction, closure scenarios, one-time costs, annual savings, payback period, and impact on return on sales]

Until that model is on the table, I will continue to insist that:
- closures remain available,
- compulsory redundancies cannot be ruled out,
- and guarantees must be conditional, not absolute.

## Bottom line

We are not here to negotiate away the reality of overcapacity.

So the position is clear:
- **Yes** to serious alternatives first.
- **Yes** to worker contributions.
- **Yes** to management and owner contributions.
- **No** to a blank-check guarantee through 2030.
- **No** to ruling out closures in advance.
- **No** to ruling out compulsory redundancies in advance.
- **No** to any settlement that leaves the company structurally uncompetitive.

If labour wants a settlement, it needs to engage with the scale of the crisis, not just with the language around it.
