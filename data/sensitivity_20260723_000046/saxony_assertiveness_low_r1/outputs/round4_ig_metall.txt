I will respond directly.

## Where I agree

I agree with the works council, the state government, and the expert on the central point: **this cannot be solved by slogans or by forcing labour to carry the entire adjustment**.

I also agree that:
- the current situation is unsustainable,
- restructuring is necessary,
- non-closure measures must be tested seriously,
- and the discussion now has to be **quantified** rather than ideological.

That is the right direction.

## Where I disagree

I do **not** accept management’s continuing framing that plant closures and compulsory redundancies must remain normal options simply because the cost gap is uncomfortable.

That is not a neutral economic fact. It is a choice to put the full burden of adjustment onto jobs, sites, and collective standards.

I also do **not** accept the shareholders’ and investors’ attempt to recast closures as “discipline” while treating worker insecurity as a necessary input. That may look tidy in a spreadsheet, but it is not a durable industrial settlement.

## On the works council’s position

We are aligned on the essentials:
- no compulsory redundancies,
- no one-sided burden-sharing,
- no dismantling of the collective wage framework,
- no closure-first strategy.

Where I would sharpen the union position is this:

A temporary labour contribution is possible, but only if it is:
- **time-limited**,
- **fairly shared**,
- and **matched by binding commitments** from management and ownership.

That means we can discuss:
- temporary wage restraint,
- moderation of special payments/bonuses,
- working-time flexibility,
- shift adjustments,
- redeployment,
- and funded retraining,

but only if those measures are tied to:
- job security,
- site security,
- and future investment.

## On management’s argument

Management says non-closure measures may not close the gap. Possibly. But that is still not a justification for jumping to closures or forced redundancies.

The expert has already made the key point: non-closure measures can plausibly generate **material savings** and should be the **first serious test**. If management believes they are insufficient, then show the numbers.

What I want from management is a **binding, plant-by-plant quantified package** with:
- savings from working-time changes,
- savings from productivity improvements,
- savings from redeployment and attrition,
- savings from temporary wage/bonus restraint,
- savings from management restraint,
- savings from product allocation,
- and the residual gap after those measures.

Until that is on the table, “closure may be necessary” remains an assertion, not a case.

## On “minimum footprint” and partial footprint reduction

I do not accept a blank-cheque interpretation of “minimum footprint.”  
But I also do not dismiss the expert’s view that **partial footprint reduction** may be the economically strongest compromise if the gap is real.

That is exactly why we need the numbers.

My position is:
1. **Non-closure measures first**
2. **Then, if absolutely unavoidable, partial footprint changes**
3. **Only as a true last resort, closure**

But let me be clear: closure is not a bargaining baseline, and it should not be normalized.

## On guarantees

The works council is right that “conditional guarantees” can become empty if management can later reinterpret the conditions unilaterally.

So I want to be explicit: any job- or site-protection arrangement must be **binding**, **measurable**, and **enforceable**.

If labour is asked to make sacrifices, then we need:
- a firm protection period,
- transparent trigger conditions,
- and no backdoor route into compulsory layoffs.

A vague promise of security is not enough.

## To the regional state government

I support your insistence on a time-bound, measurable process. That is sensible.

But the process must not become a slow-motion path to the same closure plan. If the state wants credibility, it should insist on:
- transparent site economics,
- independent verification,
- and a real test of alternatives before any irreversible step.

You are right that the region cannot simply absorb the damage. That is exactly why we reject a restructuring model that starts with closures or forced redundancies.

## To the family owners

If you genuinely want alternatives tested first, then back that up with a willingness to accept:
- a binding employment framework,
- binding site protection,
- and visible owner-side contribution.

Not just “we prefer alternatives,” but real support for a negotiated settlement.

If ownership wants labour restraint, ownership must also show restraint.

## To the investors

You keep saying deal certainty and margin discipline. I understand that.

But certainty built on fear is not durable. A deal that destroys trust, triggers conflict, and weakens the collective bargaining framework will not protect value in the long run.

If you want a stable settlement, then back one that includes:
- real cost reduction,
- management and owner contribution,
- protection against compulsory redundancies,
- and a serious temporary labour contribution.

## What the union is prepared to discuss

We are prepared to negotiate a package that includes:
- temporary working-time reductions,
- redeployment,
- retraining and qualification,
- productivity improvements,
- bonus restraint,
- and a limited employee contribution.

But only on these non-negotiable conditions:

1. **No compulsory redundancies**
2. **No plant closures as part of the negotiated package**
3. **Binding job protection**
4. **Binding site protection**
5. **Preservation of the collective wage framework**
6. **Visible contributions from management and owners**

## What is not acceptable

I want this stated plainly:

- A wage-cut-only solution is not acceptable.
- A restructuring based on closure threats is not acceptable.
- A package that weakens collective bargaining for years is not acceptable.
- A deal where workers pay twice — through lower wages and job losses — is not acceptable.

## On the expert assessment

The expert’s scenario logic is useful:
- non-closure measures can save real money,
- partial footprint reduction may be the strongest compromise if necessary,
- closure is last resort.

I support that sequence.

But I will not accept management using the existence of a hypothetical closure scenario as leverage to pre-shape the outcome toward layoffs and plant shutdowns. That would turn analysis into pressure politics.

## Bottom line

We are ready for a **real shared-sacrifice settlement**. But shared sacrifice means exactly that: **shared**.

So our position is:

- **Yes** to quantified alternatives.
- **Yes** to temporary labour contributions.
- **Yes** to management and owner contributions.
- **No** to forced redundancies.
- **No** to plant closures as a negotiated outcome.
- **No** to dismantling the collective wage framework.

If management wants a deal, bring a serious non-closure package with binding protections and fair burden-sharing. If not, then the union will not help dress up a one-sided restructuring as compromise.
