I will respond directly.

## To the works council and union: I hear your concern, but your red lines do not solve the crisis
I agree with you on one point: **the company needs a future, not a cosmetic fix**. I also agree that employees should not be the only ones contributing.

But I do **not** accept your central claim that:
- every German plant must remain open,
- no compulsory redundancies may ever be used,
- and 2030 guarantees can be made without regard to footprint economics.

That combination is not a solution to the cost crisis. It is a demand to preserve the current structure regardless of whether it is affordable.

You say “show the alternatives.” Fine — I am doing exactly that. But alternatives must be **large enough** to close the gap. If they do not, then they are not serious alternatives; they are delay mechanisms.

## On plant closures
Let me be clear: I am not advocating closures for their own sake. I am saying they remain on the table because **overcapacity is real** and because this company cannot fund excess footprint indefinitely.

If we can achieve the necessary structural savings without closing a plant, I would prefer that. But I will not promise in advance to rule closures out. That would be irresponsible to the owners, to investors, and to the company’s long-term viability.

A closure is serious and irreversible. Exactly because of that, it must remain available if the numbers require it.

## On compulsory redundancies
I prefer voluntary exits, redeployment, retraining, and working-time solutions. But I will not rule out compulsory measures if the package does not deliver enough durable savings.

That is not a threat for effect. It is a straightforward statement of reality: if the company remains overstaffed relative to demand and cost targets, then we need tools that actually reduce the cost base.

## On “no closures, no layoffs”
That position sounds protective, but it only works if it still delivers a competitive footprint. So far, I have not heard a quantified package from labour that proves it can.

If you want us to rule out closures, then bring:
- hard savings from working-time reduction,
- hard savings from redeployment,
- hard savings from productivity measures,
- hard savings from wage restraint and bonus moderation,
- and a credible timetable that closes the cost gap.

Until then, “no closures” is not a plan. It is a prohibition.

## On job and site guarantees
A blanket employment and site guarantee through 2030 is too rigid if it blocks restructuring.

I am prepared to discuss **conditional protections**:
- time-limited guarantees,
- staged review points,
- protections tied to agreed cost and capacity measures,
- and security for those sites and jobs that remain viable in the future footprint.

But I will not sign a promise that freezes the current footprint and prevents necessary adjustment.

Guarantees are only credible if they are matched by real competitiveness improvements.

## To the regional state government
You are right that closures have regional costs and should not be used casually. I do not dismiss that.

But your insistence that alternatives be exhausted first must be grounded in **time-bound, quantified action**. It cannot become an open-ended veto over change.

If we spend months proving what everyone already knows — that the status quo is expensive — then we will have wasted time we do not have. The company needs a decision that actually restores competitiveness, not a prolonged process that preserves uncertainty.

## To the controlling family owners
You say you want alternatives tested first. I agree. But I need a clear answer from ownership: **if the alternatives do not close the gap, will you support the necessary structural decisions?**

Because “prefer alternatives” is not the same as “support whatever restructuring is required.” The company needs a real mandate to fix the cost base.

If ownership is serious about long-term value, it cannot ask management to deliver margin recovery while ruling out the instruments that may be needed to achieve it.

## To the institutional investors
You are right to insist on credibility and deal certainty. A cosmetic compromise would destroy value.

But let me turn that around: **a deal that preserves too much capacity and too much cost is also not credible**. It simply shifts the problem into the future and leaves the return-on-sales target out of reach.

So yes, the package must be durable. It must also be sufficiently hard. Anything less will not restore competitiveness.

## What I am prepared to accept
I am prepared to discuss a package that includes:
- meaningful working-time flexibility,
- internal redeployment,
- retraining and qualification,
- productivity improvements,
- temporary labour-cost restraint,
- management-side restraint,
- and a credible investment commitment into future products and technology.

But only if it delivers:
1. **real structural cost reduction**,  
2. **a substantial reduction in capacity**,  
3. **a labour contribution that is durable and meaningful**, and  
4. **a business model that can fund investment through 2030**.

## What I will not accept
I will not accept:
- a cosmetic compromise,
- a deal that leaves overcapacity intact,
- a package that protects every site regardless of economics,
- or a settlement that asks management to pretend the margin problem does not exist.

I also will not accept a situation in which we are forced into repeated crisis bargaining every year because no structural decision was made now.

## My bottom line
We are willing to negotiate seriously. But the outcome must be judged by one test:

**Does it restore competitiveness on a durable basis?**

If the answer is no, then it is not acceptable — no matter how many times it is described as balanced or socially responsible.

So here is the position:
- **Alternatives yes, but only if they are sufficient.**
- **Protection yes, but only if it is conditional and compatible with restructuring.**
- **Investment yes, but only if the cost base is fixed first.**
- **No cosmetic deal. No permanent overcapacity. No empty guarantees.**

If the other stakeholders want a settlement, bring a package that closes the gap. Otherwise, management will have to use the instruments that remain available.
