## Scenario B: Partial footprint reduction
Measures:
- plant specialization
- line consolidation
- shift elimination at selected sites
- relocation of some volume
- closure of one line or one function within a plant
- reduction of non-core operations while keeping a site open

This is often the **best economic compromise** when management needs more than labor measures but wants to avoid full closures.

### Typical savings potential
- **Annual savings:** €700 million to €1.8 billion
- **One-time restructuring cost:** €500 million to €1.5 billion
- **Timing:** 12–30 months to full run-rate
- **ROS impact:** about **+0.6 to +1.5 percentage points**

### Plant-by-plant benchmark
| Plant type | One-time cost | Annual savings | Main mechanism |
|---|---:|---:|---|
| Large assembly plant (reduced shifts) | €80m–€200m | €150m–€350m | shift removal, lower fixed overhead, higher utilization |
| Powertrain/component plant (consolidated) | €100m–€300m | €200m–€500m | line consolidation, specialization, logistics simplification |
| Smaller/specialized plant (partial rationalization) | €50m–€150m | €80m–€250m | reduced footprint, function transfer, lower indirect cost |
| Network/logistics rationalization | €100m–€300m | €100m–€300m | supplier clustering, transport reduction, inventory efficiency |

### Assessment
Partial footprint reduction often delivers a **better savings-to-cost ratio** than full closure, because it preserves some fixed assets and avoids the highest social and transition costs while still removing meaningful capacity.

## Scenario C: Full plant closure
Measures:
- full shutdown of one or more German plants
- transfer of production elsewhere
- asset write-downs
- workforce reduction
- supplier network restructuring

### Typical savings potential
- **Annual savings:** €1.0 billion to €2.5 billion per major plant closure package
- **One-time restructuring cost:** €1.0 billion to €3.0 billion or more
- **Timing:** 18–48 months
- **ROS impact:** about **+0.8 to +2.0 percentage points**

### Plant-by-plant benchmark
| Plant type | One-time cost | Annual savings | Main mechanism |
|---|---:|---:|---|
| Large assembly plant closure | €500m–€1.5bn | €300m–€700m | elimination of fixed site cost and indirect labor |
| Powertrain/component plant closure | €300m–€1.0bn | €200m–€600m | fixed overhead removal, asset rationalization |
| Smaller/specialized site closure | €200m–€600m | €100m–€250m | site overhead, maintenance, staffing removal |
| Network/supplier transition costs | €200m–€700m | included above or additional | tooling transfers, logistics changes, launch risk |

### Important caveat
Closure savings are often **overstated in simple models** because they must net out:
- severance and social plan costs,
- parallel running during transfer,
- product launch disruption,
- supplier requalification,
- decommissioning,
- impairment charges,
- and sometimes higher logistics costs at the receiving site.

So while closure can produce the largest theoretical fixed-cost reduction, the **net present value** may be less attractive than it appears if execution is difficult or if the receiving plant is already near capacity.

# 3) Cash flow timing

## A. Non-closure measures
### Cash flow pattern
- **Year 0–1:** restructuring expense, then early savings begin
- **Year 1–2:** full run-rate savings often reached
- **Payback:** often within **6–18 months** if the measures are negotiated effectively

### Why it is attractive
- Low execution risk
- Lower social disruption
- Faster access to savings
- Often the best first-step solution

---

## B. Partial footprint reduction
### Cash flow pattern
- **Year 0–1:** planning costs, engineering, negotiation, line changes
- **Year 1–3:** gradual ramp of savings as capacity is removed
- **Payback:** often **2–4 years**

### Why it is attractive
- Preserves viable parts of the footprint
- Removes enough capacity to matter
- Usually less destructive than closure
- Often best for ROS recovery if the gap is structural but not extreme

---

## C. Full closure
### Cash flow pattern
- **Year 0–2:** heavy restructuring outflow, asset write-downs, dual-running costs
- **Year 2–4:** savings appear as volume transfers complete
- **Payback:** often **3–6 years** depending on transition complexity

### Why it may still be chosen
- If a plant is structurally uncompetitive
- If utilization is chronically weak
- If product allocation cannot sustain the site
- If the annual fixed-cost burden is too high to solve otherwise

# 5) What would make a closure economically necessary?

A closure becomes economically justified only if several conditions are met:

1. **Persistent underutilization**
   - low volumes with no credible recovery path

2. **No future product allocation**
   - no next-generation model or platform assigned

3. **High fixed cost per unit**
   - labor, overhead, utilities, and maintenance remain uncompetitive even after productivity work

4. **Alternatives insufficient**
   - non-closure and partial reduction do not close the cost gap

5. **Transition economics still positive**
   - after one-time costs, the present value of savings exceeds the investment required to close

If these are not all present, closure may be premature.
