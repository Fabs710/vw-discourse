I’ll be direct.

## Where we agree
I agree with management, the family owners, and the state on the core diagnosis: **the status quo is not acceptable**.  
If German operations stay structurally too expensive, margins will remain impaired, the market will keep discounting the business, and equity value will continue to suffer.

I also agree with the labor side on one important point: **employees should not be the only ones carrying the adjustment**. A prolonged strike would destroy value, and a purely one-sided cut-and-close approach would create political and industrial backlash.

## Where I disagree

### 1) “No closures, no redundancies” is not acceptable if it preserves overcapacity
To the Works Council and Union: your line is understandable, but from a shareholder perspective it is only acceptable **if it still produces a cost base that is genuinely competitive**.

If the package rules out closures and forced reductions in a way that leaves the footprint too large, then it is not a solution. It is delay. And delay is value-destructive.

We will not support a cosmetic settlement that:
- keeps excess capacity in place,
- leaves margins structurally impaired,
- or postpones the real adjustment into the future.

### 2) Broad 2030 guarantees are only acceptable if they are conditional and earned
A blanket job and site guarantee through 2030, with no meaningful flexibility, is too rigid if it blocks the restructuring needed to restore returns.

I am open to transitional protections, but only if they are tied to:
- measurable cost savings,
- productivity improvements,
- capacity adjustments,
- and a credible investment program.

Guarantees without hard economics simply freeze the problem.

### 3) “Exhaust alternatives first” must mean real alternatives, not open-ended stalemate
I agree with the state and the family owners that closures should not be the first reflex. But “alternatives first” cannot become an indefinite review process while the margin gap stays open.

Alternatives are acceptable only if they are:
- quantified,
- durable,
- large enough to matter,
- and implemented on a clear timetable.

If they do not close the competitiveness gap, then they are not serious alternatives.

## What would move us
We are prepared to support a negotiated restructuring if it delivers all of the following:

1. **A durable reduction in the cost base**
   - not temporary accounting relief,
   - but real structural savings.

2. **A meaningful capacity reduction**
   - through closures, partial closures, product reallocation, or other equivalent footprint changes,
   - but sufficient to match demand and restore returns.

3. **A material labor contribution**
   - working-time flexibility,
   - wage moderation,
   - bonus restraint,
   - redeployment,
   - and productivity commitments.

4. **Visible management and owner contribution**
   - if employees sacrifice, the top and the capital side must also contribute.

5. **Deal certainty**
   - the market needs confidence that the package will actually be implemented,
   - not blocked later by renewed vetoes or conflict.

6. **A credible path back to investment and margin recovery**
   - because without that, restructuring is just decline management.

## What we will not support
We will not back:
- a cosmetic compromise,
- a settlement that preserves structural overcapacity,
- a package that leaves the return-on-sales target out of reach,
- or prolonged conflict that burns earnings without fixing the footprint.

## To management
You are right that economics matter more than rhetoric. But you still have to demonstrate that the measures on the table are sufficient. If you want investors to support difficult change, bring **hard numbers**:
- what savings come from working-time changes,
- what from redeployment,
- what from productivity actions,
- what from labor-cost moderation,
- and what from footprint changes.

If the answer is “not enough unless a plant closes,” then say so clearly and prove it. If there is a credible non-closure path, we would prefer that — but only if it is real.

## To the Works Council and Union
Your insistence on job security is understandable, but you need to recognize that a settlement that avoids closures at all costs can destroy the very value you are trying to protect. A company with weak margins and too much capacity cannot guarantee good jobs indefinitely.

If you want a durable compromise, then the labor contribution must be meaningful enough to restore competitiveness, not just symbolic or temporary.

## To the state and family owners
Your preference for alternatives first is reasonable, but it must be disciplined and time-bound. We do not support endless negotiation while value erodes. If alternatives are genuinely sufficient, fine. If not, then the company needs the freedom to make hard structural decisions.

## Bottom line
Our test is simple:

**Does the package credibly restore competitive margins and protect long-term value?**

- If yes, we can support it.
- If no, we will not.

We are not attached to closures as ideology. We are attached to outcomes. But we will not endorse a “protected” settlement that leaves the company uncompetitive.
