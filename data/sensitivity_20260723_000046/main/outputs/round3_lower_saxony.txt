I will be direct.

## Where I agree
I agree with everyone on the basic diagnosis: **this company needs a durable turnaround**. The current cost and capacity structure is not sustainable, and future investment in products and technology is essential. I also agree that any package must be **implementable** under co-determination and cannot be merely symbolic.

I further agree with the expert’s central point: if closures are ruled out, then the alternative package must be **large enough and durable enough** to close the gap. That is the real question.

## Where I disagree
### 1) I do not accept closures as a first or default answer
Management, the family holding, and investors keep treating German closures as if they were just a normal adjustment tool. For me, they are not a normal starting point. As anchor shareholder and regional political actor, I cannot support a path that begins by gutting regional jobs and industrial capacity.

I am not saying change is optional. I am saying **there must be a serious test of alternatives before any closure is even considered**.

### 2) I do not accept compulsory redundancies as an easy fallback
I understand the logic that forced layoffs create fast savings. But I do not accept them as the default solution in a company that is central to the regional economy and embedded in a co-determined governance structure.

If management cannot show that a negotiated alternative is insufficient, then forcing redundancies would be economically risky, socially damaging, and politically unacceptable.

### 3) I do not accept a “credible” plan that breaks industrial peace
To management and capital: a plan that triggers lasting conflict, strikes, and board deadlock is not implementation certainty. It is a risk event.

To the works council and union: a plan must be financially credible, yes. But credibility also means that the company can actually execute it without destroying trust and governance.

## What I take from the expert
The expert did not say “closures are inevitable.” The expert said:
- alternatives to closures can work,
- but only if they are substantial,
- and only if they really remove cost and capacity.

That is the right frame. So the burden now is on management to quantify the gap and show, line by line, what cannot be achieved through:
- capacity rationalization,
- shift changes,
- redeployment,
- productivity gains,
- overhead reduction,
- attrition,
- voluntary exits,
- and fair, temporary employee contributions.

Until that is shown, I will not accept the claim that closures or forced redundancies are indispensable.

## To the works council and union
I hear your red lines:
- no closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- no dismantling of the wage and co-determination framework.

I support those red lines as a political and shareholder position, but I also need you to answer the expert’s challenge honestly: **what is the quantified alternative?**

It is not enough to say “capacity reduction” in general. I need to know:
- how much capacity can be removed without closures,
- how much cost reduction comes from working-time and wage measures,
- how much from overhead and management contribution,
- and what the durable run-rate saving is.

If the alternative package is too small, then it will not be credible. If it is large enough, then management must engage seriously with it.

## To management
Your insistence on hard economics is understandable. But you are still underestimating the limits of what can be imposed here.

You say:
- no closure prohibition,
- no blanket ban on compulsory redundancies,
- no guarantee through 2030 without structural change.

I can accept that you need flexibility. I cannot accept that you present closures and layoffs as the first serious answer before exhausting a negotiated package that preserves sites and jobs.

If you want my support, then come with:
1. a quantified savings gap,
2. a quantified alternative package,
3. a clear explanation of why specific measures are insufficient,
4. and a proposal that protects the regional base as far as possible.

Without that, your position is too close to a fait accompli.

## To the family holding and investors
I agree that symbolism is not enough. But I do not accept a definition of “discipline” that ignores the company’s social and regional anchoring.

A solution that is financially neat but politically explosive is not durable. It may satisfy a model, but it will create resistance, reputational damage, and long-term instability.

If you want my support, then accept a package that:
- delivers real savings,
- avoids unnecessary destruction of sites and jobs,
- and preserves the company as a regional anchor.

## My line, clearly stated
I am prepared to support a **tough negotiated restructuring** if it meets these conditions:
- no German plant closures as the starting solution,
- no compulsory redundancies unless every viable alternative is demonstrably exhausted,
- substantial capacity and cost reduction through negotiated means,
- fair burden-sharing from all sides,
- binding investment commitments,
- preservation of co-determination and the state’s veto role.

I am **not** prepared to support:
- a closure-led restructuring,
- a plan that treats regional employment as expendable,
- or a deal that weakens the state’s ability to act as an anchor shareholder.

## What I want next
I want a **quantified alternative scenario** on the table. Not slogans, not generalities.

If management wants to argue that closures are unavoidable, then it must show:
- the exact savings target,
- what proportion can be achieved without closures,
- what the remaining gap is,
- and why that gap cannot be closed through other means.

If that cannot be shown, then the pressure to close plants is premature and unacceptable.
