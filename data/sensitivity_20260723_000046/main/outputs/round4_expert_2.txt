Below is a decision-focused comparison of the three restructuring paths, using the expert’s stated ranges and standard German OEM restructuring economics. I’ll keep to finance, operations, capacity, employment, and governance feasibility.

## Executive view

A **no-closure package can be credible**, but only if it reaches the **upper end of the feasible savings range** and materially reduces the fixed-cost base through headcount, shifts, overhead, and labor-cost measures.

A **partial-footprint reduction** is the most likely compromise if the company’s savings gap is judged to be above roughly **€700m–€900m per year**.

A **closure-led restructuring** yields the largest recurring savings, but it also has the highest one-time charges, execution risk, and governance risk under German co-determination and blocking-shareholder constraints.

---

## Scenario comparison

### 1) No-closure restructuring
**Core features**
- No German plant closures
- No compulsory redundancies
- Voluntary exits, retirement bridges, attrition
- Working-time and shift reductions
- Wage moderation / bonus restraint
- Overhead and management cost cuts
- Productivity improvement

**Indicative economics**
- **Recurring savings:** about **€500m–€900m/year**
  - lower end if concessions are shallow
  - upper end if workforce reduction and overhead cuts are strong
- **One-time restructuring cost:** about **€600m–€1.4bn**
- **Employment impact:** likely **high single-digit to low double-digit %** reduction in domestic workforce over 2–4 years, mostly voluntary/natural attrition
- **Implementation risk:** **medium**
  - lower industrial conflict risk than closure-led
  - but still requires very large negotiated contributions

**When it works**
- If the real savings gap is around **€400m–€700m/year**
- Or if management is willing to accept a very disciplined, heavily front-loaded package near the top end

**Main limitation**
- If the company needs more than roughly **€900m–€1.0bn/year**, no-closure starts to look stretched unless capacity can be reduced indirectly by removing shifts and consolidating product allocation.

---

### 2) Partial-footprint reduction
**Core features**
- No immediate full closures across the footprint, but:
  - one or more lines, shifts, or functions are removed
  - product allocation is consolidated
  - certain facilities are downsized
  - workforce reductions are more structural
- Can be structured as a limited site consolidation rather than outright closure

**Indicative economics**
- **Recurring savings:** about **€700m–€1.5bn/year**
- **One-time restructuring cost:** about **€1.0bn–€2.0bn+**
- **Employment impact:** materially larger than no-closure; likely **mid- to high single-digit thousands** at a large domestic base, depending on scope
- **Implementation risk:** **medium-high**
  - still difficult under co-determination
  - but more economically effective than a pure no-closure package

**When it works**
- If management’s true gap is structurally larger than a no-closure package can cover
- If parties need a compromise between preserving the industrial base and restoring competitiveness

**Main advantage**
- Better fixed-cost reduction than no-closure, without the full political and social shock of closing a plant outright

---

### 3) Closure-led restructuring
**Core features**
- Closure of one or more German plants
- Larger workforce reduction
- Structural reduction in domestic capacity
- Stronger labor-cost and overhead reset

**Indicative economics**
- **Recurring savings:** about **€1.0bn–€2.0bn+ / year**
- **One-time restructuring cost:** about **€1.5bn–€3.0bn+**
- **Employment impact:** largest of the three; potentially **several thousand to well over ten thousand** jobs, depending on plant size and supplier spillovers
- **Implementation risk:** **high**
  - highest chance of supervisory-board deadlock
  - highest strike/industrial action risk
  - highest execution and timeline risk

**When it works**
- If the structural gap is truly large and cannot be closed by negotiated flexibility
- If governance can be aligned and labor conflict contained

**Main limitation**
- It can destroy value if it triggers prolonged disruption or fails to obtain the required approvals.

## What is “credible” under co-determination?

Given the governance structure you described, the practical question is not just economics but **implementability**.

### A no-closure deal is credible if:
- it delivers at least **€600m–€900m/year** recurring savings
- it includes **permanent** headcount and overhead reduction, not just temporary concessions
- it has a visible management contribution
- it is auditable quarter by quarter
- it avoids hidden closure mechanics that would collapse trust later

### It becomes less credible if:
- the savings are mostly temporary
- capacity reduction is too small to change the fixed-cost base
- wage restraint substitutes for real structural change
- management believes it can later reopen closures through a review process

## Key sensitivities

### 1) Required savings gap
This is the main sensitivity.

- If the true gap is **€400m–€600m/year**, no-closure is plausible.
- If it is **€700m–€900m/year**, no-closure is possible but needs to be at the top end.
- If it is **above ~€1.0bn/year**, partial-footprint reduction becomes much more likely.
- If it is **well above €1.2bn/year**, closure-led restructuring becomes financially more compelling, though not necessarily politically or governance-feasible.

### 2) How much of the saving is permanent
Capital markets will discount temporary measures heavily. Durable savings matter most.

### 3) Speed of realization
A package that saves money only after 2–3 years has less present value than one that starts immediately, even if headline annual savings are similar.

### 4) One-time cash cost
If the company is liquidity-constrained, high upfront charges may be difficult even when the long-run economics are better.

### 5) Industrial-relations stability
A lower-savings plan that is implemented cleanly can outperform a higher-savings plan that gets delayed by conflict.

## Practical conclusion

If I were framing this strictly as restructuring finance:

- **No-closure** is the best option only if management can live with savings in the **€500m–€900m/year** range and can get permanent labor-cost relief.
- **Partial-footprint reduction** is the most balanced option if the gap is larger and if the goal is to preserve as much of the domestic base as possible while still materially cutting fixed cost.
- **Closure-led restructuring** is the highest-savings route, but it is also the most expensive, risky, and difficult to implement under the governance constraints you described.
