I will be direct.

I agree with everyone on one point: the company needs a real turnaround, not a cosmetic patch. The market pressure is real, and investment in future products is necessary. But let me be equally clear: **the workforce will not pay for management’s strategic mistakes by sacrificing plants, jobs, and the job-security framework.**

### On management’s position
Management keeps saying that closures and forced redundancies may be “necessary.” That is precisely the kind of language that triggers resistance, because it sounds like a fait accompli before genuine negotiation has even begun.

I reject three things outright:

- **German plant closures**
- **compulsory redundancies**
- **breaking the historic job-security commitments**

If management comes to us with a plan built on those assumptions, then it is not a negotiation; it is an ultimatum. That will not get acceptance from the workforce, and it will not be implementable without severe conflict.

### On the family holding and investors
You both talk about “durable savings” and “competitiveness,” which is understandable from an ownership perspective. But you are treating jobs and sites as if they were just variables on a spreadsheet. They are not. They are communities, skills, supply chains, and decades of trust.

I hear the demand for real savings. Fine. But let’s be honest: **there are alternatives to closures and forced layoffs** if management is prepared to act seriously:

- capacity reductions through negotiated planning,
- internal redeployment,
- working-time adjustments,
- temporary and fair employee contributions,
- productivity improvements,
- investment discipline,
- and a binding industrial strategy for Germany.

What I do **not** accept is the claim that the only credible savings come from destroying jobs and closing plants.

### On the state government
Your support for site protection is welcome, and on that point we are aligned. But support is not enough. If the state wants to act as a serious anchor shareholder, then it must also use its influence to ensure that management does not convert a restructuring into industrial destruction.

I would expect the state to back a package with:
- **all German plants kept open**
- **no forced redundancies**
- **guaranteed investment commitments**
- **a binding 2030 employment and site framework**

### What we are prepared to discuss
We are not saying “nothing changes.” We are saying **change must be negotiated and socially responsible**. We are prepared to discuss:

- working-time flexibility,
- internal efficiency measures,
- temporary burden-sharing,
- capacity rebalancing,
- product and investment commitments,
- and productivity agreements.

But there is a hard condition: **no closures, no forced layoffs, and no erosion of co-determination.**

### The key point on credibility
Management and capital keep saying the package must be “credible.” So let me be equally blunt: **a plan is not credible if it cannot be implemented because it detonates labour peace and triggers a governance blockade.** Under co-determination, under the special shareholder structure, and under the current mood of the workforce, you cannot force through a settlement that destroys the social foundation of the company and expect stability.

We are ready for serious negotiations. But the boundary is unchanged:

- every German plant stays open,
- there are no compulsory redundancies,
- the job-security framework is preserved,
- and the workforce has a real say in shaping the future.

If those conditions are not met, then the conflict will continue — in the supervisory board, in collective bargaining, and on the shop floor.
