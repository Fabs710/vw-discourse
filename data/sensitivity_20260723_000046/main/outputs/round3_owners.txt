I’ll be direct.

## Where I agree
I agree with the expert and with management on the central economic point: **the current cost and capacity structure is not sustainable**. If the company does not restore margins, it will lose strategic room for investment and long-term value will keep eroding. That is unacceptable from an ownership perspective.

I also agree with the workforce side on one important point: **a solution that detonates labour peace and becomes unimplementable is not a solution**. A prolonged conflict would destroy value just as surely as inaction.

So yes: we need a **durable**, **implementable**, **value-restoring** restructuring.

## Where I disagree
### 1) I do not accept absolute red lines that make restructuring impossible
The works council and union keep insisting on:
- no plant closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- no erosion of existing guarantees.

I understand the political logic, but I do **not** accept those as unconditional vetoes if they prevent a credible turnaround.

The expert was clear: a no-closure package can work **only if** the alternative measures are genuinely large enough. So the burden is now on the workforce side to show, in numbers, how that gap is closed. So far, I have not seen that.

### 2) I do not accept a cosmetic “shared sacrifice” package
If the result is mainly temporary concessions, softer language, and a promise of future productivity, that is not enough. The company needs **durable** savings and **real** capacity adjustment.

A package that preserves every legacy structure but leaves the business uncompetitive would be a delay mechanism, not stewardship.

### 3) I do not accept a deal that makes governance impossible
I will not support an outcome that is so one-sided or so politically charged that it cannot survive the supervisory board, the special shareholder constraints, or the industrial relations environment. That would destroy value through deadlock.

## My reading of the expert’s scenarios
The expert’s assessment is helpful because it separates **what is economically possible** from **what is politically easiest**.

- A **pure no-closure solution** may be possible, but only if the savings are moderate or employee-side concessions are unusually deep and durable.
- A **partial-footprint reduction** looks like the most realistic compromise.
- A **closure-led restructuring** may deliver the highest gross savings, but in this governance setup it has the highest execution risk and therefore the highest value-destruction risk if forced through badly.

That is exactly the right hierarchy.

## What I can support
I could support a package if it contains all of the following:
1. **Real, quantified savings**
2. **Meaningful, durable reduction in overcapacity**
3. **A serious employee contribution**
4. **Clear management contribution and operational discipline**
5. **Implementation certainty under co-determination**
6. **Protection of long-term investment capacity**
7. **No prolonged industrial conflict**

I am open on the instrument mix:
- capacity reductions,
- shift rationalisation,
- working-time changes,
- voluntary exits,
- redeployment,
- productivity improvements,
- overhead reduction,
- and, if necessary, selective footprint rationalisation.

What matters is that the package is **material**, **durable**, and **real**.

## What I will not support
I will not support:
- a purely symbolic compromise,
- a preservation strategy dressed up as restructuring,
- a package too small to restore competitiveness,
- or an open-ended conflict that weakens the company’s core asset.

I also will not support a politically convenient solution that protects every structure today but leaves the family holding with a weaker business tomorrow.

## To the works council and union
If you want to avoid harder measures, then bring forward a **quantified alternative** that shows:
- how much capacity comes out,
- how much labour cost is saved,
- how much comes from productivity and overhead,
- and why the result is durable.

Not slogans. Numbers.

If the alternative is strong enough, I will consider it seriously. If it is not, then I will not pretend it is sufficient.

## To management
You are right to insist that the turnaround must be economically real. But do not confuse seriousness with ultimatum politics. If you want my support, you need a package that can actually pass governance and industrial relations.

That means:
- precise savings targets,
- a credible sequencing plan,
- and a realistic assessment of what can be implemented without triggering destruction.

## To the state government
I value your role as an anchor shareholder and your concern for regional stability. But stability does not mean freezing the company in place. If the alternative to change is long-term erosion, then that is not a responsible path either.

## My position, clearly stated
I support a **tough negotiated restructuring** if it restores competitiveness and margins in a way that can actually be implemented.

I do **not** support:
- a no-change compromise,
- a cosmetic settlement,
- or a deal that preserves the full footprint while leaving the company structurally weak.

If the workforce side wants to avoid closures and compulsory redundancies, then it must produce a package that closes the gap in a credible, durable way. If it cannot, then management should not be blocked from pursuing stronger structural measures.

If useful, I would now like to see a **single consolidated package proposal** with:
- annual savings target,
- one-time cost,
- employment impact,
- and which measures are temporary versus permanent.
