From the perspective of dispersed capital-market shareholders, the key issue is not who “wins” the bargaining narrative, but whether the company gets back to a credible, durable margin structure.

We agree with management, and to a degree with the family holding, on the central diagnosis: the current cost base and capacity footprint are not competitive enough. If that is not fixed, then the equity story deteriorates further, investment capacity weakens, and eventually everyone loses more.

That said, we are not aligned with any solution that is mostly symbolic or simply defers the problem.

### Where we agree
- The company needs a **durable restructuring**, not a temporary patch.
- It needs a path back to **competitive return-on-sales and margins**.
- It needs **investment capacity** for future products and technology.
- A negotiated solution is preferable if it creates **deal certainty** and avoids prolonged destructive conflict.

### Where we disagree
- We do **not** support a settlement that leaves the cost base structurally uncompetitive.
- We do **not** support “preserve everything” commitments if they prevent the savings needed to restore returns.
- We also do **not** support open-ended confrontation and strike escalation if it just destroys near-term value without producing a better end state.

The works council and union are right that a cosmetic deal is not credible. But they are wrong if they believe the company can regain competitiveness without meaningful structural change. That is not a viable investment case. If the result is merely site guarantees, job guarantees, and modest internal efficiencies while the underlying economics remain weak, then the equity remains under pressure and capital will continue to be allocated elsewhere.

### Our position
We would support a package only if it credibly delivers:
1. **Material, durable cost reduction**
2. **A real reduction in overcapacity**
3. **Visible labor-cost contribution**
4. **A clear path to margin recovery**
5. **Implementation certainty**

We are open on the method:
- closures,
- capacity reductions,
- redeployment,
- working-time flexibility,
- labor-cost concessions,
- productivity measures,

but the outcome must be measured against one test: does it restore competitive economics?

### What would be unacceptable
- A purely political compromise that protects all sites but leaves margins impaired.
- A “shared sacrifice” package that is too small to matter.
- Prolonged industrial action that erodes value more than it improves bargaining outcomes.
- Any arrangement that sacrifices future investment to preserve an oversized structure.

### Bottom line
We are not attached to closures as an ideological goal. We are attached to **financial discipline**. If management can achieve the necessary savings without closures, fine — but only if the numbers are real and durable. If it cannot, then management should not be blocked from taking tougher measures simply to preserve the status quo.

We will judge the eventual outcome by whether it restores the company’s competitiveness and shareholder value. Anything less is not good enough.
