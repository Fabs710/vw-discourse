I will respond directly and clearly.

### Where I agree
I agree with the Works Council, the state government, and even parts of management on one essential point: **the company needs a durable turnaround**. A cosmetic deal will not help anyone. The market pressure is real, investment in future products is necessary, and the company does need a credible path back to competitiveness.

I also agree that a purely symbolic compromise would be irresponsible. If employees are asked to contribute, the result must actually improve the company’s outlook.

### Where I disagree
I do **not** accept the framing that **plant closures** or **compulsory redundancies** are “just options on the table” in the same way as working-time flexibility or productivity measures.

From the union’s perspective, that crosses a line. We have already made clear that we are prepared to discuss:
- labour-cost contributions,
- working-time changes,
- capacity reductions,
- bonus waivers or similar employee-side sacrifices,
- productivity measures,
- and a negotiated future investment framework.

But we are **not** prepared to accept a restructuring in which workers bear the main burden through closures, forced layoffs, and the dismantling of the collective wage framework.

### To management
Your argument is that the current structure is uncompetitive and that structural correction is unavoidable. I hear that. But your conclusion is too one-sided.

If you want a settlement, then you need to show that management and owners are also making a real contribution. Right now, your position sounds like this: workers should take wage and job pain first, and only then maybe the rest of the system contributes. That is not a fair basis for an agreement.

A durable settlement must include:
1. **No compulsory redundancies**
2. **No German plant closures**
3. **A real employee contribution**
4. **A real management and owner contribution**
5. **Binding investment and product commitments**
6. **A negotiated reduction in overcapacity without destroying the industrial base**

If management cannot meet those conditions, then the conflict will not end at the bargaining table.

### To the Works Council
We are aligned on the red lines: no closures, no compulsory redundancies, no dismantling of the wage framework. That is the right starting point.

Where I would sharpen the position is this: we should not just say “no” — we should insist on a **shared-sacrifice package** that is concrete enough to be credible. That means we should be ready to discuss:
- temporary wage restraint or deferred elements,
- reduced or waived bonuses,
- flexible working-time arrangements,
- capacity adaptation through negotiated means,
- and an employee contribution tied to firm site and employment guarantees.

But this only works if it is **mutual sacrifice**, not workers paying alone.

### To the family holding and investors
You both say you want durability and competitiveness. Then accept the consequence: a deal that is built on open-ended insecurity, forced redundancies, or closures will not produce industrial peace and therefore will not be durable.

If you want implementation certainty, then you need an outcome the workforce can actually live with. That means:
- a fair burden split,
- credible guarantees,
- and a restructuring that does not tear up the collective framework.

A “financially clean” solution that is socially explosive is not durable. It will come back in strikes, resistance, and governance deadlock.

### To the state government
Your support for site and job protection is welcome. But support has to translate into leverage. If you are serious about preserving the industrial base, then help push management toward a negotiated package that includes:
- no forced layoffs,
- no closures,
- and a serious employment and investment framework through 2030.

That is the only kind of package that can stabilize the company and the region.

### What we are prepared to discuss
Let me be very concrete. The union is prepared to discuss:
- wage moderation or temporary concessions,
- employee-financed contribution elements,
- working-time flexibility,
- internal redeployment,
- capacity reduction without closures,
- productivity agreements,
- and a future-oriented investment package.

But the conditions are clear:
- **no compulsory redundancies**
- **no plant closures**
- **no one-sided burden shifting onto employees**
- **no dismantling of the collective agreement structure**

### Bottom line
If management wants a real settlement, it must come back with a proposal that protects jobs and sites, distributes sacrifice fairly, and gives the workforce a durable future.

If the proposal still relies on closures or forced layoffs, then I say plainly: **that will not be accepted, and we will continue to use all available industrial means to resist it.**
