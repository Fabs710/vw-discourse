I’ll be equally direct.

The union and works council continue to argue as if **site preservation itself were the solution**. It is not. Preserving every plant, every legacy guarantee, and every rigid working rule does **not** restore competitiveness. It merely postpones the reckoning and leaves the company with the same overcapacity, the same above-target cost base, and less time to fix it.

## Where I agree
I agree on three points:
- The company needs a **durable** turnaround, not a cosmetic one.
- Industrial peace matters, because a breakdown in execution would hurt everyone.
- Employee contributions must be part of any settlement.

That is where the agreement ends.

## Where I disagree — and why

### 1) “No German plant closures” is not a serious absolute
The works council says every German plant must stay open. The union repeats that closures are off the table. I have to say clearly: **that is not a credible response to structural overcapacity**.

We are not dealing with a temporary dip. We are facing:
- a shrinking European market,
- intense low-cost competition,
- a cost structure above target,
- and a brand that is missing its return-on-sales goal.

If the footprint stays untouched, the company stays uncompetitive. That is not a turnaround.

### 2) “No compulsory redundancies” cannot be treated as a blanket veto
I understand the wish to avoid forced layoffs. But if the workforce side rules out the instruments that actually reduce fixed costs, then it is asking management to carry the entire burden of correction while employees keep full protection. That is not a balanced basis for restructuring.

I am not saying compulsory redundancies are the preferred route. I am saying they **cannot be excluded in advance** if the alternative does not close the gap.

### 3) “Voluntary only” is not enough unless the numbers add up
The expert was right to say a no-closure path is only possible if the alternative package is **large enough**. That is the key point.

So I will be blunt:
- temporary wage restraint,
- bonus moderation,
- working-time flexibility,
- and redeployment

may help, but they are **not sufficient on their own** if the company needs deep, durable savings.

If you want to avoid closures and forced redundancies, then show me a package that actually delivers the required savings at scale. Not slogans. Numbers.

## To the Works Council
You say the burden is on management to prove the need for closures. I reject that framing. The burden is on all of us to produce a viable solution.

But here is the reality: if you insist that every German site remains open, then you must explain how the cost gap is closed **without** materially reducing capacity and cost. So far, I have not seen that.

Your list — redeployment, working-time changes, productivity improvements, investment discipline — is useful, but it is not yet a quantified restructuring plan. It is a set of instruments. That is not the same thing.

## To the Union
You keep saying “shared sacrifice.” Fine. Then let’s define it honestly.

A real shared-sacrifice package would require:
- meaningful employee cost contribution,
- durable capacity reduction,
- labor-cost relief that is structural, not temporary,
- and a model that restores margins.

What you are offering instead is a package that keeps the footprint intact and asks management to trust that efficiency gains will somehow fill the gap. That is not enough.

If the union wants to avoid harsher measures, it needs to accept that:
- some sites may have to shrink materially,
- some functions may have to be relocated or consolidated,
- and headcount reduction must be real, not just aspirational.

## To the State Government
You say no closures and no compulsory redundancies. I respect your political role, but I have to say this: **regional stability depends on a competitive company**.

A protected but uncompetitive structure is not a win for the region. It creates a slower decline. That is worse than a hard but workable reset.

If you want to support a durable solution, then use your influence to get the workforce side to accept real structural change, not just temporary concessions.

## To the Family Holding and Investors
I agree with you that the company needs durable savings and not symbolism. You are right to reject a cosmetic compromise.

But let me be equally clear: we will not get to a viable result by pretending that the social and governance constraints do not exist. A plan that cannot survive co-determination or industrial relations is not a plan.

So yes, I support a tough restructuring. But it has to be implementable. That means the employee side must move materially, not just rhetorically.

## What would actually move management
I am open on the instrument mix. I have said that consistently. I can support a package built from:
- capacity reductions,
- site adjustments where needed,
- voluntary exits,
- redeployment,
- shift and working-time changes,
- labor-cost concessions,
- productivity agreements,
- and investment commitments into future products.

But the package must satisfy one condition: **it must be large enough, durable enough, and credible enough to restore competitiveness and hit the return-on-sales target**.

That is the test.

## What will not move me
What will not move me is:
- an absolute no-closure rule,
- an absolute no-layoff rule,
- or a settlement that preserves the full cost base and asks management to call it a turnaround.

That is not acceptable. It would be a delay mechanism, not a solution.

## Bottom line
We need a negotiated agreement, yes. But it has to produce:
1. **real structural cost reduction**
2. **real capacity reduction**
3. **real labor-cost relief**
4. **implementation certainty**
5. **freedom to invest in future products and technology**

If the worker side cannot accept that, then it is not engaging in restructuring — it is defending the status quo. And the status quo is exactly what we cannot afford.
