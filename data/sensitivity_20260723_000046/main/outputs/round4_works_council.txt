I’ll be equally clear in round 4: **our position has not changed**.

We are prepared to negotiate a serious restructuring. We are **not** prepared to negotiate away German plants, compulsory redundancies, co-determination, or the job-security framework.

## Where I agree
I agree with the expert, the state government, and even management on one essential point: **this company needs real savings and a durable turnaround**. No one in this room benefits from a cosmetic deal.

I also agree that:
- employee contributions may be necessary,
- industrial peace matters,
- and a weak, symbolic compromise would fail.

But agreement ends there.

## Where I disagree
### 1) The “no closures first, fallback later” idea is exactly the trap we reject
The moderator’s bridge proposals all contain the same hidden problem: they keep the closure option alive as an eventual fallback.

That is not a bridge. That is a delayed threat.

We have seen this pattern before:
- first comes “no immediate closure,”
- then comes a review gate,
- then suddenly the closure is “unavoidable.”

We will not accept a staged mechanism that is designed to soften resistance now and break promises later.

If you want an agreement, then **German plants stay open**. Not “for now.” Not “until the next review.” Open.

### 2) Management is still trying to make workers pay for a strategic failure
Management talks about competitiveness, market pressure, and cost gaps. Those are real issues. But the solution cannot be to dismantle the workforce’s future.

You keep saying:
- closures cannot be ruled out,
- forced redundancies cannot be ruled out,
- footprint reduction must remain on the table.

That is a negotiating position, not an economic necessity.

The expert has already shown that a no-closure package can work if the measures are large enough. So the real question is not whether pain exists. The question is **who bears it**.

Our answer is: **not through plant closures and not through forced layoffs**.

### 3) The family holding and investors are underestimating the value of trust and implementation stability
You speak as if “credibility” only means cost cutting. It doesn’t.

A plan that provokes warning strikes, public conflict, supervisory-board deadlock, and long-term distrust is not credible. It is fragile.

If you want implementation certainty, then you need the workforce on board. And the workforce will not be on board for a plan that keeps closure and layoffs as latent threats.

### 4) The state government is right on the red lines, but it must act as if it means them
I welcome the state’s rejection of closures and forced redundancies. But words are not enough.

If management keeps pushing closure logic, then the state shareholder must use its influence, not merely comment from the sidelines. Otherwise the burden stays entirely on the workforce, and that is unacceptable.

## On the expert’s numbers
The expert’s ranges are useful, but let’s not misuse them.

Yes, a no-closure package may deliver less than a closure-led package in headline savings. But the expert also made clear that:
- closures carry large one-time costs,
- execution risk is high,
- and governance deadlock can destroy value.

That is crucial. The “cheaper” solution on paper may be the more expensive one in reality if it triggers conflict and delay.

So I reject the argument that closure-led restructuring is automatically the rational choice. It is not.

## What we are prepared to discuss
We are prepared to discuss a **serious no-closure package** built from:
- voluntary exits,
- retirement bridges,
- internal redeployment,
- shift rationalization,
- working-time flexibility,
- productivity improvements,
- overhead cuts,
- temporary wage moderation,
- bonus restraint,
- management contribution,
- and investment commitments in Germany.

But only under one condition: **every German plant remains open and no compulsory redundancies are imposed**.

That is not a tactical preference. It is the basis of any responsible settlement.

## What we are not prepared to discuss
We are **not** prepared to discuss:
- closure as a fallback,
- forced layoffs as a fallback,
- “partial-footprint reduction” that is really closure by another name,
- or review gates that are engineered to reopen the closure debate later.

We also will not accept a package that weakens co-determination by making worker protections conditional on management’s later dissatisfaction.

## To management
If you want proof that alternatives are sufficient, then bring the quantified gap and let us test the alternatives honestly.

But do not come here assuming that the only “serious” restructuring is the one that destroys sites and jobs. That assumption is exactly why conflict has escalated.

If you need the workforce to contribute, then say so plainly and negotiate the contribution. But do not ask us to sign up to our own weakening.

## To the union
We are aligned on the red lines, and I want to strengthen that line:
- any employee contribution must be **temporary**
- it must be **fair**
- and it must be **tied to binding guarantees that cannot be bypassed later**

We should not allow temporary sacrifices to become permanent insecurity.

## To the state government
If you are serious about no closures and no compulsory redundancies, then support a package that makes that possible:
- binding site guarantees,
- investment commitments,
- and pressure on management to stop using closures as leverage.

Do not let “review” become a coded word for backdoor plant closure.

## To the family holding and investors
You asked for quantified alternatives. Fine. The alternative is not abstract:
- remove costs through **voluntary and negotiated means**
- reduce capacity through **shifts, lines, and redeployment**
- cut overhead and management costs
- and contribute from the top as well

That is a serious restructuring. What is not serious is treating the workforce as the default variable while ownership keeps strategic control untouched.

## Bottom line
Let me state it one more time, clearly:

- **All German plants remain open**
- **No compulsory redundancies**
- **Co-determination preserved**
- **Job-security framework preserved**
- **Real restructuring through negotiated, socially responsible measures**
- **No closure fallback, no layoff fallback, no Trojan-horse review mechanism**

If management cannot accept that, then we are not yet at a settlement. And if that means continued resistance, then the works council will continue to use every instrument available to defend the workforce and the German industrial base.
