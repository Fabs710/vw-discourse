From the capital markets’ perspective, this discussion is now clearer: **the company needs a restructuring that is large, durable, and implementable**. Anything less will not restore margins, and anything that cannot survive governance and labour relations is not value-creating.

## Where we agree
I agree with all sides on the basic diagnosis:
- the current cost base is too high,
- overcapacity is real,
- the turnaround must be durable,
- and future investment depends on restoring operating performance.

I also agree with the expert that the issue is not whether change is needed, but **what scale of structural adjustment is sufficient**.

## Where I disagree
### To the Works Council and Union
Your insistence on:
- no plant closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- and preservation of the full job-security framework

is understandable, but as stated it is **not financeable unless you can prove the savings are still sufficient**.

So far, I have not seen that proof.

You keep saying alternative measures can work. That may be true in principle. But from an investor standpoint, principle is not enough. We need:
- quantified annual savings,
- clear durability,
- and a credible path to higher margins.

If the package preserves every site and merely shifts pain into temporary concessions, then it is not a turnaround. It is delay.

### To Management
At the same time, management should not treat closures and compulsory redundancies as a reflex answer. The expert was right that the governance environment makes brute-force restructuring risky. A plan that maximizes headline savings but fails in the supervisory board or triggers prolonged industrial conflict can destroy value.

So management must stop speaking in generalities and provide a **quantified gap-closing plan**:
- what exact annual savings are required,
- how much comes from footprint reduction,
- how much from labour cost,
- how much from overhead and productivity,
- what the one-time charges are,
- and when the run-rate improvement arrives.

Without that, the market cannot judge whether the plan is real.

## What we would support
We would support a **hybrid restructuring** if it is credible and sufficiently ambitious:
- meaningful capacity reduction,
- real labour-cost relief,
- voluntary exits and attrition where possible,
- selective footprint rationalisation if necessary,
- strong overhead discipline,
- and binding investment into future products.

That is the only route that looks capable of restoring competitive margins without creating unnecessary governance deadlock.

## What we would not support
We will not support:
- a cosmetic compromise,
- a package too small to matter,
- or a preservation strategy that leaves the company structurally uncompetitive.

We also will not support an unstable closure-led plan that cannot be implemented under co-determination and special shareholder constraints.

## Bottom line
The burden is now on the parties defending “no closures” to show, in numbers, that the alternative package is large enough to close the cost gap. If they can do that, it becomes a legitimate option. If they cannot, then management must not be prevented from taking harder structural measures.

From the capital markets’ side, the test is simple:
**does the package credibly restore return on sales, protect cash generation, and improve the company’s competitiveness on a durable basis?**

If yes, we can support it. If no, we will not back a settlement that merely postpones the problem.
