As the controlling shareholder, I will be blunt: I support a serious restructuring, but I will not support a deal that is either economically toothless or industrially explosive.

### Where I agree
I agree with management, and partly with the capital markets, on the core diagnosis:
- the current cost base is too high,
- overcapacity is real,
- margins are too weak,
- and the company needs durable savings to fund future investment.

I also agree with the works council, the union, and the state government on one important point:
- a restructuring that triggers a prolonged breakdown in labour peace would destroy value.

So yes: the status quo is unacceptable. And yes: a chaotic confrontation is also unacceptable.

### Where I disagree
#### 1) “No plant closures” as an absolute
I do **not** accept an absolute prohibition on closures if they are the only way to achieve sufficient, durable savings. The company cannot be forced to carry structurally uneconomic capacity forever.

That said, I am also not enthusiastic about closures as a first reflex. If comparable savings can be achieved through capacity reduction, redeployment, working-time changes, and negotiated contributions, that is preferable. But it must be real savings, not a cosmetic rearrangement.

#### 2) “No compulsory redundancies” as an absolute
I understand why the workforce is drawing that line, but I will not treat it as sacrosanct if it blocks a credible turnaround. If the only outcome on offer is preservation of every legacy commitment while the business remains uncompetitive, then that is not stewardship.

#### 3) A guarantee through 2030 without structural change
I do not see how a blanket employment and site guarantee through 2030 can be justified if the company is still carrying excess capacity and above-target costs. A guarantee is only as good as the economics behind it.

### On management
Management is right to insist on durable savings. Where I will push back is any implication that the ownership side should simply endorse a plan that is socially and operationally unimplementable.

If you want support from the controlling shareholder, bring:
- quantified savings,
- a credible implementation path,
- and a package that can survive co-determination and industrial relations.

What I will not back is a plan that looks good in a presentation deck but collapses in the boardroom or on the shop floor.

### On the works council and union
I respect the insistence on social responsibility, but you are still not answering the central question: **how do you close the gap?**

Saying “no closures, no forced layoffs” is not enough. If those tools are off the table, then you must show, in numbers, how alternative measures deliver the same durable effect. Otherwise you are asking the company to accept weaker economics indefinitely.

You talk about capacity reductions, working-time flexibility, redeployment, and employee contributions. Fine. Show me the scale and durability. If it is sufficient, then it is a legitimate alternative. If it is not sufficient, then you are simply postponing the problem.

### On the state government
I understand your responsibility to the region, and I do not dismiss it. But you cannot define success as “keep everything open” if the result is a weakened company.

A company that remains overstaffed, under-margined, and unable to invest will not protect the region in the long run. Regional stability depends on a competitive enterprise, not on the preservation of every site at any cost.

### What would move me
I could support a negotiated package if it meets all of the following:
1. **Durable, material cost reduction**
2. **Meaningful reduction in overcapacity**
3. **A fair employee contribution**
4. **No prolonged industrial conflict**
5. **Clear investment commitments into future products**
6. **A solution that is actually implementable under co-determination**

### What I would not support
- a purely political compromise that preserves all sites and leaves the company uncompetitive,
- a package too small to matter,
- an open-ended strike cycle,
- or a deal that damages governance control and leaves the company unable to act.

### Bottom line
I am prepared to support a tough negotiated restructuring. I am **not** prepared to support a symbolic peace treaty that protects legacy structures while the business continues to erode.

If the workforce side wants no closures and no compulsory redundancies, then it must put forward an alternative that delivers equivalent savings in a credible, durable way. If it cannot, then management should not be blocked from taking harder measures.
