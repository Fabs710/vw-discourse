I’ll be blunt.

The works council and union continue to speak as if **protecting every site automatically solves the competitiveness problem**. It does not. The state government repeats the same red lines. The family holding and investors are right to demand a package that is **durable, quantified, and implementable**. That is where I stand.

## Where I agree
I agree with three points from everyone:
- The company needs a **durable** turnaround, not a cosmetic one.
- A deal that cannot be implemented under co-determination is worthless.
- Employees will have to contribute.

I also accept the expert’s core finding: **a no-closure solution is possible in principle**.

But that is not the same as saying it is sufficient.

## Where I disagree
### 1) “All German plants remain open” is not a restructuring strategy
The works council says every plant must stay open. The union says closures are off the table. That may be politically satisfying, but it does **not** answer the cost problem.

We are facing:
- shrinking European demand,
- intense low-cost competition,
- factory costs above target,
- and a brand missing its return-on-sales target.

If the footprint stays fully intact, the company keeps too much fixed cost. That is not a turnaround. It is delay.

### 2) “No compulsory redundancies” as an absolute is not workable
I understand the desire to avoid forced layoffs. But if the employee side rules out the very instruments that reduce fixed cost, then it is asking management to solve a structural problem with partial measures only.

That is not acceptable as a blanket rule.

### 3) “Review gates” are not a substitute for real structural change
The proposed no-closure-first models with later review points are exactly what I warned against: **a cosmetic compromise today and the same fight later**.

If the package does not deliver sufficient durable savings, then a review gate simply postpones the correction. We do not have that kind of time.

## What the expert’s numbers actually mean
The expert’s scenario ranges are useful. They confirm what we have said all along:

- A **no-closure package** may deliver roughly **€350m–€900m** recurring savings.
- A **partial-footprint reduction** may deliver **€700m–€1.5bn** recurring savings.
- A **closure-led package** may deliver **€1.0bn–€2.0bn+** recurring savings.

If the cost gap is modest, fine — then a no-closure package may be enough.

But if the gap is structurally large, then no-closure becomes a weak answer unless concessions are unusually deep and durable. I have not heard that the workforce side is prepared to deliver that scale.

## To the works council
You keep saying the burden of proof is on management. In part, yes. We are providing that proof: the business model is under pressure, and overcapacity is real.

But you also need to prove your alternative is sufficient.

So far I have not seen a quantified package from you that shows:
- how much capacity comes out,
- how much recurring cost is saved,
- how much is temporary versus permanent,
- and why the result would restore margins.

Listing tools is not the same as delivering a savings package.

## To the union
You say “shared sacrifice.” Good. Then let’s define it honestly.

A real shared-sacrifice package must include:
- meaningful employee cost contribution,
- durable capacity reduction,
- labor-cost relief that is structural, not just temporary,
- and a return to target margins.

If the package keeps every site open and treats worker concessions as the main lever, that is not shared sacrifice. That is one-sided burden shifting.

Also: bonus restraint, deferred pay, and temporary working-time changes are **not enough on their own** if they do not materially change the fixed-cost base.

## To the state government
You say no closures and no compulsory redundancies. I hear the political responsibility behind that.

But stability in the region depends on a competitive company, not just a preserved footprint. A protected but uncompetitive structure creates a slower decline. That is worse.

If you want to back a no-closure solution, then you need to help produce a package that is actually large enough to work. Otherwise your position is just a veto without a viable alternative.

## To the controlling family holding and investors
I agree with you that symbolism is not enough. A cosmetic deal would destroy credibility.

But I also need to be clear: a plan that cannot survive co-determination or industrial peace is not a solution either. So yes, I support tough restructuring. But it has to be implementable.

That means:
- real savings,
- real capacity reduction,
- real labor-cost relief,
- and a path to margins that investors can trust.

## What would move management
I remain open on the instrument mix. I am not fixated on closures for their own sake. What I need is a package that closes the gap in a **durable** way.

I can support:
- capacity reductions,
- shift rationalization,
- working-time changes,
- voluntary exits,
- internal redeployment,
- productivity improvements,
- overhead cuts,
- and, if necessary, selective footprint rationalization.

What I cannot accept is a package that preserves the full cost base and calls it restructuring.

## My line remains
I will support a negotiated settlement only if it produces:

1. **real structural cost reduction**
2. **real capacity reduction**
3. **real labor-cost relief**
4. **implementation certainty**
5. **freedom to invest in future products and technology**

What I will **not** support:
- a no-change compromise,
- a cosmetic settlement,
- or a deal that preserves the full footprint while leaving the company structurally weak.

If the workforce side wants to avoid harder measures, then it must show — in numbers — that the alternative package closes the gap. If it cannot, then management should not be blocked from pursuing stronger structural change.
