I’ll speak plainly.

### Where I agree
I agree with management, the family holding, the investors, the works council, and the state government on one point: **this company needs a durable turnaround, not a cosmetic patch**. The status quo is not sustainable. We also all know that a deal that cannot be implemented under co-determination and in industrial relations is worthless.

I also agree that employees will have to contribute. We have already said that clearly.

### Where I disagree
What I do **not** accept is the ongoing attempt to make **German plant closures** and **compulsory redundancies** sound like normal starting options.

They are not normal starting options. They are the point where this becomes a confrontation over who bears the pain.

The union’s line remains:
- **no compulsory redundancies**
- **no German plant closures**
- **no one-sided burden shifting onto employees**
- **no dismantling of the collective wage framework**

That is not rhetoric. It is the boundary of any acceptable settlement.

### On the expert’s point
The expert has helped clarify the real issue: a no-closure solution is only credible if the alternative measures are **large enough**. I accept that.

But the expert did **not** say that closures and forced layoffs are the only credible route. Quite the opposite: the expert confirmed that a hybrid package can work if it is serious enough.

That is where the discussion should be. Not “closures are inevitable,” but: **what exact mix of measures closes the gap fairly?**

### To management
Your argument is basically: “We need durable savings, therefore closures and workforce reduction cannot be ruled out.”

I reject that as too one-sided.

Yes, we need durable savings. But you have not shown that the only way to get them is by breaking the site structure and threatening the workforce with forced exits. Before you ask for irreversible job destruction, you must show:
- the exact savings gap,
- how much can be covered by productivity,
- how much by overhead reduction,
- how much by capacity rationalization without closures,
- and what management and owners are contributing themselves.

You have not yet made that case.

If you come with a package that says workers should accept lower pay, more flexibility, and weaker security while management preserves all strategic freedom, that will not be accepted.

### To the family holding and investors
You are right that symbolism is useless. But your definition of “credible” is too narrow if it assumes that competitiveness can only be bought by threatening plants and jobs.

A durable settlement is not only about short-term margin optics. It is also about whether the workforce will stand behind the company for the next five years. If you break that trust, you do not get durability — you get conflict.

So if you want our support, then accept this reality:
- employees can contribute,
- but they will not pay alone,
- and they will not accept closures or forced layoffs as the price of your return-on-sales target.

### To the state government
You are right to reject closures and forced redundancies. But I would go one step further: the state should use its influence to make sure the company does not turn “restructuring” into industrial destruction by stealth.

If there is a negotiated package, it should be built around:
- **all German plants remaining open**
- **no compulsory redundancies**
- **binding investment commitments**
- **capacity adjustments through negotiated means**
- **temporary employee contribution, not permanent wage dumping**

### What we are prepared to discuss
Let me be concrete, because this cannot stay abstract.

The union is prepared to discuss:
- temporary wage moderation or deferred elements,
- reduced bonuses or special payments,
- flexible working-time arrangements,
- shift and capacity adjustments,
- internal redeployment,
- voluntary exit options,
- productivity improvements,
- and a firm contribution from management and owners.

But this only works if the burden is shared fairly and if the result protects jobs and sites.

### What would move us
A settlement could become acceptable if it contains all of the following:
1. **No German plant closures**
2. **No compulsory redundancies**
3. **A meaningful employee contribution**
4. **A visible management and owner contribution**
5. **Binding investment and product commitments**
6. **A real capacity reduction through negotiated, non-destructive measures**
7. **Protection of the collective wage framework**

If those conditions are met, we can discuss the scale and sequencing of employee contributions.

### What will not move us
What will **not** move us is:
- a “conditional guarantee” that is really just a delayed closure,
- a package that relies on layoffs after a short pause,
- or a restructuring that asks workers to sacrifice while ownership and management do not.

### Bottom line
We are ready for a serious settlement. But it has to be a **shared-sacrifice package**, not a workers-pay-first package.

If management and capital want implementation certainty, then they need to accept the only basis on which it is possible: **jobs protected, sites protected, collective framework protected, and contributions shared fairly**.

If that is not possible, then we will continue to use the tools available to us — industrially and at the bargaining table — to block a one-sided restructuring.
