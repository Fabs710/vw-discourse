I’ll respond directly, because the situation is serious and time is short.

### Where I agree
I agree with all sides on one basic point: **this company needs a durable turnaround, not a cosmetic fix**. We cannot continue with a cost structure that is above target while the European market shrinks and competitors gain ground. We also need investment in future products and technology through 2030. On that, there is no dispute.

I also agree that any solution has to be **implementable** under co-determination and the company’s governance structure. A plan that cannot survive in the supervisory board or in industrial relations is not a real plan.

### Where I disagree — and this is the core issue
What I cannot accept is the repeated attempt to define the solution in a way that **rules out the very instruments that can actually close the gap**.

#### 1) “No plant closures” is not a credible starting point
The Works Council and union keep saying every German plant must remain open. I understand why you say that. But I need to be clear: **that is not a serious answer to a structural overcapacity problem**.

If we preserve every site regardless of economics, then we are not restructuring; we are postponing the problem and shifting the burden onto the company’s future. That is unacceptable.

#### 2) “No compulsory redundancies” cannot be an absolute
I hear the red line. But if the workforce side refuses meaningful structural contribution, then it is not realistic to demand that management rule out all workforce reductions in advance. We cannot fix overcapacity, reduce labour costs, and restore margins if every adjustment is off limits.

I am not saying layoffs are the preferred route. I am saying they cannot be excluded if that is what it takes to secure a durable solution.

#### 3) A 2030 guarantee without deep structural change is not credible
The demand for site and employment guarantees through 2030 would be fine if the cost base were already competitive. It is not. So I will not sign up to guarantees that simply freeze an uncompetitive structure in place.

### To the Works Council
You say the workforce has carried the burden already. I acknowledge that. But the burden is not over. The company is under acute pressure now, and we need a contribution that matches the scale of the crisis.

You talk about capacity reductions, internal redeployment, working-time changes, productivity measures, and investment discipline. Good — but I have to be blunt: **those instruments may help, but they are not enough on their own unless they produce real, measurable savings at the scale required**.

If you want to avoid closures, then you need to present a package that actually closes the cost gap. Not in theory. In numbers. A plan that preserves all sites and all guarantees while only making marginal adjustments will not restore competitiveness.

### To the union
You say you are ready for shared sacrifice, but your framework still excludes the two measures that matter most when the cost base is structurally too high: site rationalisation and workforce reduction.

Temporary wage restraint, bonus waivers, and flexible working time are useful. They are not enough. They do not solve a structurally oversized footprint.

So let me be equally direct: **if the union wants to avoid harsher measures, it must be prepared to accept meaningful structural change**, including deeper capacity reduction and potentially site adjustments. Otherwise, you are asking management to accept a deal that does not solve the problem.

### To the state government
I appreciate your emphasis on regional stability, but “no closures” and “no deindustrialization” are not sufficient criteria for a viable shareholder position. The state has influence, yes — but influence should be used to secure a competitive company, not to preserve every legacy structure.

A company that cannot restore margins will not protect jobs in the long run. Pretending otherwise is not responsible stewardship.

### To the family holding and investors
I agree with your central message: **durable savings, not symbolism**. This is exactly the point. If we do not reduce overcapacity and labour costs in a structural way, we will not restore return on sales, and we will not free the funds needed for future investment.

But I also say this: the answer cannot be open-ended confrontation. We need a negotiated outcome that is tough enough to work and stable enough to implement. That means the employee side has to move materially.

### What I need to see
I am not asking for abstract promises. I am asking for a package that includes:
- a **real and durable reduction in overcapacity**,
- a **meaningful labour-cost contribution**,
- **credible structural savings**, not just temporary flexibilities,
- and a result that restores the brand’s margin trajectory.

I am open on the instrument mix:
- closures,
- capacity reductions,
- transfers,
- redeployment,
- working-time changes,
- labour-cost concessions,
- productivity agreements,

but the package must deliver the savings, and it must do so durably.

### Bottom line
I will not support a cosmetic compromise that preserves the full footprint and leaves the company uncompetitive. That would be a delay mechanism, not a turnaround.

If the workforce side wants to avoid the consequences of deeper restructuring, then it needs to bring forward a package that genuinely achieves the required savings and capacity reduction. If not, then management will have to pursue more forceful measures, because the company cannot afford to stand still.
