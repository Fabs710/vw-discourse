A no-closure package can be credible, but only if it is built to a scale that materially changes the fixed-cost base, not just the wage bill for one year. On the facts given, I would frame the economics as follows.

## 1) What a no-closure package would need to achieve

For a large German OEM/brand with structurally high domestic labor and overhead cost, a **credible no-closure solution** usually needs to generate roughly:

- **€500m–€900m annual recurring savings** as a minimum credible range
- with an **upper-end aspiration around €900m–€1.1bn** if the company’s savings gap is materially larger
- plus **one-time restructuring charges** that are still acceptable relative to the savings, typically **2–4x annual savings** in present-value terms if implemented cleanly

That means if the company truly needs savings in the same neighborhood as a **partial-footprint reduction** case, the no-closure package has to sit at the **very top end** of what is operationally feasible without site closure.

## 2) What instruments can get you there

A no-closure package cannot rely on temporary concessions alone. It needs a mix of:

### A. Headcount reduction without forced redundancies
This is usually the largest lever available without closures.

Typical scale in a German manufacturing restructuring:
- **8%–15% workforce reduction** over 2–4 years through:
  - attrition
  - retirement bridges
  - voluntary exits
  - internal redeployment
  - non-replacement
- For a large domestic workforce, that can yield **€150m–€350m/year** recurring savings depending on average loaded labor cost and redeployment success

### B. Working-time, shift, and utilization measures
These reduce overtime, idle capacity, and labor inefficiency.

Possible savings:
- **€75m–€200m/year**
- More if current shift structure is heavily underutilized

This includes:
- shift rationalization
- reduced premium pay
- tighter line balancing
- fewer low-load shifts
- more flexible annual working-time accounts

### C. Wage moderation / temporary concessions
These are often necessary politically, but on their own they are not enough.

Possible scale:
- **€50m–€150m/year**
- depending on scope, duration, and whether it applies to base pay, bonuses, or supplements

Important: these are often **temporary** and therefore less valuable to capital markets unless converted into durable cost relief.

### D. Overhead and management cost reduction
This is often underestimated.

Possible savings:
- **€100m–€250m/year**
- through:
  - management layer reduction
  - procurement savings
  - SG&A simplification
  - support-function consolidation
  - IT/process harmonization

### E. Productivity improvements
These can be meaningful but are the hardest to bank quickly.

Possible savings:
- **€75m–€200m/year**
- if there is genuine process simplification, fewer variants, better line performance, and lower scrap/rework

### F. Capacity rationalization without closure
This is the most important distinction.

You can reduce capacity materially without “closing” a plant by:
- removing shifts
- consolidating product allocations
- taking out lines
- moving support functions
- reducing internal logistics complexity

Potential recurring savings:
- **€100m–€250m/year**
- but only if volume is structurally shifted out, not merely delayed

## 3) What a realistic no-closure package might total

### Conservative feasible case
- Labor/headcount: €200m
- Shift/utilization: €100m
- Wage moderation: €75m
- Overhead: €125m
- Productivity: €100m

**Total: ~€600m/year recurring savings**

This is credible, but only if implementation is disciplined and the company can actually extract labor and overhead out of the base.

### Strong no-closure case
- Labor/headcount: €300m
- Shift/utilization: €150m
- Wage moderation: €100m
- Overhead: €200m
- Productivity: €150m

**Total: ~€900m/year recurring savings**

This is about the upper end of what I would call realistically financeable without closures, assuming strong employee participation and no major execution slippage.

### Very ambitious no-closure case
- Could approach **€1.0bn+** yearly, but that usually requires:
  - unusually deep voluntary exits
  - very large working-time sacrifices
  - significant product/volume improvement
  - and management commitment to permanent overhead cuts

At that point, the package is operationally close to a partial-footprint reduction in its economic effect, even if no plant is formally closed.

## 4) What the one-time cost would look like

A no-closure package is usually cheaper upfront than closure-led restructuring, but still material.

Indicative one-time charges:
- voluntary exits / severance / bridges: **€400m–€900m**
- redeployment and retraining: **€50m–€150m**
- site adaptation / process changes: **€100m–€250m**
- contract / legal / social-plan costs: **€50m–€150m**

**Total one-time cost: roughly €600m–€1.4bn**

That is often acceptable if it buys **€600m–€900m/year** recurring savings with high implementation certainty.

## 5) How it compares to partial-footprint reduction

A partial-footprint reduction usually outperforms a no-closure plan on recurring savings because it removes more fixed cost structurally.

Typical range:
- **€700m–€1.5bn/year recurring savings**
- one-time costs often **€1.0bn–€2.0bn+**
- implementation risk is higher than a pure negotiated no-closure package, but lower than full closures if the scope is limited and phased

### Comparison
| Option | Recurring savings | One-time cost | Implementation risk |
|---|---:|---:|---|
| No-closure package | €500m–€900m | €600m–€1.4bn | Medium |
| Partial-footprint reduction | €700m–€1.5bn | €1.0bn–€2.0bn+ | Medium-high |
| Closure-led restructuring | €1.0bn–€2.0bn+ | €1.5bn–€3.0bn+ | High |

## 6) The key question: what savings gap must be closed?

The decision turns on the company’s true structural gap.

If the required annual improvement is:
- **€400m–€600m**: a no-closure package is likely sufficient
- **€600m–€900m**: a no-closure package is possible, but it must be near the upper end and heavily front-loaded
- **€900m–€1.2bn**: a no-closure package is possible only if employee and management contributions are unusually deep
- **above ~€1.2bn**: no-closure becomes difficult to sustain without effectively mimicking footprint reduction through major capacity removals

## 7) What I would say is the minimum credible no-closure structure

If management wants this to be financeable, I would expect:

1. **A quantified annual savings target**
   - ideally **at least €700m/year**, if the company is under serious margin pressure

2. **A headcount reduction plan**
   - large enough to matter in run rate
   - likely **high single-digit to low double-digit %** of domestic workforce over several years

3. **Capacity reductions without legal closures**
   - shift removals, line consolidation, product allocation changes

4. **Temporary employee concessions**
   - wage restraint, bonus moderation, working-time flexibility
   - but with a path to normalization

5. **Visible management contribution**
   - overhead, executive cost, procurement, and organization simplification

6. **Binding investment commitments**
   - otherwise the workforce will treat the deal as a one-way extraction of savings

7. **Hard governance metrics**
   - monthly or quarterly tracking of actual savings versus plan

## 8) Bottom line

A **no-closure package can work**, but only if it delivers roughly **€600m–€900m/year** recurring savings, with **one-time costs around €600m–€1.4bn**, and with enough headcount and capacity reduction to change the fixed-cost base permanently.

If the company’s true gap is materially above that, then the no-closure model starts to look insufficient, and a **partial-footprint reduction** becomes the more economically credible alternative.
