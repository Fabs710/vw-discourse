I hear the red lines being stated, but I need to be equally clear: the company’s current cost and capacity structure is not competitive, and pretending otherwise will not save German jobs in the medium term. If we do not make a structural correction now, we will simply continue to lose margin, lose volume, and lose the ability to invest in the future.

Where I agree:
- We do need a durable turnaround, not a one-off cut.
- We do need investment in future products and technology.
- We do need a negotiated solution if we want it implemented in a stable way.

Where I disagree:
- “Every German plant must remain open” is not a viable starting point.
- “No compulsory redundancies” may be politically understandable, but it cannot be treated as an absolute if the workforce refuses to contribute enough to the restructuring.
- A promise through 2030, without credible capacity and cost reduction, would be a false guarantee.

The hard reality is this: German factory costs are above target, our European market is shrinking, and competitors are operating with leaner structures. We cannot meet the brand’s return-on-sales target by layering temporary measures on top of an uncompetitive footprint.

So my position is:
1. We need a substantial, permanent reduction in overcapacity.
2. We need a genuine labour-cost contribution.
3. We need flexibility on sites, staffing, and working models.
4. We need a solution that frees funds for product and technology investment.

I am open on the exact mix — closures, capacity reductions, transfers, working-time changes, or other structural measures — but only if the package delivers real, durable savings at the scale the situation demands.

What I will not accept is a cosmetic deal that preserves the full footprint and only shifts costs around. That would simply postpone the crisis and make the eventual adjustment harsher.
