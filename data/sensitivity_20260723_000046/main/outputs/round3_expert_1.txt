## My assessment

### 1) A pure no-closure settlement is possible, but only if the savings are genuinely large
A no-closure package can work if it includes enough of the following:
- meaningful headcount reduction via voluntary exits and attrition
- working-time reductions / shift rationalization
- wage moderation or deferred pay elements
- bonus restraint
- overhead reduction
- productivity and process improvements
- redeployment across plants

In finance terms, this can be viable if the company’s required run-rate savings are in the **mid-hundreds of millions of euros per year**, not if the gap is closer to a **full structural reset of €1bn+ annually**.

### 2) Partial-footprint reduction is the most likely “financeable compromise”
Given the governance structure described, the most plausible economically credible package is usually:
- **no immediate full plant closures**, but
- **real capacity reduction**, including site downsizing, line rationalization, or functional consolidation,
- combined with labor-cost measures and voluntary departures.

This often delivers the best balance of:
- recurring savings,
- lower implementation risk than closures,
- and enough structural change to be credible to capital.

### 3) Closure-led restructuring has the highest theoretical savings, but the lowest execution probability
Plant closures can produce the largest gross fixed-cost savings, but they also bring:
- large one-time restructuring charges,
- strike and conflict risk,
- likely supervisory-board deadlock,
- and possible erosion of the net present value through delay and disruption.

Under the described veto structure, that makes closure-led restructuring the **least reliable** path unless management can secure broad political and workforce accommodation.

If the required savings are modest, a **no-closure package** may be sufficient.
