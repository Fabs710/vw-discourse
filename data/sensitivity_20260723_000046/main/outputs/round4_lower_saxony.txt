I’ll be direct.

## Where I agree
I agree with the expert, the union, the works council, and even management on one basic point: **this company needs a durable restructuring, not a cosmetic deal**. And I agree that employee contributions will be necessary if we want a turnaround that can actually be financed and implemented.

I also agree that a weak “no-change” compromise would fail.

## Where I disagree
### 1) I do not accept closures as the default answer
Management and the controlling family keep speaking as if German plant closures are simply the most rational tool. For me, they are **not** the starting point. They are the last resort, if they are justified at all.

The expert’s scenario analysis is useful here: it shows that a **no-closure package can work in principle** if it is large enough. That means closures are **not automatically necessary**. Before anyone pushes that button, they must prove that the alternatives are insufficient.

### 2) I do not accept compulsory redundancies as an easy fallback
I understand the desire for durable savings. But I will not treat forced layoffs as a normal planning instrument in a company that is a central regional employer and bound by co-determination. That would put the burden overwhelmingly on the workforce and on the region.

If management wants to preserve legitimacy, it must first exhaust negotiated instruments:
- voluntary exits
- retirement bridges
- redeployment
- working-time flexibility
- shift rationalization
- productivity gains
- overhead reduction
- temporary pay moderation
- management contribution

### 3) I do not accept “review-gate” models that are really delayed closure
I am with the works council and union on this point: a “no closure for now” deal with a later review gate can easily become a Trojan horse.

If the package is negotiated, it must not be built around a hidden closure option that reopens the same conflict after a short pause. That is not stability. That is postponement.

## What I take from the expert
The expert’s ranges are important:
- **No-closure:** about **€350m–€900m** recurring savings
- **Partial-footprint reduction:** about **€700m–€1.5bn**
- **Closure-led restructuring:** about **€1.0bn–€2.0bn+**

That tells me two things.

First, the burden of proof is on management to show that the required savings are truly beyond what a negotiated no-closure package can deliver.

Second, if the gap is indeed at the upper end, then a **partial-footprint reduction** is more plausible than full closures. That is the only “hard” route I can even begin to regard as negotiable, because it preserves more of the regional industrial base and reduces the risk of a governance and industrial-relations breakdown.

## My position on the alternatives
### No-closure package
I could support a no-closure package **only if** it is genuinely large, durable, and quantified. That means:
- meaningful headcount reduction without forced layoffs
- real working-time and shift changes
- clear overhead cuts
- measurable productivity delivery
- a visible management contribution
- binding investment commitments in Germany

But I am not prepared to say now that such a package is sufficient. It must be proven.

### Partial-footprint reduction
This is the compromise path that looks most realistic from my standpoint, because it can create real savings without ripping out the company’s regional role. If management insists on material structural change, this is the path that is most compatible with:
- regional employment stability
- co-determination
- and long-term industrial viability

### Closure-led restructuring
I remain opposed to making closures the preferred route. If anyone wants to argue for closures, they must show:
- why the alternatives do not close the gap,
- why the governance risk is manageable,
- and why the regional damage is justified.

That case has not been made.

## To management
You are right that the company cannot remain structurally uncompetitive. But you are still underestimating the limits of what can be imposed here.

If you want my support, bring me a **quantified bridge plan** that shows:
1. the exact savings gap,
2. how much of it can be closed without closures,
3. what remains after employee and management contributions,
4. and why any further step is truly unavoidable.

Until then, I will not sign up to plant closures or forced redundancies as if they were inevitable facts.

## To the works council and union
I support your red lines in principle:
- no closures as the starting solution
- no compulsory redundancies
- no erosion of co-determination
- no one-sided burden shifting

But I also need you to answer the numbers question honestly. A political red line is not enough by itself. If you want to defend a no-closure solution, then show how it closes the cost gap in a durable way.

That is the only way we can defend the regional economy and the company’s long-term future.

## To the family holding and investors
I understand your demand for credibility. But credibility is not the same as “the harshest option wins.” A settlement that destroys trust, triggers industrial conflict, and weakens the company’s role as an anchor employer is not value-creating.

If you want my support, accept that:
- jobs and plants in Germany matter,
- the regional industrial base matters,
- and the state’s co-determination role is not optional.

## Bottom line
My line remains unchanged:

- I will **not** support closures as the default route.
- I will **not** support compulsory redundancies as an easy fallback.
- I **will** support a tough negotiated restructuring if it:
  - protects the regional industrial base as far as possible,
  - preserves co-determination,
  - delivers real and quantified savings,
  - and keeps the company viable long-term.

If the parties want to move this forward, I want a **single quantified no-closure package** on the table, with:
- annual savings target
- one-time cost
- employment impact
- temporary vs. permanent measures
- and the management contribution

If management cannot show that such a package is sufficient, then we can talk about more limited footprint adjustment — but not about treating closures and layoffs as the first answer.
