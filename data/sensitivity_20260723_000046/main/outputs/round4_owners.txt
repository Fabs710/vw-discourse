I’ll be direct.

## Where I agree
I agree with management, the expert, and the investors on the central point: **this company needs a durable, material restructuring**. The cost base and capacity are not where they need to be, and a cosmetic settlement would simply defer value erosion. That is unacceptable to us as owners.

I also agree with the works council, union, and state government on one equally important point: **a restructuring that cannot be implemented because it destroys labour peace or deadlocks governance will also destroy value**. So yes, implementation matters as much as economics.

That is the balance we need to hold.

## Where I disagree
### 1) I do not accept that “all German plants remain open” can be treated as a fixed principle
The works council and union keep stating this as an absolute. I understand the motive, but I do not accept it as a governance premise if it makes a credible turnaround impossible.

The expert’s ranges matter here:
- no-closure: roughly **€350m–€900m** recurring savings
- partial-footprint reduction: roughly **€700m–€1.5bn**
- closure-led restructuring: roughly **€1.0bn–€2.0bn+**

That tells us something very simple: **a no-closure solution may be sufficient only at the lower end of the required gap**. If the true structural gap is larger, then saying “no closures” in advance is not stewardship; it is forcing the company to accept underpowered restructuring.

### 2) I do not accept “no compulsory redundancies” as an absolute if the numbers do not add up
I would prefer voluntary exits, redeployment, retirement bridges, and negotiated reductions. But I will not endorse a blanket prohibition on compulsory redundancies if that prohibition prevents the company from achieving the savings needed to survive competitively.

That is not a preference for confrontation. It is a refusal to pretend that the owner’s capital can absorb an open-ended cost gap.

### 3) I do not accept review-gate proposals that are merely delayed closure threats
The moderator’s bridge ideas are understandable, but I share the works council’s suspicion of Trojan-horse designs. If a package is sold as “no closure for now” and then reopens the same fight six or twelve months later, it does not create certainty.

So I agree with the unions on one narrow point: **a fake guarantee is worse than an honest disagreement**.

But that does not mean closure can be ruled out forever. It means any package must be honest about what is temporary and what is permanent.

## My position on the scenarios
### No-closure package
I am open to it **only if** it is quantified and strong enough to restore competitiveness. That means:
- meaningful headcount reduction through voluntary measures and attrition
- working-time and shift flexibility
- wage moderation or deferred compensation
- bonus restraint
- overhead reduction
- productivity gains with measurable delivery
- visible management contribution

If that package truly gets us into a durable savings range that closes the gap, then I would prefer it over harsher structural disruption.

But I will not pretend that this is automatically sufficient. It must be proven.

### Partial-footprint reduction
This is, in my view, the most plausible compromise path if the no-closure package falls short. It preserves more of the industrial base while still delivering real fixed-cost reduction.

If management can demonstrate that selective downsizing, consolidation, or line rationalization produces the needed run-rate savings with lower disruption than full closures, that deserves serious consideration.

### Closure-led restructuring
I do not regard closures as the first answer. But I will not exclude them if they are the only way to restore the company’s competitive position and if no alternative package is sufficient.

What I will not support is pretending that closures are always “off the table” regardless of the economic outcome. That would be a recipe for slow decline.

## To the works council
You are right to warn against cosmetic compromise. I take that seriously.

But your argument still has a gap: you say closures are unacceptable, yet you have not shown a quantified alternative that reliably closes the savings gap at scale. Saying “voluntary exits, shift rationalization, redeployment, productivity” is not enough by itself. Those are instruments, not a finished plan.

If you want to defend a no-closure route, then show the numbers:
- how much recurring savings
- how much one-time cost
- how much workforce reduction
- and how long the measures last

Until then, your red lines are political positions, not an operating solution.

## To the union
I agree with you that employees should not pay alone. Any settlement must include:
- temporary employee contribution
- management contribution
- and discipline at the top

That said, you cannot demand that the company preserve full flexibility for the workforce while the owner absorbs the structural risk indefinitely. The company needs durable cost relief, not a short-lived gesture.

If you want to avoid harsher measures, then the contribution must be:
- meaningful
- measurable
- durable
- and large enough to matter in the run rate

## To management
You are correct that competitiveness cannot be restored by preserving every legacy structure. But you also need to stop speaking in ultimatums. If you want the support of governance, the plan must be:
- quantified
- sequenced
- implementable under co-determination
- and credible to labour

Do not assume that “hard” automatically means “right.” Hard but unimplementable is still value-destructive.

## To the state government
I respect your role as anchor shareholder and regional stabiliser. But stability is not the same as immobility. If alternatives are insufficient, then protecting the status quo would be worse for the region in the long run.

So I support your insistence on exhausting alternatives first. I do **not** support making closure impossible in principle.

## My line, clearly stated
I support a **tough negotiated restructuring** if it meets these conditions:
1. real, quantified, durable savings
2. meaningful employee contribution
3. visible management and owner contribution
4. implementation certainty under co-determination
5. preservation of long-term investment capacity
6. no unnecessary escalation into prolonged conflict

What I will **not** support is:
- a weak no-change compromise,
- a package that is too small to restore margins,
- or a settlement that rules out the instruments needed if the gap remains too large.

## What I want next
I want a **single quantified package** on the table, not slogans. It should show:
- annual savings target
- one-time restructuring cost
- job impact
- what is temporary and what is permanent
- and whether it closes the gap without closures

If that package is sufficient, I am prepared to support it.

If it is not sufficient, then we should stop pretending that red lines alone can solve the underlying problem.
