## 1) Executive view

Given the facts in your scenario, the central question is not whether the company needs savings — it does. The question is whether the required savings can be achieved:

1. **without closures**,  
2. **with selective footprint reduction**, or  
3. **only through plant closure(s) and materially larger headcount reduction**.

My assessment:

- A **pure no-closure package** can work **only if** employee-side concessions, productivity gains, and overhead reductions are unusually large and durable. It is feasible in principle, but the risk is that the savings are **too small or too temporary** relative to the structural cost gap.
- A **partial-footprint reduction** is usually the **most financeable compromise** in a co-determined German OEM: it can create enough cost relief to be credible while preserving a negotiated industrial base.
- A **closure-led restructuring** gives the highest accounting savings and fastest fixed-cost reduction, but it is also the **least implementable** here because of the likely two-thirds supervisory-board hurdle and the employee veto dynamics. It is also the most execution-risky because strikes, litigation, and governance deadlock can erode the expected NPV.

So, on a finance basis, the **highest-probability path to a durable agreement** is usually **partial-footprint reduction plus labor-cost and productivity measures**, not an all-or-nothing closure plan.

## 3) Scenario 1 — No-closure restructuring

### Description
- No German plant closures
- No compulsory redundancies
- Savings achieved via:
  - voluntary exits
  - retirement bridging
  - internal redeployment
  - working-time reductions
  - wage moderation / deferred compensation
  - bonus restraint
  - shift rationalization
  - overhead cuts
  - productivity improvements

### What this can realistically save
A well-designed no-closure package might deliver:

- **Headcount reduction:** 3,000–6,000 jobs
- **Annual recurring savings:** roughly **€350m–€900m**
- **One-time cash cost:** roughly **€400m–€1.2bn**

Where the range comes from:
- 3,000–6,000 exits at €120k–€250k each = **€360m–€1.5bn** one-time
- Recurring payroll savings net of replacement/redeployment = maybe **€250m–€600m**
- Wage/bonus/working-time concessions and productivity gains = **€100m–€300m+** recurring

### Finance assessment
This scenario is attractive because:
- it avoids the massive legal and industrial-relations shock of closures,
- it is more likely to pass co-determination barriers,
- and it reduces execution risk.

But it has a major weakness:
- the savings may be **insufficient** if the structural cost gap is truly large and persistent.

### Main sensitivity
The key sensitivity is whether employee-side concessions are:
- **temporary** or **durable**
- **broad-based** or only at the margins
- **backed by binding productivity delivery**

If concessions are temporary, the NPV collapses quickly.

### Bottom line
A no-closure solution is **financeable only if the target savings requirement is moderate**. If management needs something like **€1bn+ per year durable run-rate improvement**, no-closure becomes hard to justify unless concessions are unusually deep.

## 4) Scenario 2 — Partial-footprint reduction

### Description
- One or more plants are not fully closed, but are:
  - downsized,
  - reallocated,
  - converted to narrower product roles,
  - or merged into another site
- Capacity reduction is real, but the footprint is not fully dismantled
- Combined with labor-cost concessions and overhead reduction

This is often the most realistic compromise under German governance constraints.

### What this can realistically save
A partial-footprint reduction could produce:

- **Headcount reduction:** 5,000–10,000 jobs
- **Annual recurring savings:** roughly **€700m–€1.5bn**
- **One-time cash cost:** roughly **€700m–€2.0bn**

This depends on whether the company:
- removes a full shift system,
- consolidates stamping/body/assembly,
- relocates functions,
- and captures procurement/logistics savings from simpler operations.

### Why this often works better financially
It allows the company to:
- reduce fixed cost meaningfully,
- preserve some industrial base,
- avoid the full social and legal cost of a clean closure,
- and still show investors a credible structural change.

### Typical components
A credible package might include:
- 2,000–4,000 voluntary exits
- 1,000–3,000 jobs lost through attrition / redeployment
- 5–10% labor-cost moderation over a defined period
- one or two plant lines or shifts removed
- elimination of duplicate overhead functions

### Finance assessment
This is often the **best risk-adjusted NPV option** in Germany because:
- it creates enough savings to matter,
- it may be acceptable to the workforce side if paired with investment and guarantees,
- and it reduces the chance of a governance deadlock compared with full closures.

### Main sensitivity
The most important variables are:
- whether capacity cuts are real or merely re-labeled;
- whether the company can actually redeploy labor rather than pay to keep idle capacity;
- and whether the labor-side contribution is locked in long enough to matter.

### Bottom line
For a company with strong veto constraints, **partial-footprint reduction is usually the most implementable “hard” restructuring**.

## 5) Scenario 3 — Closure-led restructuring

### Description
- Closure of one or more German plants
- Larger headcount reduction
- Material capacity removal
- Potential relocation of production and/or functions
- Stronger labor-cost relief and fixed-cost base reduction

### What this can realistically save
Depending on the plant size and structure:

- **Headcount reduction:** 8,000–15,000+ jobs
- **Annual recurring savings:** roughly **€1.0bn–€2.0bn+**
- **One-time cash cost:** roughly **€1.5bn–€4.0bn+**

This is the scenario with the highest gross savings, but also the highest implementation cost and risk.

### Why the gross savings can disappoint
Closure-led restructurings often look great on paper but suffer from:
- severance and bridge costs
- asset write-downs
- remediation and site exit costs
- supplier disruption
- relocation costs
- temporary inefficiencies in the new footprint
- industrial action and delays

The “headline” savings are not the same as the **net present value**.

### Governance feasibility problem
Under the structure you described:
- co-determination gives the workforce an effective veto on a two-thirds board majority requirement,
- the family holding may support restructuring,
- but the state shareholder and workforce side are likely to resist closures,
- meaning the probability of clean execution is low unless the closure plan is exceptionally well-compensated.

### Finance assessment
Closure-led restructuring is only rational if:
- the savings gap is so large that alternatives are inadequate, and
- management is confident that the governance hurdle can be cleared, and
- the company can absorb the industrial-relations shock.

If those conditions are not met, the project can destroy value rather than create it.

### Bottom line
This is the **highest-savings / highest-risk** option. In your scenario, it appears **least feasible** on implementation grounds.

## 7) Decision logic from a finance perspective

If management’s true target is only to close a **modest margin gap**, then a no-closure package may be enough.

If the company needs a **material and durable margin reset**, then a no-closure package is usually not enough unless employee concessions are very deep and long-dated.

If management needs **large structural savings** but must operate under strong veto constraints, then the economically best compromise is often:
- **partial footprint reduction**
- combined with
- **voluntary exits**
- **working-time adjustments**
- **temporary labor-cost concessions**
- **overhead cuts**
- **investment commitments**

That tends to maximize the chance of:
- board approval,
- implementation,
- and a positive NPV.

## 8) Key risks and sensitivities

### 1) Volumes
If European demand weakens further, even a partial-footprint package may be too light.

### 2) Labor concessions duration
Temporary wage restraint is worth much less than a multi-year structural reset.

### 3) Productivity credibility
Savings assumptions must be net of transition friction; otherwise savings are overstated.

### 4) Governance deadlock
If the supervisory board cannot reach the required majority, even a financially sound plan can fail.

### 5) Industrial action
Strike costs can materially reduce the NPV of any closure-heavy plan.

### 6) Time to benefit
The longer the transition, the more cash burn and working-capital drag the restructuring imposes.
