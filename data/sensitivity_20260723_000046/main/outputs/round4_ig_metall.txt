I’ll be equally direct.

## Where I agree
I agree with the works council, the state government, and even management on one fundamental point: **the company needs a durable turnaround, not a cosmetic compromise**.

I also agree that:
- employee contributions will be necessary,
- industrial peace matters,
- and a weak deal that just postpones the problem would be unacceptable.

So yes: we need real savings, real implementation, and a settlement that lasts.

## Where I disagree
### 1) I do not accept closures as a bargaining starting point
Management is still speaking as if closures and compulsory redundancies are legitimate default options. They are not.

We have already said clearly:
- **no German plant closures**
- **no compulsory redundancies**
- **no dismantling of the collective wage framework**

That is not posturing. It is the boundary of any acceptable settlement.

### 2) I do not accept “review gate” logic that keeps closures alive
The works council is right to mistrust a staged model where:
- first there is “no immediate closure,”
- then a review,
- then a closure fallback.

That is not security; it is deferred pressure.

If a compromise is to be built, it must be built on the basis that **German plants remain open**. Full stop. Not “for now.”

### 3) I do not accept a burden-sharing story where workers pay first and most
Management, the family holding, and investors keep framing the issue as if the workforce must prove its willingness to sacrifice while ownership and management keep strategic freedom untouched.

That is one-sided.

A fair settlement requires:
- employee contribution,
- management contribution,
- and owner contribution.

If workers are asked for wage restraint, flexibility, and productivity gains, then the top must also contribute visibly.

## On the expert’s numbers
The expert’s assessment is useful because it confirms two things:
1. A **no-closure** solution is possible in principle.
2. It only works if the alternative package is **large enough**.

That is exactly why the discussion should focus on a quantified alternative package, not on closure threats.

The expert also made clear that closure-led restructuring carries:
- major one-time costs,
- high industrial-relations risk,
- and serious governance execution risk.

So the “hardest” option on paper is not automatically the best one in reality.

## To management
If you want the union to engage seriously, then bring the actual gap and show it transparently:
- what annual savings are required,
- how much comes from overhead reduction,
- how much from productivity and process changes,
- how much from working-time and shift adjustments,
- how much from management and owner contribution,
- and how much from voluntary measures.

Do that, and we can talk seriously about the mix.

But I will not accept a package that starts from:
- closures,
- forced layoffs,
- or permanent wage dumping.

## To the works council
We are aligned on the red lines. I want to reinforce one point: any employee contribution must be:
- **temporary**
- **fair**
- **measurable**
- and **tied to binding guarantees**

We cannot let “temporary concessions” become a permanent rollback of job security and collective standards.

## To the state government
You are right to reject closures and forced redundancies. But that position must be backed with real influence in the room.

If management keeps pushing closure logic, then the state should use its political and shareholder weight to stop the burden being shifted entirely onto the workforce.

## To the family holding and investors
You keep asking for credibility. Fine. Then accept this reality: **credibility in industrial relations is not achieved by threatening the workforce into submission**.

A durable settlement needs trust. If you break trust, you get strikes, deadlock, and long-term damage. That is not value creation.

## What we are prepared to discuss
We are prepared to discuss a serious package built from:
- voluntary exits,
- retirement bridges,
- internal redeployment,
- shift rationalization,
- working-time flexibility,
- productivity improvements,
- bonus moderation or deferral,
- temporary wage restraint,
- overhead cuts,
- management contribution,
- and investment commitments in Germany.

That is a real restructuring path.

## What we are not prepared to discuss
We are **not** prepared to discuss:
- German plant closures,
- compulsory redundancies,
- closure as a fallback,
- or “partial-footprint reduction” that is just a euphemism for shutting sites later.

And let me be equally clear: if management insists on keeping closures and forced layoffs on the table, then industrial conflict will remain unavoidable.

## Bottom line
Our position remains unchanged:
- **protect jobs**
- **protect the collective wage framework**
- **avoid forced layoffs**
- **share the burden fairly**
- **reach a durable settlement**

If management wants an agreement, it needs to come back with a quantified package that proves savings can be delivered **without** breaking the social contract. If it cannot do that, then it is not offering restructuring — it is offering a transfer of pain to employees.
