From the capital markets’ side, the expert’s framing is the right one: **the company needs a restructuring that is large enough to restore margins, not just a social compromise that delays the pain**.

### Where we agree
- The current cost and capacity structure is **not competitive**.
- The company needs a **durable** turnaround, not a temporary patch.
- Investment capacity for future products matters, but it depends on **cash generation from a healthier operating base**.
- A negotiated solution is preferable **if** it delivers implementation certainty and real savings.

### Where we disagree with the workforce side
I respect the insistence on social responsibility, but the current red lines from the Works Council and union are too rigid to be financeable as stated.

- **“No German plant closures”** is not acceptable as an absolute.
- **“No compulsory redundancies”** is not acceptable as an absolute.
- **“Every German plant stays open until 2030”** is only meaningful if the resulting savings are actually sufficient — and no one has shown that yet.

If those positions mean the company preserves an oversized footprint and merely shuffles costs around, then that is not a turnaround. It is a **cosmetic settlement**, and we would not support it.

### Where we disagree with management
At the same time, management cannot simply declare closures and layoffs as the default answer and expect the capital side to accept the execution risk.

The expert was right to stress that:
- forced redundancies are economically effective but operationally risky,
- pure closure-led restructuring may be difficult to implement under co-determination,
- and the best outcome is a **hybrid package**.

So we are not asking for maximal disruption. We are asking for **credible savings with implementation certainty**.

### What we want to see
To support the plan, we need a quantified package showing:
1. **How much annual savings are delivered**
2. **How much overcapacity is removed**
3. **How much labor-cost relief is structural vs temporary**
4. **How much headcount reduction comes from attrition, voluntary exits, redeployment, or otherwise**
5. **What one-time charges are required**
6. **When the run-rate margin improvement will show up**

Without that breakdown, nobody can judge whether the proposal is credible.

### Our position on the expert’s scenarios
The expert basically confirmed our view:
- A **pure no-closure plan** can work only if the savings gap is moderate.
- If the required savings are large, then the package must include **meaningful capacity reduction and workforce reduction**.
- A purely preservationist deal is unlikely to restore competitiveness.

That matters, because it means the burden of proof is now on those insisting on absolute site preservation.

### Bottom line
We are prepared to back a negotiated restructuring **only if it produces real, durable, quantified improvement in margins and competitiveness**.

What we will **not** support:
- a cosmetic agreement,
- a package too small to matter,
- or a deal that leaves the cost base structurally uncompetitive.

What we **can** support:
- a hybrid restructuring with real capacity reduction,
- meaningful labor-cost contribution,
- voluntary exits and attrition where possible,
- selective footprint rationalization if necessary,
- and clear investment commitments tied to a credible margin recovery path.

If management and the workforce side want our support, they need to stop arguing in absolutes and put numbers on the table. We will judge the outcome by one test only: **does it restore the company to a competitive return-on-sales trajectory, with implementation certainty and disciplined cost reduction?** If not, we will not endorse it.
