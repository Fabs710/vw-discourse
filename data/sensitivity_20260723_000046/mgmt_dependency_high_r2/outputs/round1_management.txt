Let me be equally clear.

I hear the concerns about trust, jobs, and the historic role of the German sites. I do not dismiss them. But I cannot accept a position that starts from “no closures, no compulsory redundancies, no change to the job-security framework” as if the company’s cost crisis were not real.

We are facing a structural competitiveness problem, not a temporary dip. German factory costs are materially too high, the European market is shrinking, and the company is missing its return-on-sales target. If we do not reduce overcapacity and labour costs in a durable way, then we will not have the funds to invest in the next generation of products, software, and technology. That would be a far worse outcome for the workforce than a hard restructuring now.

So where do I agree?

- Yes, any solution has to be negotiated.
- Yes, a purely one-sided approach would be unworkable.
- Yes, we should explore capacity adjustment, redeployment, qualification, and working-time tools where they deliver real savings.

But where I disagree is fundamental:

- “No plant closures” cannot be a blanket veto if the structure is uncompetitive.
- “No compulsory redundancies” cannot mean that every site and every job is automatically protected regardless of economics.
- “No change to existing guarantees” is not compatible with a credible turnaround.

If the employee side wants to avoid closures, then it must help deliver an alternative that is just as effective on cost and capacity. That means a genuine labour-cost contribution, not symbolic gestures. It means structural savings, not reversible tweaks. And it means a package that gets us to a competitive cost base, not one that simply delays the reckoning.

I am prepared to be flexible on the means. I am not flexible on the outcome. The outcome has to include:

- a lasting reduction in excess capacity,
- a material reduction in labour costs,
- and a credible path to restoring margins and investment capacity.

If there is a serious alternative that achieves that without closures, I will examine it. But if the answer is merely “keep everything as it is and find savings elsewhere,” that is not a solution. It is a refusal to deal with reality.

And I will say one more thing plainly: prolonged industrial conflict helps nobody. But a cosmetic deal that leaves the cost base uncompetitive is not acceptable to management, owners, or investors. We need a durable settlement that changes the economics of the business.
