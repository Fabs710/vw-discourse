I will be equally direct.

I agree with management on one point: this company needs a credible restructuring, not a cosmetic truce. And I agree with the owners and investors that simply preserving the status quo while the economics deteriorate is not an option.

But I **do not** agree that this automatically means plant closures in Germany, compulsory redundancies, or tearing up the social settlement that has made this company a regional anchor for decades.

## To management
You keep saying “structural necessity,” but you still have not shown that closures are the only way to get there. That matters.

If you want support for hard measures, then put forward:
- site-by-site economics,
- quantified savings by instrument,
- a credible capacity plan,
- and a comparison of alternatives.

Without that, your insistence on closures reads less like necessity and more like a preference for the most disruptive instrument.

And let me be clear: if the logic of the restructuring ends in a weakened industrial base in this state, then it is not a successful restructuring from my point of view. It may look efficient on paper, but it would damage the company’s long-term role as an anchor employer and supplier of skilled industrial work.

## To the works council and union
I support your red line against plant closures and compulsory redundancies. That red line is legitimate. It reflects the public interest as much as the workforce interest.

But I also need to be frank with you: saying “no closures” is not enough by itself if we want to stop management from forcing the issue. We need a package that is economically credible, not just politically defensible.

So I want you to sharpen the alternative:
- what exact capacity reductions are possible without closures,
- what voluntary exit, redeployment, working-time, and qualification measures can be mobilised,
- what employee contribution is realistic,
- and what binding investment and employment guarantees you require in return.

If there is a route that preserves sites and avoids forced redundancies while still producing real savings, that is the route I want. But it has to be concrete.

## To the family owners and institutional investors
You are right that a cosmetic settlement would be value-destructive. But a settlement that destroys trust, know-how, and industrial peace can also be value-destructive.

The company is not only a financial asset. It is a major industrial system embedded in a region, with a workforce and governance structure that cannot simply be overridden without consequences.

So I reject any assumption that the quickest path to cost reduction is necessarily the best path to value preservation. Sometimes the cheaper-looking option creates the bigger long-term bill.

## My position
My support is for a **negotiated restructuring that preserves German sites, avoids compulsory redundancies, and keeps the company investable**.

That means:
- yes to capacity adjustments,
- yes to working-time instruments,
- yes to redeployment and qualification,
- yes to a fair employee contribution,
- yes to management and ownership contributions,
- no to plant closures in this state,
- no to mass compulsory redundancies,
- and no to any settlement that strips away the state’s co-determination and veto role.

## What I need to see next
Before I consider any hardening of the position, I need:
1. a transparent, site-level business case from management;
2. a quantified alternative package that avoids closures;
3. a binding employment and investment commitment for the German sites;
4. confirmation that the governance rights under the special statute remain intact.

If those conditions are met, I am prepared to back a serious negotiated restructuring and to use my board role to make it viable.

If they are not met, then I will not endorse a plan that shifts the burden onto the region, the workforce, and the company’s industrial future.
