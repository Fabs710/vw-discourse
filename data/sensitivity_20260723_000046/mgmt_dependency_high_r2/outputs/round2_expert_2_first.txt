From a site-level restructuring finance perspective, the key question is:

**Can the company close the savings gap through negotiated workforce and capacity measures without plant closures, and if so, under what conditions?**

## Short answer
**Possibly, but only if the company’s problem is mainly labor cost and moderate excess headcount.**  
If the real issue is **structural underutilization of entire plants with high fixed overhead**, then **working-time tools and voluntary exits alone are unlikely to be enough**. In that case, the economically superior option may still be partial or full site closure, or at least consolidation of production lines and support functions.

## What typically distinguishes the scenarios

### 1) Plant closure
Economically strongest when:
- utilization is persistently low,
- fixed costs are high,
- product can be moved elsewhere,
- and the site has no strategic role that justifies a premium.

Typical effect:
- **largest recurring annual savings**
- **highest one-time restructuring cost**
- **highest labor-relations and execution risk**

In automotive manufacturing, closure is usually justified only when the site has a **large fixed-cost burden** that cannot be offset by recovery in volume.

### 2) Voluntary exits / natural attrition / early retirement
Best when:
- the needed headcount reduction is meaningful but not extreme,
- the company can wait 12–36 months for savings,
- and know-how can be preserved.

Typical effect:
- lower friction than forced layoffs
- lower social and legal conflict
- but savings are slower and may be insufficient if the plant itself is the problem

This is a good instrument for **payroll reduction**, not for eliminating an uneconomic footprint.

### 3) Working-time reduction / wage moderation / bonus restraint
Best when:
- the company needs fast unit-labor-cost relief,
- demand is cyclical,
- and the existing footprint is broadly viable.

Typical effect:
- immediate operating cost relief
- preserves jobs and know-how
- but does **not** remove fixed plant overhead

So these measures can help restore margin, but they usually do **not** solve overcapacity by themselves.

## The finance logic
The decision should be based on a simple comparison:

**Closure value = avoided fixed cost + avoided semi-fixed cost − one-time closure cost**

versus

**Alternative-package value = savings from hours/wages/attrition/redeployment − incentive cost − productivity loss − duration of the transition**

If the site’s cost base is heavily fixed, closure tends to win on long-run economics.  
If the main issue is an excessive wage bill relative to output, a negotiated package can be better.

## What the company needs to show, site by site
To judge this properly, management should disclose for each site:

1. **Output and capacity utilization**
   - installed capacity
   - actual production
   - forecast demand by model/platform

2. **Cost structure**
   - direct labor
   - indirect labor
   - plant overhead
   - utilities
   - maintenance
   - logistics
   - depreciation

3. **Avoidable vs unavoidable cost**
   - what disappears if a site closes
   - what remains and is merely reallocated

4. **One-time costs**
   - severance
   - early retirement
   - transfer costs
   - decommissioning
   - asset write-downs
   - environmental obligations

5. **Alternative savings**
   - voluntary exit uptake
   - hours reduction impact
   - wage moderation impact
   - redeployment feasibility
   - timing of savings

Without that, nobody can honestly say whether closures are necessary or whether a negotiated alternative is sufficient.

## Practical conclusion
The most plausible economically efficient outcome is usually one of these:

### Option A: Deep negotiated restructuring without closure
Feasible only if:
- the required savings are moderate,
- capacity can be reduced through shifts, lines, and hours,
- and voluntary exits plus wage concessions deliver most of the gap.

### Option B: Partial consolidation with targeted closures or sub-site shutdowns
Feasible if:
- some plants or lines are structurally uneconomic,
- but the company wants to preserve strategic production in Germany.

### Option C: Full closure of the weakest site(s)
Feasible only if:
- the fixed-cost burden is large,
- relocation is operationally possible,
- and the long-run savings clearly exceed the one-time cost and disruption.

## Bottom line
**Working-time reductions, wage moderation, redeployment, and voluntary exits can be economically credible alternatives to closures — but only if the restructuring target is mainly payroll and not plant-level fixed overhead.**  
If the company truly has a **structural capacity problem**, then those measures are usually **necessary but not sufficient**.
