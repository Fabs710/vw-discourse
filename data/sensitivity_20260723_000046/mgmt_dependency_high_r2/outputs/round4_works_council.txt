The position of the works council remains unchanged, and I will be equally clear in round 4:

**No German plant closures. No compulsory redundancies. No erosion of the job-security framework.**

Management, owners, and investors keep repeating “structural necessity,” but the expert has already made the decisive point: **you still have not proven that closures or forced layoffs are necessary**. You have argued for them as tools, not demonstrated them as unavoidable.

## To management
You say you will provide site-level data. Good — but that should have come before any talk of closures. The burden is on you to justify destructive measures, not on workers to disprove them in the dark.

And I want to be explicit: even if your numbers show a gap, that does **not** automatically entitle you to close plants or impose forced redundancies. If the gap can be closed only by destroying jobs and sites, then you need a fundamentally different strategy, not permission to break the social settlement.

You keep speaking as if “durable restructuring” means permanent cuts in the workforce and footprint. For us, durable restructuring means:
- securing the company’s future,
- preserving industrial know-how,
- protecting the German production base,
- and keeping employment stable through 2030.

That is not a sentimental position. It is a rational one. A company that tears up trust, destroys plant networks, and forces people out does not become stronger — it becomes more conflict-prone and less resilient.

## To the controlling family holding
You say you do not accept a blanket veto. But from the workforce’s side, that sounds like a demand for a blank cheque to close plants if management later claims it is convenient.

We are not granting that. The special governance structure and co-determination are there for precisely this kind of decision. They are not decorative. If you want our support, then support a package that protects the workforce, not one that treats workers as a cost item to be cut away.

You speak of value destruction. Then understand this:
- plant closures destroy value,
- compulsory redundancies destroy value,
- and dismantling job-security guarantees destroys value.

Not just social value — economic value.

## To the regional state government
I appreciate that you oppose closures and forced redundancies. But you still seem to be leaving the door half-open through “last resort” language and process conditions. That is not enough.

The state should use its role to make the red lines real:
- no plant closures in Germany,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- no weakening of co-determination,
- and no use of the crisis as a pretext to dismantle the industrial base.

A temporary moratorium is not a solution if it simply postpones the same threat. We need a substantive commitment, not a procedural pause.

## To the union
We are aligned on the fundamentals, and I want to reinforce that. Any employee contribution must remain tightly limited and conditional. It cannot become the opening wedge for closures, layoffs, or the permanent dismantling of collective protections.

If management wants contributions, it must first commit to the essentials:
1. Every German plant stays open.
2. No compulsory redundancies.
3. Job-security framework preserved.
4. Co-determination respected.

Only then can limited, temporary, and reversible workforce measures even be discussed. Anything else is coercion by another name.

## On the expert’s analysis
The expert’s breakdown was useful because it confirmed what we already know:
- closures may have the largest recurring savings,
- but they also have the highest one-time costs and highest industrial-relations risk,
- while voluntary exits and working-time tools are less destructive and reversible.

That matters because management is trying to turn “largest savings” into “therefore necessary.” That is not the same thing.

If alternatives are less disruptive and can close the gap, then closures are not justified.  
If alternatives cannot close the gap, then management must revisit the business model, product allocation, and investment strategy — not simply sacrifice plants and people first.

## What we can accept
We are not refusing restructuring. We are refusing **destructive restructuring**.

We can discuss:
- voluntary exits,
- early retirement,
- redeployment,
- internal transfers,
- qualification,
- working-time adjustments,
- temporary wage moderation,
- bonus restraint,
- capacity changes that do not involve closing plants,
- and investment commitments for future products.

But I will not blur the line: **capacity adjustment is not a code word for site closure**. And “voluntary” cannot be a disguise for pressure on vulnerable workers.

## What we need now
If management insists on making any case at all, then it must table the full site-level picture:
- fixed and variable costs,
- utilization,
- one-time closure costs,
- recurring savings from every alternative,
- and the exact gap after non-destructive measures.

And if the numbers still do not show a closure necessity, then the closure agenda must be taken off the table.

## My bottom line
I do not accept the idea that the company’s future requires breaking its historic job-security commitments.

So my position is still:

- **Every German plant remains open**
- **No compulsory redundancies**
- **Preserve co-determination and job security**
- **Negotiate only restructuring that does not sacrifice sites and livelihoods**

If management, owners, or investors want a settlement, they need to come back with a package built on those principles — not on threats dressed up as economics.
