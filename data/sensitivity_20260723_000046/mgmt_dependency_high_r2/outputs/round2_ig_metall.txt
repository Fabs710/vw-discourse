We are aligned with the works council on the fundamentals, and I will be just as clear as they are: **no plant closures, no compulsory redundancies, and no dismantling of the collective wage framework as the price of this restructuring.**

That is not a rhetorical posture. It is the boundary within which any serious bargaining can happen.

## Where we agree
We agree that the company needs a durable solution, not a cosmetic one.  
We agree that trust, know-how, and industrial peace are real economic assets.  
We agree that employees can contribute — but only as part of a **shared-sacrifice package** with binding commitments from management, owners, and, where relevant, the state.

We also agree that if management is claiming structural necessity, then it should present **hard, site-level evidence**:
- what exactly is the savings gap,
- which plants are allegedly “uneconomic,”
- what capacity issue exists,
- and what alternative savings have already been exhausted.

That evidence matters. Not because we are interested in endless delay, but because workers will not be asked to accept cuts on the basis of vague assertions.

## Where we disagree
### 1) On closures and forced redundancies
Management, owners, and investors keep treating closures and compulsory layoffs as if they were simply one option among many. For us they are **not acceptable instruments**.

Once you close a plant or force people out, you do not just reduce cost. You destroy trust, damage the regional industrial base, and create long-term capability loss. That is not a sustainable turnaround; it is a rupture.

### 2) On the burden of adjustment
We will not accept a settlement where the workforce pays first and pays most, while ownership and top management remain largely untouched.

If labour is expected to contribute through:
- wage moderation,
- bonus restraint,
- working-time flexibility,
- reduced inflows,
- or a transformation fund,

then management and owners must contribute visibly and measurably too. That means:
- credible restraint on payouts and overhead at the top,
- a real ownership contribution,
- and binding investment commitments for future products and sites.

### 3) On “competitiveness” as a blank cheque
To the investor side: we hear your insistence on margins and discipline. But competitiveness is not achieved by stripping out employment guarantees and calling that strategy. A company that loses skilled employees, social peace, and site resilience may look cheaper in the short term and weaker in the medium term.

To management: if your plan only works by making German labour carry the full adjustment, then it is not a credible restructuring. It is simply cost shifting.

## What we are prepared to discuss
We are not rejecting change. We are rejecting one-sided change.

We are prepared to discuss:
- negotiated capacity reductions,
- working-time instruments,
- redeployment across plants,
- qualification and reskilling,
- voluntary exit schemes,
- natural attrition,
- and a time-limited employee contribution.

But those measures only become negotiable if they are paired with **binding job and site security**. In plain terms: if the workforce contributes, then the company must guarantee that this contribution does not become the first step toward closures and forced layoffs later.

## Our bargaining framework
If management wants a settlement, then the package must include all of the following:
1. **No plant closures in Germany.**
2. **No compulsory redundancies.**
3. **Preservation of the collective wage framework.**
4. **Binding employment and site guarantees for a defined horizon.**
5. **A fair employee contribution only as part of a reciprocal package.**
6. **Management, ownership, and capital contributions that are real, not symbolic.**
7. **Transparency on site economics and the actual savings requirement.**

## To the regional state government
You say you reject closures and mass layoffs, and we welcome that. But that position only has value if it is used to block a destructive plan, not to soften the language around it. If the state is serious, it should support a negotiated industrial policy solution that keeps sites open and preserves decent work.

## To the controlling family and investors
If you want long-term value, you cannot pursue a restructuring that breaks the company’s social settlement. This is not just a labour-relations issue; it is a governance issue. A value-preserving restructuring must preserve the workforce that makes value possible.

## Bottom line
We are ready for a serious deal.  
We are **not** ready to bless closures, forced redundancies, or the erosion of collective bargaining standards.

If management brings forward a real shared-sacrifice package with binding guarantees and verified savings, we will negotiate hard but constructively.  
If it comes with one-sided pain for workers and permanent insecurity for the sites, then it will face resistance — including industrial action.
