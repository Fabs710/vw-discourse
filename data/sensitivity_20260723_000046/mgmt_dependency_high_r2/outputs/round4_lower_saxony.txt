## My position remains unchanged
I cannot support a restructuring that:
- closes German plants,
- triggers compulsory redundancies,
- or weakens the state’s co-determination and veto role.

Those are not bargaining preferences for me; they are core conditions. If they are violated, the package is not acceptable.

At the same time, I am **not** defending the status quo for its own sake. I support a serious restructuring if it protects jobs and sites while restoring competitiveness.

## To management
I agree with you on one point: this must be a real restructuring, not a cosmetic truce.

I also accept that the company needs material savings and that employee-side measures may have to play a role. But what I reject is your attempt to keep closures and forced redundancies as ordinary instruments from the start, before the site economics have been tested and before a reciprocal package has been exhausted.

You say the numbers will not magically protect every plant. That is true. But neither will your current assertions prove that closures are necessary. So far, you have argued from pressure, not from disclosed evidence.

If you want my support, then bring:
- site-by-site fixed and variable costs,
- utilization by plant,
- one-time closure and restructuring costs,
- recurring savings from each alternative,
- and a credible comparison of closure versus negotiated measures.

Until then, closures remain an assertion, not a necessity.

And I will say this plainly: if management treats plant closures as the default answer to every cost issue, it is not only risking labor conflict. It is threatening the company’s role as a regional anchor and damaging the very trust needed for any durable turnaround.

## To the works council
I agree with your core line: no plant closures in Germany, no compulsory redundancies, and no erosion of job security through 2030.

I also agree that “capacity adjustment” must not be used as a euphemism for site shutdowns. That warning is justified.

Where I want to support you is in pressing for a **quantified alternative package**, not just a defensive one. If we are going to hold the line on closures and forced layoffs, then the company must still see a credible path to savings through:
- redeployment,
- qualification,
- voluntary exits,
- early retirement,
- working-time flexibility,
- bonus restraint,
- and targeted internal capacity reduction.

That is the constructive route. But let me be clear: I will not use the need for savings as an excuse to accept closures by another name.

## To the union
I appreciate that you have moved beyond slogans and are at least prepared to discuss working-time flexibility, wage moderation, bonus restraint, and voluntary exits.

That is the right direction. But I share the works council’s concern: flexibility must not become a back door to plant closures or compulsory redundancies.

So yes, I can support a limited employee contribution if, and only if, it is paired with:
- binding site security,
- no forced redundancies,
- preserved co-determination,
- and credible management/ownership contributions.

What I will not accept is a one-sided “contribution” from workers while management preserves the right to destroy jobs and sites later. That is not social partnership.

## To the controlling family holding
You are right that a cosmetic settlement would destroy value. I agree.

But I reject the idea that long-term value can be protected by leaving closures and compulsory redundancies permanently on the table while demanding trust from workers and the state. That is not stewardship; it is leverage.

If you want a durable settlement, then support a package that:
- preserves the German industrial footprint,
- protects employment,
- and respects co-determination.

A family owner with a genuine long-term horizon should know that industrial peace, skills retention, and regional legitimacy are value components too. They are not “soft” extras.

## To the institutional investors
I understand your concern about risk-adjusted value and the need to avoid a cosmetic deal.

But your position becomes unacceptable when it implies that preserving competitiveness necessarily means accepting closures and forced redundancies as a normal option. That is too crude.

A value-preserving settlement can be built through:
- negotiated labour contributions,
- voluntary exits,
- internal capacity shifts,
- investment commitments,
- and management discipline.

If those measures can close the gap, then they are the better option because they carry less execution risk and less long-term damage.

So I am not saying “no change.” I am saying: **change without destroying the regional base or breaking the social settlement**.

## On the expert analysis
The expert was helpful in one critical respect: it confirmed that no one can honestly claim, at this stage, that closures are proven necessary without site-level evidence.

That matters.

Yes, closure can generate the largest recurring savings. But it also carries the highest one-time cost, the highest conflict risk, and the greatest long-term damage if the company’s industrial network is weakened irreversibly.

So the real test is not whether closures save money in the abstract. It is whether the company can achieve comparable durable savings through a negotiated package that preserves the state’s industrial base.

## What I will support
I am prepared to back a restructuring package if it includes:
- no German plant closures during the agreed period,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- binding employment and site-security commitments,
- site-level disclosure,
- measurable management and ownership contributions,
- and a serious, quantified employee contribution that is time-limited and reversible.

I can support:
- redeployment,
- qualification,
- voluntary exit schemes,
- early retirement,
- working-time flexibility,
- temporary wage moderation,
- bonus restraint,
- and targeted capacity adjustment that does **not** mean closing plants.

---

## What I will not support
I will not support:
- closures presented as inevitable before the evidence is disclosed,
- forced redundancies as a routine restructuring tool,
- a settlement that weakens co-determination,
- or a deal that offloads the burden onto the workforce while preserving management’s freedom to dismantle sites later.

---

## Bottom line
I am open to a hard, negotiated restructuring.

But it must be a restructuring that:
- protects jobs,
- protects plants,
- protects the state’s governance role,
- and keeps the company a regional economic anchor.

Anything that falls short of that is not a settlement I can support.
