## Core economic assessment
A **negotiated no-closure or mixed package can, in principle, deliver a large share of the savings** that management seeks. But whether it can **fully match closure on a durable NPV basis** cannot be determined without site-level data.

In general:

- **Closure** tends to produce the **largest recurring fixed-cost savings**
- but also the **highest one-time costs** and **highest execution / industrial-relations risk**
- while **negotiated alternatives** usually have:
  - lower one-time costs,
  - lower disruption,
  - and lower risk,
  - but may leave some fixed costs in place

So the economically correct answer is not “closure is always better” or “no-closure is always enough.” It depends on the site economics.

## What determines the outcome
The decisive variables are:

1. **How much cost is truly fixed**
   - If a plant has high fixed overhead and persistent underutilization, closure becomes more attractive.
   - If a meaningful share of cost is labor-related and reducible through working-time, wage, or headcount measures, alternatives can get much closer.

2. **One-time closure costs**
   - Severance
   - early retirement
   - decommissioning
   - environmental / asset exit costs
   - transfer and ramp-down costs

   These can be substantial and sometimes reduce the net advantage of closure materially.

3. **Contribution margin lost**
   - If shutting a site removes profitable volume, closure savings can be overstated.
   - A negotiated package that preserves output can outperform closure in some cases.

4. **Durability of concessions**
   - Temporary labor concessions are less valuable than permanent structural cost reductions.
   - The longer the savings last, the more credible the alternative becomes.

5. **Execution risk**
   - Closure has higher risk of operational disruption, litigation, labor conflict, and transition losses.
   - Negotiated measures usually execute more smoothly.

## Practical comparison of instruments

### 1) Closure
Best when:
- site utilization is structurally low,
- fixed cost is high,
- the site is not strategically needed,
- and production can be shifted with limited margin loss.

Strength:
- strongest direct reduction in recurring cost

Weakness:
- highest one-time cost
- strongest labor conflict
- risk of losing productive capacity and know-how

### 2) Voluntary exits / early retirement
Best when:
- workforce age profile supports it,
- headcount can be reduced without compulsory measures,
- and the company can absorb transition costs.

Strength:
- lower conflict
- easier to negotiate
- can reduce fixed labor cost meaningfully

Weakness:
- may be too small or too slow to close a large gap alone

### 3) Working-time changes / wage moderation / bonus restraint
Best when:
- the cost gap is partly labor-cost driven,
- and the workforce will accept multi-year concessions.

Strength:
- low one-time cost
- rapid effect on cash cost
- preserves sites and know-how

Weakness:
- savings may be insufficient if the main problem is excess plant capacity

### 4) Targeted capacity reduction without closure
Often the best compromise when:
- the company needs structural savings,
- but wants to avoid full site shutdown.

Strength:
- captures much of the benefit of closure
- lower political and industrial-relations damage

Weakness:
- requires careful line/product reallocation
- savings may be less complete than closure

## My neutral conclusion
From an industrial economics perspective:

- **A negotiated package can plausibly close a substantial part of the savings gap**
- **A mixed package is often more efficient than pure closure**
- **But a categorical no-closure solution cannot be assumed to be sufficient without site-level numbers**

So the sensible next step is a **site-by-site comparison** of:

- fixed and variable cost
- utilization
- closure savings
- alternative-savings value
- one-time costs
- NPV over 5–10 years
- and execution risk

## What data would be needed to conclude decisively
For each plant, I would need:

- current output and utilization
- fixed overhead by site
- direct labor cost
- variable cost per unit
- one-time closure / wind-down cost
- expected savings from voluntary exits
- labor-cost concession assumptions
- redeployment capacity
- product allocation assumptions
- and the cash-flow profile over time

## Bottom line
**Economically, closure is not automatically required.**  
A **large negotiated package or mixed package may be enough**, but only if it produces **durable, quantified, site-specific savings** close to what closure would deliver.
