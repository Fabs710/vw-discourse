I’ll respond directly.

## To the works council
I do **not** accept your blanket line that every German plant must remain open to 2030 and that compulsory redundancies are categorically off the table. That position is not a restructuring strategy. It is an attempt to pre-decide the answer before the numbers are tested.

I agree with you on one thing: a cosmetic deal would be useless. But the conclusion you draw from that is wrong. A durable solution requires **material and permanent** cost and capacity reduction. If the current footprint is structurally too large and too expensive, then we cannot rule out closures or deeper workforce reductions in advance.

You keep saying “show us the numbers.” Fine — but the numbers do not exist in a vacuum. We already know enough to say:
- German factory costs are too high,
- the market is shrinking,
- the brand is missing its margin target,
- and the company cannot fund future products if we keep carrying excess cost.

So yes, we will provide site-level economics. But no, that does **not** mean we accept a veto on the instruments that may be required.

## To the union
You are right that worker contributions must be part of a shared package. I agree. But “shared sacrifice” does not mean management and owners contribute a little while labour keeps all protections intact.

Your position still amounts to:
- no closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- no real change to the job-security framework,
- and only reversible employee contributions.

That is not enough to fix a structural cost problem.

If the only acceptable outcome is to preserve the full legacy settlement, then there will be no credible route back to target margins. That would be bad for workers as well, because a weak company cannot invest, cannot compete, and cannot secure jobs over time.

## To the regional state government
I understand your political responsibility. I also understand that you want to preserve the industrial base. So do I. But preserving the industrial base does **not** mean freezing every site and every guarantee regardless of economics.

A temporary moratorium or review clause may be part of a process, but I will not accept a framework that makes hard decisions impossible. If the alternative package does not close the savings gap, then closures must remain available.

If the state wants to protect the region, it should help secure a restructuring that actually restores viability — not one that postpones the problem.

## To the controlling family and investors
I agree with you on the core point: a cosmetic arrangement is worse than no arrangement. We need durable savings, not optics.

Where I push back is on the idea that management can simply be expected to do this without employee-side changes. That is not realistic. Labour cost and capacity are central parts of the problem, and they must be addressed materially.

At the same time, I will not support a settlement that undermines the company’s ability to operate by creating permanent industrial conflict. That is exactly why I am pushing for a negotiated package — but a serious one, not a symbolic one.

## What I am prepared to do
I remain open to a package that avoids unnecessary disruption **if** it delivers real structural change. That means I am prepared to discuss:
- targeted capacity reductions,
- voluntary exits,
- early retirement,
- redeployment,
- working-time flexibility,
- wage moderation,
- bonus restraint,
- and a meaningful labour-cost contribution.

But I will not accept a package that:
- excludes closures in advance,
- rules out compulsory redundancies in all circumstances,
- preserves all existing guarantees untouched,
- and leaves the company with the same uncompetitive cost structure.

## What I need next
The expert has already made the correct point: this hinges on site-level economics and on whether negotiated alternatives can truly close the gap.

So here is what management needs to table:
1. site-by-site fixed and variable cost data,
2. capacity utilization by plant,
3. the exact savings gap,
4. the savings from closure versus alternative measures,
5. one-time restructuring costs,
6. and the timetable for return to target margins.

If the employee side believes it can close the gap without closures, then it must also put forward a **quantified** alternative package, not just principles.

## My bottom line
I am not here to threaten for effect. I am here to restore competitiveness.

That means:
- **material, durable cost reduction**,
- **capacity alignment**,
- **real labour-cost contribution**,
- and, if necessary, **hard structural measures**.

A deal that protects every legacy commitment and leaves the business structurally uncompetitive is not a deal I can sign up to.
