The union shares the core concern that the burden cannot fall on the workforce alone.

We agree on several points:
- This company does need a viable path through the crisis.
- A durable solution must protect industrial peace, trust, and the long-term future of the sites.
- If employees are expected to contribute, that contribution must be part of a fair package, not a one-sided sacrifice.

But I need to be equally clear where we do not agree with management’s current logic, and where our limits lie.

First: plant closures and compulsory redundancies are not acceptable starting points. They are not a “tool” we can simply trade away. Once you close a site or fire people compulsorily, you destroy trust, know-how, and the social fabric that this company depends on. That is not a sustainable restructuring; it is an industrial rupture.

Second: wage concessions cannot be demanded in isolation. If workers are to forgo bonuses, accept flexibility, or contribute to a transformation fund, then management, owners, and the capital side must also contribute visibly. That means no attempt to push the adjustment process entirely onto wages and jobs while preserving top-down control and shareholder expectations untouched.

Third: any settlement must be durable and binding. We are not interested in vague promises of future investment while jobs are cut now. If there are capacity reductions, they must be negotiated in a way that avoids forced layoffs and protects the collective wage framework. That is the only basis on which we can even discuss labour-cost measures.

So our position is this:
- Yes to negotiated capacity adjustment.
- Yes to working-time solutions, redeployment, qualification, and a fair employee contribution.
- Yes to a shared-sacrifice package.
- No to compulsory redundancies.
- No to plant closures.
- No to dismantling collective wage standards as the price of management’s restructuring.

If management wants the union and workforce to help stabilise this company, it needs to put real guarantees and real reciprocal contributions on the table. Without that, we will not treat this as a “restructuring,” but as an attack on the workforce — and we will respond accordingly.
