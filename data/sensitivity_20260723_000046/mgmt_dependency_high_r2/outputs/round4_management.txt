We are still circling the same issue, so I’ll be equally direct.

## To the works council
You keep repeating “no closures, no compulsory redundancies, job security through 2030” as if declaring red lines makes the economics disappear. It does not.

I agree with you on one point: we need a **real** restructuring, not a cosmetic one. But your solution is not a restructuring solution; it is a refusal to allow the instruments that may be required.

Let me be clear:
- I do **not** accept a blanket exclusion of closures.
- I do **not** accept a categorical ban on compulsory redundancies if the numbers show they are needed.
- I do **not** accept a job-security framework that preserves every legacy guarantee while the cost base stays structurally uncompetitive.

You say “show the numbers first.” Fair enough — we will. But the numbers will not magically prove that every plant can stay open and every job can be protected. If the site economics show persistent overcapacity and fixed-cost burden, then the company must be able to act.

And no, “capacity adjustment” is not just a euphemism. If a site is structurally uneconomic, capacity reduction may mean closure or deep consolidation. I will not pretend otherwise just to make the room more comfortable.

## To the union
You are more pragmatic than the works council in tone, but your bottom line is still too narrow.

Yes, I welcome that you are prepared to discuss:
- working-time flexibility,
- wage moderation,
- bonus restraint,
- voluntary exits,
- early retirement,
- redeployment,
- qualification.

That is constructive. But it is not sufficient unless it produces **material, durable savings**.

What I cannot accept is a package where:
- workers give temporary concessions,
- management keeps an uncompetitive footprint,
- and the structural cost problem remains.

That would simply delay the next crisis.

You say closures and forced layoffs are destructive. I do not dispute that. But failing to restore competitiveness is also destructive. A company that cannot earn its cost of capital will not protect jobs in the medium term. The real risk to employment is not only restructuring; it is a company that keeps losing ground until it has no room left to invest.

## To the regional state government
I understand your political responsibility and your concern for the industrial base. But your position is becoming a de facto veto on any serious restructuring.

I agree that closures are a last resort, not a first reflex. But “last resort” does **not** mean “never.” If the numbers show that a site is structurally uneconomic and alternatives cannot close the gap, then the company must retain the option to close or consolidate.

A state shareholder cannot credibly ask us to preserve every plant and every job guarantee while also demanding that the company become competitive. Those two objectives are not automatically compatible.

If you want to support the industrial base, then support a restructuring that actually restores viability:
- transparent economics,
- real labour-cost contribution,
- capacity alignment,
- investment into future products.
Not a freeze that leaves the company carrying excess cost into 2030.

## To the controlling family holding
You are right about one thing: a cosmetic truce is worthless. We need durable savings. Where I disagree is the implication that management should be prevented from using the hard tools if they are justified.

We are not asking for a blank cheque. We are asking for the ability to restructure on the basis of facts.

So yes:
- we will disclose site-level economics,
- we will quantify fixed and variable costs,
- we will show closure versus alternative scenarios,
- we will present one-time costs and recurring savings.

But I will not pre-commit to a solution that excludes closures or compulsory redundancies regardless of what the numbers show. That would be irresponsible.

If the alternative package truly closes the gap with comparable durability and lower risk, I will support it. But if it does not, then the company must be able to take structural action.

## To the institutional investors
You say you want risk-adjusted value, not ideology. On that we agree.

But value is not protected by preserving every existing guarantee when the economics have changed. Nor is it protected by hoping voluntary measures will somehow replace hard structural savings.

You are right to reject a cosmetic deal. But you should also be honest: there is no credible route to restoring margins if we cannot materially reduce cost and capacity. If the site-level data shows that the negotiated alternative is insufficient, then closures and deeper workforce reductions must remain available.

## On the expert’s analysis
The expert was useful because the message is simple:
- closure delivers the biggest recurring savings,
- alternatives reduce disruption but may not remove enough fixed cost,
- only site-level economics can show where the balance lies.

That is exactly why I am insisting on disclosure and a quantified comparison.

But let me add the part the labour side keeps avoiding: if the data shows that alternatives do **not** close the gap, then the debate is over. We will not keep an uncompetitive structure just because the necessary instrument is unpopular.

## What management is prepared to table
We are prepared to put forward a site-by-site restructuring package including:
- fixed and variable cost data,
- utilization by plant,
- closure savings versus alternative savings,
- one-time restructuring costs,
- and a margin-recovery timetable.

We are also prepared to discuss:
- voluntary exits,
- early retirement,
- redeployment,
- targeted working-time measures,
- temporary wage moderation,
- and bonus restraint.

But we will do so only in a framework that recognizes the possibility that deeper structural measures are required if the alternatives underdeliver.

## My bottom line
I will not sign up to a restructuring process that pre-decides the answer in favour of preserving every plant, every job, and every guarantee.

That is not a turnaround. It is a managed decline with better PR.

What I will support is:
- a **quantified**, site-level assessment,
- a **serious** employee contribution,
- a **durable** reduction in cost and capacity,
- and, if necessary, **hard structural measures**.

If the only acceptable outcome is “no closures, no compulsory redundancies, no real change,” then we do not have a restructuring path. We have a deadlock.
