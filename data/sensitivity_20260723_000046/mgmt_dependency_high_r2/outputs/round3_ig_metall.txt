We are not prepared to accept a “restructuring” that begins and ends with the workforce paying for management’s strategic failures.

## Where we agree
We agree with the works council, the state, and the expert on the central point: **this cannot be decided on slogans**. If management claims structural necessity, then it must show the **site-level economics**:
- fixed versus variable costs,
- utilization,
- avoidable savings,
- one-time restructuring costs,
- and a comparison of alternatives.

We also agree that a **cosmetic truce** would be useless. This company needs a durable settlement, not a short pause before the next confrontation.

And yes, we are prepared to discuss **employee-side contributions**. We already said that in our counter-proposal. But that willingness is conditional on a **shared-sacrifice package**, not on workers carrying the entire burden.

## Where we disagree
### 1) On plant closures
Management, owners, and investors keep speaking as if closures are simply a normal tool. For us, they are not a normal tool. They are an extreme and socially destructive measure. We do **not** accept plant closures as the starting point, and we do **not** accept them as the first answer to a cost problem.

The works council is right to say closures destroy trust, know-how, and regional industrial capacity. The state is right that closures would damage the industrial base. We share that view.

### 2) On compulsory redundancies
We reject compulsory redundancies. Full stop.

If the company cannot reach its targets without forcing people out, then management has not done its job of designing a negotiated restructuring. The burden must be spread through:
- voluntary exit paths,
- natural attrition,
- redeployment,
- working-time measures,
- qualification,
- and a limited labour-cost contribution.

Not by forcing people out the door.

### 3) On the collective wage framework
We will not allow this crisis to become the pretext for tearing up the collective bargaining framework.

Yes, we can discuss moderation, bonus restraint, time-limited contributions, or a transformation fund. But we will not accept a settlement that permanently dismantles wage standards while management calls that “competitiveness.”

## What would be a serious package
If management wants a deal, then it must bring forward a package with all of the following:

1. **No German plant closures for the agreed period.**
2. **No compulsory redundancies.**
3. **A binding employment and site-security framework.**
4. **Transparent site-by-site data and a quantified savings gap.**
5. **A fair employee contribution that is time-limited and reversible.**
6. **Management and ownership contributions that are visible and real.**
7. **Investment commitments for future products and future work.**

That is not an unreasonable list. It is the minimum for a settlement that does not sacrifice the workforce to solve a balance-sheet problem.

## To management
You say the burden must be shared, but your own proposal still points first to closures and layoffs. That is not shared sacrifice. That is selective sacrifice.

If you want us to believe the numbers justify your approach, then publish the figures. Show:
- which sites are allegedly uneconomic,
- what savings are actually required,
- what part of the gap can be closed through working-time tools and voluntary measures,
- and why those measures would supposedly be insufficient.

Without that, your “necessity” argument is not persuasive. It is a demand for trust without evidence.

## To the controlling family and investors
If you want long-term value, then back a settlement that preserves the workforce and the industrial base.

A company does not become stronger by turning industrial conflict into a cost-cutting instrument. The social settlement is part of the value proposition here. Break that, and you create instability that no financial model can fully recover.

You say you want durable savings. So do we. But durable savings cannot come from destroying the collective framework that makes production possible in the first place.

## To the regional state government
Your role matters. If you want to be serious about protecting the region, then do not settle for “review clauses” that keep closures in play while workers make concessions today.

Use your governance role to insist on:
- transparent disclosure,
- negotiated capacity adjustment,
- no forced redundancies,
- no plant closures in Germany,
- and binding investment and employment commitments.

That would be a real industrial-policy answer.

## On the expert’s analysis
The expert was useful because it clarified the actual economics:
- closures may deliver the largest recurring savings,
- but they also carry the highest one-time cost and risk,
- while voluntary exits and working-time measures may be sufficient if the problem is mainly payroll and not a deep structural fixed-cost problem.

That is exactly why we are not accepting management’s closure-first logic.

The burden is on management to prove that the negotiated alternatives cannot close the gap. Until they do, closures are not justified.

## Our bargaining position
We are prepared to negotiate hard on a **shared-sacrifice package**, including:
- wage moderation for a limited period,
- bonus restraint,
- working-time flexibility,
- capacity reduction through internal means,
- voluntary exit and retirement pathways,
- redeployment and qualification,
- and a transformation fund with reciprocal contributions from management and owners.

But only under binding protections:
- no plant closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- no erosion of the collective wage framework,
- and no one-sided burden on labour.

## Final word
We are not here to block change. We are here to block **one-sided** change.

If management and ownership want a durable settlement, they must stop treating workers as the easiest adjustment variable. Bring us a quantified, reciprocal package with real guarantees, and we will bargain constructively.

If they do not, then they should expect resistance — including industrial action.
