## Short answer

Based on the facts provided, **it is plausible that a negotiated package of voluntary exits, working-time changes, wage moderation, bonus restraint, redeployment, and targeted capacity reduction can close a substantial portion of the savings gap**, but **whether it can match closure savings on a fully durable, net-present-value basis cannot be concluded without site-level data**.

In most industrial cases, **closure delivers the largest recurring fixed-cost saving**, but it also typically carries:
- significant one-time closure and remediation costs,
- severance and transfer costs,
- pension and accrual impacts,
- loss of volume-related contribution margin,
- and higher execution / industrial-relations risk.

A negotiated alternative can outperform closure **if**:
- the fixed-cost burden is not as concentrated as assumed,
- utilization can be improved or capacity can be removed elsewhere,
- labor-cost concessions are material and multi-year,
- voluntary exits achieve enough headcount reduction at lower cash cost,
- and the company retains enough volume to preserve contribution margin.

## What matters economically

### 1) Fixed vs variable cost
The key question is how much of the site cost base is truly fixed in the medium term.

- **Closure savings** usually capture:
  - direct labor,
  - plant overhead,
  - maintenance,
  - local support functions,
  - utilities and site occupancy,
  - and some logistics / depreciation-related burden if assets are written down or exited.
- But closure may also sacrifice:
  - contribution margin from lost production,
  - network efficiency,
  - and scale benefits in purchasing and engineering.

A negotiated package can reduce **labor-fixed costs** without losing all site contribution, which is why it can sometimes approach closure economics.

### 2) One-time costs
Closure usually has material one-time costs:
- severance and social plan costs,
- outplacement and transfer support,
- decommissioning and environmental obligations,
- lease or asset exit costs,
- pension / early retirement effects,
- inventory transfer and ramp-down costs,
- and implementation expense.

A voluntary package also has one-time costs, but generally lower and less disruptive:
- severance for volunteers,
- early-retirement subsidies,
- qualification and redeployment costs,
- temporary wage concessions administration,
- and transition costs.

### 3) Durability of savings
The decisive issue is not just gross savings, but **durable recurring savings**.

A negotiated package is only comparable if it locks in savings for long enough to offset:
- lower immediacy versus closure,
- possible reversion risk after expiry,
- and the risk that underutilization persists.

If labor concessions are temporary, the NPV can be materially weaker than closure unless the savings are sizeable and prolonged.

## Scenario logic

### Scenario A: Closure / plant shutdown
Typical financial profile:
- **Highest recurring fixed-cost reduction**
- **Highest one-time cost**
- **High execution risk**
- **Potential loss of contribution margin**
- **High uncertainty if customer disruption or supply-chain reconfiguration occurs**

Best when:
- site utilization is structurally low,
- fixed costs are hard to remove otherwise,
- product allocation can be shifted elsewhere with limited margin loss,
- and one-time exit costs are manageable relative to the medium-term savings.

### Scenario B: Negotiated no-closure package
Typical financial profile:
- **Moderate to high recurring savings**
- **Lower one-time cost**
- **Better continuity of operations**
- **Lower industrial-relations and ramp-down risk**
- **But often less absolute savings than closure**

Best when:
- headcount reduction can be achieved via voluntary means,
- working-time and pay measures are material,
- site output remains strategically useful,
- and the company can preserve enough scale to avoid network inefficiency.

### Scenario C: Mixed package
This is often the economically strongest compromise in practice:
- one or two targeted site/line actions,
- plus voluntary exits,
- plus temporary labor-cost relief,
- plus redeployment and capacity rationalization.

This can deliver most of the closure savings while limiting the one-time costs and execution risk.

## Likely conclusion from finance perspective

My neutral assessment is:

1. **If the savings gap is mainly fixed-site overhead and persistent underutilization, closure will usually have the strongest NPV.**
2. **If a meaningful share of the gap comes from labor cost, shift flexibility, and headcount reduction, a negotiated package may come close enough to make closure economically unnecessary.**
3. **A mixed package is often the most efficient solution because it captures much of the saving with lower exit cost and lower disruption.**
