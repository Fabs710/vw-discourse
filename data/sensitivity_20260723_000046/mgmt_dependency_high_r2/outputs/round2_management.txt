Let me respond directly.

I appreciate that several parties now acknowledge the scale of the problem. That matters. But I have to be equally direct: what I am hearing from the works council and the union is still not a solution to the cost crisis. It is a demand that management prove the crisis while ruling out the very instruments that would actually fix it.

That is not acceptable.

## Where I agree
I agree with three points that came through clearly:

1. **A cosmetic deal is useless.**  
   I do not want a short-term truce that leaves the company structurally uncompetitive.

2. **This must be durable and negotiated.**  
   We cannot run this company through a permanent confrontation.

3. **Employee-side measures can be part of the answer.**  
   Working-time tools, redeployment, qualification, voluntary exits, and targeted flexibility all have a role.

So far, so good.

## Where I disagree
### 1) “No plant closures” is not a viable starting point
The works council and the union are repeating a blanket veto on closures. I understand why they are doing that politically. But economically, it is not credible.

If site economics show that overcapacity exists and that certain production structures are permanently too expensive, then closures must remain on the table. I am not saying every plant must close. I am saying management will not sign up to a restructuring model that excludes the only tools capable of delivering the needed structural change.

If you want me to rule out closures in advance, you are asking me to accept a cost base that we already know is too high.

### 2) “No compulsory redundancies” cannot be absolute
I would prefer to avoid compulsory redundancies as far as possible. But I am not going to commit to a categorical ban if the company cannot otherwise reduce excess capacity at the required scale.

If the package can achieve the necessary workforce adjustment through voluntary exits, transfers, retirement pathways, and natural attrition, I will welcome that. But if it cannot, then insisting on zero compulsory redundancies becomes a barrier to restructuring, not a safeguard.

### 3) Job-security guarantees cannot remain untouched
There is a repeated appeal to preserve the existing job-security framework as if that were compatible with a major turnaround. It is not.

If the company is to regain competitiveness, the employment framework has to reflect the new reality. That does not mean reckless firing. It does mean that long-standing guarantees cannot be treated as untouchable when the business model itself has changed.

### 4) “Show us the numbers” is fair — but it does not suspend reality
Yes, site-level economics matter. Yes, we should work from hard data. That is reasonable.

But I want to be very clear: asking for more data cannot become a tactic to delay decisions indefinitely while the cost base continues to erode the business. We already know enough to conclude that German factory costs are above target, the European market is weaker, and the brand is missing its margin goal.

So yes, we can discuss site-by-site detail. No, we are not going back to square one.

## To the works council
You say every German plant must remain open to 2030. I cannot commit to that. Not because I want conflict, but because I cannot responsibly guarantee a structure that may not be financially supportable.

You also say the workforce must not bear the burden. But the reality is that all stakeholders must contribute, and labour cost is a central part of the problem. If the employee side refuses meaningful structural adjustment, then you are not defending the company. You are defending the status quo.

I also reject the implication that management “failed to solve the transition properly” and therefore should now absorb the full burden of correction. We are here to fix the economics, not assign blame in a way that blocks action.

## To the union
I hear the point about shared sacrifice, and I agree that there must be reciprocal contributions from management and owners. Fine. That is not controversial.

What is controversial is the demand that workers contribute only if all core protections remain intact. That is not a shared-sacrifice package; that is a conditional concession with no real structural change.

If the union wants to be taken seriously on a durable settlement, then it has to help deliver:
- lower fixed labour costs,
- lower capacity,
- and a more flexible employment framework.

Without that, there is no credible route to restoring margins.

## To the regional state government
You say you reject closures and mass layoffs in the state. I respect the political responsibility, but I will be blunt: if that position hardens into a veto against any meaningful restructuring, then it becomes part of the problem.

You cannot ask the company to remain a major industrial anchor while blocking the adjustments that would make that possible. Protecting every site at any cost is not industrial policy; it is denial.

## To the family owners and investors
I agree with the core point made by the owners and capital side: a cosmetic solution is worse than no solution.

We need a package that actually changes the economics of the business. That means durable reductions in cost and capacity, not symbolic gestures. On that, we are aligned.

## What I am prepared to do
I am still prepared to negotiate a package that avoids unnecessary disruption, but only if it delivers real structural change.

That means I would examine:
- selective capacity reductions,
- voluntary exit and retirement programs,
- redeployment across the group,
- working-time adjustments,
- qualification and reskilling,
- and a labour-cost contribution.

But I will not accept a package that:
- preserves every site automatically,
- excludes closures categorically,
- rules out compulsory redundancies in all circumstances,
- and leaves the company with the same uncompetitive economics.

## The next step
If the employee side wants to argue that equivalent savings can be achieved without closures, then bring forward a concrete alternative package with quantified effects:

- how much cost reduction,
- from which measures,
- by when,
- and with what binding commitment to delivery.

If that package genuinely closes the savings gap and restores competitiveness, I will consider it seriously.

If it does not, then we are back to the same conclusion: structural undercapacity and a structurally high cost base must be reduced through hard measures.

So let me be explicit: I am not drifting toward a compromise that fails the business. I am open on the instrument, not on the requirement.

We need a settlement that restores competitiveness, protects future investment, and returns the brand to target margins. Anything less is not a restructuring.
