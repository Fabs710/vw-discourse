We are aligned on the **objective**: this company must restore competitive margins and a structurally lower cost base. We are **not** aligned on the method if the result is only a cosmetic compromise that leaves the company uncompetitive.

## Where we agree
- The company needs **real restructuring**, not symbolism.
- Prolonged conflict would destroy value.
- Site-level economics matter.
- A negotiated package is preferable **if** it actually closes the gap.
- Labour-side instruments like voluntary exits, redeployment, working-time flexibility, and wage moderation are useful **if they are material**.

## Where we disagree

### To the works council
Your “no closures, no compulsory redundancies, every plant open to 2030” line is **not acceptable** to us as a blanket position.

I understand the concern about trust and industrial peace. But trust does not create competitiveness. If the site economics show persistent overcapacity and structurally high fixed costs, then closures cannot be ruled out in advance.

You are asking capital to accept the risk of preserving an uncompetitive footprint indefinitely. We will not back that.

### To the union
We appreciate that you are willing to discuss contributions. But your package still sounds too heavily weighted toward reversible measures while excluding the instruments that may actually be needed.

If the company can close most of the savings gap through:
- voluntary exits,
- early retirement,
- working-time changes,
- bonus restraint,
- and redeployment,

then fine — we would prefer that over shutdowns.

But if that package does **not** deliver durable, quantified savings, then closures and compulsory redundancies cannot be excluded simply because they are unpopular.

### To management
We agree with your insistence that this must be driven by economics, not slogans. But your case is still incomplete without site-level data.

You need to put forward, clearly and quickly:
- fixed and variable cost by site,
- utilization by site,
- one-time closure costs,
- recurring savings from closure versus alternatives,
- payback period,
- and margin impact.

Until that is on the table, no one can judge whether closures are truly necessary. But once it is on the table, we will judge it on financial merit — not on labor politics.

### To the regional state government
We respect your role and your concern for the industrial base. But a state-backed veto against hard restructuring is not a sustainable value strategy.

A temporary moratorium tied to transparency is acceptable as a process tool. A permanent closure ban is not.

### To the controlling family holding
We agree that cosmetic peace is bad. We also agree that owners should not accept a deal that merely delays the problem.

But “durable” does not mean “protect every legacy guarantee.” It means:
- cost reduction that lasts,
- execution that is credible,
- and a structure that can support future investment.

If the data shows that the no-closure route achieves that, we would support it. If not, we will not support preserving every site at the expense of margins.

## Our position
We will support a restructuring only if it delivers:
1. **durable cost reduction**
2. **credible margin recovery**
3. **measurable, site-specific savings**
4. **a clear timetable**
5. **execution risk that is acceptable**

That means we are open to a mixed package, but not to a package that merely postpones the hard decision.

## What would move us
A quantified package that shows alternatives can close the gap **without** closures or forced redundancies, and do so with acceptable payback and execution risk.

## What would not move us
- slogans about social peace,
- blanket no-closure declarations,
- or a cosmetic settlement that protects the current footprint while margins remain structurally impaired.
