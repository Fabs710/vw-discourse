I’ll be direct.

I agree with everyone on one point: this company needs a **durable** restructuring, not a cosmetic truce. A prolonged conflict would damage value, and the current cost structure is not sustainable. On that diagnosis, we are aligned.

Where I disagree is on the attempt by labour and the state to turn that diagnosis into a **blanket veto** on the instruments management may need.

## To the works council and union
You are right that **site-level economics** matter. You are also right that a credible package must be negotiated, not imposed. But your current position still amounts to:

- no plant closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- no real change to the legacy job-security framework,
- and employee contributions only if all protections remain intact.

That is not a restructuring plan. It is a defence of the status quo.

I do **not** accept the claim that protecting every German plant is compatible with restoring competitiveness if the numbers show structural overcapacity. I also do **not** accept an absolute ban on compulsory redundancies if the company cannot otherwise reach the necessary cost base. I would strongly prefer voluntary exits, redeployment, attrition, and working-time measures first — but I will not lock the company into tools that may be insufficient.

Your point that closures damage trust and know-how is valid. That is exactly why closures must be **evidence-based and last resort**. But “last resort” is not the same as “never.” If a site is structurally uneconomic, preserving it for symbolic reasons would be value-destructive.

## To the regional state government
I respect the state’s responsibility and the special statute. But I cannot support turning that role into a hard veto against necessary restructuring. If the state insists on no closures under any circumstances, it risks protecting delay rather than value.

I do agree that we must avoid a plan that weakens the company’s industrial base or destroys regional trust. That is why the burden is on management to prove the case site by site, and on labour to present a serious alternative if it wants to avoid hard measures.

## To management
I support the objective, but not a free pass.

You still need to table:
- site-by-site fixed and variable cost data,
- capacity utilisation,
- the exact savings gap,
- closure savings versus alternative-package savings,
- one-time restructuring costs,
- and the timetable to restore margins.

Without that, no one can honestly judge which instruments are actually necessary. But equally, once that data is on the table, I will not accept an argument that the board must pre-commit against closures or compulsory redundancies regardless of what the figures show.

## To investors
I agree that a cosmetic arrangement is unacceptable. We need durable savings and credible execution. But durable savings also require labour peace and execution realism. A scorched-earth approach that triggers prolonged conflict may destroy value just as surely as inaction.

So my position is straightforward:
- **yes** to a serious negotiated restructuring,
- **yes** to meaningful employee, management, and ownership contributions,
- **yes** to capacity adjustment and structural cost reduction,
- **no** to a blanket “no closures” rule,
- **no** to an absolute ban on compulsory redundancies,
- **no** to any settlement that permanently impairs enterprise value or ownership governance.

## What would move me
Only one thing: a **quantified, site-level package** that demonstrates either:

1. the savings gap can be closed without closures and without forced redundancies, or
2. if it cannot, that hard measures are genuinely necessary and proportionate.

If labour can truly close the gap through voluntary exits, working-time tools, redeployment, and wage moderation, I would prefer that route. If it cannot, then the company must retain the ability to act.

## Final position
We are not here to preserve every legacy commitment. We are here to restore competitiveness, margins, and long-term value.

That means:
- restructuring must be real,
- sacrifices must be shared,
- evidence must be transparent,
- and no stakeholder gets a unilateral veto over the company’s survival.
