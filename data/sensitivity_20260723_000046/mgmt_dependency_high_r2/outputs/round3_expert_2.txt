From a site-level restructuring and industrial economics perspective, the expert’s conclusion is the correct one: **you cannot yet prove that closures are necessary, but you also cannot exclude them without the site economics**.

## What can be said now

### 1) Closure is the strongest cost instrument
A plant closure usually delivers the **largest recurring savings** because it removes:
- plant fixed overhead,
- indirect/site support labor,
- maintenance base cost,
- utilities minimum load,
- and often some logistics duplication.

But it also creates the **largest one-time costs**:
- severance and social-plan charges,
- pension bridge costs,
- decommissioning/remediation,
- asset write-downs,
- transfer and re-sourcing costs,
- ramp-up losses elsewhere,
- and industrial-relations disruption.

So closure is only economically justified if the **present value of recurring savings** clearly exceeds those one-time costs over a reasonable horizon.

### 2) Voluntary exits are usually cheaper to execute, but weaker structurally
Voluntary exit, early retirement, and attrition:
- reduce headcount with lower conflict,
- preserve site continuity,
- and often cost less upfront than closure.

But they usually **do not eliminate fixed site costs**.  
So if the overcapacity problem is mainly plant footprint and overhead, these measures often fall short unless paired with genuine capacity reduction.

### 3) Working-time and wage measures can close payroll gaps, not structural footprint gaps
Shorter working time, wage moderation, bonus restraint, and shift flexibility:
- cut labor cost quickly,
- are reversible,
- and often have lower execution risk.

But they mostly affect **variable labor cost**, not the full fixed-cost base.  
That means they are useful when the problem is cyclical or labor-intensity driven, but often insufficient if the company has **permanent excess capacity**.

---

## What determines whether closures are necessary
The key question is whether the company’s problem is:

### A. Mainly a fixed-cost / overcapacity problem
If so, closure or deep capacity removal may be the only way to achieve durable savings.

### B. Mainly a payroll / utilization problem
If so, a negotiated package with working-time tools, voluntary exits, redeployment, and wage moderation may be enough.

That distinction cannot be made from the narrative alone. It requires site-by-site data.

---

## The minimum data needed
To compare closure vs alternatives, management should disclose for each German site:

- output volume
- utilization rate
- fixed cost base
- variable cost per unit
- direct and indirect headcount
- product allocation and future product pipeline
- one-time closure and transfer costs
- severance / pension assumptions
- ramp-up costs at receiving plants

Then each scenario should show:
- annual recurring savings,
- one-time cost,
- payback period,
- and 3–5 year NPV.

## Practical interpretation of the current bargaining positions

### Management’s case
Plausible if:
- utilization is structurally low,
- fixed costs are high,
- and alternatives cannot close the gap.

But not proven yet.

### Labour’s case
Plausible if:
- the savings gap can be closed through negotiated instruments,
- or if closures destroy too much value through transition and conflict.

But that also needs numbers.

### Investors’ position
Economically coherent in saying a cosmetic deal is not enough.  
But “closures must be possible” is not the same as “closures are economically optimal.”

## Bottom line
At this stage, the economically defensible position is:

- **Closures may be justified if the site economics show persistent excess fixed cost and low utilization.**
- **Voluntary exits and working-time measures are preferable if the gap is mainly labor cost and temporary underutilization.**
- **A mixed package is often the best risk-adjusted outcome if it can deliver comparable recurring savings with less one-time cost and lower execution risk.**

So the dispute is not about principle; it is about **measured savings gap vs measured alternative savings**.
