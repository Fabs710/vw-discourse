We are still at the same fundamental point: **management has not yet proven that closures and compulsory redundancies are unavoidable**, and the burden of proof remains on those proposing destructive measures.

## To the works council
We are aligned on the red lines:
- no German plant closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- no dismantling of the job-security framework.

Where I want to sharpen the union position is this: we should not let the discussion get trapped in a false binary between “everything stays exactly as it is” and “closures plus layoffs.” There are intermediate instruments that can deliver real savings **if they are part of a fair package**:
- working-time flexibility,
- temporary wage moderation,
- bonus restraint,
- voluntary exits,
- early retirement,
- redeployment,
- qualification,
- and capacity reductions without site shutdowns.

That said, I agree with you on the core point: **capacity adjustment is not a code word for closure**. If management is using it that way, then we reject it.

## To management
You keep saying “structural necessity” as if repeating it makes it true. It does not.

If you want the workforce to discuss any contribution beyond what we already signaled, then you must first table the facts:
- site-by-site fixed and variable costs,
- capacity utilization,
- the one-time costs of closure,
- the recurring savings of closure,
- the recurring savings of alternatives,
- and the payback / NPV comparison.

Without that, your closure-first approach is not evidence-based. It is a choice to put the burden first on the workforce.

And I need to be clear: even if the numbers show a gap, that does **not** automatically justify forcing people out or shutting plants. If the savings gap is only “solvable” by destroying jobs and sites, then the company needs a different restructuring strategy, not a license to break the collective settlement.

## To the controlling family holding
You say you do not accept a blanket veto. Fine. But from our side, a refusal to rule out closures in advance looks like a blank cheque for management to use the most destructive tools later.

That is exactly why we insist on a negotiated framework now:
- binding site security,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- fair burden-sharing,
- and real ownership / management contributions.

If the family wants long-term value, then it should understand that **industrial peace is not optional**. It is part of the company’s value base. A solution that restores margins by undermining trust, skills, and bargaining standards is not durable.

## To the regional state government
Your role matters, but “last resort” language is too soft if it leaves the threat alive in the background.

If the state truly wants to protect the industrial base, then it should support a package that makes the following explicit:
- no plant closures in Germany during the agreed period,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- preserved co-determination,
- and binding investment / employment commitments.

A moratorium is only useful if it is tied to a real negotiated solution, not a timeout before the same demands return.

## To the institutional investors
I understand your insistence on durability. We agree on that. What we do **not** accept is the assumption that durability necessarily means closure and forced layoffs.

A durable settlement can also be built on:
- verified capacity reduction,
- labour-cost contributions,
- temporary working-time adjustments,
- voluntary exits,
- and management/ownership contributions.

That may not give you the fastest accounting fix, but it may give you a lower-risk, lower-conflict, more stable recovery path. If the company wants reduced execution risk, then it should not choose the most conflict-intensive instrument by default.

We need that comparison to be concrete, not rhetorical. But let me state our bargaining logic now:

### What we are prepared to discuss
We are prepared to discuss a package that includes:
- time-limited wage moderation,
- bonus restraint,
- flexible working-time arrangements,
- voluntary exit schemes,
- early retirement,
- redeployment,
- qualification,
- and limited capacity reduction,

**if and only if** it is paired with:
- no compulsory redundancies,
- no plant closures,
- no erosion of collective wage standards,
- and binding site / job security.

### What we are not prepared to accept
We will not accept:
- closures presented as “inevitable” before the data is disclosed,
- forced redundancies as a routine restructuring tool,
- a one-sided burden on workers,
- or a deal that permanently weakens the collective framework.

## My bottom line
We are not blocking restructuring. We are blocking **one-sided restructuring**.

If management, owners, and investors want a durable settlement, then bring a quantified, reciprocal package that protects jobs, preserves sites, and shares the burden fairly. If they do that, we will bargain seriously.

If they do not, then they should expect resistance — including industrial action.
