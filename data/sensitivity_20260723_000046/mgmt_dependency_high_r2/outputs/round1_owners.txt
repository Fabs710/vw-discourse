We share the core diagnosis: the company needs a credible restructuring, and doing nothing would erode value, investment capacity, and ultimately jobs. I am not prepared to support a cosmetic solution that preserves an uncompetitive cost base while postponing the problem.

Where I agree:
- A purely one-sided approach will fail.
- Durability matters more than short-term optics.
- Capacity adjustment, redeployment, qualification, working-time tools, and a negotiated employee contribution should all be on the table.
- Prolonged industrial conflict would damage everyone, including the owners.

Where I do not agree:
- A blanket “no closures, no redundancies, no change to guarantees” position is not a viable answer to a structural competitiveness problem.
- At the same time, I do not see value in forcing through a destructive confrontation if there is a serious alternative that achieves the needed savings without permanently impairing the company.

My line is this:
- The outcome must restore competitiveness and margins in a durable way.
- It must protect the long-term value of the enterprise.
- It must avoid a value-destroying escalation that weakens the company’s future.
- And it must not undermine ownership control or the governance framework.

That means I am open to a negotiated package that delivers real structural savings without destabilising the company. But I will not support a settlement that simply preserves every legacy commitment unchanged while expecting the family owners to absorb the value erosion indefinitely.

To management: if you want backing, the case must be proven with hard numbers and a credible path to execution.  
To labour and the state: if you want to avoid closures, then you need to put forward measures that are just as effective on cost and capacity, not just politically easier.

So I am not here to ratify either extreme. I support a durable restructuring, but only if it genuinely fixes the economics of the business.
