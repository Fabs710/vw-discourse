We are aligned on the **goal**: restore a competitive cost base and margins that can support the transition and protect shareholder value. But we are not aligned on **how much discipline** is being shown in getting there.

## Where we agree
We agree with management, the family, the state, and the experts on several points:

- The company needs a **durable restructuring**, not a cosmetic deal.
- A one-sided burden on employees would be unacceptable.
- A prolonged strike would destroy value.
- The package must be **quantified plant by plant**.
- Capacity reduction, working-time flexibility, voluntary exits, redeployment, bonus restraint, and visible management/owner contributions are all legitimate tools.
- A hybrid/non-closure solution can, in principle, capture a **large share** of closure-like savings.

That last point matters. It means the discussion should not be reduced to a binary of “closures or nothing.”

## Where we disagree

### 1) “No closures” cannot be an absolute red line
We do **not** accept the union’s position that closures should be ruled out as a baseline. That would pre-decide the economics and risk preserving a structurally uncompetitive footprint.

The expert input was clear: hybrid solutions may recover much of the savings, but **not always all of them**. That leaves a real possibility that some sites are not viable under a no-closure model. We will not support locking in losses just to avoid hard decisions.

### 2) “No compulsory redundancies” only works if the headcount bridge is real
We are open to avoiding forced layoffs **if** the numbers actually work through:
- attrition,
- voluntary exits,
- early retirement,
- redeployment,
- and genuine capacity cuts.

If those measures do not deliver enough permanent savings, then ruling out redundancies in advance is not responsible restructuring. It is deferral.

### 3) Site guarantees are not a substitute for competitiveness
We understand why the workforce and state want protection. But binding guarantees only have value if the sites remain economically viable. A guarantee that freezes an uncompetitive structure does not protect value; it destroys it later.

### 4) The company still has not shown the quantified comparison we need
Everyone keeps asking for a plant-by-plant model, and that is correct. But until that model is on the table, no one should claim that the no-closure path is definitely sufficient.

## What would move us
We would support a non-closure or hybrid settlement only if it includes all of the following:

1. **A specific savings target**
2. **Plant-by-plant capacity and cost data**
3. **A credible headcount bridge**
4. **Durable, multi-year labor-cost relief**
5. **Visible management and owner contributions**
6. **Binding investment and product commitments for retained sites**
7. **A clear timeline to margin improvement**

That is the standard for a value-preserving deal.

## What we will not support
We will not support:
- a cosmetic compromise,
- temporary concessions that fade quickly,
- a blanket no-closure rule,
- or a no-layoffs promise unsupported by numbers.

We also will not support a settlement that preserves every legacy arrangement while leaving the company structurally impaired.

## Our position
We are prepared to back a **serious hybrid restructuring** if it is economically equivalent on a durable basis. But we need proof — not rhetoric — that it gets the company back to competitive margins.

If that proof shows some sites still cannot be justified, then closures must remain available. If it shows the hybrid package is sufficient, then we can support that too. But we will not endorse a political compromise that substitutes for financial reality.
