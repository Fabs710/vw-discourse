## Independent restructuring view

Based on the record so far, the right way to assess this is **not** “closure vs no closure” in the abstract, but whether a **hybrid/no-closure package** can achieve enough of the same economic effect to be credible on a plant-by-plant basis.

### Bottom line
- A **hybrid / no-closure package can often recover roughly 60–90% of closure-led savings** in favorable cases.
- It is most plausible where the problem is mainly:
  - excess capacity,
  - high labour cost,
  - duplicated overhead,
  - and underutilization.
- It is less likely to be sufficient where a plant has:
  - structurally poor layout,
  - weak product competitiveness,
  - low future-volume allocation,
  - or major capex needs just to remain viable.

So the real decision is:

1. **Hybrid restructuring only**
2. **Hybrid plus selective site rationalization**
3. **Closure-led restructuring**

## Scenario comparison

| Scenario | Savings potential | Headcount impact | Fixed-cost relief | Speed | Risk |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---|
| No-closure hybrid | Moderate to high | Moderate | Partial | Fast start, slower full effect | Medium |
| Partial closure | High | High at selected sites | High at weakest site | Medium | Medium-high |
| Closure-led | Highest | Highest | Highest | Fastest clean end-state after shutdown | High |

## What a no-closure hybrid can realistically do
A serious no-closure package can include:
- capacity reduction inside plants,
- shift elimination,
- line consolidation,
- working-time reduction,
- voluntary exits,
- early retirement,
- redeployment,
- bonus restraint,
- management overhead cuts.

### Typical headcount bridge
Without forced redundancies, the usual bridge is:
- natural attrition,
- voluntary exits,
- early retirement,
- internal redeployment,
- working-time reductions.

In practice, that often covers **about 20–40% of the needed headcount reduction**, sometimes more if the workforce has an older age profile and there is room for redeployment.

If the needed reduction is materially larger than that, then either:
- the package needs stronger labour-cost measures, or
- some site rationalization becomes hard to avoid.

---

## Why closures still matter
Closures are different because they eliminate:
- site management overhead,
- utilities and maintenance baseline,
- local logistics costs,
- site-specific support functions,
- and the fixed cost floor tied to an underused plant.

Hybrid packages reduce these costs, but usually do **not** eliminate them. That is why hybrid solutions often deliver most — but not all — of the economics.

## Plant-by-plant decision rule
For each plant, I would test:

1. **Current utilization**
2. **Target volume after restructuring**
3. **Fixed cost per vehicle**
4. **Incremental capex needed to stay viable**
5. **Product lifecycle and future allocation**
6. **Logistics complexity**
7. **Workforce age profile and exit capacity**
8. **Ability to absorb capacity elsewhere**
9. **Break-even volume after labor concessions**
10. **Residual cost gap versus target**

### Interpretation
- If the plant is still viable after internal restructuring, a hybrid package is preferable.
- If the plant remains structurally above target on fixed cost or requires major capex without a realistic future volume, then **selective closure becomes economically defensible**.

---

## Sequencing recommendation
From a restructuring perspective, the sequence should be:

1. **Quantify plant-by-plant economics**
2. **Test the hybrid package first**
3. **Measure residual gap versus target**
4. **Use selective closure only for the plants that remain structurally uncompetitive**

That is the most decision-useful framework.

---

## My conclusion
At this stage, I would **not** assume closures are necessary everywhere.  
But I also would **not** assume a no-closure package will fully solve the problem.

The correct conclusion is:

> **A hybrid package is the presumptive first test, but selective closure remains justified if one or more plants still cannot reach competitiveness after internal restructuring.**
