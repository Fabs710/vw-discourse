I will be very clear.

I agree with everyone on one basic point: this company needs a **durable restructuring**, not a cosmetic delay. And I agree that the burden cannot fall only on the workforce. Management, the controlling family, and shareholders must all contribute visibly if they want legitimacy for any settlement.

But I do **not** accept the idea that plant closures and compulsory redundancies remain routine “tools” to be kept available as a first-line option. For us, as the state and anchor shareholder, those are not neutral technical measures. They carry direct, long-term damage to:
- regional employment,
- supplier networks,
- municipal finances,
- and the company’s role as a regional economic anchor.

That is not acceptable as a default logic.

## Where I agree
I agree with the expert and with parts of the other stakeholder positions that:

- **capacity reduction is necessary**
- **working-time changes and bonus restraint can be part of the answer**
- **voluntary exits, early retirement, and redeployment should be used first**
- **management overhead must come down too**
- **a quantified savings bridge is essential**
- **future investment and product allocation must be tied to any deal**

That is the correct basis for a serious restructuring.

## Where I disagree
### 1) Closures are not the “credible default”
Management keeps saying closures must remain available if the numbers demand it. I understand that argument, but I reject it as the starting point.

The expert has now made clear that non-closure or hybrid solutions can deliver a **large share** of closure-like savings, and sometimes most of them, if the package is serious enough. That means the burden is on management to prove that a non-closure package is insufficient — not on the workforce or the state to prove the opposite before any real negotiation even starts.

So I will not support a restructuring framework that begins with plant closure as a legitimate pressure instrument.

### 2) Compulsory redundancies are not the easy fallback
Same logic here. If jobs must be reduced, then the sequence must be:
- natural attrition,
- voluntary departures,
- early retirement,
- internal redeployment,
- reduced hours,
- and negotiated burden-sharing.

Forced layoffs are the last resort, not the preferred route. If management wants them available, it must first present a concrete headcount bridge showing why those alternatives are not enough. Until then, I do not accept them.

### 3) Site guarantees are not “anti-competitiveness”
I also reject the framing that site protection is somehow incompatible with competitiveness by definition. That depends entirely on the package.

If you want labor to contribute, then labor needs something in return:
- binding investment commitments,
- product allocation,
- a credible role for the German sites,
- and a time horizon that makes sacrifices rational.

A site guarantee is not a fantasy if it is backed by real industrial planning.

## My line on the state’s role
The state’s co-determination and veto role is not decorative. It exists precisely to prevent economically and socially destructive decisions from being forced through without regard for the region.

So let me state this plainly:  
**I will not support any settlement that sidelines the state, weakens our statutory role, or turns our blocking minority into a formality.**

That is a hard limit.

## What I would support
A deal could gain my support if it includes all of the following:

1. **No plant closures unless a transparent, independent review proves no viable alternative**
2. **No compulsory redundancies as a preferred restructuring tool**
3. **A quantified restructuring package**
   - savings target,
   - capacity plan,
   - headcount bridge,
   - timing,
   - and plant-by-plant effects
4. **Visible management and owner contributions**
5. **Binding investment and product commitments for retained German sites**
6. **Preservation of co-determination and the state’s veto/blocking rights**
7. **A real social-partnership solution, not just a cost-cutting exercise**

## To management
You are right that rhetoric will not solve this. But you have not yet shown that closure threats are necessary. Before anyone contemplates closing a German plant, I expect:
- plant-level cost data,
- capacity data,
- a real comparison of non-closure versus closure savings,
- and a serious assessment of what can be achieved through internal restructuring.

If a hybrid package can get the company to the target, then it is the responsible route.

## To the family and investors
You are right that we cannot freeze the company’s footprint forever. I do not ask for that. What I ask for is a solution that preserves viable sites and avoids irreversible damage unless there is truly no alternative.

The expert has said that non-closure restructuring is operationally feasible. That means no one gets to pretend closure is the only serious option.

## To the union and works council
Your insistence on protecting jobs and sites is legitimate, and I support that. But protection cannot mean no adjustment at all. There will have to be real contributions from the workforce if a settlement is to be durable.

What I need from you is not a promise to absorb everything, but a credible package that proves:
- capacity can be reduced without plant shutdowns,
- labor costs can be adjusted fairly,
- and the sites can remain viable.

That is the path I am prepared to back.

## My bottom line
I am prepared to support a **hybrid, site-preserving restructuring** if it:
- delivers durable savings,
- reduces capacity meaningfully,
- avoids forced redundancies,
- avoids unnecessary plant closures,
- protects the regional industrial base,
- and maintains the state’s co-determination and veto role.

I am **not** prepared to support:
- closures as a default bargaining threat,
- mass compulsory layoffs,
- or a settlement that sacrifices our role as anchor shareholder and regional protector.

If management wants our support, it must bring forward a serious non-closure package with numbers, not slogans. If that package is genuinely sufficient, then I will back it. If not, then it is management’s task to explain why it failed — not for the state to surrender in advance.
