I will be direct.

The expert input confirms what we have been saying from the beginning: **a non-closure solution is operationally feasible** if management is willing to do the hard work of capacity reduction, internal reallocation, working-time changes, and genuine burden-sharing. That is precisely why I reject the constant insistence that closures must remain “on the table” as a first resort.

## Where I agree
I agree with the union, the state government, and even the expert on the following points:

- The company needs a **durable** restructuring, not a cosmetic patch.
- A **one-sided burden on the workforce** would be unacceptable.
- The package must include **visible contributions from management and owners**.
- **Future investment and product allocation** are necessary if German sites are to remain viable.
- A prolonged strike would be damaging — but let me be clear, responsibility for that lies with management if it continues to threaten the workforce with closures and forced layoffs.

## Where I strongly disagree

### 1) Plant closures are not an acceptable bargaining tool
Management, the family, and investors keep speaking as if closures are a normal efficiency instrument. They are not. A closure is a permanent destruction of industrial capacity, jobs, skills, and regional stability. Once a plant is shut, that damage does not come back with a quarterly improvement in margins.

The expert did **not** say closures are necessary. The expert said non-closure alternatives can recover a large share of the savings if they are serious enough. That is a very different statement.

So I reject the claim that “closure must stay on the table” as a default position. For us, closure is not the starting point, not the bargaining chip, and not the preferred adjustment tool.

### 2) Compulsory redundancies are not acceptable
I am not prepared to treat forced layoffs as an ordinary restructuring measure either. The workforce should not be made to pay for strategic errors through involuntary job loss.

If there is a real need to reduce headcount, then the sequence must be:
- natural attrition,
- voluntary exits,
- early retirement,
- internal transfers,
- working-time reductions,
- and other negotiated solutions.

Compulsory redundancies are the line we will not cross.

### 3) “Equivalence” must be proven by management, not assumed by shareholders
Management and shareholders keep saying that non-closure measures must be “equivalent” to closures. Fine. Then show the numbers.

If you want to argue that closing plants is necessary, you must demonstrate:
- the exact savings target,
- the gap by site,
- the cost of alternative measures,
- the headcount bridge,
- and the investment consequences.

Until then, closure threats are pressure tactics, not proof.

## On the expert’s findings
The plant-level restructuring assessment actually strengthens our position.

It says:
- non-closure restructuring can be **medium to high** in savings,
- hybrid solutions are often the most realistic,
- and capacity can be reduced without shutting plants.

That means there is a credible path that preserves sites and avoids forced redundancies. That is the path we support.

What the expert also makes clear is that **capacity reduction** is necessary. We are not denying that. But capacity reduction is not the same as plant closure. It can be done through:
- fewer shifts,
- line consolidation,
- lower utilization,
- product reallocation,
- and internal simplification.

That is the route we are ready to negotiate. Not closure.

## To management
Your position still sounds like this: “accept our framework, or we reserve the right to shut plants.” That is not a negotiation; that is coercion.

If you want a credible deal, then put forward a real non-closure package with:
- binding site guarantees to 2030,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- capacity reduction through internal measures,
- a fair contribution from management and owners,
- and real investment and product commitments.

Do that, and we can talk substance. Continue to threaten closures, and you will get resistance.

## To the controlling family and investors
You both keep saying the company cannot become a “permanent employment guarantee scheme.” That is a false framing.

Nobody is asking for a guarantee of immobility. We are asking for:
- viable sites,
- protected jobs,
- fair burden-sharing,
- and a restructuring that does not destroy the company’s industrial base.

That is not sentimentality. It is responsible industrial governance.

The expert has shown that closures are not the only way to achieve meaningful savings. So stop treating them as if they were unavoidable truth.

## To the state government
Your support for a site-preserving solution is important, and I expect you to use your blocking and supervisory-board rights accordingly. But let me be equally clear: we do not need rhetorical backing — we need you to hold the line against any attempt to force closures or compulsory layoffs through the back door.

If the state means what it says, it should insist on:
- transparent plant-by-plant disclosure,
- independent verification of alternatives,
- and a negotiated package that secures German sites.

## On what we are prepared to discuss
We are ready to negotiate serious measures, including:
- working-time adjustments,
- bonus restraint,
- voluntary exit programs,
- internal redeployment,
- capacity reductions,
- and a temporary workforce contribution.

But those measures must come with the following non-negotiables:
1. **No plant closures**
2. **No compulsory redundancies**
3. **Binding protection for German sites**
4. **Preservation of co-determination and job-security standards**
5. **Visible contributions from management, owners, and the company**
6. **A credible industrial future through 2030**

## Bottom line
A **hybrid restructuring** without closures is the only acceptable route. The expert says it is feasible if done properly, and that is the basis for negotiation.

What I will not accept is:
- closures dressed up as inevitability,
- compulsory redundancies presented as efficiency,
- or a restructuring that sacrifices workers and sites while sparing management and ownership from real discipline.

If management wants agreement, it must stop threatening the workforce and start building a genuine non-closure plan. Otherwise, it should expect us to use every lever we have — including our supervisory-board rights and industrial action — to defend the plants and the people who work in them.
