I appreciate that the discussion has moved from slogans to actual restructuring logic. The expert’s intervention is helpful, and I agree with one central point: **a non-closure path is operationally possible, but only if it is genuinely substantial, quantified, and durable**.

That said, I want to be equally clear about where I stand.

## What I agree with
I agree with the union, the works council, and the expert that:
- a one-sided burden on the workforce would be unacceptable,
- a prolonged strike would damage the company and the region,
- and any viable package must include **capacity reduction, working-time adjustments, voluntary exits, redeployment, and visible contributions from management and owners**.

I also agree that the discussion must be based on **numbers**, not just threats. If management believes there is structural overcapacity, then it should put the actual plant-by-plant and cost bridge on the table.

## Where I disagree
### 1) I do not accept plant closures as a routine restructuring tool
For the regional state, plant closures in Germany are not just another variable. They have direct consequences for:
- regional employment,
- suppliers,
- municipal finances,
- and the company’s role as an industrial anchor.

So I do **not** accept the framing that closures are simply the default “credible” option if the package is difficult. They must remain a **last resort**, not a bargaining cudgel.

### 2) I do not accept forced redundancies as the easy answer
The same applies to compulsory layoffs. If management wants our support, it must first exhaust:
- natural attrition,
- voluntary exits,
- early retirement,
- internal transfers,
- working-time reduction,
- and other negotiated measures.

If those measures are not enough, then management has a case to make. But it has not yet made that case convincingly.

### 3) I do not accept a solution that weakens co-determination or sidelines the state
Our role is not decorative. Under the special statute, we have real rights, and we will use them. Any attempt to force through a restructuring that strips away the state’s influence or bypasses co-determination would be a direct problem for us.

## What would be acceptable
A settlement could gain our support if it contains all of the following:

1. **No plant closures unless an independent, transparent review shows no viable alternative**
2. **No compulsory redundancies as the preferred instrument**
3. **A quantified restructuring package**
   - with a savings target,
   - a capacity plan,
   - and a headcount bridge
4. **Visible contributions from management and shareholders**
5. **Binding investment and product commitments** for the sites that remain
6. **Preservation of co-determination and the state’s blocking role**

## My position on the expert’s assessment
The expert did **not** say that closures are necessary. It said they are powerful, but that non-closure alternatives can recover a large share of the savings if the package is serious enough. That is exactly why I am pushing for that route.

The burden is now on management and the family to show that the alternative package cannot work — not on workers or the state to prove a negative before any real proposal is even built.

## To the union and works council
Your insistence on job security and site security is legitimate. I support that. But you also need to be ready for a package that includes real sacrifices, not only on management’s side. If the company is to remain competitive, then a negotiated contribution from labor will be part of the answer.

What I will **not** support is a deal that turns “no closures” into “no adjustment.” That would not protect the sites in the long run; it would only delay the problem.

## To management and the family
If you want my support, come forward with a package that is:
- non-closure first,
- quantitatively credible,
- and socially balanced.

Do **not** come with a preset closure plan and call it inevitability. And do not assume the state will simply accept being sidelined because the numbers are uncomfortable. We will not.

## My bottom line
I am prepared to support a **serious hybrid restructuring** if it:
- preserves German sites where possible,
- avoids forced redundancies,
- produces durable savings,
- protects the company’s long-term health,
- and maintains the state’s co-determination and veto role.

I am **not** prepared to support:
- plant closures as the first resort,
- mass compulsory layoffs,
- or a restructuring that sacrifices the regional economic anchor to solve a short-term balance-sheet problem.

If management wants to keep closures on the table, then it must prove they are truly unavoidable. Until then, the correct path is a negotiated, site-preserving solution.
