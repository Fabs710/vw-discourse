## Independent plant-level restructuring assessment

Based on the facts given and the expert input already on the table, the decision now turns on one question:

**Can a quantified no-closure or hybrid package deliver enough durable fixed-cost relief and capacity reduction to substitute for closure-led restructuring?**

### My answer
**Yes, in principle for some plants; not necessarily for all.**  
Operationally, a serious hybrid package can often capture a large share of closure economics, but not always enough to justify keeping every plant open.

---

## 1) Core restructuring logic

At plant level, closures and non-closure options differ mainly in how completely they remove cost:

### Closure-led package
Typically removes:
- direct plant labor
- indirect labor and site management
- maintenance and utilities baseline
- local overhead
- footprint duplication
- part of logistics complexity

It also gives the strongest and cleanest fixed-cost reset, but with:
- shutdown cost
- asset write-downs
- transfer/disruption risk
- supplier and ramp-up risk at receiving plants

### Hybrid / no-closure package
Can still remove a large share of the economics through:
- shift reduction
- line consolidation
- capacity rationalization
- voluntary exits / early retirement
- working-time reduction
- redeployment
- overhead cuts
- product allocation changes

But it usually leaves:
- residual site overhead
- some idle capacity
- more complex execution
- and a weaker permanent fixed-cost reset than full closure

---

## 2) Practical comparability: what “equivalent” should mean

In restructuring terms, “equivalent” should not mean equal headlines or equal job cuts. It should mean comparable:

- **durable EBITDA / EBIT improvement**
- **permanent fixed-cost relief**
- **capacity alignment to demand**
- **implementation certainty**
- **timing of savings**

If a non-closure package only creates temporary savings, it is not equivalent.

---

## 3) Plant-level decision rule

A plant should remain open under a hybrid model only if the package can deliver, on a durable basis:

1. **Most of the closure economics**
   - roughly 70% or more of closure-like fixed-cost relief is a reasonable operational threshold
   - lower may still work if the site has strong strategic value or future product allocation

2. **A credible headcount bridge**
   - attrition, voluntary exits, early retirement, redeployment, and working-time reduction must collectively cover the reduction need

3. **Meaningful capacity reduction**
   - not just labor concessions; actual output/shift/line simplification must occur

4. **Future product and investment support**
   - otherwise the site becomes a cost sink with no strategic runway

---

## 4) Plant-by-plant implications

Without plant-specific data, I cannot rank sites definitively. But the plant types usually fall into three buckets:

| Plant condition | Closures vs hybrid | Operational view |
|---|---|---|
| Modern asset, excess shifts, high labor cost | Hybrid often feasible | Good candidate for non-closure restructuring |
| Medium-strength site, some duplication, moderate underutilization | Hybrid possible but must be strong | Needs deep capacity cut and overhead reset |
| Weak legacy site, low volume, poor layout, high maintenance burden | Closure more likely | Hybrid may not remove enough fixed cost |

---

## 5) Headcount bridge: what has to be true

A non-closure package only works if headcount can come down without forced layoffs, or with very limited forced layoffs. The usual bridge is:

- natural attrition
- early retirement
- voluntary exits
- redeployment to growth areas
- working-time reduction
- selective reskilling

The risk is that these instruments often look larger on paper than they are in execution. So a serious plan must be measured against:

- expected annual attrition
- likely take-up of voluntary programs
- internal absorption capacity
- timing of savings realization

If the required reduction is too large for the workforce age profile and internal redeployment capacity, then a no-closure plan will not be operationally sufficient.

---

## 6) Fixed-cost relief: where closures still win

Closures are strongest when the problem is:
- structurally low utilization
- excessive duplication
- poor site economics
- obsolete footprint
- weak product future

In those cases, even a good hybrid package may leave too much residual cost behind.

Hybrid packages work best when the main issue is:
- too many shifts
- too much overhead
- excess labor cost relative to volume
- overlapping functions that can be centralized

---

## 7) What I would conclude from the current record

### Strongly support a hybrid / no-closure path if:
- the plants are operationally decent
- the problem is primarily excess capacity and labor cost
- product allocation can be secured
- the workforce contribution is deep enough and durable
- the headcount bridge is credible

### Support closure if:
- a site is structurally uncompetitive even after deep internal restructuring
- the residual cost base would still miss the target materially
- the site cannot be given a viable product future
- the headcount reduction cannot be achieved without repeated temporary fixes

---

## 8) Decision-useful bottom line

### My restructuring judgment
- **Non-closure restructuring is operationally possible and often preferable where viable.**
- **It can often recover a substantial share of closure savings, sometimes most of them.**
- **But it is not automatically equivalent to closure.**
- **Where a plant’s structural cost position is weak, closure remains the cleaner and more reliable fixed-cost solution.**

A plant-by-plant model showing:

Without that, no one can honestly claim that every site should stay open, nor that closure is required everywhere.
