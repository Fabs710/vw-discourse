We are at the point where the real dividing line is obvious:

**We are not accepting plant closures or compulsory redundancies as the starting point, the default tool, or the bargaining threat.**  
And we are certainly not accepting a settlement that trades away co-determination and job security in exchange for vague promises about competitiveness.

## Where I agree
I agree with the expert and with the parts of the other positions that are serious:

- The company needs a **durable restructuring**.
- A temporary patch is not enough.
- **Capacity reduction, working-time changes, voluntary exits, redeployment, bonus restraint, and management/owner contributions** are legitimate tools.
- The discussion must be **quantified and plant-specific**.
- A prolonged industrial conflict would hurt everyone.

So yes: there must be a real package.  
No: that does not mean we accept closures and layoffs as the baseline.

## Where I disagree

### 1) “Closures remain available” is not neutral language
To management, investors, and the family: repeating that closures must remain available is not an analysis. It is pressure.

The expert input did **not** say plant closures are required. It said:
- non-closure and hybrid solutions can recover a **large share** of closure-like savings,
- often **60–90%** if the package is serious,
- and only some sites may truly be so weak that closure becomes unavoidable.

That is a very different conclusion from what you keep implying.

So no, I do not accept closures as a legitimate first-line instrument.  
And I do not accept them being used to force concessions from workers before the numbers are even shown.

### 2) Compulsory redundancies are not an acceptable fallback
If headcount has to fall, the sequence is clear:

- natural attrition,
- voluntary exits,
- early retirement,
- internal redeployment,
- working-time reduction,
- negotiated burden-sharing.

Compulsory redundancies are not a routine restructuring tool. They are a break-glass measure. If management wants to invoke them, it must first prove that the non-coercive route is insufficient in concrete numbers.

Until then, I reject the normalisation of forced layoffs.

### 3) Site guarantees are not “anti-competitiveness”
I reject the argument that binding site protection somehow destroys competitiveness by definition.

That is only true if management refuses to do the actual restructuring work.  
A serious package can combine:
- internal capacity reduction,
- labour-cost contribution,
- management and owner contributions,
- investment commitments,
- and future product allocation.

That is what makes sacrifices acceptable. Without that, employees are asked to pay for a future they may never see.

### 4) “Equivalence” cannot mean worker sacrifice only
Some of you talk about “equivalence” as if it simply means enough pain has been imposed somewhere. That is not acceptable.

For us, equivalence means:
- no plant closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- binding protection for German sites,
- preservation of co-determination,
- and a fair sharing of burdens.

If your definition of equivalence removes those protections, then it is not equivalence. It is a disguised unilateral cutback.

## To management
You say you need durable competitiveness. Fine. Then show the numbers plant by plant.

Not slogans. Not threats. Numbers.

We need:
- current and target capacity by site,
- fixed-cost structure,
- savings from shift reduction and line consolidation,
- overhead savings,
- the labour share,
- the management/owner share,
- and the investment and product commitments tied to each retained site.

If a non-closure package is not enough, prove it.  
But do not ask us to pre-approve closures before that proof exists.

## To the controlling family and investors
You speak as if any refusal to close plants is emotional or unrealistic. It is not.

The expert has made clear that hybrid solutions can capture a substantial share of the economics. That means the question is not whether there is *any* alternative. The question is whether management and owners are willing to do the hard work of making that alternative credible.

And let’s be clear: if your idea of discipline is simply to close plants and cut jobs faster, then you are solving a cost issue by destroying industrial capability. That is not stewardship. That is short-termism.

## To the state government
Your role is not to accompany plant closures politely. Your role is to prevent economically and socially destructive decisions being rammed through.

So I support you only if you use your influence to insist on:
- transparent plant-by-plant disclosure,
- independent verification of alternatives,
- and a real non-closure package before any closure discussion is entertained.

Saying closures are a last resort is not enough if the company is allowed to treat them as a negotiating weapon today.

## What we are prepared to discuss
We are prepared to discuss:
- working-time reductions,
- bonus restraint,
- voluntary exit programmes,
- early retirement,
- redeployment,
- temporary labour-cost relief,
- line and shift rationalisation,
- and deeper management/owner contributions.

But those discussions happen **within** these red lines:

1. **No plant closures**
2. **No compulsory redundancies**
3. **Binding protection for German sites**
4. **Preservation of co-determination and the job-security framework**
5. **Real investment and product commitments**

That is the minimum for a legitimate settlement.

## What will not move us
What will not move us is:
- vague talk about competitiveness,
- closure threats dressed up as realism,
- a “trust us first, security later” logic,
- or a package that preserves shareholder flexibility while loading all the risk onto workers.

## My bottom line
We are ready for a **serious hybrid restructuring** — but only one that keeps every German plant open, avoids compulsory redundancies, preserves co-determination, and shares the burden fairly.

If management wants our cooperation, bring forward a quantified plant-by-plant plan that proves this is possible.  
If it cannot do that, then it should not be surprised by resistance.
