We are at the point where everyone is hiding behind the word “equivalent” while refusing to define it. So let me be just as direct: **we are not negotiating plant closures or compulsory redundancies as a baseline outcome.** If management wants those tools, it must prove that no serious alternative exists — and it has not done that.

## Where I agree
I agree with the expert, the state, and even parts of what management and the owners have said:

- The company needs a **durable** restructuring.
- A one-sided burden on the workforce would be unacceptable.
- A temporary patch is not enough.
- **Capacity reduction, working-time changes, bonus restraint, voluntary exits, redeployment, and management/owner contributions** are all legitimate parts of a package.
- A **quantified** settlement is necessary.

That is all correct. And it is exactly why we have been open to a negotiated contribution from labor.

## Where I disagree — and strongly
### 1) Closures are not the default “credible” option
Management keeps speaking as if closures are a neutral restructuring lever. They are not. They are permanent destruction of jobs, skills, supplier networks, and regional industrial capacity.

The expert did **not** say closures are unavoidable. The expert said non-closure and hybrid solutions can recover a large share of the savings if they are serious enough. That is the key point. It means closures are **not** the starting assumption.

So when management says “closures must remain available,” what it really means is: “we reserve the right to break the social compact if bargaining gets difficult.” We reject that logic.

### 2) Compulsory redundancies are not an efficiency instrument
I am not prepared to normalize forced layoffs as a restructuring tool.

If headcount has to come down, then the sequence must be:
- natural attrition,
- voluntary exits,
- early retirement,
- internal redeployment,
- working-time reduction,
- and negotiated temporary contributions.

Compulsory redundancies are the last line, not the preferred route. If management wants them on the table, it must first show that these other measures are insufficient in concrete numbers.

### 3) “Site guarantees” are not the enemy
I reject the claim that binding site security is incompatible with competitiveness. That depends entirely on the package.

If management wants us to accept sacrifices, then it must provide:
- **real product allocation**,
- **real investment commitments**,
- **real capacity reductions through internal measures**,
- and a **real headcount bridge** that avoids forced layoffs.

A guarantee is not a slogan. It is the condition that makes worker contributions acceptable. Without it, employees are asked to pay for a restructuring whose benefits they may never see.

## To management
You keep asking for a package that is “durable enough to replace closure economics.” Fine. Then put the numbers on the table.

We want, plant by plant:
- current and target capacity,
- fixed-cost structure,
- headcount by function,
- potential savings from shift reduction, line consolidation, and overhead cuts,
- the share that must come from labor,
- the share management will take,
- the share owners will take,
- and the investment plan tied to each site.

Until that exists, “closures remain available” is not analysis — it is pressure.

## To the controlling family and investors
You both speak as if protecting the footprint automatically destroys value. That is too simplistic.

The expert’s assessment says something more careful:
- non-closure packages can often deliver **60–90%** of closure-like savings where labor and utilization are the main issues;
- hybrid solutions are often the most realistic;
- closures are strongest only where a site is truly structurally weak.

That means you do **not** get to demand plant closures just because they are the cleanest solution on a spreadsheet. The workforce is entitled to insist on a solution that avoids unnecessary destruction where alternatives are viable.

## To the state government
Your position is right: closures must be a last resort, not a bargaining weapon. But that position has to be enforced, not merely stated.

If you want to use your influence responsibly, then insist on:
- transparent plant-by-plant disclosure,
- independent verification of alternatives,
- and a negotiated non-closure package before any closure discussion is even entertained.

## Our actual position
We are prepared to negotiate a serious package with these elements:

1. **No compulsory redundancies**
2. **No plant closures as a baseline**
3. **Capacity reduction through internal measures**
4. **Working-time and bonus restraint**
5. **Voluntary exits and early retirement**
6. **Visible management and owner contributions**
7. **Binding investment and product commitments**
8. **A durable collective agreement with clear duration and safeguards**

That is not a refusal to adjust. That is a refusal to let the workforce absorb the full social cost while others preserve optionality.

## What would move us
We would be prepared to discuss deeper employee contributions if management and owners commit to all of the following:

- quantified savings target,
- binding site and investment guarantees,
- no forced layoffs,
- redeployment first,
- a fair split of sacrifices,
- and a clear industrial future for the remaining German sites.

If those conditions are met, we can negotiate real labor contributions. We have never said “no adjustment.” We have said: **no one-sided adjustment, no closures by default, no compulsory redundancies.**

## What will not move us
What will not move us is:
- vague talk about competitiveness,
- claims that closures are unavoidable without proof,
- threats dressed up as realism,
- or a package that preserves shareholder comfort while pushing the social risk onto workers.

So this is the line:
**We are ready for a real restructuring package — but it must preserve jobs, rule out forced layoffs, avoid closures where possible, and share the burden fairly.**

If management comes with a quantified non-closure or hybrid plan, we will engage seriously. If it comes with closure threats and calls that “responsible restructuring,” it should expect resistance, not consent.
