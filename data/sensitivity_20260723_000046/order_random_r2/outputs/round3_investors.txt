We are prepared to support a **serious, quantified restructuring**. We are **not** prepared to support a settlement that preserves every site and guarantee while leaving the company structurally uncompetitive.

## Where we agree
We agree with management, the family, the state, the union, and the works council on the basics:

- the company needs a **durable** reset, not a cosmetic fix;
- a one-sided burden on employees would be unacceptable;
- a prolonged strike would destroy value;
- capacity reduction, working-time changes, voluntary exits, redeployment, bonus restraint, and management/owner contributions are all legitimate tools;
- the discussion has to be **numbers-based**, not slogan-based.

We also agree with the experts that **non-closure or hybrid solutions can recover a large share of the savings** if the package is serious enough. That matters.

## Where we disagree
### 1) “No closures” cannot be a blanket rule
We do **not** support an absolute no-closures position. If the company cannot get back to competitive margins and a sustainable cost base through other means, then closures have to remain available.

That is not ideological. It is basic capital discipline. Protecting every footprint regardless of economics would simply defer the problem and impair returns.

### 2) “No compulsory redundancies” only works if the alternative is real
We are open to avoiding forced layoffs, but only if there is a credible headcount bridge through:
- attrition,
- voluntary exits,
- early retirement,
- internal redeployment,
- working-time reduction,
- and enough capacity reduction to make the numbers work.

If that bridge is too weak, then ruling out compulsory redundancies in advance is just refusing to do the restructuring.

### 3) Site guarantees are not a substitute for competitiveness
Binding site promises are useful only if the retained sites are viable. A guarantee that freezes an uncompetitive structure does not protect shareholder value; it destroys it later.

## Our reading of the expert input
The expert testimony does **not** support the view that closures are unnecessary. It says:
- non-closure measures can recover a substantial share of the effect;
- hybrid packages are often the most realistic;
- but closures remain the strongest tool where structural weakness is severe.

That is the correct framework.

So the real test is not “closure or no closure” in the abstract. The test is:

**Can a quantified non-closure or hybrid package deliver durable savings, lower capacity, and enough headcount reduction to restore margins?**

If yes, we can support it.  
If no, then the stronger restructuring tools must stay on the table.

## What would move us
We would support a non-closure settlement if it includes all of the following:

1. a **specific savings target**
2. a **plant-by-plant capacity plan**
3. a **credible headcount bridge**
4. **multi-year labor-cost relief**
5. visible **management and owner contributions**
6. a clear **timeline to margin improvement**
7. deal certainty, without open-ended conflict

That is the standard we will use.

## What we will not support
We will not support:
- a cosmetic compromise,
- temporary concessions that fade quickly,
- preservation of all legacy arrangements,
- or a deal that protects sites while margins stay structurally impaired.

We also will not support a package that relies on prolonged industrial conflict to avoid hard decisions. That would destroy value.

## Our position
So our position is straightforward:

- **Yes** to a hybrid restructuring if it is economically equivalent to closure on a durable basis.
- **No** to an absolute no-closures rule.
- **No** to a no-layoffs promise unless the savings are real and quantified.
- **Yes** to a negotiated solution, but only one that restores competitiveness and protects shareholder value.

If management and labour can put forward a package that genuinely replaces closure economics, we will back it. If not, then closures and other hard tools must remain available.
