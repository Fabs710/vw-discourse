I agree with the broad diagnosis, but not with the way some of you are trying to turn “competitiveness” into a license for irreversible damage.

## Where I agree
I agree that:
- the company needs a **durable restructuring**;
- a one-sided burden on the workforce would be unacceptable;
- this cannot be solved by slogans or delay;
- the package must be **quantified plant by plant**;
- capacity reduction, working-time changes, voluntary exits, redeployment, bonus restraint, and management/owner contributions are all legitimate instruments;
- a prolonged strike would hurt the company, the workforce, and the region.

That is the shared ground.

## Where I disagree
### 1) Plant closures must not be treated as the default tool
To management, investors, and the family: I do **not** accept closures as a routine bargaining lever. They are not a neutral efficiency measure. In a regionally anchored company like this one, they mean permanent damage to jobs, suppliers, skills, and municipal stability.

The expert input was clear that **non-closure or hybrid solutions can recover a substantial share of closure-like savings**, often enough to make them serious alternatives. That means closures are **not** the starting point; they are, at most, a last-resort outcome after proof of necessity.

### 2) Compulsory redundancies cannot be your preferred fallback
I also do **not** accept forced layoffs as a normal restructuring instrument. If headcount must come down, the sequence has to be:
- natural attrition,
- voluntary exits,
- early retirement,
- internal redeployment,
- working-time reduction,
- and negotiated contributions.

If those prove insufficient, then you can argue about the next step. But until then, compulsory redundancies are not “prudence”; they are a political and social choice I will resist.

### 3) Site guarantees are not incompatible with competitiveness
To those saying site protection freezes an uncompetitive structure: that is too simplistic. A binding site commitment is meaningful when it is paired with:
- real capacity reduction,
- real labor contributions,
- visible management and owner contributions,
- and real product/investment allocation.

That is the point of a social partnership settlement: sacrifices in exchange for a future.

## What I need to see
I support the call for a plant-by-plant comparison, but it must answer the right question: not “what is the cleanest closure option,” but **whether a serious non-closure or hybrid package can match the restructuring effect well enough to preserve viable sites and jobs**.

So I want the comparison to show, for each plant:
- current and target capacity,
- fixed-cost structure,
- savings from shift reduction, line consolidation, overhead cuts, and working-time changes,
- the headcount bridge through attrition, voluntary exits, early retirement, and redeployment,
- residual cost after hybrid measures,
- closure savings for comparison,
- timing and implementation risk,
- and the investment/product commitments needed to keep the site viable.

[SUMMON: Independent plant-by-plant restructuring modeller to compare no-closure, hybrid, and closure-led scenarios, including savings by site, headcount bridge, fixed-cost relief, timing, and site viability.]

## To management
You say you need “durable competitiveness by 2030.” Fine. Then show me a package that gets there **without using closures as the first pressure instrument**.

If your model proves that a plant cannot be made viable even with deep internal restructuring, then bring that proof. But do not ask me to endorse closures before that case is made. I will not do that.

## To the controlling family and investors
I hear your concern about value destruction. I share it. But value destruction is not only created by keeping sites open. It is also created by:
- destroying trust,
- hollowing out regional legitimacy,
- forcing conflict,
- and breaking the social compact that makes this company governable.

A hybrid solution that preserves viable sites, reduces capacity, and shares the burden fairly can protect value better than a closure-first strategy that triggers permanent industrial and political fallout.

## To the union and works council
Your insistence that workers should not carry this alone is right. I support that. But I will also be candid: protection cannot mean no adjustment whatsoever.

If we want to preserve jobs and sites, the workforce contribution must be real:
- flexible working time,
- bonus restraint,
- voluntary exits,
- redeployment,
- and multi-year cost relief.

That is the price of a durable settlement. What I will not support is a package that says “no closures, no redundancies, no sacrifice” and expects the company to magically recover. That would not be credible.

## My line as anchor shareholder and political actor
I will back a **serious hybrid restructuring** if it:
1. preserves viable German sites,
2. avoids forced redundancies as far as possible,
3. delivers durable savings,
4. includes visible management and owner contributions,
5. ties sacrifices to binding industrial commitments,
6. and respects co-determination and our special statutory role.

I will **not** support:
- a blanket closure-led reset,
- a strategy that treats regional employment as expendable,
- or any settlement that sidelines the state’s blocking minority and board role.

## Bottom line
I am open to a hard restructuring. I am not open to a restructuring that sacrifices the region to solve management’s credibility problem.

If a hybrid or no-closure package can truly deliver the required savings and capacity adjustment, then that is the responsible path. If it cannot, then management must explain why — but it should not expect me to pre-approve closures as the first answer.

The next step must be the independent plant-by-plant comparison. Until then, no one should pretend the closure case has already been proven.
