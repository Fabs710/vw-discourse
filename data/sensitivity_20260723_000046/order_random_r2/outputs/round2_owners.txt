The expert’s input is useful, and it changes the discussion in one important way: **non-closure alternatives are not imaginary.** They can deliver a meaningful share of the required adjustment. That matters.

But I do **not** read the expert’s assessment as support for the union’s current position. It says clearly that a non-closure solution only works if it includes **material capacity reduction, multi-year labor concessions, voluntary exits, redeployment, and a quantified restructuring bridge**. In other words: no closures may be possible, but **not at zero pain and not at zero structural change**.

## Where I agree
I agree with the union, works council, and state government on three points:

1. **A one-sided burden on employees would be unacceptable.**
   - Management and owners must contribute visibly.
   - A deal that simply shifts all pain onto labor would be short-sighted and destabilising.

2. **A prolonged strike would destroy value.**
   - That would hurt workers, the company, and the owners.
   - I do not want to see this become a public breakdown.

3. **Future investment and product allocation matter.**
   - If German sites are to remain part of the industrial base, they need a credible role.

## Where I disagree
### 1) “No plant closures” cannot be an absolute moral precondition
The union and works council keep repeating that closures are unacceptable as a matter of principle. I understand the political and human logic, but from an ownership perspective I cannot endorse a blanket rule that prevents the company from taking the measures needed to restore competitiveness.

The expert did **not** say closures are unnecessary. It said they may be avoidable **if** the package is strong enough and if management is willing to use the full range of alternatives. That is a conditional statement, not a guarantee.

So I am not prepared to rule closures out in advance. What I am prepared to say is this:
- if equivalent, durable savings can be proven through other measures, then closures need not happen;
- if they cannot, then the company must not be trapped by slogans.

### 2) “No compulsory redundancies” is only acceptable if the alternative is real
I am open to avoiding forced layoffs if the package is credible. But the alternative cannot just be a promise of attrition and hope.

The expert was very clear:
- voluntary exits,
- early retirement,
- internal transfers,
- shift reduction,
- and wage/bonus restraint

may work — but only if they are quantified and sufficient.

If the employee side wants to keep compulsory redundancies off the table, it has to accept **a sharper package of other measures**. Otherwise it is not a restructuring plan, it is a delay mechanism.

### 3) I do not accept “site guarantees to 2030” as a substitute for economics
Binding guarantees are only meaningful if the sites remain viable. A guarantee that freezes an uncompetitive footprint is not stewardship; it is a postponement of the adjustment. I will not support turning the company into a museum of existing sites.

## What would move me
The expert’s framework points in the right direction, but only if the following are put on the table:

- a **quantified savings target**,
- a **plant-by-plant capacity plan**,
- a **headcount bridge** based on voluntary exits and redeployment,
- **multi-year labor-cost relief**,
- **management overhead reductions**,
- and a **credible investment allocation** for the retained footprint.

If the employee side is genuinely willing to deliver that, then I can support a non-closure package. But I need to see enough scale and durability to be confident the company’s margins recover.

## My bottom line
I support a **serious hybrid restructuring** if it delivers:
- durable cost reduction,
- lower capacity,
- no value-destructive strike,
- and a credible industrial future.

I do **not** support:
- a blanket no-closures rule,
- a blanket no-layoffs rule with no numerical backing,
- or any settlement that protects every legacy arrangement while leaving the company structurally weak.

So my position remains:  
**I am open to avoiding closures and forced redundancies if — and only if — the alternative package is genuinely equivalent in economic effect and durable enough to restore competitiveness.**  
If that equivalence cannot be demonstrated, then management’s restructuring tools must remain available.
