I’ll respond directly.

## First: what I agree on
I agree with everyone on one point: **this is a real crisis and a real restructuring is needed**.  
A cosmetic deal, a short delay, or a package that merely postpones the cost problem is **not acceptable**.

I also agree that:
- we need **future investment in Germany**
- we need a **durable** solution, not a temporary patch
- we need **burden-sharing**
- and we need to avoid a prolonged breakdown in industrial relations if at all possible

So let me be clear: I am not arguing for change for its own sake. I am arguing for the level of change the business actually needs.

## Where I strongly disagree

### 1) “No plant closures” is not a serious starting point
To the Works Council and the union: I understand why you reject closures politically. But as a management position, I cannot accept “every German plant stays open” as a prior condition.

If a site is structurally overcapacity or uncompetitive, then preserving it regardless of economics is not social responsibility — it is **deferred decline**. That is not a viable basis for this company.

You have not shown that the full current footprint can be sustained at competitive cost.  
What you have done is declare the outcome you want before any real adjustment. That is not negotiation.

### 2) “No compulsory redundancies” cannot be an absolute red line
I prefer voluntary tools, attrition, transfers, and phased adjustment. But I will not pretend that a restructuring of this scale can be guaranteed with zero forced workforce reduction if the numbers do not work otherwise.

If the employee side insists that no compulsory redundancies are ever possible, then it is effectively demanding that management carry the full burden while the structural problem remains. I cannot sign up to that.

### 3) Temporary concessions alone are insufficient
To the union especially: temporary wage moderation, bonus restraint, and working-time flexibility may help, but they do **not** solve a persistent cost-gap problem.

We need:
- durable capacity reduction
- durable labour-cost relief
- and a footprint that can support competitive returns through 2030

If the solution is only reversible, then the underlying problem survives.

## To the Works Council
You say plant closures are not a neutral option. I agree — they are serious decisions. That is exactly why they are on the table.

But you cannot block them in advance and still claim to be open to restructuring. If a site is uneconomic, it cannot be protected by declaration.  
And if the plan requires every site to remain open, then the burden simply shifts onto other plants, workers, and the balance sheet.

Your position protects the present footprint, not the future viability of the company.

## To the union
You say closures and layoffs are a displacement plan. I disagree.  
A failure to act would be a **slow-motion erosion plan**: weaker margins, less investment, continued pressure on jobs later, and a smaller industrial base overall.

I am prepared to discuss voluntary exits, working-time adjustments, targeted redeployment, and negotiated labour-cost contribution.  
I am **not** prepared to accept a package that leaves the cost base fundamentally unchanged and calls that a solution.

## To the regional state government
I appreciate your role and your concern for the region. But I need to be equally clear: regional stability cannot mean freezing industrial adjustment.

If your position is effectively “no closures, no forced redundancies, preserve every site,” then you are asking for a political settlement, not an economic one. That will not restore competitiveness.

If you want to help, then help deliver a package that is socially managed **and** economically real.

## To the controlling family owners and investors
I agree with your core point: value must be protected, and the solution has to be real.  
But I want to emphasize that “real” means more than symbolic capacity trimming. It means a package big enough to change the cost curve.

I will support a negotiated solution if it includes:
- meaningful capacity reduction
- real labour-cost contribution
- workforce adjustment
- and investment into the remaining German footprint

I will **not** support a settlement that preserves everything and calls it reform.

## What I am actually asking for
The company needs a package that delivers:
- a **material reduction in overcapacity**
- a **structural reduction in labour costs**
- a **credible return to margin strength**
- and a **freed-up investment base for 2030**

That means all instruments must be considered:
- site consolidation where justified
- capacity reduction
- working-time and shift changes
- voluntary exits and attrition
- internal redeployment
- employee contributions
- and, where necessary, closures or other hard decisions

## What would move me
I am open to alternatives **if** they produce the same durable effect:
- real cost savings
- real capacity reduction
- and a credible path to competitiveness

But I will not move on a package that:
- preserves all sites by principle
- rejects headcount reduction by principle
- and relies only on temporary concessions

That would be a cosmetic compromise, and I will not endorse it.

## Bottom line
We are at a point where the business either:
1. accepts a serious structural restructuring now, or  
2. drifts into a weaker position, with worse options later.

I am prepared to negotiate hard on the **how**.  
I am **not** prepared to compromise on the **need** for deep, durable change.

If the employee side and state side want a solution without closures, then bring forward a package that is genuinely equivalent in economic effect — not just politically easier. Until then, closures and workforce reductions remain on the table.
