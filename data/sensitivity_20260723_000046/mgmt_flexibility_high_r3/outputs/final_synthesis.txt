## 1. Decision context

The company faces a genuine structural crisis: weak margins, excess German capacity, high labour costs, and an EV-transition burden in a shrinking and highly competitive European market. What makes this decision unusually hard is not the diagnosis, but the governance environment: co-determination, family control, and a regional blocking stake give multiple actors effective veto power.

The live choice is no longer whether restructuring is needed, but whether it can be done **without** plant closures and compulsory redundancies, or whether management’s preferred deeper restructuring instruments must remain available as a fallback. The discussion has also revealed a procedural gap: stakeholders are arguing over principles without a shared quantified comparison of what each package delivers by 2027 and 2030.

---

## 2. Stakeholder assembly

- **Group & Brand Management**: drives the restructuring, prioritizes competitiveness, durable cost reduction, and capacity adjustment.
- **Works Council**: plant-level employee representation; co-determination actor; insists on full job and site protection.
- **Industrial Union**: collective-bargaining counterpart with strike capacity; aligned with workforce red lines, but open to negotiated burden-sharing.
- **Regional State Government**: anchor shareholder and political actor; opposes closures/forced layoffs as a starting point, but wants a real restructuring.
- **Controlling Family Holding**: majority voting control through governance; supports structural adjustment and keeps hard instruments available.
- **Institutional Investors**: capital-market shareholders; prioritize durable margin recovery and credible economic impact.

Standing/weight is highest for management, controlling family, state, and union/works council because they combine formal governance power, blocking rights, or mobilization capacity. Institutional investors matter financially but are structurally weaker in governance.

---

## 3. Final positions

### Group & Brand Management
Management insists the restructuring must materially reduce overcapacity and labour cost, with real effects visible by 2027 and 2030. It is willing to use voluntary exits, redeployment, working-time changes, and investment commitments, but refuses to preclude closures or compulsory redundancies if the gap cannot otherwise be closed.

### Works Council
The works council rejects plant closures, compulsory redundancies, and any transition design that embeds either as a fallback. It is open to working-time changes, redeployment, product reallocation, voluntary exits, and temporary employee contributions, but only within a framework that keeps every German plant open and preserves full co-determination.

### Industrial Union
The union is aligned with the works council on the core red lines: no closures and no forced layoffs. It is willing to discuss negotiated contributions, temporary wage moderation, bonus restraint, and flexible tools, but only if they sit in a framework that excludes compulsory measures and protects jobs and sites.

### Regional State Government
The state rejects closures and forced redundancies as a starting point and wants a socially managed restructuring that preserves the regional industrial base. It is prepared to support change if it is economically real and politically defensible, but has not yet specified a hard governance mechanism to prevent “last resort” from becoming a procedural cover for later cuts.

### Controlling Family Holding
The family holding backs management’s insistence on durable structural change and refuses to lock in every site or rule out workforce reduction in advance. It prefers managed transitions, but keeps closures and compulsory redundancies on the table as necessary tools if the economics demand them.

### Institutional Investors
Investors want credible, durable cost and capacity reduction with enough certainty to restore margins and support equity value. They are open to voluntary and managed measures, but will not support a package that preserves all sites and legacy guarantees while leaving the structural cost problem intact.

---

## 4. Genuine conflicts (structurally incompatible positions)

1. **No-closure principle vs. closure fallback**
   - Employee side and state want closures excluded from the deal.
   - Management, family, and investors require closures to remain available if needed.

2. **No compulsory redundancies vs. workforce adjustment as a necessary tool**
   - Labour treats forced layoffs as unacceptable.
   - Capital-side actors see them as potentially unavoidable.

3. **Full site preservation vs. footprint optimization**
   - Labour wants every German plant to remain open.
   - Management/family/investors see some site adjustment or consolidation as potentially necessary.

4. **Temporary/reversible concessions vs. permanent structural cost reduction**
   - Labour prefers negotiated, reversible burden-sharing.
   - Management and investors want durable, one-way cost-base change.

5. **Co-determination as veto vs. managerial flexibility**
   - Labour/state insist on full co-determination and no unilateral decisions.
   - Management/family seek implementable restructuring authority.

These are not semantic disputes; they are real incompatibilities over which instruments may legitimately be used.

---

## 5. Areas of convergence

- All parties accept the company has a real competitiveness and overcapacity problem.
- All reject a cosmetic or short-term fix.
- All agree the solution must be durable.
- All accept that future investment in Germany is necessary.
- All prefer a negotiated outcome over open-ended conflict.
- All recognize burden-sharing is unavoidable.
- All acknowledge that a prolonged strike would be value-destructive.

This convergence is important: the disagreement is not about diagnosis, but about admissible instruments and who bears adjustment risk.

---

## 6. Key trade-offs for the decision-maker

1. **Job and site security vs. economic adequacy**
   - The more fully jobs and sites are protected, the harder it becomes to guarantee margin recovery and capacity rationalization.

2. **Social peace vs. speed and scale**
   - A socially protected settlement is more stable but may underdeliver on cost reduction; a harder restructuring may be more effective but triggers conflict and governance resistance.

3. **Temporary burden-sharing vs. structural reform**
   - Reversible employee contributions help negotiations, but may not solve a persistent fixed-cost problem.

4. **Governance feasibility vs. operational effectiveness**
   - Any solution must pass multiple veto points; the “best” economic deal is irrelevant if it cannot be ratified.

5. **Credible fallback vs. trust**
   - Management wants a fallback to preserve leverage and realism; labour sees any fallback as a built-in threat that destroys trust and undermines durable peace.

---

## 7. Integrative pathways

### Pathway A: Two-stage restructuring with independent review
Start with a defined test period using only non-closure measures: working-time changes, redeployment, shift reconfiguration, voluntary exits, attrition, bonus restraint, and contributions from management/owners. An independent review then assesses whether targets are met, but there is **no automatic closure trigger**—only a fresh governance decision.

**Value:** preserves labour/state trust while giving management a real performance test.

### Pathway B: Hard no-closure package with stronger burden-sharing
All German plants remain open for the agreement term, compulsory redundancies are excluded, and workforce adjustment relies on voluntary and managed instruments. In exchange, management, owners, and investors commit to explicit savings contributions, binding investment, product allocation commitments, and measurable margin targets.

**Value:** strongest chance of social acceptance; hardest to satisfy the economic side unless savings are proven.

### Pathway C: Site-preservation with differentiated roles
Rather than treating all sites identically, classify sites by function and future role while keeping them open. Adjust products, volumes, and specialized functions across the footprint, combined with redeployment and investment.

**Value:** could achieve footprint optimization without overt closure language; still requires quantified capacity reduction.

### Pathway D: Managed workforce-transition corridor
A compromise corridor limits forced measures to final-resort conditions with joint triggers and independent review.

**Value:** could satisfy management/family if tightly designed, but labour has already signaled this is likely to be seen as a disguised route to forced cuts.

---

## 8. Open questions

- What exact savings and capacity reduction are required by 2027 and 2030?
- What is the baseline cost gap, and what portion can non-closure measures realistically close?
- What objective threshold would count as “economic equivalence” to closures/layoffs?
- What binding governance mechanism will the state use to make “last resort” real?
- What concessions are management, family, and investors prepared to make if closures and forced layoffs are removed from the initial package?
- Can a package be designed that preserves all sites while still materially reducing fixed cost and overcapacity?
- What is the legal and industrial-relations risk of any fallback mechanism under co-determination?

---

## 9. Recommended next step

Hold a **quantified scenario comparison session** before any further bargaining on principles. The parties should compare 2–3 packages using the same template:

1. baseline cost/capacity,
2. 2027 target,
3. 2030 target,
4. measures used,
5. whether closures/forced redundancies are included,
6. governance trigger and review mechanism,
7. expected margin impact and implementation risk.

This is the only way to convert the debate from red lines to testable choices.

---

## 10. Emergent insights that only appeared through multi-turn interaction

1. **The real deadlock is now about admissible instruments, not diagnosis.**  
   Early rounds showed broad agreement on the problem; later rounds revealed the central conflict is whether closures and compulsory redundancies are legitimate bargaining tools or disallowed from the outset.

2. **“Durable” means different things to different sides.**  
   Management and capital mean permanent cost and capacity reduction; labour and the state hear permanent insecurity unless the definition is constrained.

3. **The union and works council are aligned on red lines, but the state is only conditionally aligned.**  
   The state opposes hard cuts politically, yet has not translated that into a concrete governance rule—leaving its “last resort” language ambiguous.

4. **A fallback mechanism itself has become the object of distrust.**  
   What management sees as realism, labour sees as a pre-authored threat that undermines any negotiated concessions.

5. **The company’s governance structure matters as much as the economics.**  
   Because several actors can block, an economically strong but politically unratifiable solution is not viable; this became much clearer only after repeated rounds.

6. **All sides now want quantified proof, but none has supplied it.**  
   The repeated request for 2027/2030 scenario math indicates that the next decisive move is evidentiary, not rhetorical.

7. **There is a possible middle ground around differentiated sites, not equal treatment.**  
   The discussion moved from binary “close/no close” to more workable notions of product allocation, specialization, and capacity rebalancing—an important opening that was not present in the initial positions.
