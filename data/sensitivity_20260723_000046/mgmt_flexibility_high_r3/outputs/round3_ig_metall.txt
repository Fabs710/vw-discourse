We are clear on the problem. We are not clear on the remedy.

## Where I agree
I agree with all sides on three points:
- this company has a **real competitiveness and overcapacity problem**;
- a **cosmetic deal is not enough**;
- any settlement must be **durable** and must include **future investment in Germany**.

I also agree that burden-sharing is unavoidable. We are not defending the status quo just to defend it.

## Where I disagree — sharply

### 1) Plant closures are not a neutral instrument
To management, owners, and investors: saying closures “cannot be ruled out” is not the same as proving they are necessary.

A plant closure is a permanent industrial break. It destroys jobs, skills, and trust, and it would be a drastic escalation in a company with this history and governance structure. For us, closures are not a starting point. They are a last-resort claim that has not been justified.

So far, you have argued from pressure, not from proof.

### 2) Compulsory redundancies remain unacceptable
We reject the idea that forced layoffs are simply part of a serious restructuring.

If the only way your model works is by compelling people out, then your model is not balanced. It is shifting the burden onto workers who did not create the strategic missteps and who are now expected to absorb the pain while others preserve strategic freedom.

That is not acceptable.

### 3) “Durable” must not mean permanent insecurity for labour
Management and capital keep using “durable” to justify permanent cost cuts on employees, while treating job security and collective standards as obstacles.

That is not burden-sharing. That is one-sided risk transfer.

A durable deal must be durable for **the company and the workforce**.

## To management
You still have not demonstrated that:
- every German plant is uneconomic,
- forced redundancies are unavoidable,
- or that the same economic effect cannot be achieved through less destructive means.

You say working-time changes, redeployment, attrition, and voluntary exits are insufficient. Maybe. But you have not shown that exhaustively. Before you talk about closures and forced cuts, you must prove that socially responsible instruments cannot close the gap.

What remains on the table from our side:
- working-time adjustments,
- shift and line reconfiguration,
- internal redeployment,
- natural attrition,
- voluntary exits,
- temporary wage moderation or bonus restraint,
- and management-side contributions.

What is not on the table:
- closures,
- compulsory redundancies,
- or a permanent dismantling of the collective wage framework.

## To the works council
We are aligned on the fundamentals. Your insistence that the burden must not fall on workers alone is right.

But I want to be equally direct: “no closures” cannot just be a slogan. If we want to hold that line, we need a credible package that delivers real savings. That means the employee side is prepared to discuss temporary, negotiated contributions — but only if there are binding guarantees and a fair share from management and owners.

## To the regional state government
Your stance against closures and forced redundancies is the right one. But support has to become governance, not just language.

If you mean “last resort,” then use your blocking position accordingly. The state should not allow a restructuring that makes workers carry the main burden while management reserves the right to tear up the social contract.

## To the family owners and investors
You all keep asking for “economic equivalence.” Fine. Then show us a package that is truly equivalent **without** destroying sites and livelihoods.

Do not hide behind the claim that closures are efficient just because they are easy to write into a model. Long-term value is not protected by detonating trust, provoking strike escalation, and weakening the company’s industrial base.

## What we can discuss
We are prepared to negotiate seriously on:
- temporary wage moderation,
- bonus restraint,
- working-time flexibility,
- shift and line changes,
- voluntary exits,
- natural attrition,
- internal redeployment,
- and a jointly supported restructuring fund,

**if and only if** there are binding protections on jobs and sites and meaningful contributions from management and owners.

## What we reject outright
We reject any package built around:
- plant closures,
- compulsory redundancies,
- permanent insecurity for labour,
- or the one-sided weakening of the collective wage framework.

## Bottom line
We are ready for a serious shared-sacrifice package. But “shared sacrifice” means exactly that: **shared**.

If the company wants a real solution, bring forward a package that:
- keeps German sites open,
- excludes forced layoffs,
- preserves collective standards,
- and distributes the burden fairly across labour, management, owners, and capital.

If that is not possible, then do not pretend that closures and compulsory redundancies are the only serious answer. They are the most confrontational answer, not yet the justified one.

If the debate is going to continue, it should now move to a concrete package that tests:
- how much capacity reduction is truly needed,
- what management and owners will contribute,
- and what binding job and investment guarantees will accompany any employee contribution.

Until then, our position stands.
