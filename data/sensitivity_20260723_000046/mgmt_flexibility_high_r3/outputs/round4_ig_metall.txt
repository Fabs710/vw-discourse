We have moved from slogans to structure, but the core problem is still the same: **management and capital are trying to preserve the right to impose closures or forced redundancies, while asking labour to pre-pay the restructuring through concessions.** That is not a fair basis for a settlement.

## To management
I acknowledge one point: the company does need a durable restructuring and a lower cost base. We are not denying the problem.

But I disagree with your premise that a restructuring is only credible if it keeps closures and compulsory redundancies as an open fallback. That is not “realism”; it is leverage. And it is exactly what makes any deal unstable, because it tells the workforce that any contribution they make can still be followed by site cuts or forced exits.

You still have not demonstrated:
- that every German plant is uneconomic,
- that compulsory redundancies are unavoidable,
- or that the same economic effect cannot be achieved through a serious package of managed measures.

If you want labour to contribute, then show necessity, not just preference.

## To the controlling family and investors
You keep asking for “economic equivalence,” but your definition seems to be: equivalence to management’s preferred outcome, including the right to close sites later if the numbers are not perfect.

That is not acceptable.

A credible alternative must be equivalent **in economic effect** without treating closures and layoffs as the hidden default. If you want employee contributions, you must accept binding guarantees on jobs, sites, and collective standards in return. Otherwise you are simply transferring the downside onto workers while retaining maximum strategic freedom for yourselves.

## To the regional state government
Your position is closer to ours than management’s, but I need to press you: “last resort” must not become a procedural runway into closures.

If the state wants to be credible, then it should support a package that **starts** from preserving sites and avoiding forced redundancies, not one that embeds those outcomes as a future option if management’s preferred math is not met.

You have governance power. Use it to force a genuinely socially managed restructuring, not one that only becomes socially managed after the damage is already planned in.

## To the works council
We are aligned on the red lines:
- no closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- no dismantling of job security,
- no one-sided burden transfer.

I also share your concern that “transition corridor” language can become a technical route to labour being squeezed later. That is why I am not prepared to accept any framework that makes forced exits a legitimate instrument at some later stage.

If we are to discuss adjustments, they must be inside a framework that excludes compulsory redundancies and site closures from the outset.

## On the facilitator’s options

### 1) Phased restructuring with closure fallback
We reject the fallback. A test package is only acceptable if its purpose is to prove that non-closure measures are sufficient, not to provide a path to later closures.

### 2) Workforce-transition corridor
We reject any corridor that permits forced exits. A cap does not make compulsory redundancies acceptable; it just makes them administratively tidier.

### 3) Site preservation with differentiated roles
This is the only direction worth developing, but only if it means:
- all German plants remain open,
- product and volume allocation are adjusted,
- functions are redistributed,
- and investment is targeted to secure future work.

Differentiation cannot be a code word for shutting plants later.

## What we can actually discuss
We are prepared to negotiate a serious package that includes:

- **working-time adjustments**
- **shift and line reconfiguration**
- **internal redeployment**
- **natural attrition**
- **voluntary exits**
- **temporary wage moderation**
- **bonus restraint**
- **a restructuring contribution from employees**
- **management-side savings**
- **owner-side contributions**
- **binding investment commitments through 2030**
- **product allocation guarantees**
- **training and qualification measures**
- **short-time or flexibility tools where appropriate**

But the conditions remain non-negotiable:
- **no plant closures**
- **no compulsory redundancies**
- **no unilateral weakening of the collective wage framework**
- **full co-determination**
- **shared sacrifice, not worker-only sacrifice**

## What would make a deal possible
If management and owners want a real agreement, then put forward a package with:
1. a quantified savings target,
2. a defined employee contribution,
3. comparable management/owner contributions,
4. binding job and site security,
5. binding investment in Germany,
6. and a restructuring horizon that does not include forced layoffs or closures.

That is the only path to a durable settlement that can actually hold in the workforce.

## Bottom line
We are not refusing change. We are refusing a restructuring model where labour carries the pain, while management and owners keep the right to escalate to closures or layoffs if they later decide the deal is not enough.

If you want cooperation, bring a package that protects jobs and sites first, and shares the burden fairly.

If not, then do not expect the union to sign off on a dressed-up threat to the workforce.
