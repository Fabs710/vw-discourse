I will be equally direct from the regional state’s perspective.

## What I agree with
I agree with management, the family owners, and investors on one point: **this cannot be a cosmetic restructuring**. The company has a genuine competitiveness problem, and doing nothing or doing too little would only make the eventual correction harsher.

I also agree that:
- the solution must be **durable**,
- **burden-sharing** is necessary,
- and the company needs a credible **industrial plan** with future investment in Germany.

So I am not here to defend stagnation.

## Where I disagree — fundamentally
I do **not** accept the claim that the only serious restructuring path is one built around **plant closures and compulsory redundancies**.

That is too narrow, too risky, and too damaging for a company that is also a regional economic anchor. It treats the workforce and the German footprint as the easiest place to cut, rather than testing all reasonable alternatives first.

### On plant closures
To management, the family owners, and investors: saying closures “cannot be ruled out” is not the same as proving they are necessary.

A closure is not a neutral efficiency measure. It is a permanent industrial and social break. For a company with this governance structure and this regional responsibility, closures in Germany must be a last resort, not the starting point.

### On compulsory redundancies
I also do **not** accept compulsory redundancies as an acceptable default instrument.  
If the restructuring only works by forcing people out, then the company has not yet shown that it has exhausted socially responsible alternatives.

That does **not** mean no adjustment at all. It means the burden must be managed through:
- voluntary departures,
- natural attrition,
- redeployment,
- working-time and shift flexibility,
- product and capacity reallocation,
- and negotiated contributions on both sides.

### On “durable” cost relief
I agree that temporary measures alone are insufficient. But “durable” cannot be used as a code word for permanently weakening job security while preserving management flexibility untouched.

A durable settlement must be durable for **the company and the workforce**.

## To the Works Council and union
I share your core concern: **no unilateral destruction of sites, no one-sided shifting of the burden onto workers, no empty promises**.

Your insistence that the workforce must not pay alone is justified.

That said, I would caution against framing every form of capacity adjustment as unacceptable in advance. If the company is to remain viable, some real changes will be needed. The question is not whether there will be adjustment, but **how it is designed** and **who carries the cost**.

What I can support is:
- binding site and employment security,
- negotiated and temporary employee contributions,
- flexibility in working time and production organization,
- and investment commitments that make the German footprint future-proof.

What I cannot support is a restructuring that looks balanced on paper but in practice shifts all adjustment risk to labour.

## To management
You are right that a purely symbolic deal is not enough. But you are wrong if you think the way to restore competitiveness is to make closures and forced cuts the first language of the process.

You have not yet convinced me that every German site is uneconomic or that the same savings cannot be achieved through a mix of:
- better capacity coordination,
- working-time changes,
- internal redeployment,
- voluntary instruments,
- and a fairer distribution of costs between labour, management, and owners.

If you want support from the state, you need to show that you have genuinely tested those options before reaching for closures.

## To the controlling family and investors
You both speak about long-term value, and I take that seriously. But long-term value is not protected by hollowing out the company’s regional base or triggering a permanent rupture with the workforce.

A settlement that damages trust, legitimacy, and industrial stability may look clean in a model. In real life it carries strike risk, political backlash, weaker commitment, and lasting damage to the company’s social license to operate.

That is value erosion, not value protection.

## What I would support
I am prepared to support a negotiated restructuring package if it includes all of the following:
- **preservation of German sites wherever economically possible**
- **no compulsory redundancies as the first resort**
- **a fair, temporary, negotiated employee contribution**
- **meaningful contributions from management and owners**
- **binding investment commitments for Germany through 2030**
- **full respect for co-determination and the special governance rights that exist here**
- **a credible industrial plan that secures future products and technologies in Germany**

## What I cannot support
I will not support:
- a blanket closure agenda,
- a restructuring that guts regional employment,
- a process that sidelines the state and co-determination,
- or a deal that asks workers to carry the main burden while others reserve all strategic freedom.

## Bottom line
I am open to a serious negotiated solution. But it must be a solution that:
1. restores competitiveness,
2. preserves the company as a regional anchor,
3. and protects the governance balance that has sustained it.

If management comes back with a package centered on closures and forced redundancies, I will oppose it. If it comes back with a genuine social-partnership restructuring that preserves the core German footprint while achieving real savings, then I am prepared to engage constructively.
