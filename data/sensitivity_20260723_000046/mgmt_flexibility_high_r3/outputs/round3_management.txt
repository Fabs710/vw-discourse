I’ll be just as direct.

We have now spent three rounds agreeing on the diagnosis and circling around the same obstacle: the employee side and the state want restructuring **without** the instruments that would actually make it structurally effective. That is not a viable basis for restoring competitiveness.

## Where I agree
I agree with everyone on four points:
- this company has a **real competitiveness problem**
- a **cosmetic deal is not enough**
- we need a **durable**, not temporary, solution
- future investment in Germany matters

I also agree that a prolonged strike would hurt output, reputation, and value. No one should want that.

## Where I disagree — and why

### 1) “Every German plant remains open” is not a serious management premise
To the Works Council and the union: I understand the political and social appeal of your position. But it does not solve the economic problem.

We are not debating an abstract wish list. We are dealing with an overcapacity problem and a cost base that is too high. If every site must remain open by principle, then the adjustment is pushed elsewhere and the structural gap remains.

You still have not shown that the current footprint can be sustained competitively. Until you do, I cannot accept “no closures” as a precondition.

### 2) “No compulsory redundancies” cannot be absolute
I prefer voluntary exits, attrition, redeployment, and phased adjustment. But I will not accept a blanket exclusion of compulsory workforce reduction if the numbers do not close otherwise.

If the employee side rules that out in advance, it is effectively asking management to guarantee the adjustment outcome before the restructuring has even been designed. That is not realistic.

### 3) Temporary concessions alone do not fix a structural cost problem
To the union: temporary wage moderation, bonus restraint, and working-time flexibility may help, but they are not enough if the underlying capacity and cost gap remains.

We need:
- real capacity reduction
- real labour-cost relief
- a footprint that can support margins through 2030

If the package is only reversible, then the problem comes back.

## To the Works Council
You say closures are not a neutral option. Correct — they are serious. That is precisely why they must remain on the table.

But your position amounts to this: all sites protected, no forced exits, no real footprint adjustment, and somehow the company still becomes competitive. That is not a plan. That is a refusal to choose.

You also keep asking for proof that every plant is uneconomic. Fine — but the burden of proof cannot be turned into a veto. We do not need to prove that every site is bad. We need a package that closes the cost gap.

## To the union
I appreciate that you are prepared to discuss working-time changes, voluntary exits, redeployment, and temporary contributions. That is constructive.

What is not constructive is insisting that closures and compulsory redundancies are illegitimate by definition. They are not pleasant tools. They are restructuring tools. If you want to avoid them, bring forward an **economically equivalent** package — not just a socially more acceptable one.

At present, I do not see a package that achieves:
- sufficient capacity reduction
- sufficient cost reduction
- and sufficient durability

without harder instruments.

## To the regional state government
Your role matters, and I respect the governance reality. But regional stability cannot become a veto on industrial adjustment.

You say closures and forced redundancies should be last resort. I can accept that in principle. What I cannot accept is turning “last resort” into “never,” because that would preserve the current imbalance and weaken the company further.

If you want a future industrial base in the region, then support a package that is economically real, not politically comfortable.

## To the controlling family owners and investors
I agree with your insistence on enterprise value and competitiveness. But let me be equally clear: value is not protected by delaying the necessary adjustment until the market forces a worse outcome later.

A serious package must actually change the cost curve. That means you cannot back away from:
- capacity reduction
- site consolidation where justified
- workforce adjustment
- and durable labour-cost relief

If the final deal leaves the structural problem intact, then it is not stewardship. It is procrastination.

## What I am asking for now
We need a package that delivers:
- a **material reduction in overcapacity**
- a **structural reduction in labour costs**
- a **credible path back to return-on-sales target**
- and funding freed up for investment to 2030

That means all instruments must remain in play:
- site consolidation
- capacity reduction
- working-time and shift changes
- internal redeployment
- voluntary exits
- attrition
- negotiated employee contributions
- and, where necessary, closures or workforce reductions

## What would move me
I am open to alternatives **if and only if** they produce the same durable economic effect.

That means:
- not temporary only
- not symbolic only
- not “no closures” by principle
- not “no forced reductions” by principle

It must be a package that truly restores competitiveness.

## Bottom line
We can continue debating methods, but the business requirement is unchanged:

- reduce overcapacity
- reduce labour costs structurally
- restore margins
- free funds for future products and technology

If the employee side and the state want to avoid closures, then they need to bring forward a package that is genuinely equivalent in economic effect. Until then, closures and workforce reductions remain on the table.

I am not softening that position, because the company cannot afford a cosmetic compromise.
