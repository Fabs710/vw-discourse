I will be direct.

## Where I agree
I agree with all sides on the diagnosis:
- the company has a **real competitiveness and overcapacity problem**;
- a **cosmetic deal will fail**;
- the solution must be **durable**;
- and the company needs a **credible investment path in Germany**.

I also agree that burden-sharing is necessary. The state does not deny that the workforce, management, owners, and investors all have to contribute in some form.

## Where I disagree sharply

### 1) Plant closures cannot be the starting point
Management, the family owners, and investors keep saying closures “remain on the table.” I understand that as a theoretical statement. But as a negotiating posture, it is unacceptable to treat closures as a normal first-line instrument.

A plant closure is not a neutral efficiency measure. It is a permanent damage event for jobs, skills, suppliers, and the regional economy. For a company of this importance, closures in Germany must be a true last resort, and nothing so far has shown that every alternative has been exhausted.

So no: I do **not** accept a package that starts from closures as if they were just another management tool.

### 2) Compulsory redundancies cannot be the default answer
I also reject the normalizing of forced layoffs. If the restructuring only works by compelling people out, then the company has not yet demonstrated a socially responsible solution. It has only shown that it wants labour to absorb the adjustment first.

That is not acceptable in a company that is a regional anchor and a co-determined industrial institution.

### 3) “Durable” must not mean permanent insecurity for employees
Management and capital use “durable” to justify permanent structural cost cuts. I understand the economic logic, but I do not accept a definition of durability that is one-sided.

A durable solution must be durable for:
- the company’s competitiveness, **and**
- the workforce’s security, **and**
- the region’s industrial base.

If durability means making workers carry permanent insecurity while others preserve strategic freedom, then I cannot support it.

## To management
You say every instrument must remain in play. I say not every instrument is acceptable as a default assumption.

Before you talk about closures and forced reductions, you still need to show that you have genuinely exhausted alternatives such as:
- working-time adjustments,
- shift and line reconfiguration,
- product allocation,
- internal redeployment,
- natural attrition,
- voluntary exits,
- management-side savings,
- and targeted efficiency gains.

If those have not been tested seriously, then closures are not justified.

## To the family owners and investors
You keep asking for economic equivalence. Fine. Then prove it without insisting on the destruction of sites and jobs.

A model that is “clean” financially but creates strike risk, political backlash, reputational damage, and long-term social conflict is not value-preserving. It is value erosion.

If you want long-term value, then support a restructuring that keeps the company rooted in Germany, not one that hollows out its regional base.

## To the works council and union
I am aligned with your core concern: the burden must not be shifted onto workers alone.

But I also want to be practical. If we want to preserve jobs and sites, we cannot simply say “no” to adjustment. There must be room for:
- temporary employee contributions,
- flexibility in working time,
- shift changes,
- internal redeployment,
- voluntary exits,
- and capacity optimization short of closures.

What I cannot support is a line that says any structural change is illegitimate. It isn’t. The question is whether it is designed responsibly.

## What the state can support
The state can support a negotiated restructuring if it meets these conditions:
- **preservation of German sites wherever economically possible**
- **no compulsory redundancies as a first resort**
- **no unilateral dismantling of co-determination or job-security frameworks**
- **binding investment commitments for Germany through 2030**
- **real contributions from management and owners**
- **capacity adjustments through socially managed means first**

That is the basis for a serious solution.

## What I cannot support
I cannot support:
- a closure-led restructuring,
- a package built around forced redundancies,
- or a deal that weakens the company’s regional role and then calls that modernization.

## On “equivalent” alternatives
If management wants alternatives to closures, then bring forward a package that is genuinely equivalent in economic effect **without** destroying sites and livelihoods.

That means:
- real savings,
- real capacity reduction,
- real investment,
- and shared burden.

It does **not** mean using closures simply because they are the fastest instrument on paper.

## Bottom line
The state is prepared to support serious restructuring. But not at any price.

We will support a solution that:
- restores competitiveness,
- preserves the company as a regional anchor,
- protects jobs as far as possible,
- respects co-determination,
- and distributes the burden fairly.

We will **not** support a solution that begins from the assumption that closures and forced layoffs are the only serious answer.

If the parties want progress, then the next step must be concrete scenarios that test how much can be achieved through:
- capacity changes,
- working-time measures,
- redeployment,
- voluntary instruments,
- and shared contributions,

before anyone treats plant closures or compulsory redundancies as necessary.
