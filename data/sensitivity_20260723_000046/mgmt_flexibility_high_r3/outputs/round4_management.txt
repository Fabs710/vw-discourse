I’ll respond directly.

## To the works council and union
I hear your red lines. I also hear the refusal to engage with the actual economics.

You keep saying:
- every plant must remain open,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- no weakening of guarantees,
- and no closure fallback.

That is not a restructuring framework. That is a demand that the company solve a structural overcapacity problem **without** the instruments that can actually fix it.

Let me be precise: I do **not** accept an approach that pre-commits to preserving the full German footprint regardless of economic reality. If we lock in every site and rule out workforce reduction in advance, then we are simply postponing the crisis and preserving an uncompetitive cost base. That would be irresponsible to the company, its future investment capacity, and the majority of employees whose jobs depend on a viable business.

## Where I agree
I agree on three things:
- a cosmetic deal is useless;
- we need durable change, not a one-year patch;
- we should prefer voluntary exits, redeployment, working-time solutions, and internal flexibility where they genuinely deliver savings.

## Where I disagree
I disagree fundamentally with your claim that closures and compulsory redundancies must be ruled out from the outset.

Why?

Because you have **not** demonstrated an economically equivalent package. You list instruments, but you have not shown that they close the gap in:
- overcapacity,
- fixed-cost base,
- and margin restoration through 2030.

That burden cannot be shifted onto management simply by saying “find another way.” We are finding another way only if it is real, measurable, and sufficient.

A package that avoids closures but leaves the cost base structurally too high is not a win. It is a delay.

## To the regional state government
Your stance is understandable politically, but it cannot become a veto on necessary industrial adjustment.

“Last resort” cannot mean “never.” If the company is to remain a major industrial employer in this region, then the footprint must be made economically sustainable. Protecting every existing configuration regardless of cost would undermine the company’s ability to invest, innovate, and compete.

That said, I agree with you that closures must not be casual or reflexive. They must be justified by a serious necessity test. But the test has to be **real**, not a procedural shield against any change.

## To the controlling family owners and investors
You are right that value destruction through drift is unacceptable. You are also right that the market will not reward a cosmetic compromise.

Where I part company with you is this: you keep asking for “economic equivalence” while treating closures and compulsory redundancies as permanently available fallback options. That is not a balanced negotiation stance; it is leverage disguised as openness.

If you want my support for a non-closure pathway, then I need a package that is demonstrably equivalent in economic effect:
- durable labour-cost reduction,
- real capacity reduction,
- and a credible return to target margins.

So far, I do not see that package on the table.

## To all employee-side stakeholders
Let me be equally frank: “shared sacrifice” cannot mean that only the company’s financial performance is sacrificed to preserve the current footprint.

If we refuse to reduce overcapacity and lower costs structurally, then we will not be investing into the next generation of products and technology. That would endanger the long-term industrial base more than any restructuring measure.

The company needs:
- material savings,
- more efficient capacity,
- lower labour costs,
- and room to invest to 2030.

That is not ideological. It is survival.

## What I am willing to accept
I remain open to a package built around:
- working-time changes,
- shift and line reconfiguration,
- product allocation changes,
- internal redeployment,
- natural attrition,
- voluntary exits,
- temporary employee contributions,
- management-side savings,
- and binding investment commitments.

But I repeat: those tools are acceptable **only if** they produce sufficient durable economic effect.

## What I am not willing to accept
I will not sign up to:
- every German plant remaining open by principle,
- an absolute ban on compulsory redundancies,
- a restructuring that leaves the structural cost problem intact,
- or a deal that protects legacy guarantees while the brand continues to miss its return-on-sales target.

That would be a cosmetic compromise, and we cannot afford it.

## On the facilitator’s options
### 1) Phased restructuring with a closure fallback
I do not rule this out. In fact, I think it is the only honest way to proceed if the parties want to test whether voluntary, socially managed measures can deliver enough savings.

We can start with non-closure measures, but only with:
- clear targets,
- deadlines,
- and an objective review of whether the gap is actually closed.

If the gap remains, then deeper structural measures must stay available.

### 2) Workforce-transition corridor
I could accept a managed transition corridor **if** it allows the company to reach the required economic effect.

But let me be clear: a corridor that excludes compulsory measures entirely is not sufficient if the numbers still do not work. A corridor can smooth the transition; it cannot become a shield against necessary adjustment.

### 3) Site preservation with differentiated roles
This is potentially useful, but only if it is linked to real capacity reduction and actual footprint optimization.

Differentiation is not enough if it merely reshuffles the same cost problem across sites. Some sites may have to absorb less volume, some functions may be consolidated, and some capacity may need to exit the system if the economics demand it.

## The non-negotiable business requirement
The non-negotiable requirement from management is unchanged:

- restore competitiveness,
- reduce overcapacity,
- reduce labour costs structurally,
- hit the brand return-on-sales target,
- and free funds for future products and technology to 2030.

If a package cannot do that, it is not acceptable, no matter how politically convenient it looks.

## My bottom line
I am prepared to negotiate seriously. I am **not** prepared to negotiate myself into a weak restructuring.

Bring me a package that:
- delivers real, durable cost reduction,
- materially reduces overcapacity,
- includes a genuine labour-cost contribution,
- and restores the company’s ability to invest and compete.

If the answer is only temporary concessions, soft promises, and a permanent ban on hard choices, then we are not solving the problem. We are deferring it.

If needed, I’d now want a **quantified scenario comparison**: what savings, capacity reduction, and margin impact each side’s preferred package actually delivers by 2027 and 2030.
