I will be direct.

## Where I agree
I agree with management, the state, labour, and the works council on the basic diagnosis:

- the company has a **real competitiveness and overcapacity problem**;
- a **cosmetic solution is not acceptable**;
- the outcome must be **durable**;
- **future investment in Germany** is necessary;
- and **burden-sharing** is unavoidable.

I also agree that a prolonged strike or a governance breakdown would destroy value. That would hurt the company, the workforce, and the family holding alike.

## Where I disagree

### 1) “Every German plant remains open” is not a binding principle
To the works council and union: I understand why you insist on this, but I cannot accept it as a starting premise.

If the footprint is structurally oversized or materially uncompetitive, then refusing any site adjustment in advance is not stewardship. It is preservation of the status quo.

And the status quo is already eroding margins.

### 2) “No compulsory redundancies” cannot be absolute
I prefer voluntary exits, attrition, redeployment, and managed transitions. But I will not accept an absolute ban if the numbers do not close otherwise.

If workforce adjustment is ruled out before the restructuring is designed, then the company is being asked to guarantee the outcome without being allowed the tools to achieve it. That is not credible.

### 3) Temporary concessions alone do not fix a structural problem
To labour and the state: working-time changes, shift reconfiguration, bonus restraint, and temporary wage moderation may help. But they are not enough if the underlying capacity and cost gap remains.

A durable solution has to be durable in the economic sense:
- lower fixed cost base,
- better capacity utilisation,
- restored margins,
- and a footprint that can support investment through 2030 and beyond.

## To the works council
You say closures are not a neutral option. Correct — they are serious. That is exactly why they must remain available if alternatives fail.

But your position still amounts to: all sites protected, no forced exits, no real footprint adjustment, and somehow the company becomes competitive anyway. That is not a plan. That is a refusal to choose.

You also ask for proof that every plant is uneconomic. We do not need to prove that every site is bad. We need a package that closes the cost gap. If some sites cannot carry their economics, we cannot pretend otherwise.

## To the union
I appreciate that you are prepared to discuss working-time changes, voluntary exits, redeployment, and temporary contributions. That is constructive.

What is not constructive is making closures and compulsory redundancies illegitimate by definition. They are not pleasant tools. They are restructuring tools. If you want to avoid them, bring forward an **economically equivalent** package — not just a socially easier one.

At present, I do not see an alternative that achieves:
- sufficient capacity reduction,
- sufficient cost reduction,
- and sufficient durability.

## To the regional state government
Your role matters, and I respect the governance reality. But regional stability cannot become a veto on industrial adjustment.

I agree closures and forced redundancies must not be casual or reflexive. They should only be pursued if alternatives have genuinely been tested and found insufficient.

But “last resort” must not become “never.” If it does, the company will remain uncompetitive and the region will ultimately lose more through underinvestment and weaker future employment.

## To the controlling family holding and investors
I agree with the insistence on enterprise value and competitiveness. But value is not protected by delaying the necessary adjustment until the market forces a worse outcome later.

A serious package must actually change the cost curve. That means:
- capacity reduction,
- workforce adjustment,
- and durable labour-cost relief.

If the final deal leaves the structural problem intact, then it is not stewardship. It is postponement.

## What I am prepared to support
I would support a package with these features:

- material reduction in overcapacity;
- structural reduction in labour costs;
- workforce adjustment primarily through voluntary and managed means;
- binding investment commitments in Germany;
- and a credible path back to margin recovery.

I am open on the instruments:
- working-time changes;
- shift and line adjustments;
- internal redeployment;
- voluntary exits;
- attrition;
- negotiated employee contributions;
- and, where economically necessary, site consolidation or closure.

## What I will not support
I will **not** support:
- a deal that preserves all sites by principle and calls that restructuring;
- a package that leaves the structural cost problem intact;
- or an arrangement that sacrifices long-term enterprise value to avoid any difficult decision.

## On the facilitator’s scenarios

### 1) Phased restructuring with a closure fallback
I do not rule this out. In fact, it is the only honest way to proceed if the parties want to test whether voluntary, socially managed measures can deliver enough savings.

We can start with non-closure measures, but only with:
- clear targets,
- deadlines,
- and an objective review of whether the gap is actually closed.

If the gap remains, deeper structural measures must stay available.

### 2) Workforce-transition corridor
I could accept a managed transition corridor if it allows the company to reach the required economic effect.

But a corridor that excludes compulsory measures entirely is not sufficient if the numbers still do not work. A corridor can smooth the transition; it cannot become a shield against necessary adjustment.

### 3) Site preservation with differentiated roles
This is potentially useful, but only if it is linked to real capacity reduction and actual footprint optimisation.

Differentiation is not enough if it simply reshuffles the same cost problem across sites. Some sites may have to absorb less volume, some functions may be consolidated, and some capacity may need to exit the system if the economics require it.

## Bottom line
The company needs to:
- restore competitiveness,
- reduce overcapacity,
- reduce labour costs structurally,
- hit the return-on-sales target,
- and free funds for future products and technology.

If the employee side and the state want to avoid closures, they need to bring forward a package that is genuinely equivalent in economic effect. Until then, closures and workforce reductions remain on the table.

I am not softening that position. The company cannot afford a cosmetic compromise.

If helpful, I would next want a **quantified comparison** of the competing packages: what savings, capacity reduction, and margin impact each delivers by 2027 and 2030.
