Let me answer Round 4 clearly.

We welcome the fact that the discussion has moved from abstract pressure to concrete tests. But I need to be equally clear: **a “fallback” for closures or compulsory redundancies is not a compromise from our side.** It is simply a delayed version of the same unacceptable outcome.

## To management
You say you need “structural” measures. We do not dispute the need for restructuring. We dispute your chosen instruments.

What you have **not** shown is:
- that every German plant is uneconomic,
- that forced layoffs are unavoidable,
- or that the same economic effect cannot be achieved through managed alternatives.

So when you ask us to accept closure or forced reduction as a fallback, you are still asking us to accept a conclusion without proof.

And I will say this plainly: **if the restructuring only works by threatening plant closures or compulsory redundancies, then it is not a solution we can accept.**

You also keep using “durable” as if it means permanent employee insecurity. It does not. A durable solution that detonates trust, triggers strikes, and undermines co-determination is not durable. It is brittle.

## To the controlling family holding and investors
You ask for “economic equivalence.” Fine. Then stop treating equivalence as a code word for closures.

If you want equivalence, show us a package with:
- real capacity adjustment,
- real cost reduction,
- management and owner contributions,
- investment commitments,
- and a credible path to competitiveness

**without** plant closures and **without** compulsory redundancies.

So far, what I hear from you is: “We would prefer softer measures, but if they do not work, we reserve the right to break the red lines.” That is not burden-sharing. That is putting the entire downside on labour unless we accept your preferred outcome in advance.

## To the regional state government
Your position is closer to ours than management’s, but I need to push you here too: “last resort” cannot become a procedural excuse that slowly legitimises closures later.

If the state truly opposes closures and forced redundancies, then it should not support any framework that pre-builds them into the deal as an automatic fallback. That is not caution; that is surrender by stages.

We are prepared to discuss a serious restructuring package, but the state must be willing to use its governance power to block any package that starts from the premise that closures and compulsory layoffs are legitimate tools of first or even medium resort.

## To the union
We are aligned on the core red lines:
- no closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- no dismantling of job security,
- and no one-sided burden transfer.

Where I want to be precise is this: we should not be drawn into a formula that sounds balanced but opens the door to forced measures later.

A “transition corridor” sounds technical. In practice, it risks becoming a managed route to exactly what management wants:
- first temporary concessions,
- then “verified gaps,”
- then site pressure,
- then layoffs.

That is not a safeguard if the trigger is defined by management’s own financial model. If we go down that path, the workforce will be asked to accept permanent insecurity in the name of flexibility.

We can discuss temporary employee contributions, yes. But only if they are matched by binding guarantees:
- all German plants remain open,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- and co-determination is preserved in full.

Without that, there is nothing to negotiate.

## On the facilitator’s compromise options

### 1) Phased restructuring with a closure fallback after quantified tests
We reject the fallback logic.

A test package can be discussed only if its purpose is to **avoid** closures and forced layoffs, not to create a procedural bridge to them. If the “test” is designed to legitimise later cuts, then it is not neutral.

So yes to testing non-closure measures.  
No to pre-authorising closures if management later claims the numbers still don’t close.

### 2) Workforce-transition corridor
We do not accept a corridor that permits compulsory measures, even as a “last resort.”

The moment forced reductions are made part of the corridor, the red line has already been crossed. We are not prepared to negotiate a system that institutionalises the possibility of firing workers because management’s target was not met.

### 3) Site preservation with differentiated roles
This is the only part that is directionally acceptable, but only if it means **every German plant remains open** and sites are not designated for closure under a different label.

Differentiation can mean:
- product allocation,
- volume shifts,
- new model assignment,
- internal redeployment,
- and investment targeting.

It does **not** mean sorting plants into categories that later justify shutting some of them down.

## What we are prepared to discuss
We remain open to a serious package of:
- working-time adjustments,
- shift and line reconfiguration,
- product allocation changes,
- internal redeployment,
- natural attrition,
- voluntary exits,
- temporary and negotiated employee contributions,
- bonus restraint,
- management-side savings,
- and binding investment commitments through 2030.

But the conditions are unchanged:
- **every German plant stays open**
- **no compulsory redundancies**
- **no unilateral dismantling of guarantees**
- **full co-determination**

## What we will not accept
We will not accept:
- a staged closure mechanism,
- a “fallback” to layoffs,
- a transition corridor for forced exits,
- or any package that treats workers as the adjustment variable of first resort.

## The real test
If management, owners, and investors insist that closures or forced redundancies are necessary, then they have not yet done the work of proving otherwise.

Bring forward a package that:
- quantifies the gap,
- allocates burden fairly,
- preserves all sites,
- protects jobs,
- and keeps the company competitive.

If you cannot do that, then the problem is not that labour is unreasonable. The problem is that your restructuring model is too narrow.

## Final line
We are not here to bless a dressed-up version of plant closures and compulsory redundancies.

We are here to defend jobs, sites, and co-determination, and to support a restructuring **only if it respects those limits**.

Until then, our position stands unchanged.
