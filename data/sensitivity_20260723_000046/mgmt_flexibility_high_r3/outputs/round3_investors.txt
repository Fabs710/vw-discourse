We agree on the diagnosis, but not yet on a credible remedy.

From the capital markets side, the key test is simple: **does the package restore competitive margins fast enough, durably enough, and with enough certainty to justify holding the stock?** If not, it is not a solution — it is delay.

## Where we agree
We agree with management, the family owners, and to a degree the state that:
- this is a **real overcapacity and cost-base problem**;
- a **cosmetic deal** would be value-destructive;
- the company needs a **durable** restructuring;
- and future **investment in Germany** only makes sense if the operating base becomes competitive.

We also agree with labour and the state on one important point: a prolonged strike would destroy value. It would hurt earnings, execution, supplier confidence, and the share price. So we do want a negotiated outcome if it is economically real.

## Where we disagree
### 1) “No closures” is not a credible absolute
To the works council and union: I understand your political line, but from an investor perspective a blanket no-closures rule is not acceptable if the footprint is structurally oversized.

If some sites are uneconomic, protecting every plant by principle means the cost gap remains. That is not social responsibility; it is a refusal to address the problem at the right scale.

We are not demanding closures for their own sake. We are saying they cannot be ruled out if they are required to restore the cost base.

### 2) “No compulsory redundancies” cannot be untouchable
Voluntary exits, attrition, redeployment, and working-time measures are all preferable. But if the numbers do not close otherwise, then an absolute ban on forced workforce reduction is not compatible with a serious restructuring.

What matters is not preserving every current job. What matters is restoring a viable margin structure that can support future investment and employment.

### 3) Temporary concessions are not enough
To labour and the state: temporary wage moderation, bonus restraint, and reversible flexibility may help, but they do not solve a persistent structural problem.

A package that is only temporary will be read by the market as postponement. We will not support a deal that leaves the company with:
- structurally high fixed costs,
- excess capacity,
- and no clear path back to return-on-sales target.

That would be a poor outcome for shareholders and, eventually, for everyone.

## What would move us
We would support a package that clearly delivers:
- **material, durable cost reduction**
- **real capacity reduction**
- **disciplined workforce adjustment**
- **binding investment commitments for the remaining German footprint**
- **a credible path back to margin recovery**

We are open on the instruments:
- voluntary exits
- natural attrition
- internal redeployment
- working-time and shift flexibility
- negotiated employee contributions
- targeted site consolidation
- and, where economically necessary, closure of structurally uneconomic capacity

We are not attached to any single tool. We are attached to the result.

## What we will not support
We will not support:
- a deal that preserves every site and calls that restructuring;
- a settlement that keeps the structural cost problem intact;
- or a package that protects legacy guarantees while margins stay impaired.

That would be a cosmetic compromise, and markets will discount it immediately.

## To management
You are right that the solution must be durable and substantial. But durability must not become code for maximum disruption. The package has to be enforceable and financially meaningful, not just tough-sounding.

If management wants shareholder support, bring forward a plan that shows:
- how much overcapacity is removed,
- how much labour cost comes out permanently,
- what the economic impact is by 2027–2030,
- and how much investment capacity is restored.

## To the works council and union
You keep asking for “economic equivalence” without closures. Fine — then show us the math. A credible alternative must be economically equivalent, not just socially preferable.

If you can deliver a package with:
- real capacity reduction,
- real cost reduction,
- and a credible return to competitive margins

without closures, we will listen. But if the package relies mainly on temporary concessions and keeps the footprint basically intact, it is not enough.

## To the regional state government
Regional stability matters, but a protected structure with weak economics is not a long-term solution. The region is better served by a viable company than by a company that delays adjustment until the market forces a worse outcome.

## Bottom line
Our position remains:
- **Yes** to serious restructuring
- **Yes** to burden-sharing
- **Yes** to investment in Germany
- **No** to cosmetic compromise
- **No** to an outcome that leaves margins structurally impaired

If the next package credibly restores competitiveness, we can support it. If it does not, we will not pretend a politically comfortable arrangement is economically acceptable.
