We appreciate that the discussion has become more concrete. From a capital-markets perspective, that is useful, but our position has not changed on the essential question:

**Does the package credibly restore competitive margins, reduce overcapacity durably, and do so with enough certainty to justify holding the equity?**

If the answer is no, then the deal is not a solution; it is delay.

## Where we agree

We agree with management, the family holding, and to some extent the state on the diagnosis:
- the company has a **real competitiveness and cost-base problem**;
- a **cosmetic settlement is unacceptable**;
- the solution must be **durable**, not a short-lived patch;
- and future investment in Germany only makes sense if the operating base becomes more efficient.

We also agree with labour and the state that a prolonged strike would be value-destructive. It would hurt earnings, execution, supplier confidence, and the share price. So we want a negotiated outcome if it is economically real.

## Where we disagree

### 1) “Every German plant remains open” is not credible if the footprint is structurally oversized
To the works council and union: your position is understandable from a workforce perspective, but from an investor perspective it is not acceptable as an absolute.

If the German footprint is genuinely overcapacity, then ruling out site adjustment in advance means the company keeps too much fixed cost and too much unused capacity. That is not a durable restructuring. It is a postponement of the problem.

We are not asking for closures as an ideological preference. We are saying they cannot be ruled out if they are necessary to restore competitiveness.

### 2) “No compulsory redundancies” cannot be an untouchable red line
Voluntary exits, attrition, redeployment, working-time changes, and internal mobility are all preferable. We would much rather see headcount reduction happen through those means.

But if the numbers do not close otherwise, then an absolute ban on compulsory workforce reduction is not compatible with a serious restructuring. The market will not reward a deal that preserves every current guarantee while leaving the cost base structurally impaired.

### 3) Temporary concessions are not enough
To labour and the state: temporary wage moderation, bonus restraint, and reversible flexibility may help, but they do not solve a persistent structural problem.

A package that is mainly temporary will be read by the market as deferral. We would not support a deal that leaves the company with:
- structurally high fixed costs,
- excess capacity,
- and no clear path back to return-on-sales target.

That would be a weak outcome for shareholders and, eventually, for everyone.

## What would move us

We would support a package that clearly delivers:
- **material and durable cost reduction**
- **real capacity reduction**
- **disciplined workforce adjustment**
- **binding investment commitments for the remaining German footprint**
- **a credible path back to margin recovery by 2027 and beyond**

We are open on the instruments:
- voluntary exits
- natural attrition
- internal redeployment
- working-time and shift flexibility
- negotiated employee contributions
- targeted site consolidation
- and, where economically necessary, closure of structurally uneconomic capacity

We are not attached to any single tool. We are attached to the result.

## What we will not support

We will not support:
- a deal that preserves every site and calls that restructuring;
- a settlement that keeps the structural cost problem intact;
- or a package that protects legacy guarantees while margins stay impaired.

That would be a cosmetic compromise, and markets will discount it immediately.

## To management
You are right that the solution must be durable and substantial. But durability must not become code for maximum disruption. The package has to be enforceable and financially meaningful, not just tough-sounding.

If management wants shareholder support, it should bring forward a plan that shows:
- how much overcapacity is removed,
- how much labour cost comes out permanently,
- what the economic impact is by 2027 and 2030,
- and how much investment capacity is restored.

## To the works council and union
You keep asking for “economic equivalence” without closures. Fine — then show us the math.

If you can deliver a package with:
- real capacity reduction,
- real cost reduction,
- and a credible return to competitive margins

without closures, we will listen. But if the package relies mainly on temporary concessions and keeps the footprint basically intact, it is not enough.

## To the regional state government
Regional stability matters, but a protected structure with weak economics is not a long-term solution. The region is better served by a viable company than by a company that delays adjustment until the market forces a worse outcome.

## To the controlling family holding
We support your insistence on enterprise value and competitiveness. But value is not protected by simply insisting that harder measures remain available forever. The final package has to be credible, not just theoretically strict.

If you want capital-markets confidence, the deal needs:
- clear targets,
- clear timing,
- and a credible mechanism if the initial measures do not close the gap.

## Bottom line

Our position remains:
- **Yes** to serious restructuring
- **Yes** to burden-sharing
- **Yes** to investment in Germany
- **No** to cosmetic compromise
- **No** to an outcome that leaves margins structurally impaired

If the next package credibly restores competitiveness, we can support it. If it does not, we will not pretend a politically comfortable arrangement is economically acceptable.

If helpful, we would next want a **quantified scenario comparison**: what savings, capacity reduction, and margin impact each package actually delivers by 2027 and 2030.
