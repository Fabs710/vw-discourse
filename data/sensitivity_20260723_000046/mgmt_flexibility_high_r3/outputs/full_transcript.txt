[Works Council (Betriebsrat) (Plant-level employee representation under co-determination) — Round 1]

- **No compulsory redundancies**
- **No dismantling of the job-security framework**

- closing a German plant
- forced redundancies

If management wants a viable solution, it must come with **binding guarantees for sites and jobs**, not demands for surrender. The workforce will not accept being asked to pay for a crisis it did not create by giving up its future.

[Industrial Union (Trade union and collective-bargaining counterpart) — Round 1]

If the proposal protects jobs and sites and spreads the burden fairly, we are prepared to negotiate seriously.

## 6. Key trade-offs for the decision-maker

1. **Job and site security vs. economic adequacy**

3. **Temporary burden-sharing vs. structural reform**
   - Reversible employee contributions help negotiations, but may not solve a persistent fixed-cost problem.

4. **Governance feasibility vs. operational effectiveness**

5. **Credible fallback vs. trust**
   - Management wants a fallback to preserve leverage and realism; labour sees any fallback as a built-in threat that destroys trust and undermines durable peace.

## 7. Integrative pathways

### Pathway A: Two-stage restructuring with independent review

### Pathway C: Site-preservation with differentiated roles
Rather than treating all sites identically, classify sites by function and future role while keeping them open. Adjust products, volumes, and specialized functions across the footprint, combined with redeployment and investment.

**Value:** could achieve footprint optimization without overt closure language; still requires quantified capacity reduction.

### Pathway D: Managed workforce-transition corridor
A compromise corridor limits forced measures to final-resort conditions with joint triggers and independent review.

**Value:** could satisfy management/family if tightly designed, but labour has already signaled this is likely to be seen as a disguised route to forced cuts.

## 8. Open questions

- What exact savings and capacity reduction are required by 2027 and 2030?
- What is the baseline cost gap, and what portion can non-closure measures realistically close?
- What objective threshold would count as “economic equivalence” to closures/layoffs?
- What binding governance mechanism will the state use to make “last resort” real?
- What concessions are management, family, and investors prepared to make if closures and forced layoffs are removed from the initial package?
- Can a package be designed that preserves all sites while still materially reducing fixed cost and overcapacity?
- What is the legal and industrial-relations risk of any fallback mechanism under co-determination?

---

## 9. Recommended next step

Hold a **quantified scenario comparison session** before any further bargaining on principles. The parties should compare 2–3 packages using the same template:

1. baseline cost/capacity,
2. 2027 target,
3. 2030 target,
4. measures used,
5. whether closures/forced redundancies are included,
6. governance trigger and review mechanism,
7. expected margin impact and implementation risk.

This is the only way to convert the debate from red lines to testable choices.

## 10. Emergent insights that only appeared through multi-turn interaction

1. **The real deadlock is now about admissible instruments, not diagnosis.**  
   Early rounds showed broad agreement on the problem; later rounds revealed the central conflict is whether closures and compulsory redundancies are legitimate bargaining tools or disallowed from the outset.

2. **“Durable” means different things to different sides.**  
   Management and capital mean permanent cost and capacity reduction; labour and the state hear permanent insecurity unless the definition is constrained.

3. **The union and works council are aligned on red lines, but the state is only conditionally aligned.**  
   The state opposes hard cuts politically, yet has not translated that into a concrete governance rule—leaving its “last resort” language ambiguous.

4. **A fallback mechanism itself has become the object of distrust.**  
   What management sees as realism, labour sees as a pre-authored threat that undermines any negotiated concessions.

5. **The company’s governance structure matters as much as the economics.**  
   Because several actors can block, an economically strong but politically unratifiable solution is not viable; this became much clearer only after repeated rounds.

6. **All sides now want quantified proof, but none has supplied it.**  
   The repeated request for 2027/2030 scenario math indicates that the next decisive move is evidentiary, not rhetorical.

7. **There is a possible middle ground around differentiated sites, not equal treatment.**  
   The discussion moved from binary “close/no close” to more workable notions of product allocation, specialization, and capacity rebalancing—an important opening that was not present in the initial positions.
