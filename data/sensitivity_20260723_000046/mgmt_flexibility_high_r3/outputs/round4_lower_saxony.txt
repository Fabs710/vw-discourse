I’ll be direct.

## Where I agree
I agree with all sides on the diagnosis:
- the company has a real competitiveness and overcapacity problem;
- a cosmetic deal is not enough;
- the solution must be durable;
- future investment in Germany is necessary;
- and burden-sharing is unavoidable.

I also agree that a prolonged strike would hurt the company, the workforce, the region, and shareholder value. No one should want that.

## Where I disagree sharply

### 1) I do not accept closures as a starting point
To management, the family holding, and investors: saying closures “remain on the table” is not a neutral technical statement. In practice it becomes leverage against the workforce and the region.

A plant closure is not just another instrument. It is a permanent industrial break with consequences for:
- jobs,
- skills,
- suppliers,
- the regional economy,
- and the company’s social legitimacy.

For a company that is central to the state’s economy and identity, closures in Germany can only ever be a true last resort. Nothing in this discussion has yet shown that all alternatives are exhausted.

So no: I will not accept a package that begins from closures as if they were normal restructuring tools.

### 2) I do not accept compulsory redundancies as the default answer
I also reject the normalization of forced layoffs.

If a restructuring only works by compelling people out, then the company has not yet demonstrated a socially and economically balanced solution. It has only shown that it wants labour to absorb the burden first.

That is not acceptable in a co-determined company with regional responsibility.

### 3) “Durable” must not mean permanent insecurity for labour
Management and capital keep using “durable” to justify permanent cuts on employees. I understand the business logic, but I do not accept a definition of durability that is one-sided.

A durable solution must be durable for:
- the company’s competitiveness,
- the workforce’s security,
- and the region’s industrial base.

If durability means hollowing out trust, weakening co-determination, and making jobs permanently insecure, then the company may save cost in the short term but damage its long-term foundations.

## To management
You say you need structural measures. Fine. I do not deny the problem.

But you still have not demonstrated that:
- every German plant is uneconomic,
- forced redundancies are unavoidable,
- or that the same economic effect cannot be achieved through a serious package of managed alternatives.

You are asking us to accept closures or compelled exits as fallback options before you have proven necessity. That is not a convincing case.

If you want my support, show a quantified comparison:
- what savings are achieved by non-closure measures,
- what capacity can be adjusted through working-time, shifts, product allocation, redeployment, attrition, and voluntary exits,
- and what residual gap remains after those measures.

Without that, closures and compulsory redundancies remain politically and economically unjustified.

## To the works council and union
I largely share your core concerns:
- no closures as a first choice,
- no forced layoffs as a default,
- no dismantling of job-security protections,
- and no one-sided burden transfer.

You are right to insist that any employee contribution must be:
- negotiated,
- temporary,
- reversible where possible,
- and matched by contributions from management and owners.

What I would add is this: the employee side should not simply say “no” to adjustment. If the company is to remain a regional anchor, there must be a credible restructuring path. That means we need serious discussion of:
- working-time adjustments,
- shift and line reconfiguration,
- product allocation,
- internal redeployment,
- voluntary exits,
- natural attrition,
- temporary wage moderation or bonus restraint,
- and binding investment commitments.

But that discussion only makes sense if jobs and sites are genuinely protected first.

## To the regional state government
Your stance is close to mine in principle, but I want to be very clear on the state’s role.

If you say closures and forced redundancies are last resort, then that must mean something real in governance, not just language. The state cannot support a package that starts from irreversible plant cuts and then calls that responsible restructuring.

Use your blocking position accordingly. The state’s responsibility is to secure:
- regional employment,
- industrial continuity,
- co-determination,
- and a viable long-term footprint in Germany.

I will support restructuring. I will not support regional disarmament dressed up as modernization.

## To the controlling family holding and investors
You all keep asking for “economic equivalence.” Fine. Then show it.

But equivalence does **not** mean that closures and forced layoffs are simply the assumed benchmark and everything else must be measured against that. That is a narrow framing.

If you want a durable settlement, then an equivalent package must achieve:
- real savings,
- real capacity adjustment,
- real management and owner contributions,
- and real investment capacity for the future,

without destroying the company’s regional base.

If you cannot show that, then your model is not balanced. It is just more aggressive.

## What I can support
I can support a negotiated restructuring package if it includes:
- **preservation of German sites wherever economically possible**
- **no compulsory redundancies as a first resort**
- **working-time and shift adjustments**
- **product and volume reallocation**
- **internal redeployment**
- **natural attrition and voluntary exits**
- **temporary employee contributions**
- **management-side savings**
- **owner-side contributions**
- **binding investment commitments in Germany through 2030**
- **full co-determination preserved**

That is a serious restructuring path. It may not satisfy everyone’s first preference, but it is the only kind of path I can responsibly support.

## What I cannot support
I cannot support:
- a closure-led restructuring,
- a package built around forced redundancies,
- a deal that weakens co-determination,
- or a settlement that shifts the main burden onto workers while preserving maximum strategic freedom for capital.

That would be socially destructive and politically reckless for the region.

## On the facilitator’s scenarios

### 1) Phased restructuring with a closure fallback
I am skeptical. A “fallback” easily becomes a pre-authorized threat. If the purpose of the test is to prove that non-closure measures are sufficient, then fine.

But I will not endorse a framework that quietly builds in future closures as an automatic next step. If the parties want a test phase, it must be genuinely aimed at avoiding closures, not legitimizing them in advance.

### 2) Workforce-transition corridor
I am more open to this than to a closure fallback, but only if it means:
- voluntary exits first,
- internal redeployment first,
- no compulsory redundancies as the default,
- and strong joint governance over triggers.

If it becomes a technical route to forced cuts, then it is not acceptable.

### 3) Site preservation with differentiated roles
This is the most promising direction, if it means:
- all German plants remain open,
- product allocation is adjusted,
- functions are redistributed,
- investment is targeted,
- and volume is balanced across the footprint.

Differentiation is not a code word for closures. It must be a tool for preserving the company’s industrial base while making it more efficient.

## What would move me
What could genuinely move me is a concrete package with:
- quantified savings from non-closure measures,
- clear capacity adjustments,
- management and owner contributions,
- binding job and site safeguards,
- and investment commitments that secure the German footprint.

That is the kind of deal I could defend politically and economically.

## Bottom line
The company needs restructuring. That is not in dispute.

But I will not support a restructuring that treats plant closures and compulsory redundancies as routine instruments or as the price of participation. The region cannot absorb that, the workforce will not accept that, and the company would damage its own long-term legitimacy.

Bring forward a package that restores competitiveness **without gutting jobs and sites**, and I will engage constructively. Bring forward a closure-led model, and I will oppose it.
