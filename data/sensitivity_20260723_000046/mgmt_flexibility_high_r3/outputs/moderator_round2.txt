## Moderator Summary — Round 2

### 1) What has been agreed
Across all stakeholders, there is broad agreement that:
- the company faces a **real competitiveness and overcapacity problem**;
- a **cosmetic or short-term fix is not sufficient**;
- any solution must be **durable**;
- **future investment in Germany** is necessary;
- **burden-sharing** will be required from more than one side;
- a negotiated outcome is preferable to prolonged conflict, if it can be made credible.

### 2) Where the core disagreements remain
The main disputes are still unresolved on four points:

- **Plant closures**:  
  Management, the owners, and investors keep closures on the table as a possible instrument. The works council, union, and state government reject closures as a starting point and want them treated only as a last resort.

- **Compulsory redundancies**:  
  Employee representatives and the state reject forced layoffs. Management and investors say they cannot rule them out if the economic gap cannot otherwise be closed.

- **How “durable” the solution must be made**:  
  Management and capital-side stakeholders emphasize structural, lasting cost and capacity reduction. Labour and co-determination stakeholders insist that “durable” must not mean permanent insecurity or one-sided concessions from employees.

- **How much can be achieved without structural cutbacks**:  
  Labour and the state argue that alternatives such as working-time changes, redeployment, voluntary exits, and product/capacity shifts should be exhausted first. Management and investors say those measures alone are unlikely to deliver sufficient savings or capacity reduction.

### 3) What key trade-offs are still unaddressed
Several important trade-offs remain unresolved:

- **Job and site security vs. competitiveness**  
  Can the company restore margins without closing sites or forcing layoffs?

- **Temporary concessions vs. structural reform**  
  Are reversible employee contributions enough, or must there be permanent footprint reductions?

- **Social peace vs. speed and scale of restructuring**  
  A more socially protective solution may be slower or smaller; a faster solution may create conflict and resistance.

- **Who bears how much of the burden**  
  The balance between employee contributions, management concessions, owner/investor returns, and possible state influence remains undefined.

- **What “economically equivalent” alternatives would look like**  
  The employee side says alternatives should be tested; management and investors say any alternative must produce equivalent economic effect. That equivalence has not yet been demonstrated.

### 4) Brief refocus for the next round
For Round 3, the discussion should move from positions to **comparative scenarios**. Each side should be asked to identify:

- what level of **capacity reduction** is non-negotiable;
- what forms of **workforce adjustment** they consider acceptable;
- which **employee, management, and owner contributions** they are prepared to make;
- what **binding investment commitments** could accompany any compromise;
- and what concrete combination of measures would count as **economically equivalent** without relying on permanent insecurity.

A useful next step would be to test 2–3 specific restructuring packages against the same criteria: economic effect, social impact, legal/governance feasibility, and implementation risk.
