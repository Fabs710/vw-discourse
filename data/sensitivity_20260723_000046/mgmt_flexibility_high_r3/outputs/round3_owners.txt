We appreciate the sharper framing in Round 3. Let me be equally clear from the controlling shareholder side.

## Where we agree
I agree with management, the state, labour, and the works council on several basics:

- this is a **real competitiveness problem**, not a public-relations issue;
- a **cosmetic** settlement would destroy value;
- the solution must be **durable**;
- **future investment in Germany** is necessary;
- and **burden-sharing** is unavoidable.

I also agree that a prolonged strike, public escalation, or governance breakdown would damage the company and therefore the family holding as well.

## Where I disagree

### 1) “No closures” as a principle is not compatible with stewardship
To the works council, union, and state: I hear the political and social case, but I do **not** accept “every German plant remains open” as a binding starting condition.

If parts of the footprint are structurally overcapacity or materially uncompetitive, then refusing closure in advance is not protection of the company. It is protection of the status quo.

And the status quo is exactly what is eroding margins.

### 2) “No compulsory redundancies” cannot be absolute
I would prefer voluntary exits, attrition, redeployment, and targeted capacity reductions over forced measures. But I will not accept an absolute exclusion if the economic gap cannot otherwise be closed.

If the package only works when management is guaranteed that headcount reduction is off-limits, then the restructuring is not credible.

### 3) Temporary measures alone are not enough
To labour and the state: working-time changes, shift reconfiguration, temporary wage moderation, and bonus restraint may help. But they do not solve a structural cost and capacity problem on their own.

A durable solution must be durable in the economic sense:
- lower fixed cost base,
- better capacity utilisation,
- restored margins,
- and a footprint that can support investment through 2030 and beyond.

## To management
I agree with your core point: the company needs a restructuring that changes the cost curve, not a symbolic reset.

Where I would caution you is this: if the process becomes a maximalist confrontation that triggers a prolonged strike or damages the brand and governance stability, that also destroys value. So yes to structural realism, but not to unnecessary escalation.

If you want my support for harder instruments, you need to show:
- necessity,
- proportionality,
- and a credible future industrial plan for the remaining footprint.

## To the works council
You say closures are not a neutral option. Correct — they are not neutral. They are serious.

But seriousness cuts both ways. If you insist that:
- every plant stays open,
- no compulsory redundancies are possible,
- and the job-security framework cannot be touched,

then I do not see how the cost base becomes competitive. That position effectively asks owners to fund permanence of the current structure even if it is no longer market-conform.

That is not something I can support.

## To the union
I recognize the constructive elements you put forward:
- voluntary exits,
- redeployment,
- working-time flexibility,
- temporary contributions,
- and negotiation rather than pure confrontation.

That is the right direction.

But I cannot accept a line that says closures and compulsory reductions are illegitimate by definition. They are not desirable instruments. They are restructuring instruments. The question is whether they are economically necessary in some form.

If you want to avoid them, bring forward a package that is truly **economically equivalent**, not merely socially easier.

## To the regional state government
I respect the state’s governance role and its responsibility for the region.

But regional stability cannot become a veto on structural adjustment. If the company remains uncompetitive, then the region ultimately loses more through underinvestment, weaker margins, and future job erosion.

That said, I do agree with you on one point: closures and forced layoffs must not be used casually. They should only be pursued if alternatives have genuinely been tested and found insufficient.

## What I would support
I would support a package with these features:

- material reduction in overcapacity;
- structural reduction in labour costs;
- workforce adjustment primarily through voluntary and managed means;
- binding investment commitments in Germany;
- and a credible path to margin recovery.

I am open on the instruments:
- working-time changes;
- shift and line adjustments;
- internal redeployment;
- voluntary exits;
- attrition;
- negotiated employee contributions;
- and, where economically necessary, site consolidation or closure.

## What I will not support
I will **not** support:
- a deal that preserves all sites by principle and calls that restructuring;
- a package that leaves the structural cost problem intact;
- or an arrangement that sacrifices long-term enterprise value to avoid any difficult decision.

## The key test
The real question is not whether we all prefer softer instruments. We do. The question is whether softer instruments can deliver the same economic effect.

If the answer is yes, I am open.  
If the answer is no, then harder measures remain necessary.

So for the next step, I would ask the other sides to stop repeating red lines and instead table a package that shows, in concrete terms:
- how much capacity reduction they accept,
- what labour-cost relief they can deliver,
- what management and owner contributions they expect,
- and what binding guarantees accompany the deal.

Until then, closures and workforce reductions remain on the table, because value preservation requires realism, not slogans.
