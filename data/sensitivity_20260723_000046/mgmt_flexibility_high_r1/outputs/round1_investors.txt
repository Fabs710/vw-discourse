From the perspective of dispersed institutional shareholders, our standard is simple: **we support the path that credibly restores competitive margins and protects long-term enterprise value**. We are not attached to any particular instrument — closures, capacity cuts, working-time changes, or negotiated contributions — but we are highly sensitive to outcomes that leave the cost base structurally too high or the conflict unresolved for too long.

### Where we agree
We agree with management, the family holding, and even the employee side on one point: **a cosmetic settlement is not acceptable**.

The company’s German cost base is clearly under pressure, the European market is weaker, and the brand cannot keep missing its return-on-sales target while hoping for better conditions later. If the restructuring does not materially change the economics, then shareholders will continue to bear the downside through weaker margins, weaker investment capacity, and further valuation erosion.

We also agree that **prolonged industrial conflict is value destructive**. A long strike, uncertainty over implementation, and repeated governance deadlock would be damaging in its own right.

### Where we disagree
We do **not** accept the idea that preserving every plant and all legacy guarantees is compatible with a credible restructuring if the resulting package fails to move costs enough.

The works council and union are right to say workers should not bear the entire burden. But they are not right if that position becomes a de facto veto on material change. If the cost base remains uncompetitive, then the company will not generate the cash needed for future products or the EV transition. That is not in employees’ interest, and it is certainly not in shareholders’ interest.

At the same time, we also do **not** support management pushing for closures simply because that is the cleanest headline solution. If closures trigger a governance blockage, a strike, or a settlement that is delayed and weakened, then the result may be worse than a negotiated alternative. The issue is not whether closures are ideologically attractive; the issue is whether the final package is **credible, durable, and implemented on time**.

### Our position
We will back **whatever solution materially restores competitiveness**, provided it has the following qualities:

1. **Durable savings**
   - Not one-off accounting relief.
   - Not temporary measures that unwind quickly.
   - Real structural cost reduction.

2. **Clear margin improvement**
   - A believable path back to target return-on-sales.
   - Not just “future discussions” or vague efficiency promises.

3. **Implementation certainty**
   - Avoid prolonged conflict that destroys value while negotiations continue.
   - The deal has to be enforceable and timely.

4. **No open-ended burden sharing without results**
   - If employees are asked for concessions, they must translate into measurable and lasting competitiveness gains.
   - If the company asks for flexibility rather than closures, that is fine — but only if the economic effect is comparable.

### On employee red lines
We hear the insistence on no compulsory redundancies and no plant closures. We understand why those are politically and socially important. But from an investor standpoint, those are acceptable only if the alternative package is truly sufficient. If they become absolute barriers to necessary restructuring, then they are not sustainable positions; they are value impairments.

### On management and owners
Management should not treat restructuring as a binary choice between “closures” and “nothing.” There is room for negotiated alternatives, but only if they are financially equivalent in substance.

The family holding and the state should also be careful not to confuse governance stability with economic effectiveness. Blocking a poor deal is reasonable. Blocking all meaningful cost adjustment is not.

### Bottom line
Our support goes to the option that can **credibly deliver durable cost reduction, restore margins, and avoid a drawn-out destructive confrontation**. If that requires closures, we will not object in principle. If that can be achieved without closures through a real and enforceable alternative, we would also support that.

What we will not support is:
- a symbolic compromise,
- a deal that preserves structural overcapacity,
- or a prolonged standoff that keeps destroying value.

Bring forward a package that actually fixes the economics. Without that, none of the stakeholder narratives matter to us.
