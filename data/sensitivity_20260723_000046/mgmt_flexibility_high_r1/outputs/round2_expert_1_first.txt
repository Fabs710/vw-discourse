## 2) What can be inferred about the savings gap

The text does **not** provide numbers, so no exact savings gap can be calculated. Still, several strong inferences are possible:

### Hard inference
Management says:
- German cost base is “well above target” and above competitors.
- The brand is missing its return-on-sales target.
- Structural overcapacity exists.
- Savings must be “durable,” not temporary.

That combination usually means the gap is **not a one-off liquidity issue**, but a **recurring margin problem**. In such cases, the needed adjustment is typically composed of:

1. **Fixed-cost reduction**  
   Plant overhead, maintenance, support functions, management layers.

2. **Capacity correction**  
   Lower output volume and fewer shifts, ideally removing uneconomic capacity.

3. **Labour-cost reduction**  
   Wage moderation, reduced benefits, flexibility, variable-pay restraint.

4. **Workforce reduction**  
   Often the largest durable savings item, either via closures or attrition packages.

### Economic implication
If the company is a large incumbent auto OEM with German manufacturing costs above peer benchmarks, then **small concessions are unlikely to close the gap**. A credible package normally needs to be **material relative to the plant cost base**, not symbolic.

## 3) Closure scenario: economic profile

### What closures do well
Plant closures are the bluntest way to reduce:
- fixed manufacturing overhead,
- depreciation burden on underutilized assets,
- duplicate logistics and support costs,
- and workforce size tied to a site.

They also send a strong signal to capital markets that overcapacity is being removed.

### What closures do poorly
In this case, closures face major implementation frictions:
- **supervisory board two-thirds requirement**
- **co-determination with employee parity**
- **works council veto-like blocking capacity in practice**
- **trade-union strike capability**
- **state shareholder veto/blocking position under special law**
- **family owner influence but not unilateral operational control**

So the closure scenario may be **economically efficient in theory**, but **implementation-constrained in practice**.

### Key risk
A closure plan that is blocked, delayed, or triggers a prolonged strike can destroy value through:
- lost production,
- supplier disruption,
- reputational damage,
- delayed model launches,
- and forced concessions later.

So closure is not automatically “best” unless it is **actually executable**.

## 4) No-closure scenario: what would be required to be credible

A no-closure solution is only credible if it delivers **comparable economic effect**. That means it must be broad and durable.

### Likely components
1. **Working-time and shift flexibility**  
   Useful, but usually not enough alone.

2. **Temporary wage moderation / deferred increases / bonus restraint**  
   Helps cash flow and margins, but if temporary, the effect fades.

3. **Voluntary exits, retirement bridging, attrition**  
   Durable if large enough, but slow.

4. **Capacity rationalization without formal site closure**  
   Fewer lines, fewer shifts, maybe specialization by plant.

5. **Overhead and management cuts**  
   Necessary for credibility, though usually insufficient alone.

6. **Product allocation and investment commitments**  
   Important for labour acceptance and long-term viability, but not itself a cost cut.

### Main limitation
The no-closure route can preserve industrial peace only if the employee contribution is **large, measurable, and durable**. If it relies mainly on temporary relief, it will not solve structural overcapacity.

### Closure-led restructuring
More likely to achieve the needed structural savings **per unit of capacity removed**.

### Negotiated no-closure restructuring
More likely to be **implementable** if:
- the savings requirement is moderate,
- the cost gap can be closed by combined measures,

### My judgment
If management’s diagnosis of “structural overcapacity” is accurate, then **a no-closure package is only viable if it achieves at least a substantial fraction of closure-level savings**, not merely a token compromise.

In practical industrial terms:
- **temporary labour concessions** can help bridge a cycle;

So if the underlying problem is truly structural, the no-closure solution must contain **permanent footprint adjustment**, even if not called “closure.”

## 6) Governance and implementability under German co-determination

This is a major constraint and cannot be wished away.

### Relevant implementation constraints
- **Parity co-determination** on the supervisory board means employee representatives can block or delay major structural decisions unless the required supermajority is achieved.
- A **two-thirds requirement** for plant closure/relocation gives the workforce an effective veto.
- **Works councils** cannot be ignored in operational restructuring; practical implementation depends heavily on negotiated arrangements.
- **Union strike capability** raises the cost of imposed restructuring.
- A **state shareholder with blocking rights** adds another veto layer.
- The **controlling family** can shape strategy but not necessarily force execution against other blocking coalitions.

### Economic meaning
In such a governance structure, the feasible set is not “what is economically best in the abstract,” but “what can be passed, executed, and sustained.”

Therefore:
- **Pure closure strategy** = high theoretical savings, high blocking risk.
- **Pure no-closure strategy** = higher political/industrial feasibility, but may underdeliver economically.
- **Hybrid negotiated strategy** = most plausible path, if it can be made sufficiently deep.

## 7) What a credible hybrid would look like

A viable compromise would usually have four pillars:

1. **Structural capacity reduction**
   - fewer shifts,
   - line consolidation,
   - specialization by site,
   - possibly partial shutdown of functions rather than headline plant closures.

2. **Durable labour-cost contribution**
   - wage moderation,
   - benefit restraint,
   - longer working hours / flexibility,
   - bonus moderation.

3. **Workforce reduction through non-coercive means**
   - voluntary exits,
   - retirement pathways,
   - redeployment,
   - training for transition.

4. **Binding future commitments**
   - investment,
   - product allocation,
   - model pipeline assurance,
   - time-limited protection clauses.

This is the only kind of package that can plausibly reconcile:
- economics,
- labor peace,
- and supervisory-board feasibility.
