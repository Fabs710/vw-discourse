I will be equally direct.

## To management
I agree with you on one point: **this company needs a real structural solution, not a cosmetic patch**. I also agree that temporary measures alone will not be enough if the problem is as deep as you say.

Where I **do not** agree is your attempt to make **German plant closures and compulsory redundancies the default measure of seriousness**.

That is not a neutral economic conclusion. It is a choice with major regional and political consequences, and in this governance structure it is a choice that carries **high execution risk**. If you push it into deadlock, you do not create competitiveness; you create delay, conflict, and value destruction.

You say the employee side has not proven a no-closure package can close the gap. Fair enough. But then you must also stop speaking as if closures are already justified before the plant-level numbers are on the table. Right now, that proof is missing on both sides.

## To the Works Council and Union
I agree with you that:
- cosmetic deals are useless,
- workers should not carry the whole burden,
- and a restructuring that destroys trust will backfire.

I also agree that **a no-closure package can be serious** if it includes:
- reduced shifts,
- voluntary exits,
- productivity gains,
- overhead cuts,
- and binding investment commitments.

Where I **do not** agree is your insistence that every German plant must remain open in the exact same footprint while still expecting the company to regain competitiveness. A legally open plant is not automatically a healthy plant. If there is genuine underutilization, we need to discuss **real capacity reduction inside open sites**.

But let me be clear: I am **not** prepared to support any solution that becomes a disguised closure strategy, a hollowing-out of sites, or a backdoor attack on regional jobs.

## To the controlling family and investors
I understand your concern for durability and implementation certainty. That is legitimate.

But I want to remind you that in this company, **execution is not just a technical issue; it is a governance issue**. A plan that is theoretically efficient and practically ungovernable is not value-creating.

So I do **not** support a simplistic “closures if needed” line. If a negotiated open-site restructuring can deliver credible, durable savings while preserving jobs and avoiding a governance war, then that is the better outcome for long-term value too.

## To the state government
Your position is the one I can support most clearly:
- no closures as the starting point,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- real restructuring,
- and preservation of the industrial base.

But I also want to be honest: if the response to “no closures” is just to freeze everything and hope for the best, that will not work either. The company does need a **measurable** reduction in cost and capacity.

So the state should not back symbolic protection. It should back a **negotiated, quantified, open-site restructuring** that:
- keeps plants in the state,
- preserves co-determination,
- protects jobs as far as possible,
- and still changes the economics.

## My line as anchor shareholder
I will support a tough restructuring **only if it protects the regional industrial base and preserves the state’s co-determination and veto role**.

That means:

- **No German plant closures as the preferred route**
- **No compulsory redundancies**
- **No stripping of co-determination**
- **No deal that makes the state a passive bystander**
- **Yes to serious burden-sharing**
- **Yes to capacity reduction inside open sites if truly necessary**
- **Yes to voluntary exits, working-time flexibility, productivity gains, and overhead cuts**
- **Yes to binding investment and product commitments for German sites**

## On the “plant-level numbers”
This is where I think the table is finally at the right point.

We need a **plant-by-plant comparison** of what the company can actually save without closures, and what remains if formal closures are ruled out.

[SUMMON: neutral plant-level restructuring analyst to model site-by-site savings from reduced shifts, voluntary exits, overhead cuts, productivity gains, and capacity consolidation inside open plants, versus a closure-led scenario.]

That analysis should answer:
1. What is the residual savings gap after open-site measures?
2. Which plants are structurally underloaded?
3. How much capacity can be removed without closures?
4. What is the real implementation risk of closure versus open-site restructuring?

Until we have that, no one should claim the closure route is proven necessary — and no one should claim the open-site route is automatically sufficient.

## What would move me
I am open to a negotiated solution if it meets three conditions:
1. it **preserves jobs and plants in the state**,
2. it **restores competitiveness in a durable way**,
3. and it **retains our co-determination and veto role**.

If a hybrid package can do that, I will engage constructively.

If it cannot, then I will not be bullied into endorsing plant closures that damage the region and weaken the company’s long-term anchor role.

## Bottom line
I am **not** here to block change for its own sake.

I am here to ensure that the company is restructured in a way that is:
- economically credible,
- socially durable,
- regionally responsible,
- and compatible with the governance structure that protects workers and the public interest.

So yes, restructure — but **not by gutting the regional industrial base**.
