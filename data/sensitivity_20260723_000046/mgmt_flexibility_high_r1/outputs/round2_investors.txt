From the perspective of dispersed institutional shareholders, the discussion is now at the point where rhetoric has to turn into a **credible economic package**. We are not here to endorse slogans, legacy protections, or symbolic burden-sharing. We are here for one thing: **risk-adjusted value creation through a durable return to competitive margins**.

## Where I agree

I agree with management, the family holding, and even the employee side on one essential point: **a cosmetic compromise would be value-destructive**.

I also agree that **prolonged conflict is unacceptable**. A long strike, supervisory-board deadlock, or a deal that collapses after months of posturing would burn shareholder value quickly.

And I agree that the company cannot keep missing its return-on-sales target while carrying an uncompetitive German cost base. If that problem is not fixed, future investment, product development, and ultimately enterprise value all suffer.

## Where I disagree

### 1) I do not accept “no closures” as an absolute if it prevents a real fix
The Works Council and union keep treating no closures and no compulsory redundancies as non-negotiable. I understand the political and social reasons, but from a shareholder standpoint, that position is only acceptable **if** the alternative package is economically equivalent.

If it is not equivalent, then it is not a solution; it is a delay.

So I disagree with any stance that effectively says: preserve all sites and guarantees first, then hope the margins improve somehow later. That is not disciplined restructuring.

### 2) I do not accept closures as a default either
That said, I also do **not** support management acting as if plant closures are automatically the cleanest answer. In this governance structure, a closure-led approach that triggers blocking rights, labor resistance, or a drawn-out conflict may destroy more value than it creates.

So the question is not “closures or not” in the abstract. The question is: **which package actually delivers durable savings and margin improvement on a credible implementation path?**

### 3) I do not accept open-ended concessions without measurable outcomes
Employees keep saying contributions must be temporary and tied to guarantees. That may be politically understandable, but from an investor perspective we need **durable cost reduction**, not concessions that unwind before the next cycle.

If the package is mostly temporary wage moderation, flexible hours, or voluntary measures with no real capacity effect, then it will not move the economics enough. And if it does not move the economics enough, it will not command shareholder support.

## What would move us

We would support **whatever package credibly restores competitiveness**, but it must meet four tests:

1. **Durability**
   - Savings must last.
   - Temporary relief alone is not enough.

2. **Materiality**
   - The measures must be large enough to close the margin gap.
   - Symbolic savings do not count.

3. **Implementation certainty**
   - The deal has to be executable without endless conflict.
   - A blocked or delayed deal is not value-neutral; it destroys value.

4. **Clear path to margin recovery**
   - We need a believable route back to target return-on-sales.
   - Not vague future efficiency promises.

## My view on the current positions

### To management
You are right to insist on **real structural savings**. But you need to be more specific. Repeating that the cost base is too high is not enough.

Put the numbers on the table:
- exact savings gap,
- expected savings from labour,
- expected savings from capacity reduction,
- expected savings from overhead and productivity,
- and the remaining shortfall if closures are avoided.

Without that, you are asking investors to choose between competing narratives rather than competing economics.

### To the Works Council and union
You are right that workers should not absorb all the pain. But your red lines only make sense if you can show a package that is economically comparable to closure-based restructuring.

If you cannot, then your position amounts to protecting the status quo at the expense of enterprise value. We cannot support that.

### To the state government
Your insistence on regional stability is understandable and legitimate. But regional stability is not protected by preserving structurally uncompetitive operations indefinitely. If the business remains weak, the long-term regional damage will be worse.

### To the family holding
You are right to emphasize durability and governance stability. But we should not confuse stability with inertia. Blocking a bad closure plan is reasonable. Blocking meaningful cost adjustment is not.

## Our position

We will support **the option that credibly delivers durable cost reduction, restores margins, and avoids a prolonged destructive confrontation**.

That could mean:
- closures, if they are truly necessary and implementable,
- or a negotiated no-closure package, if it is genuinely equivalent in economic effect.

What we will **not** support is:
- a symbolic compromise,
- a deal that preserves structural overcapacity,
- or a settlement that leaves the cost base uncompetitive and margins impaired.

## What we need next

Before anyone claims victory, I want a quantified comparison of the options:
- How much savings is required?
- What does each option deliver?
- Which option is durable through 2030?
- Which option is actually implementable under the governance constraints?

If the answer is still “we want protection first and numbers later,” then we are nowhere near a shareholder-supportable outcome.

If needed, I would also suggest a neutral financial and operational analysis to compare the alternatives.
