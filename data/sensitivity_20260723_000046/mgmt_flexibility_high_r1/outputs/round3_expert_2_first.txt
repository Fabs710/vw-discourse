From a plant-level restructuring and manufacturing economics perspective, the key question is:

**Can a no-closure package plausibly deliver savings comparable to a closure-led restructuring, while avoiding forced layoffs?**

My assessment: **yes, sometimes partially; rarely fully, and only if the underlying gap is not dominated by true fixed-cost overcapacity.** The answer depends on the size and composition of the required savings.

## 1) What a no-closure package can realistically deliver

A negotiated package without plant closures can usually produce savings from five buckets:

### A. Working-time adjustments
Examples:
- temporary reduction in weekly hours,
- shorter shifts,
- removal or deferral of overtime,
- flexibility in shift systems,
- temporary sabbaticals / short-time style arrangements.

**Typical impact:** medium in the short term, but limited in permanence unless paired with structural workforce reduction.  
**Economic effect:** helps absorb volume decline, but does not fully remove fixed plant overhead.

### B. Voluntary exits
Examples:
- early retirement,
- severance-supported voluntary departure,
- internal transfer to other sites or functions,
- attrition control.

**Typical impact:** meaningful if the company has an aging workforce or surplus indirect staff.  
**Economic effect:** can reduce headcount without compulsory redundancies, but the savings arrive gradually and usually require upfront cash costs.

### C. Overhead reduction
Examples:
- plant management layer reduction,
- central support streamlining,
- procurement savings,
- maintenance and service renegotiation,
- reduction of indirect labor,
- postponement of nonessential spending.

**Typical impact:** real, but often smaller than promised in labor disputes.  
**Economic effect:** can contribute materially, but usually not enough on its own to solve a major capacity problem.

### D. Productivity gains
Examples:
- higher line utilization,
- lower scrap and rework,
- better labor productivity,
- standardization,
- reduced changeover times,
- improved logistics and material flow.

**Typical impact:** potentially significant, but requires operational discipline and time.  
**Economic effect:** very valuable, but not a substitute for eliminating structurally excess capacity if the market has permanently shrunk.

### E. Capacity changes without closure
Examples:
- one-shift operation instead of two,
- product-line consolidation within a site,
- mothballing of lines,
- shifting some models to other plants,
- conversion of a plant from full production to component, SKD, or niche production.

**Typical impact:** this is the most important lever if closures are off the table.  
**Economic effect:** can remove a substantial portion of fixed cost while preserving the site, but only if the site can be economically downsized and the remaining volume is sufficient to cover residual fixed costs.

---

## 2) What a closure-led restructuring saves that a no-closure package often does not

A plant closure eliminates:
- direct labor tied to the site,
- local overhead,
- maintenance and utilities,
- depreciation absorption burden from underutilization,
- logistics and internal complexity,
- and, crucially, **structural fixed cost that remains even after headcount cuts**.

That is the main accounting distinction.

If a plant is genuinely **subscale** relative to its cost base and product volume, then keeping it open with fewer people often still leaves:
- too much building cost,
- too much utility and maintenance cost,
- too many indirect roles,
- too much line complexity,
- and poor fixed-cost absorption.

In that case, a no-closure solution can lower labor cost, but the **unit cost** may remain uncompetitive.

---

## 3) The central test: is the problem labor cost, or excess fixed capacity?

This is the decisive analytical point.

### If the main problem is:
- wage level,
- too much indirect labor,
- excess paid time,
- overtime inefficiency,
- and some overhead bloat,

then a no-closure package can often capture a large share of the needed savings.

### If the main problem is:
- permanent volume decline,
- too many sites for the expected output,
- duplicated production footprints,
- underutilized lines and buildings,
- and a product mix that no longer supports the installed base,

then no-closure measures usually **do not** fully close the gap.

In automotive manufacturing, a plant that is operating far below designed volume can have a very high fixed-cost burden per vehicle. In that case, a negotiated labor package may reduce cash cost, but it often does **not** restore competitiveness to target.

---

## 4) What “comparable savings” would actually require

To be comparable to a closure-led plan, a no-closure package would need all of the following:

1. **A substantial headcount reduction**, preferably largely through voluntary means  
2. **A durable reduction in paid hours or labor cost per vehicle**  
3. **Material overhead cuts at both plant and corporate level**  
4. **A significant reduction in installed capacity usage, even if the site remains open**  
5. **A credible product and volume commitment** to keep the remaining footprint efficient  
6. **An enforceable mechanism** to ensure savings are not temporary

Without all six, the package is usually inferior to closure on pure cost economics.

---

## 5) Likely plant-level outcome if closures are excluded

If German plant closures are politically or governance-wise off the table, then the realistic alternative is usually:

- one or more sites move to **reduced-shift or reduced-model** operation,
- indirect headcount falls,
- voluntary exits do most of the workforce reduction,
- labor cost is moderated for a defined period,
- some investment is committed to keep the remaining sites productive,
- and the company accepts that savings are **partial**, not perfect.

That can be enough if the required gap is moderate.

It is less likely to be enough if management is trying to remove a very large structural cost gap quickly.

---

## 6) My judgment on feasibility

### Feasible:
A no-closure package can work if:
- the required savings are moderate,
- the company can absorb gradual headcount reduction,
- there is enough remaining volume to keep open sites efficient,
- and management is willing to accept phased restructuring rather than immediate footprint elimination.

### Not fully feasible:
A no-closure package is unlikely to match a closure-led plan if:
- one or more plants are deeply underutilized,
- the savings target is very large and immediate,
- and the company needs a sharp reduction in fixed manufacturing cost.

---

## 7) Accounting and operational implication

From an accounting standpoint:
- **closures** create restructuring charges but can improve future cost base materially;
- **voluntary exit programs** also create one-time charges, but often less permanent relief than a closure;
- **temporary labor measures** reduce current-period expense but do not solve structural underabsorption;
- **capacity reductions without closure** may improve economics if they reduce fixed-cost burden enough, but only if the residual operation remains efficient.

So the core issue is not whether savings exist. It is whether the package:
- removes enough fixed cost,
- is durable,
- and leaves a viable operating model.

---

## Bottom line

**A no-closure, no-forced-layoff package can deliver meaningful savings, but it usually cannot match the full economic effect of closing structurally excess plants unless the overcapacity is modest or the remaining footprint can be sharply downsized.**
