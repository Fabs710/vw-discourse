Yes. From a plant-level restructuring and accounting perspective, the central issue is whether the company can remove enough **fixed cost and underutilized capacity** without formal closures to make the German footprint economically viable.

## Bottom line

A **no-closure package can be viable** only if it does all of the following:

1. **Cuts installed capacity materially**  
   Not just wages — actual shifts, lines, and output capacity.

2. **Reduces labor cost durably**  
   Through voluntary exits, working-time changes, wage moderation, bonus restraint, and/or benefit adjustments.

3. **Lowers overhead and site fixed costs**  
   Management layers, support functions, maintenance footprint, logistics inefficiencies, and plant-level indirect labor.

4. **Improves productivity measurably**  
   Higher output per head, better line efficiency, fewer indirect hours, better utilization.

If those four items do not close most of the gap, then a no-closure solution is not economically equivalent to closure-led restructuring.

## What the likely economic trade-off looks like

### 1) Closure-led restructuring
**Strengths**
- Largest permanent reduction in fixed cost.
- Best way to remove chronic underutilization.
- Strongest accounting impact on cost per vehicle if volume is consolidated.

**Weaknesses**
- High one-time restructuring charges:
  - severance,
  - asset impairment,
  - decommissioning,
  - contract termination,
  - remediation.
- High execution risk under the current governance structure.
- More likely to trigger delay, conflict, and value leakage before savings arrive.

### 2) No-closure open-site restructuring
**Strengths**
- Lower governance and social shock.
- Can be implemented if negotiated.
- Can preserve optionality and avoid major impairment charges tied to full site shutdowns.

**Weaknesses**
- Savings can be too soft if they rely mainly on temporary concessions.
- Fixed costs may remain embedded if plants stay open with low utilization.
- Risk of “hollowed-out” sites that are legally open but economically weak.

### 3) Hybrid package
This is the most credible route operationally if closure is blocked:
- keep plants formally open,
- remove shifts and consolidate models,
- reduce installed capacity,
- use voluntary exits and labor-cost relief,
- bind the deal to measurable savings and volume assumptions.

This is usually the best compromise **if** the resulting economics are close enough to the closure benchmark.

## Plant-level economics: what matters most
At plant level, the decisive variables are:

- **utilization rate**
- **fixed cost per unit**
- **direct labor hours per vehicle**
- **indirect labor burden**
- **energy and logistics cost**
- **asset base and depreciation load**
- **one-time restructuring cost vs. annual run-rate savings**

A plant with low utilization and high fixed overhead cannot be made competitive by wages alone.  
If the structure remains underloaded, the company is carrying excess installed capacity.

## Accounting treatment implications
If the company pursues restructuring, the accounting effects depend on the path:

### If plants close
Likely recognition of:
- restructuring provisions,
- severance obligations,
- impairment of PPE,
- inventory write-downs if product mix changes,
- contract termination costs,
- decommissioning liabilities.

### If plants stay open but capacity is reduced
Potentially lower immediate impairment, but still:
- restructuring provisions for workforce measures,
- OPEX savings over time,
- possible impairment if assets become underutilized,
- and potentially higher depreciation burden per unit if capacity is not fully absorbed.

A no-closure package can look cleaner in the short term, but if it leaves stranded assets, the economics may still deteriorate through low utilization and weak margin recovery.
