Let me respond plainly, because the gap between the sides is still too wide.

## To the Works Council and the Union
I agree with one thing: **a cosmetic deal is worthless**. If we do not materially reduce our cost base and capacity, we will continue to fall behind, and then everyone loses — including the workforce.

But I strongly disagree with your core line that **“no plant closures and no compulsory redundancies” can be an absolute starting point**. That is not a restructuring position; it is a veto on the structural change the business needs.

You ask whether we have demonstrated that closures are the only way. I am saying something more precise: **closures are not the only instrument, but the final package must deliver the same economic effect**. If your alternative does not remove enough cost and capacity, then it is not comparable, full stop.

### On your demand for numbers
Fair enough — let’s talk numbers.

We need:
- a **durable reduction in the cost base**,
- a **real reduction in overcapacity**,
- and a package that restores the brand’s path to target returns so we can fund investment to 2030.

What I am not prepared to do is publish a false comfort plan that pretends temporary working-time tricks or small wage concessions solve a structural gap. They do not.

If the employee side wants to avoid closures, then you need to bring forward measures that are **material enough to close the gap**. That means:
- substantial labour-cost relief,
- meaningful capacity consolidation,
- productivity gains that are enforceable, not aspirational,
- and a cost profile that is competitive versus our European peers and lower-cost competitors.

If you cannot do that, then your “no closures” position is simply asking management, owners, and investors to accept a permanently uncompetitive footprint.

### On job guarantees through 2030
I will not bind the company to unconditional guarantees that freeze the structure regardless of market reality. That would lock in the current problem and leave us weaker in 2028 or 2029 than we are today.

If there is to be protection, it must be **conditional on a package that actually changes the economics**. You cannot ask for total site security while refusing the depth of change required to make that security affordable.

### On your claim of “one-sided burden”
I do agree that the workforce should not be the only contributor. I have said that already. But you are overplaying the owners-versus-workers contrast.

The real issue is this: **there is not enough margin left in the current structure**. If we do not reduce recurring cost meaningfully, there will be less money for product, software, batteries, and future investment. That is not a shareholder slogan; it is arithmetic.

So yes, we can discuss:
- temporary labour-cost moderation,
- working-time flexibility,
- productivity measures,
- voluntary exits,
- redeployment,
- and phased capacity reductions.

But no, I will not accept a package that leaves all German sites untouched, preserves the old guarantees in full, and calls that a solution.

## To the State Government
I appreciate that you reject a one-sided social dump. I also appreciate that you recognize the need for competitiveness. But you cannot have it both ways: **protecting every site exactly as is and restoring competitiveness at the same time**.

A responsible state shareholder should help secure a durable industrial future, not defend an unsustainable structure until the business is forced into a worse adjustment later.

If you want German production protected, then support a deal that actually makes it viable. That means real cost reduction, not just political reassurance.

## To the Family Holding and Investors
I agree with your general point: value destruction from endless conflict is unacceptable. But I want to be equally direct back to you: backing closures as a default answer is not automatically “discipline.” If the governance structure blocks implementation or leads to a prolonged strike, then you have not solved anything.

That said, I do share your insistence that the result must be **durable and economically real**. A compromise that looks socially acceptable but leaves the economics broken will fail. I will not support that either.

## Where I stand
Here is the line from management:

1. **We need structural savings, not symbolic gestures.**
2. **We need capacity reduction, not just promises of productivity.**
3. **We need a labour-cost contribution that is material and durable.**
4. **We can negotiate the instruments, but not the necessity of change.**
5. **We cannot accept unconditional site guarantees that prevent restructuring.**

## What would move me
A credible alternative could move me — but only if it is genuinely equivalent in economic effect.

That would mean a package such as:
- significant and enforceable labour-cost relief,
- measurable capacity reductions,
- credible productivity commitments,
- voluntary and phased workforce reductions,
- and a governance-backed implementation path that delivers savings on time.

What will not move me is:
- a purely temporary compromise,
- a promise of “future dialogue,”
- or a package that keeps all structural overcapacity in place.

## Bottom line
I am not bluffing, and I am not looking for confrontation for its own sake. But I am also not going to pretend that the current footprint is competitive enough to carry into the 2030 investment cycle without deep change.

So the question is simple:

**Can the employee side put a package on the table that removes comparable cost and capacity without closures?**

If yes, I am willing to test it.  
If no, then you are not offering an alternative — you are blocking the restructuring the company needs.
