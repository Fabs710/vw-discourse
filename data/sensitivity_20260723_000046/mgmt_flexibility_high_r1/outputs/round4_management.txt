Let me respond plainly.

## To the Works Council and the Union
I hear your red lines:
- no plant closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- no dismantling of the collective framework,
- no hollowing-out of co-determination.

I understand why you are taking that position. But I do **not** accept it as a basis for solving the problem.

You keep saying management has not proven closures are necessary. I am telling you the reverse: **you have not yet shown that a no-closure package can deliver the structural, durable savings this company needs.** Temporary concessions, reduced shifts, voluntary exits, and productivity promises are not enough unless they close the fixed-cost gap in a lasting way.

A deal that preserves every site but leaves the cost base uncompetitive is not a solution. It is postponement.

## On your demand for “plant-level numbers”
You are right about one thing: the discussion must be quantified.

That is exactly why I am not accepting slogans like “no closures by principle” or “the workforce will somehow carry the burden if needed.” We need a **plant-by-plant, site-by-site** comparison showing:
- current fixed cost,
- expected volume,
- residual utilization,
- labour-cost contribution,
- overhead contribution,
- and what remains after open-site restructuring.

If the employee side believes every German plant can stay open and still get the company back to target, then show the numbers. Not assertions. Numbers.

## To the State Government
I agree with you on one point: a socially destructive, value-destroying shock would be unacceptable. We do not want a wrecking ball either.

Where I disagree is your tendency to treat “no closures” as a principle that somehow resolves the economics. It does not. The state cannot wish away overcapacity, shrinking demand, and a cost base that is above target.

If the state wants to protect the industrial base, then it should support a package that is **actually competitive**, not one that preserves appearances while the business continues to underperform.

## To the controlling family and investors
I agree with you on the need for:
- durable savings,
- implementation certainty,
- and a real path back to margin recovery.

Where I disagree is any assumption that a no-closure package is automatically preferable because it sounds gentler. It is not preferable if it leaves us with the same structural problem in two years.

And let me be clear: I do **not** need closure for its own sake. I need a **structural reset**. If the employee side can produce a package that is genuinely equivalent in economic effect, I will consider it. But “equivalent” means:
- real capacity reduction,
- durable labour-cost relief,
- overhead cuts,
- and enforceable implementation.

Not a temporary pause.

## My view on the expert analysis
The expert said what matters most: if the gap is moderate, a hybrid may work; if the gap is large and driven by structural overcapacity, then closures or equivalent footprint reduction become economically stronger.

That is exactly the point.

We are not arguing over whether restructuring is needed. We are arguing over whether the scale of the problem can be solved **without formal closures**. I remain skeptical, because the plant-level evidence has not been produced.

## What I will accept
I remain open to a negotiated package if it is strong enough. That means I can discuss:
- working-time flexibility,
- voluntary exits,
- redeployment,
- productivity measures,
- overhead reduction,
- reduced-shift operation,
- product and investment commitments,
- and time-limited labour-cost relief.

But only if the package delivers **material, durable savings** and reduces capacity in a way that genuinely changes the cost base.

## What I will not accept
Let me be explicit:
- no cosmetic compromise,
- no package that leaves the German footprint effectively intact,
- no permanent protection of an uncompetitive structure,
- no deal that asks management to sign up to irreversibility while labour keeps all options open,
- no outcome that freezes the current cost base and calls it “socially responsible.”

## On “closure by stealth”
I am not interested in relabelling tricks. If the alternative package delivers the same economic effect as closures through reduced shifts, model consolidation, and site-level capacity reduction, then it is the economic substance — not the label — that matters.

That said, if the no-closure solution merely delays the same problem, then it is not equivalent and I will not pretend otherwise.

## What needs to happen next
We need the data. Specifically:

[SUMMON: neutral plant-level operations and restructuring analyst to model the residual savings gap by German site under three scenarios: closure-led, no-closure with open-site capacity reduction, and hybrid package with time-limited labour concessions.]

I want a clear answer to four questions:
1. What savings are achievable without formal closures?
2. How much capacity can be removed while keeping plants open?
3. What residual gap remains versus the target?
4. Which sites are structurally underloaded even after open-site restructuring?

Until then, nobody should claim that closures are either proven necessary or proven unnecessary.

## Bottom line
My position remains unchanged:
- we need a **durable structural reduction in cost and capacity**,
- I am open on the instrument,
- but I am **not** open to a compromise that leaves the company uncompetitive.

If the no-closure camp can close the gap with a real, enforceable, plant-level package, I will consider it.

If not, then the company will have to face the uncomfortable reality that serious restructuring cannot be done by wishful thinking alone.
