I appreciate the clarity from all sides. Let me be equally clear from the perspective of the controlling shareholders: **we will not back a solution that merely postpones the competitiveness problem, nor will we support a value-destructive confrontation that weakens the company for years to come.**

### Where I agree
I agree with management that the cost base and capacity situation are real, and that the company cannot continue on the current trajectory and still fund future investment in product, software, and EV competitiveness.

I also agree with the works council, the union, and the state on one important point: **a durable outcome has to be negotiated and stable**. Endless conflict would destroy value faster than any restructuring plan. Labour peace matters.

### Where I disagree
I do **not** agree with a posture of absolute red lines that would make any meaningful restructuring impossible.

- “No closures, ever” is not a serious answer if some sites are structurally uneconomic.
- “No forced redundancies” may be a legitimate preference, but if the business cannot adapt at all, then the burden simply shifts into worse outcomes later: declining margins, weaker investment, and ultimately greater job loss through erosion.
- Equally, I do **not** support management using restructuring language as a blank cheque to impose a one-sided cut that damages the company’s industrial legitimacy and triggers long conflict.

### What matters to us
Our priority is not cosmetic consensus; it is **long-term enterprise value**. That means:

1. **A credible and durable restoration of margins**
2. **A structurally lower cost base**
3. **Enough flexibility to invest in future products**
4. **No outcome that permanently impairs the enterprise or undermines governance stability**

If there is a path to achieve those aims **without** broad plant closures and without a destructive labour dispute, we are open to it. But it must be economically real, not politically comforting.

### On the employee side
If workers and their representatives want to avoid closures and compulsory layoffs, then they need to accept that this cannot be achieved by slogans alone. There must be a **material contribution** on labour cost and capacity utilization, not just symbolic gestures.

That means I would look favorably on:
- working-time flexibility,
- temporary labour-cost moderation,
- productivity improvements,
- capacity consolidation,
- and a package that supports future investment.

What I would **not** find acceptable is a settlement that preserves all structures unchanged, protects every historical guarantee, and simply asks ownership to absorb the loss. That would not be stewardship; it would be value destruction.

### On management
I also want to be direct with management: if you want support for a difficult restructuring, you must show that the plan is not just about force, but about **durability**. If you push too hard for closures without exhausting negotiated alternatives, you risk creating a fight that the governance structure can block and that the company may not recover from quickly.

### Bottom line
The family holding is prepared to support a tough restructuring **if it restores competitiveness and protects the long-term value of the company**. We are **not** prepared to support:
- a purely symbolic compromise,
- an open-ended conflict,
- or a package that breaks governance stability or permanently damages the enterprise.

So the burden now is to produce a solution that is genuinely competitive in economic terms and durable in labour terms. If that cannot be done, then I will not pretend otherwise: we will not endorse an arrangement that fails to fix the underlying problem.
