I will be equally direct.

## My position
The state government will **not** support a solution that includes **German plant closures** as the default or as a disguised ultimatum. We also will **not** support compulsory redundancies as the price of management’s strategy. That remains our line.

At the same time, I do **not** accept a purely defensive posture that leaves the company’s cost base untouched. The company does need a durable restructuring. The question is **how** to do it without gutting the regional industrial base.

## Where I agree
I agree with management, the investors, and the family holding on one central point:  
**a cosmetic deal would be irresponsible**.

I also agree with the expert assessment that:
- temporary concessions alone are unlikely to solve a structural problem,
- a hybrid package is the most plausible route,
- and implementation certainty matters.

So yes: we need more than gestures. We need a package that actually changes the economics.

## Where I disagree
### With management
I disagree with the way you present closures as the benchmark for seriousness.

You have not shown that you have exhausted serious alternatives. You keep saying the employee side must prove a no-closure package can work. That is too convenient. If closures are being used as leverage, then management must also prove:
- the exact savings gap,
- what portion comes from labour,
- what portion from management and overhead,
- what portion from capacity reduction,
- and what residual gap truly cannot be closed without plant shutdowns.

Until those numbers are laid out transparently, closures remain a **threatened option**, not a justified necessity.

And I am not prepared to lend the state’s political or governance weight to a closure-first approach.

### With the Works Council and union
I agree with your rejection of plant closures and forced layoffs. That is right.

But I do **not** accept that the answer is simply “no closures, full guarantees, and the rest will somehow work itself out.” That is not enough to convince anyone that the company remains competitive through 2030.

If the employee side wants my support, then I need to see willingness for:
- meaningful working-time flexibility,
- voluntary exits,
- reduced-shift or reduced-model operation where necessary,
- temporary and fair labour-cost relief,
- productivity improvements,
- and capacity adjustment that is real, not rhetorical.

What I will not support is a package that preserves every structure exactly as-is while calling that a negotiated solution.

## What the state can support
I am prepared to back a **serious negotiated restructuring** if it meets these conditions:

1. **No German plant closures**
2. **No compulsory redundancies**
3. **Binding protection for jobs and sites through 2030**
4. **Real burden-sharing from management, owners, and employees**
5. **Material capacity adjustment without shutting plants**
6. **Investment and product commitments for German sites**
7. **Preservation of co-determination and the state’s veto role**

That is not a “nice to have.” That is the minimum basis for legitimacy in this company.

## On the expert’s hybrid logic
I think the expert is right to say that a hybrid is the most plausible path.

But let me sharpen that:  
A hybrid is acceptable **only if it is socially responsible and economically real**.

That means:
- not just temporary wage restraint,
- not just promises of future efficiency,
- but a package with measurable, durable savings.

However, those savings must come from **structural adjustments short of closures**:
- reduced shifts,
- capacity consolidation within sites,
- voluntary exits,
- redeployment,
- overhead cuts,
- productivity gains,
- and a fair time-limited employee contribution.

If management insists that only plant closures count as real restructuring, then that is not a neutral economic claim. It is a choice to prefer the most destructive tool.

## To the investors
You are right to insist on durability and implementation certainty.

But I will remind you: a plan that is theoretically superior and politically impossible is not superior in practice. In this governance structure, execution is part of value. If a closure-led plan leads to deadlock, strikes, and months of conflict, then it destroys the very value it claims to create.

So yes, we need durable savings. But we need them in a form that can actually be implemented without blowing up the company’s social compact.

## To the controlling family
I agree with your point that the deal must be enforceable and not just symbolic.

Where I differ is that you should not equate enforceability with closures. A negotiated no-closure restructuring can be enforceable if it is built properly:
- clear metrics,
- time-bound obligations,
- binding commitments on both sides,
- and board-level monitoring.

That is the path I support.

## What I need to see next
If management and capital want the state to engage constructively, then bring forward a quantified comparison of:
- closure-led restructuring,
- no-closure negotiated restructuring,
- and a hybrid compromise.

For each, we need:
- expected savings,
- duration,
- effect on capacity,
- implementation risk,
- and impact on jobs and sites in this state.

## My bottom line
The state will **back a tough deal**, but not a demolition plan.

I am open to a restructuring that:
- preserves the company as a regional anchor,
- protects jobs and sites as far as possible,
- reduces costs materially,
- and keeps co-determination intact.

I am **not** open to a settlement that treats German plants as disposable or the state’s role as decorative.
