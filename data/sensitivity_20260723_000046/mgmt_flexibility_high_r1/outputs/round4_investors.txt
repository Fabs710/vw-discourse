We are aligned on the diagnosis, but not yet on the remedy.

## What we agree on
- The company needs a **durable structural reset**.
- A **cosmetic deal** would be value-destructive.
- **Prolonged conflict** would also destroy value.
- Temporary measures alone are unlikely to be enough if the problem is structural.

That is the right framing.

## Where we disagree

### 1) To the Works Council and Union
Your insistence on **no plant closures, no compulsory redundancies, and binding job protection through 2030** is understandable from a workforce perspective, but it is **not yet enough for shareholders**.

We are not ideological about closures. We do **not** want closures for their own sake. But we also will **not** support a package that leaves the company with an uncompetitive cost base simply because it avoids hard choices.

So far, you have not shown that an open-site restructuring can deliver:
- equivalent durable savings,
- enough capacity reduction,
- and a credible return to target margins.

If you want us to back a no-closure solution, it has to be **economically equivalent**, not just socially preferable.

### 2) To management
We agree with your diagnosis, but you still have not given a **quantified bridge** that proves closures are necessary.

You keep saying “structural overcapacity” and “durable savings,” which may be true. But from an investor standpoint, that is not enough. We need the actual comparison:
- savings gap,
- labour contribution,
- management/overhead contribution,
- productivity contribution,
- capacity reduction contribution,
- and the residual shortfall if closures are excluded.

Until that is on the table, closure remains an option, not a proven necessity.

### 3) To the State Government
We agree that a destructive closure-first path could create deadlock and value destruction.

But we also cannot support a position that effectively freezes the industrial footprint and calls that competitiveness. The state’s preference for regional stability matters, but it does not substitute for a viable cost base.

### 4) To the controlling family
You are right that implementation risk matters. A neat plan that cannot pass governance is not value-creating.

But the same logic cuts both ways: a no-closure plan that is too weak to restore margins is also not value-creating. We need the numbers, not just the politics.

## Our position
From a capital-markets perspective, the only acceptable outcome is one that credibly restores:
- **competitive margins**,
- **durable cost discipline**,
- and **implementation certainty**.

That means we are open to:
1. a closure-led package, **if** it is truly implementable and economically superior;
2. a no-closure package, **if** it produces comparable durable savings;
3. a hybrid, **if** it is substantial enough to close the gap.

But we are **not** open to:
- a symbolic compromise,
- an arrangement that preserves structural overcapacity,
- or a deal that leaves the company competitively impaired.

## What would move us
A serious no-closure package could still earn our support if it includes:
- material permanent capacity reduction inside open sites,
- meaningful voluntary exits,
- credible overhead cuts,
- real productivity gains,
- durable labour-cost relief,
- and binding enforcement to 2030.

If that package closes the gap, then fine — closures are not required.

If it does **not** close the gap, then shareholders should not be asked to pretend that preserving every site is the same as fixing the business.

## What we need now
Not more slogans. Not more red lines alone. We need a **side-by-side quantified comparison** of:
- closure-led restructuring,
- open-site no-closure restructuring,
- and a hybrid compromise.
