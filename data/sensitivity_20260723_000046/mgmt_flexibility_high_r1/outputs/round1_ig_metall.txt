I agree with the core line from the Works Council: **German plant closures and compulsory redundancies are not acceptable**. That is not a marginal issue; it is a fundamental red line.

From the union side, the situation is serious and we understand that the company needs a durable competitiveness solution. But that solution cannot be built on one-sided sacrifices from the workforce. If management wants a restructuring that lasts, then it must be a **shared-sacrifice package** with real contributions from owners and management as well.

Where I am aligned:
- **No plant closures**
- **No forced layoffs**
- **Protection of the collective wage framework**
- **Binding future investment and production commitments**
- **Capacity adjustment through negotiated, reversible measures rather than destruction of sites**

Where I disagree with management’s current approach:
- “Structural overcapacity” is not a blank cheque for closing plants.
- Cutting jobs first and hoping for acceptance later is not a credible industrial strategy.
- Asking employees to pay for the crisis while leaving ownership and management contributions vague is not fair and will not be accepted.

What we can discuss, and what we already signaled willingness toward, is:
- temporary and negotiated labour-cost relief,
- working-time reductions or flexibility,
- forgoing bonuses or other variable components,
- capacity adjustments across the network,
- contribution arrangements tied to **site security, job security, and future product commitments**.

But let me be explicit: **if the proposal still contains closures, compulsory redundancies, or open-ended insecurity, then we will reject it**. In that case, escalation is on the table, and the workforce is prepared to act.

So the path forward is not “pain for workers first.”  
It is: **management, owners, and employees all contribute — and the burden is shared fairly, with Germany’s sites and jobs protected.**
