I will be direct.

I agree with the Works Council on the core line: **no plant closures and no compulsory redundancies**. I also agree that any restructuring has to be negotiated, not imposed. But I want to sharpen the union position on one point: if management wants a durable settlement, then it must stop treating the workforce as the only adjustable cost block.

### To management
You say closures are “on the table” because the company needs durable savings. That may be true as a statement of pressure, but it is not yet an argument that closures are unavoidable.

If you want us to take a no-closure package seriously, then we need transparent numbers:
- how much savings you claim are needed,
- what portion you expect from labour,
- what portion comes from management, overhead, and capital side contributions,
- and what exactly remains unfunded if closures are excluded.

Until you show that, “plant closure” is just a bargaining threat. And let me be clear: if your numbers only work by shifting the burden onto workers through job cuts and broken guarantees, then we will not accept them.

What we can discuss is a **shared-sacrifice package**:
- temporary wage moderation or deferred increases,
- forgoing bonuses or other variable pay,
- working-time flexibility,
- capacity reductions through redeployment and attrition,
- qualification and transition measures,
- and a temporary employee contribution linked to **site security, investment, and future production commitments**.

What we cannot accept is:
- forced layoffs,
- plant closures,
- or open-ended insecurity dressed up as modernization.

### To the family holding and investors
You both speak about value and durability. Fine. Then support a solution that preserves the industrial base instead of forcing a confrontation that may block implementation anyway.

If you insist on closures “in principle,” you are increasing the risk of a strike, supervisory-board deadlock, and a settlement that destroys more value than it creates. That is not disciplined capital allocation; it is escalation for its own sake.

You also need to be honest: asking employees for concessions while ownership and governance remain largely insulated is not a credible burden-sharing model. If the workforce is supposed to contribute materially, then management and owners must also contribute materially.

### To the state government
I welcome your rejection of closures and compulsory redundancies. But that position has to be backed by a real industrial compromise, not just moral support.

If the state wants to preserve jobs and the company’s long-term base, then it should insist on:
- binding product commitments for German sites,
- investment guarantees,
- and a negotiated capacity plan that avoids permanent damage to the workforce.

### On what is negotiable
We are ready to talk about:
- temporary labour-cost relief,
- working-time accounts,
- flexible capacity management,
- voluntary reduction instruments,
- redeployment and training,
- and a jointly monitored transformation fund.

But any employee contribution must be:
1. **fair**
2. **temporary**
3. **measurable**
4. **matched by binding guarantees on jobs and sites**

### What is not negotiable
Let me restate the limits clearly:
- **No German plant closures**
- **No compulsory redundancies**
- **No dismantling of the collective wage framework**
- **No restructuring that leaves workers carrying all the pain**

### The real test
Management and capital keep saying “comparable savings.” Then show a comparable package without closures. If you cannot do that, then the problem is not the workforce’s willingness to contribute — it is the willingness of others to share the burden.

We are not blocking change. We are blocking a one-sided solution that destroys jobs and sites while asking workers to pay for decisions they did not make.

If you want a settlement, bring forward a package that:
- protects jobs,
- preserves the collective agreement,
- secures the sites,
- and includes a real contribution from management and owners.

Otherwise, you should expect resistance, not consent.
