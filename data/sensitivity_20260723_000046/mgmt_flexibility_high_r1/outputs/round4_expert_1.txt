Below is a neutral, economics-and-implementation assessment of the decision space, limited to what can be inferred from the record.

## 1) What is actually being debated
This is not a binary “closure vs. no closure” dispute. It is a question of whether the company can generate **durable structural savings** sufficient to restore competitiveness **without** formal German plant closures and compulsory redundancies, given:
- a high-cost domestic footprint,
- weak European demand,
- a need for lasting margin improvement,
- and a governance structure with multiple effective veto players.

The practical question is whether **open-site capacity reduction plus labour-cost relief** can replicate enough of the economics of closure-led restructuring.

---

## 2) The savings gap: what can and cannot be inferred

### Hard constraint
No shared numerical base case has been provided for:
- current German fixed cost by plant,
- target cost reduction,
- expected volume by model/site,
- or the savings already secured elsewhere.

So the exact gap **cannot be calculated** from the record.

### What can be said qualitatively
Management’s framing implies the residual gap is **material**, not marginal. That matters because:
- if the gap were small, temporary measures and open-site productivity could plausibly bridge it;
- if the gap is large and driven by structural underutilization, then **capacity removal** becomes economically central.

### Practical inference
A no-closure package can only be judged equivalent if it delivers savings from four channels:
1. labour-cost relief,
2. overhead reduction,
3. shift/capacity rationalization,
4. productivity gains.

If these do not close most of the gap, then the open-site solution is not economically equivalent to closures.

---

## 3) Closure-led scenario vs. no-closure scenario

### A. Closure-led restructuring
**Economic logic**
- Best for removing fixed costs quickly and permanently.
- Improves utilization at remaining plants.
- Usually stronger on paper for structural overcapacity problems.

**Costs and risks**
- High one-time restructuring cost.
- Higher execution risk in this governance setting because closure decisions face:
  - co-determination veto risk,
  - worker resistance,
  - strike risk,
  - political resistance from the state shareholder,
  - and potential board deadlock.
- Long implementation horizon if contested.

**Assessment**
Economically strong **if implementable**. But implementability is the weak point here.

---

### B. No-closure negotiated package
Typical instruments:
- reduced shifts,
- line consolidation,
- voluntary exits,
- working-time moderation,
- productivity improvements,
- overhead cuts,
- investment/product commitments,
- temporary labour-cost concessions.

**Economic logic**
- Avoids the governance and social shock of formal closures.
- Can still reduce capacity materially if shifts and lines are removed.
- Can be faster to implement if negotiated successfully.

**Limits**
- Savings may be less durable if concessions expire and utilization stays weak.
- If plants remain formally open but underloaded, fixed costs may persist.
- Risk of “capacity hollowing” without sufficient cash savings.

**Assessment**
This is plausible only if the package contains **real structural capacity reduction**, not just temporary wage relief.

---

### C. Hybrid compromise
A hybrid usually means:
- no immediate formal closures,
- but real installed-capacity reduction inside open sites,
- plus quantified labour contribution,
- plus a review mechanism and conditional fallback.

**Economic logic**
- Best chance of being implementable under current veto constraints.
- Can preserve value by avoiding a governance war.
- Allows management to claim structural change without triggering the full closure conflict.

**Risks**
- If fallback closures are included, trust deteriorates.
- If the package is too soft, it becomes delayed restructuring.
- Requires precise enforcement and metrics.

**Assessment**
From an implementation standpoint, this is the most credible path **if** the savings are sufficiently large and durable.

---

## 4) Residual gap: likely shape, not exact size
Because the numbers are missing, the residual gap can only be described in ranges of plausibility:

- **Small gap:** open-site measures could realistically suffice.
- **Moderate gap:** hybrid solution may close most of it, with some capacity reduction.
- **Large gap:** formal closures or closure-equivalent footprint reduction become economically stronger.

The repeated language from management and capital suggests they believe the gap is at least **moderate and structural**. The employee side believes the gap can be closed by open-site restructuring. That disagreement is fundamentally about **how much fixed capacity is truly surplus**.

---

## 5) Implementability under German co-determination and veto constraints

### Key feasibility constraint
A closure plan is not just an economic choice; it is a **governance and labor-relations test**.

Given:
- 50/50 supervisory board composition,
- 2/3 majority requirement for major plant decisions,
- effective employee veto,
- controlling shareholder influence,
- and a state shareholder with blocking leverage,

a closure-led plan has **very high execution risk**.

### Consequence
Even if closure is economically efficient, it may fail or be delayed enough to destroy value.

That shifts the practical bargaining range toward:
- negotiated capacity reduction,
- voluntary exits,
- working-time changes,
- and time-limited labour concessions.

### Works council and union constraints
- They can materially impede forced redundancies and closures.
- They can also mobilize industrial action once the peace obligation has ended.
- This makes forced restructuring costly and uncertain.

### Bottom line on feasibility
- **Closure-led:** economically strong, procedurally weak.
- **No-closure hybrid:** economically uncertain, procedurally stronger.
- **Pure no-change:** not credible.

---

## 6) Most likely economically workable shape
The most implementable solution is probably:
- no immediate formal German closure,
- material reduction in shifts and installed capacity inside open plants,
- voluntary exits and redeployment,
- time-limited labour-cost relief,
- overhead rationalization,
- productivity commitments,
- and explicit investment/product support.

That would need to be quantified against a closure benchmark.

If the package does **not** produce near-equivalent durable savings, then it will likely be viewed by management and investors as insufficient.

---

## 7) What data would settle the question quickly
The single most useful information set would be, by plant:
1. current installed capacity and utilization,
2. labour cost per vehicle,
3. fixed overhead per vehicle,
4. expected future volume,
5. savings from shift removal or line consolidation,
6. savings from voluntary exits,
7. one-time restructuring costs,
8. required pay or benefit concessions,
9. impact on return on sales.

With that, one can compare:
- closure-led savings,
- open-site savings,
- and the residual gap.

---

## 8) Concise conclusion
- The dispute is about **durable structural savings**, not rhetoric.
- A closure-led plan is usually the strongest economic tool for excess capacity, but it faces the highest implementation risk in this governance setup.
- A no-closure solution can work only if it includes **real capacity reduction inside open sites**, not just temporary labour concessions.
- The most realistic route is a **hybrid negotiated package**, but only if it is quantitatively close to the closure benchmark and enforceable through 2030.
- Without plant-level numbers, no one can credibly prove that closures are either necessary or avoidable.
