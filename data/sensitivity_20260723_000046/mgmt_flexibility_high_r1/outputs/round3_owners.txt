I will be direct.

## My position
The company needs a **durable structural reset**. I do not support a cosmetic compromise, and I do not support a deal that leaves the German cost base fundamentally uncompetitive.

At the same time, I am **not** willing to back a closure-first route simply because it is the bluntest instrument. In this governance structure, implementation risk is part of value. A plan that triggers deadlock, strikes, or a prolonged legitimacy crisis can destroy more value than it saves.

So the controlling shareholder’s standard is simple:

- **real savings**
- **durable effect**
- **implementable under co-determination and veto constraints**
- **no value-destroying escalation**
- **no permanent impairment of the enterprise**

## To management
I agree with you on the core diagnosis:
- the cost base is too high,
- overcapacity exists,
- and temporary gestures are not enough.

Where I disagree is your implied shortcut from diagnosis to closures. You still have not shown, in a quantified way, why a negotiated structural package cannot close the gap. Repeating that closures are “on the table” is not a substitute for analysis.

What I need from you is not rhetoric, but a clear comparison:
1. exact savings gap,
2. labour contribution,
3. management and overhead contribution,
4. capacity reduction contribution,
5. residual shortfall if closures are excluded.

If the residual gap is still large after serious negotiated measures, then I will not pretend otherwise. But until you show that, I will not endorse a closure-led path as the default.

### What I do not accept
- unconditional job guarantees that freeze the structure
- a compromise that preserves all legacy arrangements
- a purely temporary labor-cost patch
- a strategy that cannot pass governance and then turns into public conflict

## To the Works Council
I agree with you on several points:
- a cosmetic deal is worthless,
- workers should not carry the entire burden,
- prolonged conflict would damage the company,
- and any settlement must be socially legitimate.

But I disagree with your absolute red line that **no German plant closures and no compulsory redundancies** can ever be discussed. If that becomes a veto on any material restructuring, then it shifts the burden from labor protection to long-term enterprise weakness. That is not stewardship.

What I can support is a negotiated package that avoids closures **if** it is economically equivalent and durable. That means:
- material labor-cost relief,
- real capacity adjustment,
- voluntary exits and redeployment,
- productivity enforcement,
- and time-bound protection tied to measurable contributions.

What I cannot support is:
- “no closures” as a slogan,
- no structural change,
- and full security regardless of economics.

## To the Union
I share your concern that the workforce must not be the only adjustable block. That is legitimate.

But I do not accept the claim that closures are just bargaining theatrics. In a company facing structural undercapacity and margin pressure, they are a real option if no equivalent alternative exists.

Your position will only be persuasive to me if it moves from principle to economics:
- what savings are actually delivered,
- how durable they are,
- and how they compare to the closure-based benchmark.

Temporary wage moderation alone is not enough. If you want to avoid closures, the package must still be structurally meaningful.

## To the State Government
I understand your concern for regional stability and the company’s industrial role. I share the objective of preserving the industrial base.

Where I differ is on your tendency to treat site protection as an overriding principle. Protecting every site as-is is not the same as protecting the company. If the business remains weak, the long-term regional damage will be worse.

I can support a negotiated no-closure solution, but not a solution that simply freezes an uncompetitive footprint.

## To the Institutional Investors
I agree with your focus on:
- durable savings,
- materiality,
- implementation certainty,
- and margin recovery.

That is the correct test.

Where I disagree is any assumption that closures are inherently the cleanest answer. They are only useful if they can actually be implemented. In this governance environment, that is not guaranteed. A theoretically superior plan that cannot be executed is not superior.

So I share your standard, but I insist that execution risk be treated as central, not secondary.

## On the expert assessment
The expert is right on the key point: a purely temporary package is unlikely to solve a structural issue.

I also agree that a hybrid negotiated solution is the most plausible route **if and only if** it is substantial enough to be economically equivalent. That means it must include:
- durable labor-cost relief,
- voluntary exits,
- reduced capacity,
- overhead cuts,
- productivity improvements,
- and binding implementation discipline.

What I will not support is a “hybrid” that is really just a softer label for delay.

## My requirement going forward
If the employee side wants to avoid closures, then it must put forward a package that is **quantified**, **durable**, and **credible**.

I would expect:
1. a quantified savings plan,
2. a clear contribution from labor,
3. a clear contribution from management and overhead,
4. a real capacity adjustment,
5. and a credible mechanism ensuring savings last through 2030.

If that package closes the gap, I am open to it. If it does not, then we should not pretend a no-closure solution is adequate.

## What I will not support
Let me be explicit:

- no cosmetic compromise
- no open-ended conflict
- no permanent damage to the enterprise
- no outcome that weakens governance control
- no restructuring that leaves the company structurally uncompetitive

## Bottom line
I am prepared to support a **negotiated restructuring without closures** only if it delivers **comparable economic effect** and **durable implementation**.

I am **not** prepared to support a settlement that protects legacy structures while the company continues to miss its target.

So the next step is not more slogans. It is a quantified comparison of the options. If none of the non-closure alternatives can truly close the gap, then we will have to face that reality.
