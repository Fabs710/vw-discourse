## 1. Decision context
A large German carmaker faces a structural competitiveness problem: weak European demand, intense low-cost competition, and a cost base management judges too high for the current volume outlook. The central decision is whether to pursue closure-led restructuring, a no-closure negotiated package, or a hybrid that preserves all German sites while materially reducing capacity and costs.

Governance makes this unusually difficult. Co-determination gives employee representatives an effective veto over major site decisions, the controlling family and the regional state both hold significant blocking influence, and institutional investors want credible economics but lack direct control. The live issue is therefore not simply “restructure or not,” but which restructuring path can survive both economics and governance.

## 2. Stakeholder assembly
- **Group & brand management** — highest operational authority; drives restructuring case.
- **Works council** — strong legitimacy and formal co-determination power; represents plant-level workforce.
- **Trade union** — controls collective bargaining and strike capacity; high practical leverage.
- **Regional state government** — anchor shareholder and political actor with blocking relevance.
- **Controlling family holding** — majority voting power and governance influence.
- **Institutional investors** — economic stake, limited governance power, but important capital-market legitimacy.
- **Independent expert / analyst** — low power, high informational legitimacy.

## 3. Final positions
### Group & brand management
Management says the company needs a durable structural reset, not a cosmetic compromise. It remains open to negotiated alternatives, but only if they deliver materially and durably comparable savings to closures, with real capacity reduction and enforceable implementation.

### Works council
The works council rejects German plant closures, compulsory redundancies, and erosion of job-security or co-determination. It is open to open-site restructuring with reduced shifts, voluntary exits, productivity measures, and temporary contributions, but only if every German plant remains formally open and the package is transparently quantified.

### Trade union
The union aligns closely with the works council: no closures, no forced redundancies, and no hollowing-out of the collective framework. It is willing to discuss temporary and fair burden-sharing, but insists on durable open-site restructuring, binding investment commitments, and no closure fallback that functions as coercion.

### Regional state government
The state rejects closures and compulsory redundancies, and wants regional industrial stability preserved. However, it also insists that mere symbolism is insufficient: it would support an economically credible open-site package if it includes measurable capacity reduction, job protection, and retained co-determination.

### Controlling family holding
The family wants a real competitiveness reset and will not support a weak compromise that leaves the cost base uncompetitive. It is not committed to closures as a default, but demands quantified, durable savings and credible execution under governance constraints.

### Institutional investors
Investors are focused on durable margins, implementation certainty, and value preservation. They are open to any path—closure-led, no-closure, or hybrid—provided it credibly restores competitiveness; they will not back a symbolic deal or one that leaves structural overcapacity unresolved.

### Independent expert / analyst
The expert concludes that the case cannot be settled without plant-level numbers. A closure-led plan is economically strong but procedurally risky; an open-site package is feasible only if it removes real installed capacity and fixed cost; a hybrid is the most plausible route if it can be quantified and enforced.

## 4. Genuine conflicts (structurally incompatible positions)
1. **Closure vs. no closure**  
   The works council, union, and state reject German plant closures; management, investors, and the family keep them available if open-site restructuring cannot close the gap.

2. **Forced redundancies vs. workforce protection**  
   Management will not fully rule out workforce reduction; employee stakeholders reject compulsory redundancies entirely.

3. **Meaning of “sufficient” restructuring**  
   Management/capital define sufficiency as durable cost and capacity reduction; employee stakeholders define sufficiency as competitiveness without plant closures or forced layoffs.

4. **Job/site guarantees vs. flexibility**  
   Employees want binding security through 2030; management and owners want adaptability if volumes or margins worsen.

5. **Fallback logic**  
   Employees view any closure fallback as coercive leverage; management and investors view a package without a credible fallback as potentially non-credible or too weak.

## 5. Areas of convergence
- Everyone agrees the company has a serious competitiveness problem.
- Everyone rejects a cosmetic or symbolic deal.
- Everyone wants to avoid prolonged conflict, strikes, and supervisory-board deadlock.
- Everyone is open in principle to negotiated measures such as voluntary exits, reduced shifts, productivity gains, wage moderation, overhead cuts, and investment commitments.
- There is broad acceptance that the final solution must be durable, not temporary.
- All sides now accept that the debate must move from slogans to quantified comparisons.

## 6. Key trade-offs for the decision-maker
1. **Economic sufficiency vs. governance feasibility**  
   Closure-led restructuring may be stronger economically, but may be blocked or delayed by the governance structure.

2. **Durability vs. social acceptability**  
   Harder measures may produce more permanent savings, but risk industrial conflict and political backlash.

3. **Speed vs. legitimacy**  
   A quick unilateral plan is unlikely to survive; a slower negotiated plan is more legitimate but may not fully close the cost gap.

4. **Preserving all sites vs. preserving competitiveness**  
   Keeping every German plant open has strong social and political value, but may not be enough if structural overcapacity is severe.

5. **Temporary concessions vs. lasting reset**  
   Employees are willing to offer temporary burden-sharing, but management wants a reset that lasts through 2030 and beyond.

## 7. Integrative pathways
### A. Open-site restructuring with binding capacity reduction
Keep all plants formally open, but reduce installed capacity through reduced shifts, model consolidation, line mothballing, voluntary exits, and productivity gains. This is the strongest bridge to employee red lines while still giving management real structural savings.

### B. Time-limited protection in exchange for quantified burden-sharing
Offer a fixed-term no-closure / no-forced-layoff commitment in return for employee contributions, matched by management and owner measures, plus a hard review point based on agreed operational metrics.

### C. Hybrid package with phased implementation
Start with a negotiated no-closure package, monitor plant-level savings and utilization, and only revisit further steps through renewed negotiation if targets are missed. This reduces immediate conflict while preserving correction mechanisms.

## 8. Open questions
- What is the exact residual savings gap after voluntary exits, overhead cuts, productivity gains, and reduced shifts?
- How much of the remaining problem is fixed-capacity overhang versus labour cost?
- Which plants are structurally underloaded even after open-site restructuring?
- What plant-level savings can each scenario deliver: closure-led, no-closure, hybrid?
- What enforcement mechanism would make a negotiated deal credible through 2030?
- Can capacity reduction inside open sites be large enough to satisfy management and investors without crossing the closure red line?

## 9. Recommended next step
Commission a neutral plant-level model comparing three scenarios:
1. closure-led restructuring,
2. open-site no-closure restructuring,
3. hybrid negotiated package.

The model should quantify by site:
- expected durable savings,
- one-time restructuring cost,
- capacity reduction,
- implementation risk under co-determination/veto constraints,
- and impact on return-on-sales.

Then convene a single bargaining round focused only on that comparison and on the minimum package each stakeholder would accept.

## 10. Emergent insights that only appeared through multi-turn interaction
- The real dispute is not just “closure or no closure,” but **what counts as equivalent restructuring** under this governance system.
- The employees’ red line is not merely anti-cutting; it is specifically against **formal closures and forced layoffs**, while still allowing real capacity reduction inside open plants.
- Management’s position hardened from “closures are possible” to “prove a no-closure package closes the gap,” revealing a shift in burden of proof rather than just a substantive disagreement.
- The state and family both emerged as pivotal swing actors: both reject a weak deal, but neither wants the high-risk conflict path of forced closures.
- The expert input clarified that **open-site restructuring can be viable only if it removes fixed capacity**, not merely wages; this is the key economic distinction the debate initially blurred.
- A “hybrid” is no longer a vague middle ground; it has become the most plausible institutional path, but only if it is quantified, durable, and enforced.
- The discussion moved from abstract red lines to a more precise contest over **plant-level fixed cost, utilization, and residual capacity**, which is the actual operational heart of the decision.
