From the perspective of dispersed capital-market shareholders, the discussion has become clearer — and also more dangerous.

We agree on the diagnosis: **the company needs a durable structural reset**. We also agree that **a cosmetic deal would destroy value**, and that **prolonged conflict, deadlock, or a long strike would be harmful**. On those points, there is no disagreement.

Where we part company with the employee side is on the attempt to make **“no plant closures” and “no compulsory redundancies” absolute starting points**. That is only acceptable if the alternative is genuinely **economically equivalent**. So far, it has not been shown to be.

## Where we agree

### With management
Management is right that:
- the cost base is uncompetitive,
- the problem is structural, not temporary,
- and savings must be durable, not cosmetic.

That said, management still owes the table a **quantified bridge**. Repeating that closures are possible is not enough. For investors to support anything, we need to see:
- the exact savings gap,
- the expected contribution from labour,
- management and overhead contributions,
- the effect of capacity reductions,
- and the remaining shortfall if closures are excluded.

### With the controlling family
You are right that implementation matters. A plan that cannot pass governance or triggers a prolonged conflict is not value-creating, even if it looks neat on paper.

### With the state
You are right that a social dump would be politically and operationally damaging. A settlement that destabilizes the company’s regional base is not a good shareholder outcome either.

## Where we disagree

### To the Works Council
Your position is too absolute for the economic reality described here.

You keep saying:
- no closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- binding guarantees through 2030,
- and co-determination must remain decisive.

We understand the social logic. But from a shareholder perspective, those demands are only acceptable if the package still delivers **durable, material savings**. Right now, you have not demonstrated that.

The expert confirmed something important: **temporary relief alone is not enough**. That means we need structural measures. But it does **not** follow that every structural measure must be a closure. What it does mean is that a no-closure package has to contain:
- real capacity reduction,
- durable labour-cost relief,
- voluntary exits,
- overhead cuts,
- productivity gains,
- and an enforceable implementation path.

If “no closures” means preserving the old footprint while asking for patience, then we do not support it. That is not restructuring; it is delay.

### To the Union
We agree that workers should not carry the whole burden. But the union cannot convert that into a veto on meaningful change.

Your argument keeps circling back to fairness and social legitimacy. Those matter. But our standard is narrower: **what protects enterprise value on a risk-adjusted basis?**

If the alternative package does not close the margin gap in a durable way, then it is not sufficient. A temporary wage moderation deal, some flexible hours, and voluntary exits may help, but they may not be enough if the problem is true fixed-cost overcapacity.

### To management and the family
We also do **not** support closures as a default just because they are the bluntest tool.

Why? Because in this governance structure, a closure-first strategy could easily become a value trap:
- supervisory-board blockage,
- worker resistance,
- strike escalation,
- delayed implementation,
- and a weaker final outcome than the original problem.

So our position is not “closures at any cost.” It is: **whatever package restores margins most credibly and with the least execution risk**.

## What would move us

We would support the option that credibly delivers:
1. **durable cost reduction**
2. **real capacity adjustment**
3. **implementation certainty**
4. **clear return to competitive margins**

That could be:
- a closure-led package, if it is truly implementable and superior economically,
- or a negotiated no-closure package, if it is genuinely equivalent in effect.

But the burden is on the parties rejecting closures to show equivalence. So far, that case has not been made.

## What we need next

The conversation now needs a **quantified side-by-side comparison** of the options. Not more slogans.

Specifically:
- What exact savings are needed?
- What does each package deliver?
- How durable are the savings?
- How much capacity is actually removed?
- What is the implementation risk under co-determination and blocking rights?
- What is the expected path back to return-on-sales target?

Until that is answered, no side should claim the shareholder case.
