Let me respond point by point, because we are now at the stage where clarity matters more than repetition.

## To the Works Council
I agree with you on two things:
1. **A cosmetic deal is worthless.**
2. **The company needs a durable solution, not a temporary patch.**

Where I strongly disagree is your insistence that **no German plant closures and no compulsory redundancies must be absolute starting points**.

That position may be understandable from a workforce perspective, but it is **not compatible with the scale of the competitiveness problem we are trying to solve**. If your proposal leaves the German cost base structurally uncompetitive, then it is not a solution; it is delay.

You keep demanding that management prove closures are necessary. I am telling you the burden is simpler than that: **you need to show that your no-closure alternative closes the gap in a durable way**. Not with slogans, not with temporary gestures, but with hard savings that last through 2030.

So far, you have not done that.

### Where I disagree most
- **No closures as a categorical rule**: unacceptable.
- **No forced reductions of workforce where needed**: also unacceptable.
- **Binding job guarantees through 2030 in unconditional form**: incompatible with restructuring reality.

If we lock in today’s footprint regardless of market reality, we will still be facing the same problem in two years — only with less time and less money left to fix it.

## To the Union
I agree that burden-sharing must not fall on workers alone. That is true.

But I do **not** accept the attempt to turn “shared burden” into “employees contribute only if nothing structural changes.” The whole point of restructuring is to change the structure.

You say closures are just a bargaining threat. No — they are a real option if the economics require them. We are not threatening for sport. We are facing a hard reality:
- shrinking European demand,
- intense competition,
- a cost base above target,
- and a brand that is missing its return target.

That is not rhetoric. That is arithmetic.

### What I can discuss
I remain open to:
- temporary labour-cost moderation,
- working-time flexibility,
- voluntary exits,
- redeployment,
- productivity measures,
- overhead reduction,
- and capacity consolidation.

But those measures must be **material**. If they do not deliver comparable savings, then they do not solve the problem.

### What I cannot accept
- No closures, whatever the economics.
- No compulsory redundancies, whatever the economics.
- No meaningful structural correction, but full site security.
- No real cost contribution from labour, but full preservation of the old framework.

That is not a restructuring. That is freezing the problem in place.

## To the State Government
I appreciate that you reject a social dump. I also agree that the company remains important for the region.

But you are still underestimating the downside of preserving every site exactly as it is. **Protecting the appearance of stability while the business keeps underperforming is not industrial policy.** It is postponement.

If we do not make the company competitive, then the long-term damage to jobs, investment, and regional standing will be worse. So yes, I welcome your support for a negotiated solution — but only if it is a solution that actually changes the economics.

I do not accept the argument that co-determination or regional politics can be used to block all serious structural adjustment. Those rights matter, but they do not erase the need for change.

## To the Controlling Family Holding
I agree with you on one important point: **implementation matters**. A plan that cannot be executed is worthless.

Where I disagree is your implication that this means closures should be avoided by default. Not necessarily. What matters is whether the package is economically real and enforceable.

You also correctly say that temporary concessions alone are insufficient. I agree. That is exactly why I am insisting on **durable** savings, not symbolic ones.

So if a negotiated alternative can truly deliver:
- material labour-cost relief,
- real capacity reduction,
- measurable productivity gains,
- and a credible path back to return-on-sales targets,

then I will consider it. But if the answer is “some flexibility, some voluntary exits, and some promises,” then it is too small.

## To Institutional Investors
I agree with your emphasis on:
- durability,
- materiality,
- implementation certainty,
- and a clear path back to margin recovery.

Those are the right criteria.

Where I disagree is any assumption that a no-closure package is automatically preferable if it sounds less confrontational. It is not. If it does not remove enough cost and capacity, then it fails shareholders, the company, and the future investment agenda.

You asked for a quantified comparison. Fair enough. The core test is this:

> Does the employee-side package produce **comparable economic effect** to closure-based restructuring?

If not, then it is not a serious alternative.

## On the expert assessment
The expert was right to say that a hybrid is the most plausible path **if it is sufficiently substantial and durable**.

That is precisely my point.

A hybrid is only acceptable if it contains **real structural change**:
- not just temporary concessions,
- not just a pause,
- not just deferred pain.

It must include enough of the following to actually fix the cost base:
- permanent labor-cost relief,
- headcount reduction through voluntary and phased means,
- material capacity reduction,
- overhead cuts,
- productivity enforcement,
- and binding implementation.

If that can be achieved without closures, fine — but then the savings must be real, not rhetorical.

## What management needs to see next
I am not asking for ideology. I am asking for a workable package.

Bring me:
1. a quantified savings gap,
2. a breakdown of what labour can contribute,
3. a breakdown of what capacity and overhead can contribute,
4. the residual shortfall if closures are excluded,
5. and a credible explanation of how that shortfall is still closed.

If the employee side can close that residual gap with a durable, enforceable package, then we can test it.

If not, then we will have to move to structural measures that the business can actually afford.

## My line remains unchanged
- I want a durable, structural reduction in cost and capacity.
- I am open on instruments.
- I am not open to a fake compromise.
- I am not open to freezing an uncompetitive structure.
- I am not open to a deal that protects every legacy arrangement while the brand keeps missing its target.

## Bottom line
I am prepared to negotiate hard on **how** the restructuring is done.

I am **not** prepared to negotiate away the necessity of a structural reset.

If the employee side wants to avoid closures, then it must put forward a package that is **economically equivalent** and **durable through 2030**. Until then, closures remain on the table — not as a threat for its own sake, but because the company cannot afford a solution that leaves the cost base fundamentally intact.
