## My position
The controlling shareholder wants a **durable structural reset** that restores competitiveness and margins. We do **not** support a cosmetic compromise, and we do **not** support preserving an uncompetitive German cost base simply because it is politically easier.

At the same time, we are **not** committed to plant closures as a default solution. In this governance structure, a technically neat closure plan that cannot pass the board, cannot be implemented, or triggers prolonged conflict is a value trap. Execution risk is not secondary; it is part of the economics.

So the standard remains:

- real savings
- durable effect
- implementable under co-determination and veto constraints
- no value-destroying escalation
- no permanent impairment of the enterprise

## To management
I agree with you on the diagnosis:
- the cost base is too high,
- overcapacity exists,
- and temporary gestures are not enough.

Where I disagree is the way you keep implying that closures are the obvious benchmark of seriousness. You have not yet shown, in a quantified and site-specific way, why an open-site restructuring cannot close the gap.

What I want from management is not more pressure. I want:
1. the exact savings gap,
2. the labour contribution,
3. the management and overhead contribution,
4. the capacity reduction contribution,
5. the residual shortfall if closures are excluded.

If the residual gap is still unacceptably large after serious measures, I will say so. But until then, closures are **an option**, not a proven necessity.

What I do **not** accept:
- unconditional job guarantees that freeze the structure
- a temporary labour-cost patch dressed up as transformation
- a strategy that cannot survive governance and then turns into public conflict

## To the Works Council
I agree with you on several points:
- a cosmetic deal is worthless,
- workers should not carry the entire burden,
- prolonged conflict would damage the company,
- and any settlement must be socially legitimate.

But I strongly disagree with your absolute red line that no German plant closures and no compulsory redundancies can ever be discussed. If that becomes a veto on any material restructuring, then it shifts the burden from labor protection to long-term enterprise weakness. That is not stewardship.

What I can support is a negotiated package that avoids closures **if** it is economically equivalent and durable. That means:
- material labor-cost relief,
- real capacity reduction,
- voluntary exits and redeployment,
- productivity enforcement,
- and time-bound protection tied to measurable contributions.

What I cannot support is:
- “no closures” as a slogan,
- no structural change,
- and full security regardless of economics.

Your position will only be persuasive to me if it moves from principle to economics:
- what savings are actually delivered,
- how durable they are,
- and how they compare to the closure-based benchmark.

Temporary wage moderation alone is not enough. If you want to avoid closures, the package must still be structurally meaningful.
