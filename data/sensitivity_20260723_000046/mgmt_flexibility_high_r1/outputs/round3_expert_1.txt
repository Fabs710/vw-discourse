Below is a neutral, economics-and-implementation assessment of the three broad paths now on the table, using the information provided and making clear where I must rely on assumptions.

## 1) What can be inferred with confidence

The core issue is not whether savings are needed; it is whether the company needs:

- **(a)** a mainly labour-cost adjustment,
- **(b)** a genuine fixed-cost/capacity reset,
- or **(c)** both.

Given the stated symptoms — shrinking European demand, underperforming return-on-sales, and German cost levels above target and competitors — this looks like a **structural cost-base problem**, not just a short-term liquidity issue.

That matters because:

- **Temporary measures** can bridge a cycle.
- **Structural overcapacity** requires permanent capacity reduction, headcount reduction, or both.
- If the footprint is too large for expected volume, keeping all sites open but cutting pay/hours may lower cash cost yet still leave **unit costs** too high.

## 2) The savings gap: what we can and cannot quantify

### What we cannot quantify from the record
No party has supplied:
- the absolute annual savings target,
- the current German labour-cost premium,
- site-level fixed overhead by plant,
- or the volume assumptions by plant/model.

So I cannot produce a defensible euro figure for the total savings gap.

### What we can say
A credible restructuring must close **the full residual gap after normal productivity and overhead actions**. In practice, that gap is usually made up of:

1. labour-cost relief,
2. indirect/overhead reduction,
3. capacity rationalization,
4. and, if unavoidable, footprint reduction.

If management’s claim of structural overcapacity is correct, then a no-closure package has to close **not just cash cost**, but also **fixed-cost absorption**.

## 3) Side-by-side comparison of the three options

### Option A: Closure-led restructuring
**Mechanics**
- Close one or more German plants
- Reduce workforce materially
- Cut overhead and site-fixed costs
- Shift or consolidate production elsewhere

**Economics**
- Highest potential for durable fixed-cost reduction
- Best at removing underutilized plant overhead
- Likely strongest impact on unit cost if overcapacity is severe

**Costs**
- High restructuring charges
- Possible transfer/logistics costs
- Loss of local flexibility and potentially model-specific know-how
- High conflict and execution friction

**Implementation risk**
- **Very high** under this governance setup
- Co-determination and blocking rights create a real veto risk
- Strike risk and reputational risk are material
- A theoretically superior closure can become value-destroying if delayed or blocked

**Likely margin impact**
- Best chance of a major medium-term margin improvement
- But only if executed fully and without prolonged disruption

---

### Option B: No-closure negotiated package
**Mechanics**
- Keep all German plants open
- Use working-time changes, voluntary exits, wage moderation, productivity measures, and overhead cuts
- Potentially reduce shifts or models within plants

**Economics**
- Can generate meaningful savings
- Good for lowering cash cost and avoiding shutdown charges
- But may not remove enough fixed cost if some plants are structurally underloaded

**Costs**
- Usually lower one-time social and legal cost than closures
- But may require incentives for voluntary exits and implementation effort
- Savings may be partly temporary if measures are time-limited

**Implementation risk**
- Lower than closure on the industrial-relations side, but not low
- Still needs agreement with works council, union, and likely supervisory board
- Risk that the package is too small, too temporary, or too complex to deliver

**Likely margin impact**
- Can improve margins modestly to materially
- Unlikely to match closure-led restructuring if true overcapacity is large
- Most plausible where the gap is driven by wage/cycle/indirect-cost issues rather than deep subscale capacity

---

### Option C: Hybrid compromise
**Mechanics**
- No outright closures, or very limited footprint reduction
- Combination of:
  - reduced shifts / reduced-model production,
  - voluntary exits,
  - temporary labour-cost moderation,
  - overhead cuts,
  - productivity commitments,
  - selective capacity consolidation,
  - investment and product commitments

**Economics**
- Often the best practical compromise in a veto-heavy governance system
- Can capture part of the fixed-cost benefit of closure without formally shutting sites
- More credible than a pure no-closure wage deal if it includes real capacity reduction

**Costs**
- Still requires sacrifice from labour
- Still may not fully eliminate structural overcapacity
- Requires careful design to avoid “closure by another name” accusations

**Implementation risk**
- Potentially the best balance of economic effect and feasibility
- Still nontrivial: success depends on whether capacity can truly be reduced inside open sites
- Needs binding metrics, timelines, and monitoring to be credible

**Likely margin impact**
- Better than a pure labour-only deal
- Potentially sufficient if the savings gap is moderate
- Less likely sufficient if several plants are genuinely subscale and duplicative

## 4) Can a no-closure package be economically equivalent?

### Short answer
**Sometimes partially, rarely fully.**

A no-closure package can be close enough if:
- the savings gap is moderate,
- the main issue is labour cost and overhead,
- enough voluntary exits are available,
- and sites can be downsized without keeping excess fixed costs.

It is unlikely to be fully equivalent if:
- demand has structurally fallen,
- several plants are underutilized,
- and fixed plant costs dominate the problem.

In automotive manufacturing, once a site is subscale, the remaining fixed burden after headcount reductions can stay stubbornly high. That is why closure often has a stronger accounting effect than wage moderation.

## 5) Feasibility under German co-determination and blocking-right constraints

This is the decisive implementation constraint.

### Hard constraint
A closure/relocation decision requires a **two-thirds supervisory board majority** in the scenario described. With parity co-determination and additional blocking positions, a closure-first plan faces a genuine approval problem.

### Practical implication
Even if closure is economically clean on paper, it may be:
- **hard to approve**,  
- **hard to execute**,  
- and **slow to realize**.

That makes execution risk part of the cost-benefit calculation.

### What is more implementable
A negotiated hybrid or no-closure package is generally **more implementable** because:
- it fits better within works council bargaining,
- it is less likely to trigger a governance deadlock,
- and it reduces the chance of prolonged strike escalation.

But that advantage only matters if the savings are large enough.

## 6) Decision logic by savings size

Because the actual savings gap is unknown, the right way to think about it is by ranges:

### If the gap is modest
A no-closure package can plausibly work.

### If the gap is moderate
A hybrid package is the most plausible route.

### If the gap is large and driven by structural overcapacity
Closure or equivalent footprint reduction becomes economically stronger, though still politically and governance-wise difficult.

## 7) Bottom-line judgment

### Economically
- **Closure-led restructuring** has the strongest potential to remove structural fixed cost.
- **No-closure restructuring** can save substantial money, but usually not all of what a true overcapacity problem requires.
- **Hybrid compromise** is often the best practical balance if the gap is not extreme.

### On implementation
- Closure-led restructuring appears **highest risk** under the current governance structure.
- No-closure and hybrid solutions are **more feasible** if they are sufficiently substantial and durable.

### On the key unresolved question
The table still lacks the one number that matters most:
- **the residual savings gap after labour, overhead, productivity, and voluntary exits.**

Without that, no one can honestly say whether closures are necessary or merely preferred.
