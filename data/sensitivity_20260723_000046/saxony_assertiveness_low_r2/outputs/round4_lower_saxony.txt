I will be direct.

## Where I agree
I agree with the expert, management, and the capital side on one essential point: **this is a structural competitiveness problem, not something that can be solved with slogans or a cosmetic compromise**. The company needs a durable reset, and I also agree that prolonged conflict would damage value, jobs, and future investment.

I also agree that the toolbox must include:
- voluntary exits,
- redeployment,
- qualification and retraining,
- working-time flexibility,
- overhead simplification,
- and a meaningful contribution from management and owners.

So yes: **there must be change**.

## Where I disagree
### 1) I do not accept closures as the default answer
Management keeps saying closures must remain on the table. I understand that as an abstract statement. I do **not** accept it as the starting point of this process.

For the state, plant closures in Germany are not a routine efficiency measure. They mean:
- loss of industrial capacity,
- damage to regional supply chains,
- loss of jobs,
- and lasting political and social harm.

That is not something I will treat as a normal restructuring lever.

The expert did **not** say closures are necessary. The expert said a no-closure route can work **if** it removes enough fixed cost and capacity. That means the burden is still on management to prove, site by site, that internal measures truly fail. So far, that proof has not been delivered.

### 2) I do not accept compulsory redundancies as the first-line tool
If voluntary exits and internal redeployment do not deliver enough, then that is a serious problem. But I will not treat forced redundancies as the default answer.

The company should first exhaust:
- voluntary programmes,
- internal transfers,
- qualification,
- working-time adjustments,
- and capacity reduction through non-destructive means.

Compulsory redundancies may be discussed only if they are truly unavoidable and proven necessary. We are not there yet.

### 3) I do not accept any attempt to weaken the state’s co-determination and veto role
This is not a side issue. Our special law and board role exist precisely so that the company cannot simply optimize itself at the expense of the regional economy.

Any package that sidelines the state, weakens our veto rights, or reduces our board role would be fundamentally unacceptable.

## To the works council
I support your insistence that the workforce must not become the shock absorber for management’s strategic errors. You are right to demand real numbers, not threats.

But I would also say: a pure “nothing changes” position is not enough either. The company does need material adjustment. The question is whether that can be achieved through:
- shift reductions,
- voluntary exits,
- internal redeployment,
- temporary working-time changes,
- overhead simplification,
- and product allocation changes,

without closures and without forced redundancies.

That path must be tested seriously.

## To the union
Your willingness to discuss temporary and limited employee contributions is constructive. But I agree with you that concessions must be:
- temporary,
- reversible,
- and tied to binding guarantees.

I would add one point: if workers are asked to contribute, then management and owners must visibly contribute too. That means restraint at the top, a credible investment commitment, and no backdoor attempt to weaken collective protections.

## To management
You are asking for a “material” contribution from labour and say closures must remain possible. I hear that. But I need the same discipline from you.

Bring a **site-by-site quantified plan** showing:
- current utilization,
- what capacity can be removed internally,
- how many jobs are affected,
- what annual savings result,
- what the one-time restructuring costs are,
- and why that still supposedly leaves a gap that only closures can fill.

Without that, closures are not a solution. They are leverage.

And I will be blunt: a package that shifts risk onto workers while preserving strategic uncertainty is not acceptable to the state.

## To the controlling family
You are right that a cosmetic compromise would be worthless. But long-term value is not protected by pressuring workers into insecurity.

If the family wants durability, it should support:
- a real no-closure path where possible,
- meaningful owner contribution,
- and a restructuring model that preserves trust.

If the family instead backs a strategy that normalizes closures and forced layoffs, it will get conflict, not value creation.

## To investors
You are right to demand credibility. But credibility is not the same as closures.

A no-closure package can still be credible if it delivers:
- meaningful capacity reduction,
- genuine fixed-cost relief,
- a real employee contribution,
- and improved utilization.

That is the standard I would apply. If those conditions are not met, then the economic case is not yet proven.

## My position
The regional state government is prepared to support a serious restructuring, but only if it:
1. protects the company’s core German industrial footprint,
2. avoids closures unless they are truly unavoidable and proven,
3. avoids compulsory redundancies where alternatives exist,
4. preserves our co-determination and veto role,
5. and secures real investment and product commitments.

So my answer is:
- **No** to closures as the first answer.
- **No** to compulsory redundancies as the first answer.
- **No** to any attempt to weaken the state’s role.
- **Yes** to a quantified, socially responsible restructuring that preserves jobs and sites wherever economically possible.

## What I need next
I support the expert’s call for numbers. Before any decision on closures or layoffs, I want a **site-by-site scenario comparison** showing:
- annual savings from internal measures,
- annual savings from consolidation or shift reduction,
- annual savings from closure/rationalization,
- one-time restructuring costs,
- headcount effects,
- and the margin impact through 2030.

Until that is on the table, closures are not justified.

## Bottom line
I am not here to block every change. I am here to prevent the company from solving a competitiveness problem by gutting its regional base.

So my line is clear:
- **No closures as a starting point**
- **No forced redundancies as the starting point**
- **Yes to a serious, quantified restructuring**
- **Yes to preserving jobs, plants, and co-determination where economically possible**

If management can prove, with hard numbers, that a no-closure path cannot work, then we can discuss the least damaging alternative. Until then, the burden of proof remains with those demanding closures.
