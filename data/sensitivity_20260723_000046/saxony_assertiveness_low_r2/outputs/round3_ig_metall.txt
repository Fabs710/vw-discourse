I will answer directly.

## First, where I agree
I agree with the works council, the state, and the expert on the central point: **this is a real structural competitiveness problem, and a cosmetic compromise would be worthless**.

I also agree that:
- voluntary exits, redeployment, qualification, working-time flexibility, and internal capacity reduction are all part of the toolbox;
- management, owners, and shareholders must contribute too;
- and a prolonged conflict would damage the company and the workforce.

So yes, I accept the need for a **durable** solution. I do **not** accept a fake one.

## Where I disagree — and strongly
### 1) Closures are not a starting point, and not acceptable as a threat tactic
Management still speaks as if closures remain a normal option “if needed.” From the union’s perspective, that means the company is still trying to force concessions through fear.

The expert did **not** prove that closures are necessary. The expert said internal restructuring can work **if** it removes enough fixed cost and capacity. That is exactly why I reject closure talk as the default bargaining instrument.

If management wants the workforce to accept sacrifices, it must first show — with numbers — that internal measures truly cannot close the gap. Until then, closures are not justified.

### 2) Compulsory redundancies are not acceptable
I will say this plainly: **forced layoffs remain off the table**.

If the company needs adjustment, then adjustment must come through:
- voluntary exit programmes,
- internal redeployment,
- qualification,
- working-time changes,
- and negotiated capacity reduction.

Once management starts reserving compulsory redundancies, it destroys trust and turns bargaining into coercion. That is not a basis for a durable settlement.

### 3) Job-security guarantees are not a “luxury”
The works council is right that a binding security framework is essential. I will go further: **without a binding job-security and site-security framework, there is no legitimate employee contribution**.

Employees are not being asked to fund the restructuring with their livelihoods. If they contribute, they need a hard guarantee that the company is not simply buying time before shutting sites or forcing dismissals later.

## What the expert’s answer actually means
The production specialist’s analysis supports a very specific conclusion:

- A **no-closure package can work only if it is structural**, not cosmetic.
- It must remove enough fixed cost, overhead, and underutilized capacity.
- Temporary concessions alone are not enough.

I accept that.

But that does **not** translate into “therefore closures must happen.” It means management has to prove its case properly. We still have not seen a **site-by-site quantified plan** showing:
- current and post-restructuring utilization,
- savings from shift removal,
- savings from voluntary exits,
- overhead reductions,
- one-time restructuring costs,
- and the remaining gap.

Without that, closures are just a threat dressed up as economics.

## To management
You say the workforce must offer a “material” contribution. Fine — but material does **not** mean unilateral pain.

If you want a serious settlement, then bring a package with:
1. **No compulsory redundancies**
2. **No plant closures**
3. **Binding site and job security through 2030**
4. **Measured capacity reduction through internal measures**
5. **A real employee contribution**
   - bonus moderation
   - deferred elements
   - working-time flexibility
   - voluntary programmes
6. **Management restraint at the top**
7. **Owner/shareholder contribution**
8. **Investment and product commitments for German sites**

That is what a balanced package looks like. If you need more cost relief than that, then show the numbers and show why internal measures fail.

## To the works council
We are aligned on the red lines, and I support your insistence that workers should not be the sole shock absorber.

I do want to be precise on one point: a labor contribution must be **real enough to matter**, not symbolic. But it must remain **temporary, reversible, and tied to binding guarantees**. I will not support open-ended wage sacrifice or a weakening of the collective framework.

So yes to:
- temporary bonus moderation,
- deferred compensation,
- working-time adjustments,
- voluntary exits,
- redeployment,
- retraining.

No to:
- forced layoffs,
- closures,
- and dismantling the collective wage framework as a price of admission.

## To the regional state government
I appreciate that you are resisting a lazy closure logic. But ambiguity is dangerous here. “Serious adjustment” cannot become a polite phrase for accepting closures later.

If the state wants to be a real anchor shareholder, it should back:
- binding protection of the core German sites,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- a negotiated industrial plan,
- and clear investment commitments.

That is the kind of political backing that helps stabilise the settlement.

## To the controlling family
You are right that a cosmetic compromise is worthless and that long-term value matters. But long-term value is not served by pressuring the workforce into insecurity.

If ownership wants labour peace and competitiveness, then it must support:
- a real no-closure path,
- material owner contribution,
- and a restructuring model that preserves trust.

If the family supports a hard line that normalizes closures and forced layoffs, then it will get conflict, not value creation.

## To investors
You are right to demand a real restructuring. But you are wrong if you think only closures and layoffs can produce durable value.

A settlement can be economically credible if it:
- removes enough fixed cost,
- improves utilization,
- reduces overhead,
- and delivers measurable savings over time.

What I will not support is a deal that sacrifices jobs and security while leaving the company’s future uncertain anyway. That is not restructuring. That is social damage without a guaranteed payoff.

## My position in operational terms
The union is prepared to discuss a package only if it includes:

- **No compulsory redundancies**
- **No plant closures**
- **Binding job and site security through 2030**
- **Voluntary exit options only**
- **Capacity reductions through internal measures**
- **Working-time flexibility**
- **Qualification and redeployment**
- **A fair employee contribution**
- **Management and owner contribution**
- **A transparent site-by-site savings plan**

## What I need to see next
If management wants the discussion to advance, it must bring a quantified plan showing:
- how much savings are needed,
- which German sites contribute what,
- how much can be achieved through internal measures,
- what the one-time restructuring costs are,
- and why closures would still be necessary.

Until then, I do **not** accept closures, and I do **not** accept compulsory redundancies.

## Bottom line
The union remains open to a serious settlement. But the settlement must be fair, durable, and socially responsible.

That means:
- **no forced layoffs**
- **no plant closures**
- **no dismantling of the collective framework**
- **real shared sacrifice**
- **real guarantees**
- **real investment commitments**

If management wants cooperation, it must stop treating the workforce as the default adjustment reserve. If it refuses, we still have the strike weapon.
