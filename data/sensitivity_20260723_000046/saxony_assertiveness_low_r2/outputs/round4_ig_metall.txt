I’ll be direct.

## Where I agree
I agree with the works council, the state, and the expert on the main point:

- this is a real structural competitiveness problem,
- a cosmetic compromise would be worthless,
- and any serious solution has to be durable, not a delaying tactic.

I also agree that the toolbox must include:
- voluntary exits,
- redeployment,
- qualification,
- working-time flexibility,
- overhead reduction,
- and contributions from management, owners, and shareholders.

So yes: **employees are prepared to contribute**.  
But only in a package that is fair and balanced.

## Where I disagree
### 1) “Closures remain on the table” is not a neutral position
Management keeps repeating that closures must remain possible. From our side, that is not a sober technical statement. It is pressure.

The expert did **not** prove that closures are necessary. The expert said a no-closure path can work **if** it removes enough fixed cost and capacity. That means management still has not shown that internal measures are insufficient.

So I reject the idea that workers should concede closures in advance.  
That is not bargaining; that is threat-based restructuring.

### 2) Compulsory redundancies are not acceptable
I need to say this plainly: **forced layoffs remain off the table**.

If the company wants adjustment, then adjustment must come through:
- voluntary programmes,
- internal redeployment,
- retraining,
- working-time changes,
- and negotiated capacity reduction.

Once management reserves compulsory redundancies, trust collapses. And without trust, there is no durable settlement.

### 3) A labor contribution must not become one-sided pain
We are not rejecting contribution. We are rejecting a model where employees absorb the bulk of the pain while:
- closures stay possible,
- job insecurity rises permanently,
- and management/owners treat their own contribution as optional.

That is not balanced. That is shifting the crisis onto the workforce.

## To management
If you want a serious deal, bring a **site-by-site quantified plan**. Not slogans. Not threats. A real plan.

We need to see:
1. current utilization by site,
2. savings from shift reduction,
3. savings from voluntary exits,
4. savings from overhead cuts,
5. one-time restructuring costs,
6. and the remaining gap after internal measures.

Until then, you have **not** proven that closures are necessary.

And I will be very clear: if you cannot make the no-closure path work, that does **not** mean the workforce should accept plant shutdowns. It means management must search harder for a fair restructuring model.

## To the works council
We are aligned on the red lines:
- no closures,
- no forced layoffs,
- no dismantling of the collective framework.

That is the correct line. The workforce should not become the company’s adjustment reserve.

At the same time, I want to be precise: a labor contribution must be **material enough to matter**. Temporary bonus restraint, deferred elements, working-time flexibility, and voluntary exits are all discussable. But they must be tied to binding security, not used as a wedge to weaken the wage framework.

## To the regional state government
You are right to resist a lazy closure-first logic. But I want you to be more explicit:

- no closures unless truly unavoidable and proven,
- no compulsory redundancies where alternatives exist,
- no weakening of the state’s role through ambiguity.

“Industrial base preservation” must mean more than polite language. It must mean **binding protection of the German footprint** unless management can prove there is no other way.

## To the controlling family
If you want long-term value, then support a solution that preserves trust. That means:
- no blanket closure strategy,
- no forced layoffs,
- real management contribution,
- and a real commitment to German sites.

If ownership wants the workforce to carry part of the burden, then ownership must also carry part of it. That includes restraint at the top and clear investment commitments.

## To investors
You are right that the company needs durable savings. But durable savings do **not** automatically require closures and forced redundancies.

A serious no-closure package can still be economically credible if it delivers:
- meaningful fixed-cost reduction,
- real capacity reduction,
- improved utilization,
- and recurring savings, not just temporary optics.

If the package does not do that, then we will oppose it. But you have not yet shown that only closures can achieve those economics.

## What would move us
We are willing to discuss a **real shared-sacrifice package** if it includes all of the following:

- **No compulsory redundancies**
- **No plant closures**
- **Binding job and site security through 2030**
- **Voluntary exits only**
- **Internal capacity reduction**
- **Working-time flexibility**
- **Qualification and redeployment**
- **A fair employee contribution**
- **Management and owner contribution**
- **A transparent site-by-site savings plan**
- **Investment and product commitments for German sites**

That is a negotiable, responsible path.

What is not acceptable is a deal where:
- workers give up income and security,
- management keeps closures available,
- and the company still expects the workforce to sign off on its own weakening.

## On the expert’s scenario logic
The expert’s framing was useful, but it cuts both ways.

Yes, internal measures must be deep enough.  
But the expert did **not** say that closures are the only solution.

So the burden remains on management and capital side to show, with numbers, that:
- no-closure measures cannot close the gap,
- and that the social damage of closures is truly unavoidable.

Until that proof exists, closures are not justified.

## Bottom line
The union’s position remains:

- **No forced layoffs**
- **No plant closures**
- **No dismantling of collective protections**
- **Yes to meaningful shared sacrifice**
- **Yes to a durable, negotiated solution**
- **Yes to management/owner contribution**
- **Yes to site-by-site transparency**

If management wants cooperation, it must stop treating the workforce as the default shock absorber.  
If it refuses, we still have the strike weapon — and members expect us to use it if necessary.
