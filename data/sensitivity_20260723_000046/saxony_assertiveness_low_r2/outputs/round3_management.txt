I will answer directly.

## First: what I agree with
I agree with everyone on the **diagnosis**, not on the proposed constraints:
- The company has a **serious structural competitiveness problem**.
- A **cosmetic compromise** would be useless.
- Prolonged conflict would damage value, output, and the brand.
- Voluntary tools, redeployment, qualification, and working-time flexibility should absolutely be part of the toolbox.
- Management, owners, and shareholders must contribute as well.

So far, so good.

## Where I disagree — sharply

### 1) “No closures” as an absolute is not acceptable
The works council and union keep repeating that closures are off the table. I do **not** accept that.

Why? Because it answers the politics of the issue, not the economics. If a site is structurally subscale, underutilized, and too expensive for 2030, then preserving it simply because closure is unpopular is not responsible management. It preserves overcapacity and leaves the brand uncompetitive.

The expert has already made this clear:
- internal measures can work **only if** they remove enough fixed cost and capacity,
- otherwise they do not solve the problem.

So no, I am not prepared to sign away closures in advance.

### 2) “No compulsory redundancies” as an absolute is also not acceptable
I prefer voluntary solutions. I want voluntary solutions. But I will not rule out compulsory measures if the savings required are not delivered otherwise.

The company needs a **durable** cost reset, not a temporary truce. If voluntary exits and internal redeployment do not produce enough structural relief, then the restructuring must still be able to bite.

### 3) Blank guarantees through 2030 are incompatible with real restructuring
A binding job-security framework may sound attractive, but an unconditional guarantee through 2030 freezes the very adjustment we need to make.

I can accept stability only if it is tied to a package that actually fixes the cost base. If the guarantee becomes a shield against any meaningful restructuring, it is not a solution — it is a block.

### 4) A “no-closure package” is not enough unless it delivers the same economics
This is the key point.

The union says: temporary concessions, bonus moderation, deferred compensation, working-time flexibility, voluntary exits, and internal capacity reduction. I’m fine discussing those. But they must add up to **real, recurring savings** and **real capacity reduction**.

If the package only spreads pain, postpones decisions, and leaves the cost base structurally too high, then it fails. We do not need a socially acceptable way to keep the company uncompetitive.

## To the works council
You say closures are always destructive and should never be used. I disagree.

That is not a serious industrial principle; it is a veto. The question is not whether closures are pleasant. They are not. The question is whether they are necessary to restore competitiveness. If internal measures can achieve the same result, fine. But you have not shown that yet.

You keep asking management to prove that internal measures fail. Fair enough — but that cuts both ways. If you want to rule out closures, **you** need to show how the same structural savings are achieved without them, site by site, in real numbers.

Until then, “no closures” is just a position, not an answer.

## To the union
I appreciate that you are more practical than the works council in acknowledging temporary labor contributions. That is constructive.

But I need to be equally clear: temporary measures alone are not sufficient if they do not translate into durable cost reduction. Deferred bonuses, working-time changes, and voluntary exits are useful only if they materially reduce the cost base and capacity.

And I will not accept the idea that any meaningful employee contribution automatically becomes “dismantling protections.” That is a false line. There is a difference between:
- a real contribution to restore competitiveness, and
- the destruction of collective rights.

We need the first. The company cannot survive on the second, but it also cannot survive on symbolic concessions.

## To the regional state government
You are right to reject a lazy closure-first approach. But I hear too much ambiguity.

“Protect the industrial base” is not the same as “preserve every current site structure.” If you want the state’s role to be meaningful, then support a hard but workable restructuring — not a permanent brake on change.

I need the state to help create the conditions for adjustment, not simply to constrain every difficult decision. If the package is credible and economically necessary, I expect you to engage constructively rather than defaulting to political resistance.

## To the controlling family
I agree with you on one point: a cosmetic compromise is worthless.

But support for “long-term value” also means supporting the hard decisions that long-term value requires. If the family wants durability, it must accept that some German capacity may need to come out of the system. If the numbers show we can avoid that and still close the gap, I’m open. If they do not, then we cannot hide behind sentiment.

I will not back a structure that preserves every site and every guarantee while failing the return-on-sales target.

## To investors
You are right to insist on a real restructuring.

But let me be blunt: value is not protected by preserving the status quo. If the company stays overstaffed, underutilized, and too expensive, then the market will punish us anyway. So yes, your demand for structural savings is aligned with management’s position.

What I will not support is a deal that looks “balanced” in press language but does not deliver:
- lower fixed costs,
- lower labor costs,
- reduced capacity,
- and faster margin improvement.

## What a credible package must include
A real deal must contain all of the following:

1. **Durable capacity reduction**
2. **A material labor-cost contribution**
3. **Meaningful simplification of the footprint**
4. **Voluntary exits where possible**
5. **Working-time and flexibility measures**
6. **Management restraint at the top**
7. **Owner/shareholder contribution**
8. **Investment and product commitments to 2030**

That is the minimum for a serious settlement.

## On the economics
Let’s stop pretending the answer is obvious. The specialist made the right point: the decisive variable is utilization and fixed-cost absorption.

So here is the straight logic:
- If the no-closure package can remove enough fixed cost and capacity to restore margins, then it is viable.
- If it cannot, then closures or consolidation remain necessary.

That is not a threat. That is arithmetic.

And right now, the employee side has **not** demonstrated that a no-closure package can meet the required savings at the required speed. They have described instruments, not proven sufficiency.

## My bottom line
I am prepared to negotiate a serious restructuring package.

But I will not accept:
- a blanket no-closure rule,
- a blanket no-compulsory-redundancy rule,
- or unconditional guarantees through 2030
if they prevent the company from restoring competitiveness.

If the employee side wants to avoid closures, then it must show — with numbers, not slogans — how a no-closure package delivers the same durable economic result.

Until then, closures, consolidation, and compulsory measures remain on the table.
