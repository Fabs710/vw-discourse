I will answer the question directly.

## The short answer: **No**
A no-closure package may help, but **by itself it does not give management a free hand to keep all options open and still demand that the workforce give up plants, protections, and security**. The burden is on management to prove — with site-by-site numbers — that internal measures are insufficient. Until that is shown, **closures and compulsory redundancies remain unacceptable**.

## Where I agree
I agree with the expert and with parts of what management, the family holding, and investors are saying:

- This is a real competitiveness problem.
- A cosmetic compromise would be worthless.
- Labor peace matters.
- Voluntary exits, redeployment, qualification, working-time changes, and some employee contribution can all be part of a serious package.
- We need facts, not slogans.

That part is not in dispute.

## Where I disagree
### 1) Management is still using closures as leverage, not evidence
You keep saying closures are “on the table if needed.” That may sound balanced to you, but from the workforce side it means one thing: **a threat**.

The expert did not say closures are mandatory. The expert said internal restructuring can work **if** it removes enough fixed cost and capacity. That is a very different statement. It means management must first prove, in numbers, that it has exhausted the non-destructive options.

So no, I do **not** accept the framing that because a closure *might* be economically plausible in some abstract scenario, the workforce must concede closures in advance. That is not negotiation; that is pressure.

### 2) “Material employee contribution” cannot mean dismantling job security
Management and investors keep stretching the phrase “durable savings” until it becomes a demand for:
- wage restraint,
- weaker guarantees,
- more flexibility,
- and ultimately a softer labour framework.

That is precisely where we draw the line.

We are prepared to discuss:
- temporary moderation,
- bonus restraint,
- voluntary programmes,
- internal redeployment,
- working-time flexibility,
- and qualification.

We are **not** prepared to trade away:
- the historic job-security framework,
- protection against compulsory redundancies,
- or the continued existence of every German plant.

If the only way to make a package “credible” is to make workers permanently insecure, then the package is not balanced — it is one-sided.

### 3) Blanket guarantees are not the problem; bad restructuring is
Management says guarantees through 2030 are too rigid. I disagree.

A binding guarantee is not a luxury. It is the minimum condition for trust after management has already shattered trust by putting closures on the table. If the company wants the workforce to contribute, then it must first remove the fear that the result will simply be closures and dismissals later.

The problem is not guarantees. The problem is a restructuring model that tries to fix strategic errors by threatening the workforce’s existence.

## To the expert
Your assessment is useful, but it does not change our core position.

You said internal measures can work if:
- overcapacity is moderate,
- headcount is reduced meaningfully,
- shifts are removed,
- overhead is simplified,
- and utilization rises enough.

Fine. Then show us those numbers. Show us:

- how much can be saved at each German site,
- what the utilization is today,
- what it becomes after internal measures,
- how many jobs are affected,
- and why that still supposedly requires closures.

Until that is done, the expert’s answer actually supports our position: **internal measures have not been ruled out**. That means closures are not justified.

## To management
If you want a serious discussion, bring a **site-by-site industrial plan**. Not a headline. Not a threat. A plan.

We need:
1. current utilization by site,
2. savings from shift reduction,
3. savings from voluntary exits,
4. savings from overhead cuts,
5. impact on production allocation,
6. one-time restructuring costs,
7. and the remaining gap after those measures.

If you cannot show that internal measures fail, then there is no basis for closure demands.

And let me be crystal clear: **we will not help management normalize closures by pretending they are just another tool.** They are not acceptable.

## To the union
We are aligned on the red lines, and I welcome that.

But I want to make something equally clear: “balanced” cannot become code for a managed erosion of protections. If concessions are temporary, reversible, and tied to binding guarantees, then they are discussable. If they become the first step in dismantling the framework, then we will oppose them just as hard.

## To the regional state government
You are correct that the company needs real change. But you are still too willing to speak in the language of “serious adjustment” without clearly rejecting closures and compulsory redundancies.

That ambiguity is dangerous. It gives management room to keep escalating pressure while claiming it is simply exploring options. The state should not facilitate that.

If you want the workforce to cooperate, then support a **binding no-closure path**, not a vague promise of “industrial base preservation” that can later be reinterpreted as consolidation, shutdown, or relocation.

## To the controlling family
You say you want to avoid value destruction. Then stop supporting a strategy that destroys trust first and only later asks for cooperation.

If the family wants long-term value, it should back:
- all German sites staying open,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- binding job-security through 2030,
- and a genuine co-determination role in shaping the restructuring.

That is the only basis on which the workforce will accept sacrifices.

## To investors
You want competitiveness. So do we. But value is not created by threatening workers into submission.

If you push too hard on closures and layoffs, you may get accounting relief for one year and industrial damage for a decade. That is not a durable solution. A company with broken labour relations, damaged know-how, and public conflict is not a stronger investment story.

## Our position in operational terms
We are prepared to discuss a package only if it includes all of the following:

- **All German plants remain open**
- **No compulsory redundancies**
- **Job-security guarantees through 2030**
- **Voluntary exits only**
- **Capacity adjustment through internal measures**
- **Working-time flexibility**
- **Qualification and redeployment**
- **A fair contribution from management, owners, and shareholders**
- **Real co-determination in shaping the plan**

Anything short of that does **not** meet the workforce’s minimum conditions.

## Bottom line
So the answer to the round-3 question is:

- **Can a no-closure package restore competitive margins?**  
  **Possibly, if it is real, quantified, and structural.**

But that does **not** mean:
- closures are now justified,
- compulsory redundancies are acceptable,
- or job-security guarantees should be dismantled.

The expert has not proved closures are necessary. Management has not proved internal measures are insufficient. Until they do, the works council’s position stands:

**No plant closures. No forced layoffs. No dismantling of job security.**

If management wants a credible path forward, it should come back with the site-level numbers — and stop treating the workforce as the default shock absorber for its own strategic failures.
