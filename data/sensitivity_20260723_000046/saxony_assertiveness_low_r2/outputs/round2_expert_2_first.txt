From an industrial and production-planning standpoint, the decisive question is:

**Can a no-closure package, using capacity reductions, working-time changes, voluntary exits, and labor-cost measures, generate enough durable savings to close the competitiveness gap?**

## Short answer
**Possibly, but only under fairly strict conditions.**  
If the required structural savings are **moderate**, a negotiated no-closure package can work. If the savings requirement is **large and permanent**, then internal measures alone usually will not be enough unless they include substantial headcount reduction, major shift removal, and real simplification of the footprint.

## What I can assess with confidence

### 1) The main cost lever is utilization, not wages alone
In car manufacturing, the biggest driver of unit cost is often **fixed-cost absorption**:
- plant overhead,
- indirect labor,
- maintenance,
- utilities baseline,
- logistics complexity,
- and underused capacity.

A labor-cost concession helps, but **wage moderation by itself rarely fixes a structural overcapacity problem**. If a plant stays open at low utilization, unit costs can remain too high even after concessions.

### 2) A no-closure package can save meaningful money
Using typical German auto-site economics, a combined package can plausibly deliver:

- **Labor-cost contribution:** about **3% to 8%** of the relevant wage bill  
- **Voluntary exits / attrition:** often the biggest permanent labor saving if scaled sufficiently  
- **Shift removal / capacity balancing:** can be substantial if it allows a full layer of overhead to come out  
- **Overhead simplification:** often underappreciated, but material if duplicated functions are removed

### 3) But savings depend heavily on the starting point
Without site-level data, I can only give ranges.

For a large German automotive site or cluster:

- **Internal capacity reduction only:** roughly **€10m to €150m per year** depending on utilization and how much overhead can actually be removed
- **Labor-cost measures across multiple sites:** often **€30m to €80m+ per year**
- **Voluntary exits / attrition at scale:** potentially **€45m to €165m gross annual savings** if hundreds to over a thousand positions are removed
- **Closure of a medium-to-large plant:** often **€100m to €300m+ annual ongoing savings**, but with major one-time costs

These are planning ranges, not site-specific forecasts.

## Scenario test

### Scenario A: No closure, modest internal adjustment
Measures:
- voluntary exits only
- limited working-time flexibility
- some bonus moderation
- minor overhead cuts
- no major capacity removal

**Assessment:**  
This is unlikely to solve a deep cost problem. It may buy time, but if overcapacity is structural, the plant/network will remain too expensive.

### Scenario B: No closure, but real capacity reduction
Measures:
- remove a shift or consolidate a line
- transfer some volume between German sites
- 500–1,500 voluntary exits over time
- labor-cost moderation
- overhead and indirect labor reduction

**Assessment:**  
This can be credible **if** the company’s overcapacity is moderate and if utilization after restructuring rises to a healthier level. This is the strongest no-closure route.

### Scenario C: One site closure or consolidation
Measures:
- close or materially repurpose one subscale site
- transfer volume to remaining plants
- reduce duplicate overhead and logistics complexity

**Assessment:**  
This usually gives the largest durable savings and the clearest unit-cost improvement. It is often the economically strongest option if the network is genuinely oversized.

## Utilization benchmark
A simple planning rule:

- **Below ~70% utilization:** serious cost inefficiency
- **70%–85%:** workable only with lean overhead and favorable mix
- **85%+**: generally much healthier for a large auto plant

If German sites are below these levels and volume cannot be restored, then keeping all sites open without deep headcount and overhead cuts will probably preserve too much fixed cost.

## My neutral judgment
A **no-closure package is economically feasible only if it includes material permanent reductions in headcount, shifts, and overhead**. If it is limited to temporary concessions and voluntary-only soft measures, it will likely fall short of what is needed for durable competitiveness.

A **closure or consolidation becomes more likely** when:
- multiple sites are underutilized,
- product overlap is high,
- the needed savings are several hundred million euros annually,
- and voluntary/internal measures cannot remove enough fixed cost fast enough.

## What data would be needed to decide properly
To move from ranges to a real answer, I would need for each German site:
1. annual volume and planned volume by model
2. current and post-restructuring utilization
3. headcount split: direct, indirect, engineering, logistics
4. fully loaded labor cost
5. fixed overhead by site
6. shift structure
7. one-time closure or restructuring costs
8. volume-transfer feasibility between plants
9. product allocation constraints and investment needs

## Bottom line
- **No closure can work** if the overcapacity gap is **modest to moderate** and the package is genuinely structural.
- **No closure will probably not be enough** if management needs a **large, durable reset** in fixed cost and capacity.
- The deciding variable is not the slogan; it is **how much fixed cost and underutilized capacity can actually be removed**.
