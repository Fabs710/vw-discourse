[Works Council (Betriebsrat) (Plant-level employee representation under co-determination) — Round 1]
As Works Council, I will be very clear from the outset:

We will not accept plant closures in Germany. We will not accept compulsory redundancies. And we will not accept the breaking of long-standing job-security commitments as the price of a restructuring that management created through its own strategic choices.

Our priority is to secure:

1. Every German plant remains open.
2. No forced layoffs.
3. Binding job and site guarantees through 2030.
4. A real co-determination role for the workforce in shaping any restructuring.
5. A socially responsible path to competitiveness that does not dump the burden on employees alone.

We are prepared to discuss alternatives that protect sites and jobs: capacity adjustments, internal reorganization, working-time measures, qualification, voluntary instruments, and a fair negotiated contribution from management, ownership, and all stakeholders. But any proposal based on closures, mass layoffs, or unilateral cuts to wages and guarantees is unacceptable.

Management cannot simply hand the workforce a fait accompli and expect compliance. If that is the direction, it will face fierce resistance from the works council and the workforce. We are here to defend jobs, plants, and the future of industrial employment in Germany.

3. **Utilization**
   The biggest driver of unit cost. If a plant runs at 70% of designed output, the cost per vehicle is much higher than at 90–95%.

4. **Network complexity**
   Too many plants or line variants create subscale operations, duplicated logistics, and higher changeover/coordination cost.

So the central planning issue is not only “how many jobs can be cut,” but **how much volume can be removed from the highest-cost structures without losing too much output flexibility**.

For a mature automotive plant, **variable costs are not the main issue in a restructuring fight**. The big savings come from reducing:
- headcount,
- indirect labor,
- shifts,
- overhead,
- and underutilized capacity.

A useful planning approximation is:

- **1% reduction in direct production labor** rarely produces 1% total plant cost reduction, because fixed costs remain.
- **Reducing a plant’s throughput while keeping the site open can increase unit cost unless headcount and overhead fall roughly proportionally.**
- **Closing a line or shift can save materially only if it removes an entire layer of labor and overhead.**

Labor-cost measures alone rarely fix a serious overcapacity problem. They help, but they do not remove structural fixed cost unless tied to headcount and capacity changes.

**Likely savings range:**

**Limitation:**

- **85%+**: generally much healthier for a large automotive facility

If German plants are sitting at low utilization because of lost volume, then keeping all of them open without major labor and overhead reduction will preserve too much fixed cost.

## 6) When internal measures can replace closures

1. The overcapacity is **modest rather than extreme**
2. There is **room to remove a shift or line**
3. Headcount can be reduced meaningfully through **voluntary exits and attrition**
4. The company can secure a **permanent labor-cost contribution**
5. Product allocation can be improved so that remaining sites operate at **higher utilization**
6. There is a realistic implementation horizon of **2–4 years**

If these conditions are met, a combination package can often deliver **€100m to €250m+ annual savings across the German footprint** without closure, depending on scale.

## 7) When closures become hard to avoid

Closures become economically hard to avoid if:

- multiple sites are all underutilized,
- there is significant overlap in product families,
- the company needs a large cost reset quickly,

# 1) Economic baseline logic

For an auto plant network, the main savings levers are:

- **Fixed-cost removal**: plant overhead, maintenance, planning, utilities base load, indirect labor
- **Capacity reduction**: fewer shifts, line consolidation, or full site closure
- **Labor-cost reduction**: voluntary exits, working-time changes, pay moderation
- **Utilization improvement**: fewer underfilled sites, better line loading
- **One-time restructuring costs**: severance, transfer costs, shutdown costs, decommissioning, logistics re-routing

The key rule is:

> If a site is materially underutilized, keeping it open preserves a large fixed-cost burden.  
> If the same volume can be concentrated into fewer lines/sites, recurring cost falls materially.

# 3) Interpretation by scenario

## Scenario 1: No-closure internal measures
This means:
- voluntary exits
- redeployment
- working-time flexibility
- temporary pay moderation or allowances adjustment
- overhead simplification
- some shift trimming without closing plants

### Likely economics
This can produce **real savings**, but usually not enough if the network has true structural overcapacity.

Typical recurring savings:
- indirect labor reduction: **€100m–€250m**
- overhead simplification: **€80m–€200m**
- working-time / wage-related savings: **€50m–€150m**
- logistics / process simplification: **€50m–€100m**

**Total:** about **€300m–€700m/year**

### Main limitation
You do not remove enough fixed cost if all sites remain open and major lines remain underloaded.  
That means:
- plant-level overhead remains
- maintenance and utilities remain
- many indirect functions remain
- excess capacity still exists

### Industrial conclusion
A no-closure package is **plausible only if the actual overcapacity is modest**.  
If the company has a large fixed-cost burden, this scenario usually **does not fully close the margin gap**.

- one or more shifts eliminated

- voluntary exits plus redeployment

# 4) Utilization logic

The central industrial question is not just “how many jobs are cut?” but:

> How much capacity is removed, and how much better is the remaining network utilized?

### Typical utilization pattern
If a network is running at, say, **70% utilization**, then:
- fixed costs are spread over too few units
- any wage or energy cost pressure is magnified
- each remaining car carries too much overhead

### Expected utilization improvement
- **Scenario 1:** +2 to +5 percentage points
- **Scenario 2:** +5 to +10 percentage points
- **Scenario 3:** +10 percentage points or more in remaining sites

That matters because utilization improvement creates a second-order effect:
- less idle equipment
- less underused labor
- lower cost per vehicle
- better absorption of depreciation and overhead

# 5) Margin impact through 2030

A rough rule in auto manufacturing:

- **€500m recurring savings** can be meaningful but may only partially fix a large margin gap
- **€1.0bn+ recurring savings** can materially change operating margin if volume holds
- however, if market volume falls further, savings are partly offset by under-absorption

### Approximate margin effect
