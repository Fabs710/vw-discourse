We’ll be blunt.

## Our position
As dispersed institutional shareholders, we are **not** here to defend any particular site, any particular job guarantee, or any political comfort zone. We are here to protect the **risk-adjusted value of the equity**.

That means we support:
- a **serious restructuring**
- **durable fixed-cost reduction**
- **credible capacity reduction**
- a return to **competitive margins**
- and a deal that is **implementable**, not just rhetorically balanced

What we will **not** support is:
- a cosmetic compromise that leaves the cost base uncompetitive
- a settlement that merely defers the problem
- or a structure that keeps the company trapped below target margins while conflict drags on

## Where we agree
We agree with management, the family holding, and the specialist on the core financial point:

- the problem is **structural**
- the margin gap will not close with slogans
- internal measures only work if they remove **enough fixed cost and enough capacity**
- prolonged labor conflict destroys value

We also agree with the employee side on one narrow point:
- a **strike war** would be value-destructive and should be avoided if there is a credible deal on the table

## Where we disagree

### To the works council and union
Your position remains too absolute.

You keep saying:
- no closures
- no compulsory redundancies
- full job security through 2030
- voluntary measures only

That may be understandable from a labor standpoint, but it is **not acceptable to us as an investment outcome** unless you can show the same economics another way.

The specialist has already said the key thing: a no-closure package is only viable if it removes enough fixed cost and capacity. That is the test. Not sentiment. Not precedent. Not the fact that closures are painful.

So we do **not** accept a blanket no-closure commitment as an endpoint.  
We do **not** accept unconditional job-security guarantees if they freeze the structure.  
And we do **not** accept a package where employee concessions are temporary and reversible while the company’s cost base remains structurally impaired.

If you want to avoid closures, then you need to show, site by site, how the same recurring savings are achieved:
- lower headcount
- lower overhead
- fewer shifts
- better utilization
- and a real reduction in fixed cost

Until that is proven, your proposal is not a solution; it is a constraint.

### To management
We support the need for hard restructuring, but we are not satisfied with leverage-based language.

“Closures remain on the table” is not enough.  
“Compulsory redundancies remain possible” is not enough.  
We need a **quantified plan**.

Bring us:
- site-by-site utilization
- annual savings from internal measures
- annual savings from consolidation / shift reduction
- annual savings from closure / rationalization
- one-time restructuring costs
- and the expected EBIT / ROS impact through 2030

Without that, investors cannot assess whether the plan truly restores value or simply shifts pain around.

We also want management restraint at the top to be real, not symbolic. If labor is asked to contribute materially, so should management compensation, overhead, and capital allocation discipline.

### To the regional state government
We respect your anchor role, but we do **not** accept a governance stance that effectively treats closures as politically impossible regardless of economics.

Protecting the industrial base is not the same as preserving every current site structure. If some footprint reduction is necessary to restore competitiveness, then the state should help shape a workable deal — not veto economics by default.

### To the controlling family
You are right that a cosmetic compromise is worthless. We agree. But the same standard applies to governance: a structure that preserves every site and guarantee while missing the margin target is not stewardship; it is value destruction by inertia.

If the family wants long-term value, it should support:
- real structural adjustment
- clear savings targets
- and a settlement that allows the company to invest from strength, not from weakness

## What would move us
A package would need to satisfy all of the following:
1. **durable capacity reduction**
2. **meaningful fixed-cost removal**
3. **clear recurring savings**
4. **credible margin recovery**
5. **implementability without endless conflict**
6. **management and owner contribution**
7. **enough flexibility to adapt if the numbers miss**

That does **not** require closure as a first principle, but it does require closure to remain a real option if the no-closure route cannot close the gap.

## Our practical view
The most investable outcome is likely the one that produces the best balance of:
- recurring savings
- execution certainty
- and minimal industrial disruption

From where we sit, that is **not** a blanket no-closure deal.  
It may be a **consolidation / shift-reduction / voluntary-exit package** if it is deep enough.

But if that package does not reach the needed savings, then plant rationalization must stay on the table. We would rather see a difficult but credible restructuring than a “peaceful” compromise that preserves a structurally impaired margin.

## Bottom line
We are prepared to support a serious settlement. But only if it is:
- economically credible
- durable
- quantified
- and capable of restoring competitive margins

So our answer is:

- **Yes** to a real restructuring
- **Yes** to voluntary measures if they are large enough
- **Yes** to internal measures if they remove enough fixed cost
- **No** to a cosmetic compromise
- **No** to a blanket no-closure endpoint
- **No** to guarantees that hard-code uncompetitiveness
