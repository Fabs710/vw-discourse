I will speak clearly as the regional state government and anchor shareholder.

## First: where I agree
I agree with all sides on one basic point: the company needs a **durable** competitiveness answer, not a cosmetic compromise.

I also agree with management and investors that:
- the German cost base is too high,
- overcapacity is real,
- and simply freezing the current structure would not be responsible.

And I agree with the workforce side that:
- workers must not be made the sole shock absorber,
- voluntary and socially responsible instruments must come first,
- and long-term industrial capability in Germany has real value.

## Where I disagree — and strongly
I do **not** accept that plant closures should be treated as the normal or default solution.

The expert just said something important: closures may be economically plausible in some scenarios, but they are not automatically necessary. That is exactly why I cannot support management’s attempt to keep closures as a standing threat while asking the workforce to concede first. If there are viable internal alternatives, those must be tested seriously before any closure path is even contemplated.

I also do **not** accept compulsory redundancies as the first answer. If the company wants the state’s support, it must show that it has exhausted less destructive tools.

And I will be explicit: I will not support any restructuring that sidelines the state’s co-determination and veto role, or that weakens our ability to defend the regional industrial base.

## On the expert’s assessment
The specialist’s contribution is helpful because it clarifies the real issue: **utilization and fixed cost absorption**.

That means the debate should not be reduced to “closures versus no closures.” The real question is:
- how much capacity must actually come out,
- whether that can be done through shift reductions, volume transfers, voluntary exits, and labor-cost measures,
- and whether the company is prepared to make binding investment and product commitments in Germany in return.

That is the right frame.

But I also take from the expert’s answer that internal measures can work only if they are **material**. So I do not read this as a blank cheque for everyone to keep everything unchanged. I read it as a warning against lazy closure logic. If management wants to argue for closures, it must quantify why the alternatives are insufficient site by site.

## My minimum position
For the state to support a settlement, it must include:

1. **Binding protection of the major German sites**
2. **No compulsory redundancies as the first-line measure**
3. **Serious internal capacity reduction before any closure is used**
4. **A real employee contribution, but time-limited and reversible**
5. **Management and owner contributions as well**
6. **Concrete investment and product allocation for German plants**
7. **Preservation of the state’s co-determination and veto role**

## Where I am prepared to be pragmatic
I am not insisting that every current structure remain untouched.

If the numbers truly require adjustment, then I could support:
- shift elimination,
- voluntary exits,
- redeployment,
- working-time changes,
- temporary pay moderation,
- and targeted consolidation of production,
provided that this is done in a way that preserves the industrial core and does not become a disguised closure strategy.

What I will not support is a package that:
- starts with closures,
- uses fear to force concessions,
- and then calls that “balanced.”

## To management
You now have more clarity from the expert. Use it.

If you want the state’s backing, bring a **site-by-site plan** showing:
- current utilization,
- the savings achievable from internal measures,
- the number of jobs affected,
- what investment is tied to the package,
- and why closure would still be necessary if those measures are implemented.

Without that, I will not treat closures as justified.

## To the works council and union
I hear your insistence on jobs, sites, and social responsibility. That is legitimate and I support it.

But I also need to be honest: if the company truly has structural overcapacity, then some **material** adjustment will be unavoidable. The question is not whether there is any change at all, but whether change can happen without industrial destruction.

So I encourage you to engage on a serious package of:
- voluntary exits,
- working-time flexibility,
- temporary labor-cost moderation,
- internal redeployment,
- and capacity balancing,
if and only if it is paired with binding site and investment guarantees.

That is a real negotiation. Refusing every adjustment while demanding full guarantees would not be credible to the other stakeholders, and it would weaken your position.

## To the controlling family
You say you want long-term value and no industrial war. Then help produce a settlement that avoids unnecessary closures and preserves the company’s regional anchor role.

If ownership wants flexibility from labor, it must also accept:
- no reckless use of closure threats,
- no pushing the burden downward only,
- and no short-term spreadsheet logic that damages long-term industrial value.

## To investors
You are right that a cosmetic compromise is not enough. But you are wrong if you think value can only be protected by threatening German plant closures.

A durable solution can also come from:
- higher utilization through better allocation,
- genuine overhead simplification,
- measured capacity reduction,
- voluntary workforce reduction,
- and a stable social settlement that prevents prolonged conflict.

That kind of settlement protects value better than a confrontation that burns cash, trust, and political capital.

## My bottom line
The regional state government is prepared to support a **serious restructuring package** — but only one that:
- protects the company’s industrial base in Germany,
- avoids closures unless they are truly unavoidable and transparently justified,
- avoids compulsory redundancies where alternatives exist,
- and preserves our co-determination and veto role.

So I am **not** endorsing management’s hard-line framing.  
I am **not** accepting closures as the starting point.  
And I am **not** giving anyone a blank check.

If the company wants my support, it must come back with a quantified, site-specific, socially responsible plan that proves it can restore competitiveness **without hollowing out the regional anchor**.
