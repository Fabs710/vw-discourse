From a site-level production-planning perspective, the key question is this:

**Can the company achieve a durable cost reduction large enough to fix its competitiveness gap without German plant closures, using internal capacity reductions, working-time changes, voluntary exits, and labor-cost measures instead?**

The short answer is: **possibly yes, but only if the underlying overcapacity is modest-to-material and if management is willing to combine several levers.** If the structural gap is large, then internal measures alone may not be enough.

## 1) What can be said with confidence

In car manufacturing, the economics are usually driven by four big blocks:

1. **Fixed manufacturing overhead**  
   Plant management, indirect labor, maintenance, utilities baseline, depreciation, logistics interfaces, quality systems.

2. **Direct labor**
   Wage rates, bonus components, paid time, shift patterns, overtime, and working-time guarantees.

3. **Utilization**
   The biggest driver of unit cost. If a plant runs at 70% of designed output, the cost per vehicle is much higher than at 90–95%.

4. **Network complexity**
   Too many plants or line variants create subscale operations, duplicated logistics, and higher changeover/coordination cost.

So the central planning issue is not only “how many jobs can be cut,” but **how much volume can be removed from the highest-cost structures without losing too much output flexibility**.

## 2) The main quantitative rule

For a mature automotive plant, **variable costs are not the main issue in a restructuring fight**. The big savings come from reducing:
- headcount,
- indirect labor,
- shifts,
- overhead,
- and underutilized capacity.

A useful planning approximation is:

- **1% reduction in direct production labor** rarely produces 1% total plant cost reduction, because fixed costs remain.
- **Reducing a plant’s throughput while keeping the site open can increase unit cost unless headcount and overhead fall roughly proportionally.**
- **Closing a line or shift can save materially only if it removes an entire layer of labor and overhead.**

## 3) Transparent scenario framework

Because no site-level data were provided, I have to use industry-planning ranges. I will therefore give **bounded estimates**, not a single point forecast.

### Typical assumptions for a high-cost German passenger-car site
- Total annual site cost: broadly **€500m to €1.5bn**, depending on size and product mix.
- Direct and indirect workforce per major assembly site: often **3,000 to 10,000+**.
- Fully loaded annual labor cost per employee in Germany: often **€80k to €120k+** depending on grade and overhead allocation.
- A site running well below design utilization may have **20% to 40% avoidable fixed-cost burden**.

## 4) What different restructuring options can save

### A. Internal capacity reduction only, no closure
Examples:
- remove a shift,
- reduce overtime,
- consolidate production runs,
- increase line speed slightly,
- move some volume to another German plant,
- use voluntary exits and attrition.

**Likely savings range:**  
- If the site is materially underutilized, **€30m to €150m per site per year** is plausible.  
- If the site is already near efficient utilization, savings may be only **€10m to €50m per year**.

**Limitation:**  
This works only if workforce and overhead are actually reset. If volume falls but most of the cost base stays, unit cost can worsen.

### B. Labor-cost measures without closures
Examples:
- bonus moderation,
- temporary wage restraint,
- removal or reduction of special guarantees,
- working-time flexibility,
- deferred compensation,
- longer adjustment cycles.

**Likely savings range:**  
- For a large workforce, a **3% to 8% labor-cost contribution** can be significant.  
- On a site with €300m of annual labor cost, that equals roughly **€9m to €24m per year**.  
- Across several sites, if the labor base is €1bn, then **€30m to €80m+ per year** is feasible.

**Limitation:**  
Labor-cost measures alone rarely fix a serious overcapacity problem. They help, but they do not remove structural fixed cost unless tied to headcount and capacity changes.

### C. Voluntary exits / attrition / redeployment
This is usually the most politically and operationally manageable way to reduce labor cost.

**Likely savings range:**  
- If 500 to 1,500 positions are reduced over time at a fully loaded cost of €90k to €110k, annual gross savings could be **€45m to €165m**.  
- Net savings will be lower after severance, retraining, redeployment, and ramp-down costs in year 1–2.

**Limitation:**  
The savings are delayed. It is useful if the restructuring horizon is 2–4 years, not if immediate relief is needed.

### D. Plant closure
This is the strongest lever if a site is genuinely subscale or redundant.

**Likely savings range:**  
- A medium-to-large site closure can often release **€100m to €300m+ per year** in ongoing fixed cost and labor cost, sometimes more if the site is highly underutilized.
- One-time closure costs are substantial: severance, decommissioning, write-downs, logistics changes, and transfer costs.

**Limitation:**  
Closure gives the biggest durable unit-cost improvement, but it is also the most disruptive and expensive in the short term.

## 5) The utilization test

A plant should be evaluated on a simple question:

> Is the plant’s post-restructuring utilization high enough to justify keeping the site open?

As a planning benchmark:
- **Below ~70% utilization:** serious inefficiency risk
- **70%–85%:** acceptable only if the product mix is favorable and overhead is lean
- **85%+**: generally much healthier for a large automotive facility

If German plants are sitting at low utilization because of lost volume, then keeping all of them open without major labor and overhead reduction will preserve too much fixed cost.

## 6) When internal measures can replace closures

Internal measures can be a credible substitute for closures if all of the following are true:

1. The overcapacity is **modest rather than extreme**
2. There is **room to remove a shift or line**
3. Headcount can be reduced meaningfully through **voluntary exits and attrition**
4. The company can secure a **permanent labor-cost contribution**
5. Product allocation can be improved so that remaining sites operate at **higher utilization**
6. There is a realistic implementation horizon of **2–4 years**

If these conditions are met, a combination package can often deliver **€100m to €250m+ annual savings across the German footprint** without closure, depending on scale.

## 7) When closures become hard to avoid

Closures become economically hard to avoid if:

- multiple sites are all underutilized,
- there is significant overlap in product families,
- the company needs a large cost reset quickly,
- voluntary exits are too slow or too limited,
- and labor-cost concessions are too small to matter.

If management needs a **very large permanent reduction** in fixed cost, say **several hundred million euros annually**, then internal adjustment alone may not be enough unless the company is willing to take substantial headcount reductions and sharp capacity simplification.

## 8) My planning judgment

Based on the facts in your scenario, management’s claim that German operations carry a structural overcapacity problem is **economically plausible**. That does **not** automatically prove that closures are the only solution.

A credible no-closure package would need to combine:
- meaningful headcount reduction,
- shift and capacity cuts,
- labor-cost moderation,
- overhead simplification,
- and product allocation changes.

If the employee side is offering only “voluntary, reversible, temporary” adjustments without significant permanent cost relief, that is unlikely to solve a genuine structural competitiveness gap.

## 9) Practical takeaway

From a production-planning standpoint:

- **If the required savings are modest to moderate**, a negotiated package without closures can work.
- **If the required savings are large and durable**, then at least one of the following is usually necessary:
  - a plant closure,
  - a major line consolidation,
  - or a very substantial workforce and capacity reduction at existing sites.

## 10) Bottom line

My neutral assessment is:

- **Internal restructuring can sometimes match closure economics, but only if it removes enough fixed cost and capacity.**
- **Labor-cost concessions help, but they are rarely sufficient on their own.**
- **The decisive variable is utilization and fixed-cost absorption.**
- **Without transparent site-by-site volume, headcount, and cost data, no one can honestly claim that all closures are unnecessary.**
- **Equally, no one can prove closures are mandatory without showing the size of the overcapacity and the savings gap.**
