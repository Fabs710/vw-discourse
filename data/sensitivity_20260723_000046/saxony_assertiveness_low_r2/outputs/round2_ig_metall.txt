I will be equally direct.

## Where I agree with the works council
I agree with the core principle: **no plant closures as the starting point, no compulsory redundancies, and no rewriting of job security as if it were a nuisance rather than a foundation of industrial peace**.

I also agree that workers must not be made to carry the entire burden of management’s strategic failures. If the restructuring is to be legitimate, then management, owners, and shareholders must contribute materially.

## Where I need to sharpen the union position
That said, I do **not** want us to blur important distinctions.

A **binding job-security framework through 2030** is a legitimate demand, but it has to be tied to a credible package that actually delivers economic relief. If the company is expected to keep all sites, all jobs, and all guarantees unchanged while also fixing a deep cost problem, that is not a solution — it is a postponement.

So my position remains:

- **No compulsory redundancies**
- **No plant closures in Germany as the preferred route**
- **No unilateral wage cuts or guarantee-breaking**
- **Yes to a negotiated employee contribution, but only in a balanced package**
- **Yes to capacity reduction, but through internal, reversible, and socially responsible measures**

## What I reject from management’s line
Management keeps framing this as though closures and layoffs are the obvious consequence of market pressure. I reject that framing.

If closures are being treated as the default bargaining weapon rather than the last and carefully justified resort, then management is not negotiating — it is coercing. That is not acceptable, and it will trigger resistance.

I also reject the idea that the workforce contribution must be “substantial” in the sense of simply lowering wages or stripping guarantees. A real contribution can come from several sources:

- temporary waivers or reductions in bonuses,
- working-time flexibility,
- deferred wage elements,
- voluntary exit arrangements,
- internal redeployment,
- qualification and retraining,
- and capacity adjustments.

But it must be **time-limited, fair, and matched by commitments from management and owners**.

## To the regional state government
I share your rejection of closures as a first tool. But “not supporting closures” is not yet enough. The workforce needs a clear answer on **what replaces them**.

If the state wants to act as a serious anchor shareholder, it should support:
- binding investment commitments,
- future model allocation to German plants,
- protection against forced redundancies,
- and a negotiated industrial plan that preserves the core footprint.

Otherwise, the state is just expressing concern while the pressure remains on the workforce.

## To the controlling family
You say you do not want industrial war or value destruction. Then help ensure that the burden-sharing is real.

If owners want labour peace and long-term value, they must accept:
- no unilateral restructuring by threat,
- no pretending that labour alone must absorb the adjustment,
- and no using “competitiveness” as a blanket justification for dismantling protections.

I will also be frank: if ownership pushes a hard line that violates the red lines I just named, then it will not get labor peace. It will get conflict.

## To investors
You are right about one thing: a cosmetic compromise is useless. But you are wrong if you think the only credible restructuring is one that ends in closures or forced job cuts.

A deal can be economically real without becoming socially destructive. But it has to include measurable savings and capacity adjustments, not just rhetoric.

What investors should understand is this:
- a stable workforce, protected sites, and labor peace are themselves value drivers;
- a strike, reputational damage, and political conflict are not “efficiency gains”;
- and a deal that keeps the company operational while lowering cost through negotiated, reversible means is better than a scorched-earth approach.

## What I am prepared to consider
If management actually wants a settlement, then the union can discuss a package along these lines:

1. **No compulsory redundancies**
2. **No plant closures in Germany**
3. **A binding site and job security framework**
4. **Temporary employee contribution**, for example:
   - bonus moderation,
   - deferred wage components,
   - limited working-time concessions,
   - or a crisis fund
5. **Management and owner contribution**:
   - pay restraint at the top,
   - reduced dividends or equivalent capital support,
   - and visible participation in the sacrifice
6. **Capacity adjustments through internal measures**
7. **Investment and product commitments** for German sites
8. **Qualification and redeployment** for all affected employees

That is the kind of shared-sacrifice package that can be defended to the membership.

## What I will not accept
Let me say this plainly:

- no closures dressed up as “industrial logic”;
- no forced layoffs under a different name;
- no wage cuts without guarantees;
- no open-ended insecurity;
- no deal in which only workers pay while management and owners stay insulated.

If that is the direction, then we will escalate. Warning strikes were only the beginning, not the end, of our leverage.

## Bottom line
The union is still prepared to negotiate a serious restructuring package. But the package must be fair, balanced, and durable.

If management wants savings, it must accept shared sacrifice and job security. If it wants flexibility, it must give guarantees. If it wants employee contributions, it must stop treating closures and dismissals as the default answer.

That is the line.
