I will be direct as well.

## Where I agree
I agree with everyone on the central diagnosis:
- the company has a serious competitiveness problem,
- a cosmetic compromise would be useless,
- and prolonged conflict would damage value and employment.

I also agree that:
- voluntary instruments should be used first,
- qualification, redeployment, and working-time flexibility belong in the package,
- management, owners, and shareholders must contribute,
- and any settlement must be economically durable.

## Where I disagree
### 1) I do **not** accept closures as the default answer
Management keeps saying closures remain on the table “if needed.” That may be true as a theoretical option, but it is not an acceptable starting position for this discussion.

For us as the regional state and anchor shareholder, plant closures in Germany are not a neutral efficiency measure. They mean loss of industrial capacity, damage to regional supply chains, and lasting political and social harm. That is not something I will treat as routine restructuring.

If a closure is ever to be considered, management must first show, site by site, why internal measures cannot deliver the same economic effect. That proof has not been provided here.

### 2) I do **not** accept compulsory redundancies as the first-line tool
I understand management’s point that the economics must add up. But forcing redundancies before exhausting voluntary and internal measures is not acceptable to me.

The expert has made clear that internal restructuring can work if it removes enough fixed cost and capacity. That means the burden is still on management to demonstrate that less destructive options truly fail. Until then, compulsory redundancies remain a line I cannot support.

### 3) I do **not** accept a restructuring that weakens the state’s co-determination and veto role
This is not a side issue. The state’s role exists precisely to prevent regional economic damage and to ensure that industrial decisions are not made purely from a short-term financial logic.

Any package that tries to sideline the state, weaken our veto rights under the special statute, or reduce our board role would be fundamentally unacceptable.

## To the works council
I share your concern for jobs, sites, and social partnership. Your insistence that the burden must not fall only on workers is right.

Where I would urge precision is this: a pure “no change whatsoever” position is not enough to win a durable outcome. The company does need material adjustment. The question is whether that can be achieved through:
- shift reductions,
- voluntary exits,
- internal redeployment,
- temporary working-time changes,
- overhead simplification,
- and product allocation changes,

without closures and without forced redundancies.

That is the path I would still want tested seriously.

## To the union
Your willingness to discuss a temporary and limited employee contribution is constructive. But I agree with you that concessions must be tied to binding guarantees and not become a blank cheque.

I would add: if the contribution is to be politically and socially sustainable, it must be matched by visible contributions from management and owners, including restraint at the top and a clear investment commitment for the German sites.

## To management
You keep asking for a “material” labor contribution and say closures must remain possible. I hear that. But I need the same discipline from you:

Bring a **site-by-site quantified plan** showing:
- current utilization,
- what capacity can be removed internally,
- how many jobs are affected,
- what annual savings result,
- what the one-time restructuring costs are,
- and why that still supposedly leaves a gap that only closures can fill.

Without that, closures are not a solution. They are a threat.

And I will say plainly: a package that merely shifts risk onto workers while preserving strategic uncertainty is not acceptable to the state.

## To the controlling family
You are right that cosmetic compromise is worthless. But a hard line that ignores the regional anchor function of this company is also not acceptable.

If the family wants long-term value, it should support a restructuring that preserves:
- the industrial base in Germany,
- the know-how in the plants,
- and a stable social partnership.

That means giving real backing to a no-closure path if it can be made to work.

## To investors
You are right to insist on credibility. But credibility is not the same as plant closures.

A no-closure package can still be credible if it delivers:
- meaningful capacity reduction,
- genuine fixed-cost relief,
- a real employee contribution,
- and improved utilization.

That is the standard I would apply. If those conditions are not met, then the economic case is not yet proven.

## My position
The regional state government is prepared to support a serious restructuring, but only if it:
1. protects the company’s core German industrial footprint,
2. avoids closures unless they are truly unavoidable and proven,
3. avoids compulsory redundancies where alternatives exist,
4. preserves our co-determination and veto role,
5. and secures real investment and product commitments.

So my answer is:
- **No** to closures as the first answer.
- **No** to compulsory redundancies as the first answer.
- **No** to any attempt to weaken the state’s role.
- **Yes** to a quantified, socially responsible restructuring that preserves jobs and sites wherever economically possible.
