Below is a neutral, site-level industrial economics comparison of the three restructuring paths. Because no actual plant-by-plant dataset was provided, I have to use **transparent assumptions and ranges** typical for a large German automotive passenger-car footprint.

## What is missing to make this exact
To calculate this precisely, I would need for each German site:
- annual output by model and shift
- current utilization by body, paint, final assembly, and logistics
- direct headcount and indirect headcount
- fixed overhead allocation

- variable cost per vehicle
- capex required to keep each site competitive
- product pipeline by site through 2030

Without that, the best we can do is a **bounded scenario model**.

---

# 1) Economic baseline logic

For an auto plant network, the main savings levers are:

- **Fixed-cost removal**: plant overhead, maintenance, planning, utilities base load, indirect labor
- **Capacity reduction**: fewer shifts, line consolidation, or full site closure
- **Labor-cost reduction**: voluntary exits, working-time changes, pay moderation
- **Utilization improvement**: fewer underfilled sites, better line loading
- **One-time restructuring costs**: severance, transfer costs, shutdown costs, decommissioning, logistics re-routing

The key rule is:

> If a site is materially underutilized, keeping it open preserves a large fixed-cost burden.  
> If the same volume can be concentrated into fewer lines/sites, recurring cost falls materially.

# 2) Scenario comparison

## Assumed footprint for illustration
I assume a German passenger-car footprint with:
- several final-assembly / major production sites
- total German annual production around **1.2–1.5 million vehicles**
- current average utilization roughly **65–75%**
- structurally high fixed costs from excess capacity and overhead

I then compare three options:

1. **No closure + internal measures**
2. **Consolidation / shift reduction + voluntary exits**
3. **Closure / rationalization**

# 3) Interpretation by scenario

## Scenario 1: No-closure internal measures
This means:
- voluntary exits
- redeployment
- working-time flexibility
- temporary pay moderation or allowances adjustment
- overhead simplification
- some shift trimming without closing plants

### Likely economics
This can produce **real savings**, but usually not enough if the network has true structural overcapacity.

Typical recurring savings:
- indirect labor reduction: **€100m–€250m**
- overhead simplification: **€80m–€200m**
- working-time / wage-related savings: **€50m–€150m**
- logistics / process simplification: **€50m–€100m**

**Total:** about **€300m–€700m/year**

### Main limitation
You do not remove enough fixed cost if all sites remain open and major lines remain underloaded.  
That means:
- plant-level overhead remains
- maintenance and utilities remain
- many indirect functions remain
- excess capacity still exists

### Industrial conclusion
A no-closure package is **plausible only if the actual overcapacity is modest**.  
If the company has a large fixed-cost burden, this scenario usually **does not fully close the margin gap**.

---

## Scenario 2: Consolidation / shift reduction + voluntary exits
This means:
- one or more shifts eliminated
- production concentrated into fewer lines or fewer sites
- voluntary exits plus redeployment
- some capacity removed without necessarily closing a plant outright

### Likely economics
This usually captures a lot more fixed-cost relief than scenario 1.

Typical recurring savings:
- shift elimination / line consolidation: **€250m–€500m**
- overhead reduction from fewer operating lines: **€150m–€350m**
- voluntary exit savings: **€150m–€300m**
- logistics/network simplification: **€100m–€200m**

**Total:** about **€700m–€1.3bn/year**

### One-time cost
This is more expensive than scenario 1 because you pay for:
- severance / voluntary packages
- transfer and retraining
- line reconfiguration
- temporary inefficiency during ramp-down

Typical one-time cost: **€500m–€1.2bn**

### Industrial conclusion
This is often the **best compromise economically** if the company wants to avoid plant closures but still get meaningful recurring savings.  
It can work if:
- volume is not collapsing too far
- production can be rationally concentrated
- utilization after consolidation rises enough

This scenario is the **most plausible “no formal closure” route** that still delivers substantial fixed-cost reduction.

---

## Scenario 3: Closure / rationalization
This means:
- one or more plants shut down, or major functions moved out
- capacity removed permanently
- fixed overhead materially taken out of the network

### Likely economics
This gives the largest recurring savings because it removes the deepest fixed-cost base.

Typical recurring savings:
- site overhead elimination: **€300m–€700m**
- maintenance/utilities/base plant cost removed: **€150m–€300m**
- indirect labor removed: **€200m–€400m**
- supply/logistics simplification: **€100m–€300m**
- structural labor and support cost reduction: **€250m–€400m**

**Total:** about **€1.0bn–€1.8bn/year**

### One-time cost
This is also the most expensive and disruptive:
- severance and social plan costs
- shutdown and decommissioning
- duplicate logistics during transfer
- write-downs or impairment-related operational charges
- transitional inefficiency

Typical one-time cost: **€1.0bn–€2.5bn**

### Industrial conclusion
If the core issue is **substantial overcapacity**, closure/rationalization is the most economically powerful tool.  
It is also the hardest to implement and the most conflict-prone.

---

# 4) Utilization logic

The central industrial question is not just “how many jobs are cut?” but:

> How much capacity is removed, and how much better is the remaining network utilized?

### Typical utilization pattern
If a network is running at, say, **70% utilization**, then:
- fixed costs are spread over too few units
- any wage or energy cost pressure is magnified
- each remaining car carries too much overhead

### Expected utilization improvement
- **Scenario 1:** +2 to +5 percentage points
- **Scenario 2:** +5 to +10 percentage points
- **Scenario 3:** +10 percentage points or more in remaining sites

That matters because utilization improvement creates a second-order effect:
- less idle equipment
- less underused labor
- lower cost per vehicle
- better absorption of depreciation and overhead

---

# 5) Margin impact through 2030

A rough rule in auto manufacturing:

- **€500m recurring savings** can be meaningful but may only partially fix a large margin gap
- **€1.0bn+ recurring savings** can materially change operating margin if volume holds
- however, if market volume falls further, savings are partly offset by under-absorption

### Approximate margin effect
If the brand needs, for example, a **multi-point ROS improvement**, then:

- **Scenario 1**: usually insufficient on its own
- **Scenario 2**: can plausibly move the company close to target
- **Scenario 3**: most likely to achieve the target, but only if market volumes do not deteriorate further and the transition does not destroy execution

---

# 6) Practical judgment

## If the question is:
### “Can a no-closure package restore competitive margins?”
My answer is:

**Only if** the required savings are modest and the current overcapacity is limited.  
For a large German carmaker with structurally high fixed costs, a pure no-closure solution is **often too shallow** unless it includes very large voluntary exits, shift reductions, and overhead removal.

## If the question is:
### “Is consolidation without formal plant closures enough?”
My answer is:

**Possibly yes** — this is the most credible middle path.  
It can deliver much of the economic benefit of closures while avoiding the symbolic and political break of shutting a plant outright.

## If the question is:
### “Which scenario is economically strongest?”
My answer is:

**Closure/rationalization** is strongest on recurring savings and utilization, but carries the largest one-time cost and implementation risk.

---

# 7) Bottom line

### Bounded conclusion
- **No-closure internal measures:** likely **not enough** if overcapacity is structurally large
- **Consolidation / shift reduction + voluntary exits:** likely the **best balance** between economic credibility and social feasibility
- **Closure / rationalization:** most powerful economically, but highest disruption and highest implementation risk

### Short answer
If the company truly needs a **deep and durable fixed-cost reset**, then:
- **voluntary measures alone are unlikely to be sufficient**
- **consolidation without full closure may be enough if executed aggressively**
- **full closure becomes necessary if the remaining network still cannot reach target utilization and margin**
