Let me be equally clear from management’s side:

I hear the insistence on no closures, no compulsory redundancies, and no breaking of guarantees. But I cannot accept a “business as usual” response when the company’s German cost base is structurally uncompetitive and the brand is missing its return-on-sales target in a shrinking, brutally contested market.

We are not in a debate about whether pressure exists. The pressure is already here:
- German factory costs are far above target.
- European demand is shrinking.
- Chinese and other lower-cost competitors are attacking our market position.
- We need funds to invest in future products and technology through 2030.

So I will respond directly to the red lines you have set:

1. **Plant closures**
   I am not announcing closures for drama. I am saying plainly that if we cannot achieve a durable reduction in overcapacity and cost through negotiation, then plant closures remain on the table. That is not a threat for its own sake; it is the consequence of an economic reality we cannot ignore.

2. **Compulsory redundancies**
   I would prefer to avoid them, but I will not pretend that headcount reductions can be excluded if the structural savings are too small. Voluntary instruments are preferable, but they must deliver real numbers, not symbolic gestures.

3. **Job-security guarantees through 2030**
   I understand the importance of stability, but a blanket guarantee that freezes the current structure would be incompatible with the scale of adjustment needed. A guarantee can only be part of a package that also creates credible cost relief.

4. **A “fair contribution”**
   I agree in principle that the burden cannot fall on employees alone. But let’s be honest: the workforce contribution must be substantial, not marginal. We need a genuine labour-cost contribution and capacity reduction, not just cooperation language.

Where I agree with you:
- Voluntary instruments are preferable to forced measures.
- Qualification and redeployment should be part of any settlement.
- A negotiated solution is better than an open-ended confrontation.

Where I disagree:
- I do not accept that all German sites must remain open regardless of economics.
- I do not accept that the workforce can insist on full guarantees while the company absorbs all the adjustment.
- I do not accept a cosmetic compromise that leaves the cost base unchanged and simply postpones the crisis.

So here is the management position:
We need a **durable restructuring package** that delivers:
- a substantial reduction in capacity,
- a meaningful labour-cost contribution,
- structural simplification,
- and a path back to competitiveness and investment capacity.

If the employee side is prepared to discuss that seriously, we can talk about how to structure it. If not, then we will have to pursue the harder route, including plant-level measures that neither of us should want.

I will not sign up to a deal that preserves jobs on paper but leaves the company unable to compete by 2030.
