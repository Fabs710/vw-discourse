We are now at the point where I want to separate **what is politically comfortable** from **what is economically necessary**.

## Where I agree
I agree with the management side, the investors, and the specialist on the central diagnosis:

- the company has a real structural competitiveness problem,
- a cosmetic compromise will not fix it,
- prolonged conflict would destroy value,
- and the solution must be durable, not symbolic.

I also agree that the toolbox should include:

- voluntary exits,
- redeployment,
- qualification and retraining,
- working-time flexibility,
- overhead simplification,
- and a meaningful contribution from management, owners, and employees.

That part is not in dispute.

## Where I disagree
### 1) A blanket “no closures” rule is not a governance position I can support
To the works council and union: I respect the social mandate, but your position is still too absolute.

You say:
- no plant closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- all German sites stay open,
- binding job-security through 2030.

That may be understandable from a labour perspective, but it is **not yet an economically credible answer** unless you can show, in numbers, that the same structural savings are achieved another way.

The specialist has already said the key point: a no-closure package can work only if it removes enough fixed cost and enough capacity. So the real question is not whether closures are painful. They are. The question is whether they are avoidable **without sacrificing competitiveness**.

You have not shown that yet.

### 2) “No compulsory redundancies” cannot be absolute either
I prefer voluntary solutions. I want voluntary solutions. But I will not pre-rule out compulsory measures if the structural savings are not delivered otherwise.

This is not a tactical preference. It is a value question. If we lock in a structure that is permanently too expensive, we will erode the company’s long-term value anyway. That would be a failure of stewardship.

### 3) Blank guarantees through 2030 are too rigid unless they are tied to a real restructuring result
I understand why the workforce wants security. But unconditional guarantees can become a brake on the very adjustment the company needs.

A guarantee is only acceptable if it sits inside a package that actually fixes the cost base and restores margins. Otherwise it is not stability — it is paralysis.

## Where I side with management — but with a condition
Management is right that the company needs a **material and durable** cost reset.

But management also still has not provided the one thing that would make the discussion honest:
a **site-by-site quantified plan**.

Not a headline. Not a threat. A plan showing:

- current utilization,
- savings from internal measures,
- savings from consolidation or shift reduction,
- one-time restructuring costs,
- employment effects,
- and the recurring margin uplift through 2030.

Without that, “closures remain on the table” is leverage, not strategy.

## To the works council
I understand your mandate. But you are currently asking the owners to accept a structure that may preserve employment optics while leaving the company uncompetitive.

That is not stewardship either.

If you want to exclude closures, then show how the same recurring savings are achieved without them:
- how much capacity is removed,
- which shifts are eliminated,
- what overhead comes out,
- how utilization rises,
- and what the remaining gap is after internal measures.

Until then, your position is a demand, not a solution.

## To the union
Your willingness to discuss temporary concessions is more constructive than a flat refusal, and I recognize that.

But temporary concessions are only useful if they lead to **durable** savings. If they just defer the problem, they are not enough.

And I do not accept the argument that any meaningful contribution from labour is automatically the dismantling of labour rights. That is too sweeping. There is a difference between:
- a fair contribution to restore competitiveness,
- and the destruction of the collective framework.

We need the first.

## To the regional state government
I respect the state’s role and the importance of the industrial base. But I also need to be blunt: “no closures ever” is not a workable governance principle if the economics prove otherwise.

The state should support a durable settlement, not freeze the footprint regardless of cost. Protecting the industrial base is important. Protecting every current site structure at any price is not.

## My position on closures
I do **not** want closures as the first answer.  
I do **not** want them used reflexively.  
But I will **not rule them out in advance** if the numbers show they are necessary to restore competitiveness.

That is the hard truth. Sentiment does not replace arithmetic.

## My position on employee contribution
A fair contribution from the workforce is possible, but it must be meaningful:
- working-time flexibility,
- voluntary exits,
- temporary pay moderation,
- internal redeployment,
- and capacity reduction.

What is not acceptable is a package where labour gives nothing material while the company is still asked to carry an overexpensive structure.

## What would move me
A credible package would need to satisfy three tests:

1. **It restores margins and competitiveness in a durable way**
2. **It avoids prolonged instability and labour conflict**
3. **It is economically quantified, site by site**

That is where the discussion has to go now.

## Bottom line
I am prepared to support a serious restructuring path. But it must be:

- economically credible,
- durable,
- and not a disguised preservation of the status quo.

If the no-closure route can truly restore competitive margins, prove it.  
If it cannot, then we should stop pretending that every painful decision can be avoided.

Until then, I will not endorse any blanket commitment that would permanently impair the company’s competitiveness or the owners’ ability to govern for the long term.
