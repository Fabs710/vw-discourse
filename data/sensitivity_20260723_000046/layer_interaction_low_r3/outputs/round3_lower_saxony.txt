I will be equally clear: I share the view that this company needs a **real, durable restructuring**. I do **not** support a purely symbolic package that leaves the cost base untouched and merely postpones the problem.

But I also do **not** accept the premise that this can only be achieved through closures or by keeping closures as an ever-present threat over the workforce and the state.

### To management
You are right that “hope” is not a strategy. But a restructuring strategy that starts from plant closures and forced redundancies as the default instrument is also not a responsible answer in a company that is so deeply rooted in the region and subject to co-determination.

What I need from management is not rhetoric about seriousness, but a **quantified, credible alternative**:
- permanent shift removal where needed,
- line consolidation,
- lower complexity,
- overhead and management contribution,
- durable labour-cost relief,
- and binding investment commitments that secure future volume.

If you can show that a no-closure package achieves the necessary savings and capacity adjustment, then that is the path I will support. But I will not endorse closures simply because they are administratively cleaner.

### To the works council and union
I understand and respect your refusal to accept plant closures and forced redundancies as the first answer. That is a legitimate position, and it reflects the company’s social partnership tradition.

Where I need to push back is on any suggestion that guarantees alone are enough without a hard economic package. They are not. If the company’s break-even point stays too high, then the next crisis will be worse.

So yes: I am prepared to back a package that avoids closures and compulsory redundancies — **but only if it is a real footprint and cost reset**, not a promise to preserve everything unchanged. That means:
- measurable capacity reduction,
- durable efficiency gains,
- fair but real employee contributions,
- and enforceable investment commitments.

What I cannot support is a deal that simply says “no closures” without answering how the structural overcapacity is removed.

### To the controlling family holding and investors
You are right that a weak compromise would be value-destructive. I do not dismiss that. But I do not accept the conclusion that the only way to protect value is to keep closure options permanently live.

The long-term value of this company also depends on stability, industrial peace, and preserving the German base as a competitive anchor. A solution that devastates trust, triggers prolonged conflict, and weakens the regional industrial ecosystem can destroy value just as surely as high costs can.

So I am prepared to support hard restructuring — but not a destructive one.

### To the expert
Your assessment is useful, and I accept the core point: **some capacity has to come out** if the company is overcapacitated. But you also make clear that this does **not automatically require plant closures**. That matters.

What would move me is a package that shows:
- how much output is removed,
- how fixed costs come down,
- how labour costs are permanently reset,
- and how future investment supports the remaining footprint.

That is the factual test. Not whether the word “closure” appears in the plan.

### My position
I am willing to support a negotiated restructuring that:
1. preserves the German sites where possible,
2. avoids compulsory redundancies,
3. includes real employee contributions,
4. delivers hard, measurable cost and capacity reductions,
5. and secures future investment and products in Germany.

I am **not** willing to support:
- plant closures as the starting point,
- irreversible damage to regional employment,
- or any process that sidelines the state’s co-determination and blocking role.

So my message is simple:  
Bring me a package that proves the company can become competitive **without sacrificing its role as a regional economic anchor**. If that package exists, I will support it. If it does not, then the burden is on management and the owners to explain why they are choosing conflict over a negotiated industrial future.
