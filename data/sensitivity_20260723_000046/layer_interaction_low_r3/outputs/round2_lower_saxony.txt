I will be direct.

I agree with management and the owners on one point: the company needs a **credible, durable restructuring**. A purely symbolic package that leaves the cost base unchanged would not be responsible, and it would ultimately threaten the company’s future in Germany.

But I do **not** agree that this means plant closures must remain on the table as the starting point, or that the workforce should be asked to accept a structural break with job security before any serious alternative has been exhausted.

### To management
You are right that “hope” is not a strategy. But neither is presenting closures as the default instrument and calling that seriousness.

If you want support from us, you need to show a package that is economically real **without** destroying the regional industrial base. That means:
- capacity reductions through internal reorganization,
- working-time and flexibility measures,
- targeted labor-cost relief,
- productivity improvements,
- management and overhead contribution,
- and binding investment commitments.

What I have not heard from you is a convincing demonstration that these instruments cannot deliver meaningful savings at scale. Saying “closures are one of the few instruments” is not enough when the consequences for jobs, suppliers, and regional stability are so severe.

### To the works council and union
I understand and support the core of your position: **no mass job destruction, no casual abandonment of co-determination, and no treatment of the workforce as a disposable cost factor**. That is a legitimate position, and the state will stand behind that principle.

At the same time, I will not pretend that nothing has to change. The company does need a real contribution from labour if it is to remain competitive. But that contribution must be part of a **fair, reciprocal, negotiated package** — not something imposed under the threat of plant closures.

Your package design points are a reasonable basis for negotiation:
- no closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- internal flexibility first,
- investment in the affected sites,
- and a co-determined process.

That is the right framework for a serious discussion. I am prepared to support it as the starting point for talks.

### To the controlling family owners and investors
I hear your concern about durability and value erosion. That concern is valid. But you are too quick to equate “durable” with “structural cuts at any price.”

A settlement that breaks the social partnership, triggers prolonged conflict, and damages the company’s role in its home region is not value-preserving. It may produce an accounting gain in theory, but it creates political, industrial, and reputational risk that can easily outweigh it. Long-term value depends on maintaining a viable industrial base, not just cutting quickly.

### What I can support
I can support a negotiated restructuring if it includes:
- preservation of the German sites,
- avoidance of compulsory redundancies,
- real but fair employee contributions,
- measurable productivity and capacity measures,
- clear investment commitments for future products and technologies,
- and protection of the company’s role as a regional anchor.

### What I cannot support
I cannot support a process in which:
- closures are treated as the default bargaining chip,
- the workforce is asked to pre-accept site destruction,
- the state’s co-determination and blocking role is sidelined,
- or “competitiveness” is used as a blanket justification for irreversible damage in Germany.

So my position is not an exercise in politeness:  
**We are open to a hard restructuring, but not to a destructive one.**  
If there is a serious alternative to closures that truly restores competitiveness, then it should be put on the table now. If not, then management should be honest that it is choosing conflict over cooperation.
