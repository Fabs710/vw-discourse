Yes. Based on industrial restructuring logic, there is a clear answer:

## Short answer
A **no-closure package** can be economically equivalent to a closure-based restructuring only if it removes **roughly the same amount of fixed cost, capacity, and break-even volume** as closing a plant would.  
If it does not, then it is not equivalent — it is only a softer compromise.

## What “equivalent” means in practice
For a carmaker with overcapacity and high domestic cost, equivalence usually requires all four of these:

1. **Capacity removal**
   - Not just fewer shifts temporarily.
   - A permanent reduction in available output capacity, typically through:
     - shift elimination,
     - line consolidation,
     - product/variant rationalization,
     - site-level footprint shrinkage,
     - and/or permanent idling of capacity.

2. **Fixed-cost reduction**
   - Closure is powerful because it removes whole layers of:
     - labor,
     - overhead,
     - maintenance,
     - logistics,
     - energy,
     - internal transport,
     - and local support functions.
   - A no-closure package must replicate enough of this to matter.

3. **Labor-cost reset**
   - Durable concessions are usually needed:
     - wage moderation or freeze,
     - bonus reduction,
     - longer working time without full compensation,
     - higher flexibility,
     - reduced allowances,
     - lower headcount via attrition/redeployment,
     - or temporary/partial return of guaranteed benefits.
   - Temporary concessions alone are usually not enough.

4. **Break-even improvement over 3–5 years**
   - The question is not whether the deal is politically easier.
   - The question is whether the company can reach a lower break-even volume and better cash generation over several years.

## Practical threshold
Without company-specific plant data, I cannot give a precise euro number. But as a rule of thumb:

- A credible non-closure package must deliver **most of the fixed-cost savings of the closure option**, not just a fraction.
- If it only recovers, say, **20–40%** of the savings from closing a site, it is usually **not equivalent**.
- In many automotive cases, “equivalence” means the package must achieve **roughly 70–100% of the closure case’s steady-state EBITDA/fixed-cost effect**, adjusted for one-time closure costs and execution risk.

## Important nuance
A **capacity reduction without closure** can be real if it is structurally deep enough.  
Examples:
- one shift removed permanently,
- a whole line or model family consolidated elsewhere,
- non-core variants eliminated,
- plant-specific overhead taken out,
- supplier and logistics network simplified,
- and workforce reduced through attrition, transfer, and negotiated departures.

But if the site remains broadly intact and the main effect is only scheduling flexibility, then the savings are usually **too small**.

## What would make a no-closure package credible
It would need:
- a quantified reduction in annual fixed cost,
- a quantified reduction in installed capacity,
- a lower break-even volume,
- a workforce adjustment path,
- a multi-year labor-cost settlement,
- and binding product/investment commitments that support the new footprint.

## Bottom line
A no-closure package becomes economically equivalent only when it:
- **permanently removes sufficient capacity**,
- **cuts fixed costs at closure-like scale**,
- and **improves 3–5 year cash flow enough to lower break-even materially**.

If those conditions are not met, then the package is not a substitute for closures — it is only a delay mechanism.
