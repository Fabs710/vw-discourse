Yes. I can answer this in the restructuring-economics frame.

## Bottom line
A **non-closure package** is economically equivalent to a **closure-based restructuring** only if it produces, in steady state, **roughly the same reduction in fixed cost, installed capacity, and break-even volume** over a 3–5 year horizon.

If it does not, then it is not equivalent — it is simply a less disruptive compromise with a weaker cost reset.

## What has to be matched
In a carmaker, closing a plant is powerful because it removes an entire cost stack:

- direct labor
- supervision and plant overhead
- maintenance and facilities
- logistics and internal transport
- utilities and site services
- local quality/engineering support
- some depreciation and support cost
- a chunk of complexity cost in procurement and planning

A no-closure package must replicate enough of that to matter. That usually means a combination of:

1. **Permanent capacity removal**
   - fewer shifts, permanently
   - line consolidation
   - model/variant reduction
   - footprint shrinkage inside remaining plants
   - transfer of volume elsewhere

2. **Permanent fixed-cost reduction**
   - headcount reduction through attrition, redeployment, or negotiated departure
   - management and overhead cuts
   - simplification of support functions
   - lower plant-level complexity

3. **Durable labor-cost reset**
   - wage moderation or freeze
   - reduced bonuses/allowances
   - longer working time or more flexibility without full compensation
   - reduced guaranteed extras
   - lower cost per vehicle over multiple years

4. **A lower break-even point**
   - not just a temporary profit bounce
   - a structurally lower volume needed to cover fixed cost

## Practical threshold
Without plant-level data, I cannot give an exact euro figure. But the rule of thumb is:

- if the non-closure package captures only **20–40%** of the steady-state savings from a closure case, it is **not equivalent**
- if it captures roughly **70–100%** of the closure case’s steady-state fixed-cost effect, it can be considered economically comparable
- the comparison must be on a **steady-state** basis, not just year 1 cash flow

That is especially important because closures typically have:
- higher one-time restructuring charges
- but also larger recurring savings

So the key question is not “which is cheaper to announce?” but:

> **Which option gives the better 3–5 year net present value after restructuring costs?**

## How to test equivalence
Use a like-for-like model with these lines for each option:

### A. Annual recurring savings
- labor cost
- overhead
- facilities and site costs
- logistics
- maintenance
- energy
- complexity/procurement savings

### B. One-time restructuring costs
- severance
- write-downs
- relocation
- legal/transition
- duplicate running costs during ramp-down

### C. Capacity and utilization effects
- installed units per year removed
- average utilization after restructuring
- impact on line loading and fixed-cost absorption

### D. Break-even volume
- vehicles per year needed to cover fixed cost
- compare before/after
- lower break-even is the real test of durability

### E. 3–5 year cash flow
- annual free cash flow
- payback period
- NPV under a reasonable discount rate

## What would make a non-closure package credible
It would need to show all of the following:

- permanent removal of enough capacity to create a real gap versus current overcapacity
- a fixed-cost reduction close to the closure case
- workforce adjustment that is not just temporary flexibility
- a durable labor-cost contribution
- a materially lower break-even volume
- clear product and investment commitments that support the remaining footprint

## What would not be enough
These are usually insufficient on their own:

- shift swaps without permanent capacity reduction
- temporary working-time measures
- short-lived wage concessions
- vague productivity promises
- redeployment that only shifts cost within the group
- guarantees that preserve every site but leave the cost base intact

## My economic conclusion
A no-closure deal can work **only** if it is a **true footprint and cost reset**.

If the package does not remove enough capacity and fixed cost to get close to the closure case’s steady-state economics, then it is not a substitute. It is a delay mechanism.
