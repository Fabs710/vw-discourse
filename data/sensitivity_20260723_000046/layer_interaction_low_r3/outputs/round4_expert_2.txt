Yes — on the industrial-restructuring question, the decisive issue is **not the label** (“closure” vs “no closure”), but whether the package removes enough **fixed cost, capacity, and break-even volume** to restore durable competitiveness.

## Short answer
A non-closure package is economically equivalent to a closure-based restructuring only if, on a steady-state basis, it achieves **most of the same structural effect**:

1. **Permanent fixed-cost reduction**
2. **Real permanent capacity removal**
3. **Meaningful reduction in break-even volume**
4. **Comparable 3–5 year net cash-flow outcome after restructuring costs**

If it does not materially change those four variables, then it is not equivalent — it is only a softer compromise.

## What “equivalence” means in practice
A closure usually removes:
- direct plant fixed costs,
- indirect overhead,
- site support costs,
- underutilized capacity drag,
- and some future capex requirements.

So a non-closure package must compensate through measures such as:
- permanent shift elimination,
- line consolidation,
- product/platform simplification,
- support-function cuts,
- labor-cost moderation,
- redeployment and voluntary exits,
- and possibly asset decommissioning inside the remaining footprint.

If the company keeps the same sites, same shifts, and same overhead structure, then the economics usually do **not** match a closure case.

## The key metrics
### 1) Fixed cost
The test is whether annual fixed cost falls by roughly the same amount as in the closure scenario, **net of any new costs** created by reconfiguration.

Examples of fixed-cost drivers:
- plant management and supervisory staff
- indirect labor
- maintenance and utilities
- logistics and internal transport
- overhead allocation
- depreciation / underutilization burden

A wage cut alone does **not** solve a fixed-cost problem if the plant network remains oversized.

### 2) Capacity removal
Closures remove capacity outright.  
A non-closure package needs **permanent capacity reduction**, not just temporary volume loss.

That usually means:
- removing a shift permanently,
- eliminating a line,
- consolidating multiple variants onto fewer assets,
- or transferring a product permanently to another site.

A key question is: **Does the remaining network still have excess installed capacity relative to realistic demand?** If yes, the restructuring is incomplete.

### 3) Break-even volume
This is often the most practical metric.

The package must lower the volume at which the German footprint breaks even enough that it can operate profitably under realistic market demand.

If break-even volume remains above expected sales, then the company still carries structural overcapacity, even if headline labor concessions look large.

### 4) 3–5 year cash flow
This is the medium-term viability test.

You need to compare:
- one-time restructuring costs
- severance / transfer / retraining costs
- capex for reconfiguration
- annual savings stream
- possible productivity loss during transition

A no-closure package can be acceptable if its **3–5 year net present value** is broadly comparable to the closure case. If not, it is not equivalent economically.

## What would make a non-closure package credible
For a package to be close to closure-level economics, I would expect:
- **permanent** shift reductions,
- **real** line or product rationalization,
- **durable** labor-cost contribution,
- **substantial** overhead reduction,
- and a verified fall in break-even volume.

It must not rely mainly on:
- temporary wage moderation,
- one-off bonuses,
- short-term hours reductions,
- or symbolic guarantees without structural change.

## What I cannot specify numerically
I cannot honestly give you a universal threshold like “X% capacity reduction” or “€Y million savings” without company-specific data. That would be guesswork.

To set the threshold properly, the independent expert needs:
- plant-by-plant fixed cost
- installed vs. effective capacity
- utilization by line and model
- labor cost by site
- one-time restructuring cost
- forecast volumes by product program
- capex required for reconfiguration

Only then can one compare:
- closure case vs.
- no-closure package vs.
- hybrid footprint reduction

on a like-for-like basis.

## Bottom line
The right decision rule is:

> A non-closure package is economically equivalent only if it delivers a **durable reset of fixed cost, capacity, and break-even volume**, with a **3–5 year cash-flow result** similar to the closure case.

If it does not, then it is not equivalent — regardless of how politically preferable it may be.
