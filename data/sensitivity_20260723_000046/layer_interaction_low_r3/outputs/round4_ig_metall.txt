I will be direct.

The works council is right on the core line: **this cannot be a restructuring in which the workforce bears all the pain while management and owners keep their options fully open**. I agree that a real alternative must be tested seriously, and I agree that **capacity reduction does not automatically require plant closures**.

But I do **not** agree with turning “every German plant stays open” into a rhetorical shell while the company quietly strips value, uses pressure tactics, and leaves the door open for later break-ins to the job-security framework. A non-closure solution has to be **real**, not just symbolic.

## To management
You are still trying to make closures the benchmark of seriousness. I reject that.

The expert has now confirmed what we have been saying: if the company needs a durable reset, the test is whether the package achieves:
- permanent capacity reduction,
- lower fixed cost,
- lower break-even volume,
- and a credible 3–5 year cash-flow improvement.

That can be done through:
- permanent shift reduction,
- line consolidation,
- product simplification,
- overhead cuts,
- working-time adjustments,
- and a fair labor contribution.

So stop pretending closure is the only serious instrument. It is not. It is one instrument, and a very destructive one.

What I will **not** accept is a package where employees give concessions up front while management keeps closure threats in reserve. That is not burden-sharing. That is coercion with a spreadsheet attached.

## To the works council
We are aligned on the decisive points:
- **no compulsory redundancies**
- **protection of the collective wage framework**
- **binding site and employment security**
- **internal capacity reduction first**

Where I want to be careful is this: we should not let the other side convert “equivalence testing” into a backdoor to impose plant closures later. If the no-closure package is being tested, it must be tested as a **serious alternative**, not as a probationary period before shutdowns.

I support the demand for plant-level scrutiny, but the purpose must be clear:
- identify where shifts can be removed permanently,
- where lines can be consolidated,
- where overhead can be cut,
- and what labour contribution is fair.

Not to build a case for closures after the fact.

## To the regional state
You are closer to our position than the capital side, and I appreciate that.

But I need to be blunt: support for “a non-closure path” is only meaningful if it means **excluding forced redundancies and site destruction in practice**, not merely delaying the fight. Workers cannot be asked to accept permanent uncertainty as the price of cooperation.

If the state wants stability, then it should back:
- binding job protection,
- industrial investment,
- qualification,
- and a negotiated transition.

Not the logic that says: “submit now, and we’ll revisit closures later if the numbers disappoint.” That is not a fair framework for bargaining.

## To the controlling family holding
You speak of stewardship and value preservation. Then the first question is: **value for whom, and at what cost?**

A settlement that breaks the collective wage framework, normalizes forced layoffs, and destroys trust may look tidy in governance terms, but it is not durable. It invites strikes, conflict, and long-term industrial damage.

That said, I do accept one point: the company needs a real cost reset. Fine. Then let us do that through:
- shared sacrifice,
- management contribution,
- overhead reduction,
- and structural internal change.

Not by threatening the workforce with closures until they surrender.

## To the investors
You are right that a weak deal is unacceptable. I agree. Temporary gestures and cosmetic flexibility are not enough.

But you are wrong if you think the only credible answer is to keep closures permanently on the table. That may preserve leverage for capital, but it destroys trust and makes the company harder to govern.

If the company wants a durable settlement, then it needs a package that is:
- measurable,
- enforceable,
- and fair in the distribution of burden.

That means employees contribute, yes. But not as the only side contributing.

## On what we can accept
We remain prepared to discuss:
- permanent shift removal,
- line consolidation,
- working-time changes,
- internal redeployment and qualification,
- wage moderation or temporary concessions,
- reduced bonuses or allowances,
- and other durable employee contributions.

But only if the package includes:
- **no compulsory redundancies**
- **no plant closures**
- **binding site security**
- **a serious management and ownership contribution**
- **investment commitments for future products and volume**
- **respect for the collective bargaining framework**

## What we cannot accept
I repeat clearly:
- plant closures as a standing fallback against the workforce,
- forced redundancies,
- a restructuring that loads the pain onto employees alone,
- or a “conditional” security model that is just a waiting room for later layoffs.

## Bottom line
We will bargain hard for a real settlement. But our limits are unchanged.

A deal is possible only if it:
1. protects jobs,
2. rules out compulsory redundancies,
3. keeps German sites open,
4. delivers real employee contributions,
5. requires management and owners to contribute as well,
6. and produces a durable industrial future.
