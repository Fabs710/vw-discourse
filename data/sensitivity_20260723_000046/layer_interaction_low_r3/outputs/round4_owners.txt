We appreciate that the discussion is now focused on substance rather than slogans.

### Where we agree
- **A real restructuring is necessary.** A cosmetic package would destroy value.
- **The company needs durable savings, not temporary relief.**
- **A negotiated solution is preferable to a prolonged industrial conflict.**
- **Investment in future products and technology must be part of any settlement.**
- **Management, labour, and public stakeholders all have to contribute.**

### Where we disagree
I do **not** accept the premise that **“no closures”** and **“no compulsory redundancies”** can be fixed principles regardless of the economics.

That is not stewardship. If the business remains structurally overcapacitated, then refusing to keep structural options on the table simply shifts the problem into the future and erodes value more slowly, but no less surely.

I also do **not** accept a blanket job and site guarantee to 2030 if it removes the ability to adjust to market reality. A guarantee that prevents correction is not stability; it is rigidity.

### On the works council and union
You are right that capacity reduction does **not automatically** require closures. I agree with that.

But you have not yet shown that internal measures alone can produce closure-level economics. The expert has made the key point: a non-closure package is only acceptable if it removes **roughly the same fixed cost, capacity, and break-even volume** over time.

So if you want to rule out closures, then the burden is on you to show:
- permanent capacity removal,
- durable fixed-cost reduction,
- a real labour-cost reset,
- and a lower break-even point.

Not sentiment. Not symbolism. Numbers.

### On the regional state
I understand your concern for the regional base. We share that concern.

But I cannot support a categorical no-closure stance if the independent comparison shows that the company cannot become competitive that way. Preserving every site without regard to economics may protect appearances for a while, but it would be value-destructive in the long run.

### On management
I agree with management that a weak compromise is unacceptable. The company needs a genuine cost reset.

Where I would caution management is this: closures should not be used as a blunt bargaining club if a credible non-closure package can deliver the same result. The goal is not to close plants for its own sake. The goal is to restore competitiveness.

### What would move me
I would support a **non-closure solution** if it is genuinely equivalent to a closure-based restructuring on a steady-state basis.

That means:
- permanent capacity reduction,
- real fixed-cost reduction,
- durable labour-cost contribution,
- management and overhead cuts,
- and a credible investment path that supports the revised footprint.

If that package is proven, then closures become unnecessary.

If it is **not** proven, then I will not pretend that a softer package is enough simply because it is politically easier.

### What I want next
We need the independent comparison now — not more general debate.

[SUMMON: jointly appointed independent restructuring adviser to compare plant-by-plant the no-closure package versus a closure-based restructuring on fixed cost, installed capacity, break-even volume, and 3–5 year cash flow, and to identify the minimum threshold at which the no-closure package becomes economically equivalent.]

### Bottom line
I am open to a negotiated solution without closures.  
I am **not** open to a solution that preserves all options for labour while denying management and owners the ability to restore competitiveness.

If the numbers show equivalence, I will support the no-closure path.  
If they do not, then we should stop calling it a solution.
