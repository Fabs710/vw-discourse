Yes — a **no-closure package can sometimes match closure-level savings**, but only if it is designed as a **true footprint and cost reset**, not just a labour-relations compromise.

## Bottom line

From an industrial restructuring perspective, the critical question is:

> Can the non-closure package remove enough **capacity, fixed cost, and cash burn** to achieve materially the same 3–5 year economic outcome as a closure?

My assessment is:

- **Possible in principle:** yes
- **Likely in practice without very hard measures:** no
- **If it avoids closures but still cuts shifts, lines, headcount, overhead, and future capex needs:** it can get close
- **If it mainly relies on temporary wage concessions, voluntary exits, and guarantees:** it will usually fall short

## What closure-level savings usually mean economically

A plant closure typically delivers savings in three buckets:

1. **Fixed-cost removal**
   - plant overhead
   - utilities and maintenance
   - local logistics and support
   - management layers
   - depreciation/carrying costs if assets are written down or mothballed

2. **Capacity removal**
   - fewer shifts
   - fewer direct labour hours
   - lower indirect labour
   - less complexity
   - better utilization at remaining sites

3. **Cash-flow improvement**
   - lower annual operating losses
   - reduced working capital tied to excess output
   - lower future capex to keep an uneconomic site alive
   - sometimes one-off restructuring charges, but better medium-term EBIT/FCF

If a non-closure package does not produce comparable effects in these areas, it is not economically equivalent.

---

## What a credible no-closure package would need to contain

For a German automotive manufacturing footprint, a credible alternative to closure would usually require **most or all** of the following:

### 1) Real capacity removal
Not just “flexibility,” but measurable output reduction:
- elimination of one or more shifts
- consolidation of models or platforms
- removal of duplicate lines
- reduction in site-specific complexity

If a plant keeps its buildings but loses a shift or a full line, that can be economically meaningful. If it keeps the same output profile, it is not a real restructuring.

### 2) Permanent labour-cost reset
Temporary wage freezes are weak. What matters is durable reduction in unit labour cost:
- lower base pay or frozen progression
- removal or partial removal of legacy premiums/guarantees
- longer working time for the same fixed pay is also a form of cost reduction
- lower entry cost for new hires
- revised bonus and allowance structures

The key is permanence. A concession that expires in 12–24 months usually only delays the problem.

### 3) Headcount reduction without simply shifting costs elsewhere
You can reduce workforce through:
- voluntary departures
- early retirement
- natural attrition
- redeployment to growth areas

But if the site remains structurally overstaffed, the savings are limited. In my experience, voluntary programmes often cost money upfront and do not fully replace structural headcount reduction unless paired with output reduction.

### 4) Overhead and management cuts
These matter, but they are rarely sufficient alone.
Typical range: useful, visible, but usually not enough to close a large competitive gap.

### 5) Lower future capex burden
A closure avoids or reduces:
- renewal capex for ageing footprint
- compliance capex
- tooling spend on low-volume sites
- sustaining capex for underutilized infrastructure

A no-closure package must offset this by decommissioning redundant assets or materially reducing maintenance and renewal obligations.

---

## Where the economics usually break

The most common failure mode is this:

- management wants structural savings,
- labour offers concessions,
- but the concessions are **temporary**, **partial**, or **conditioned on guarantees**,
- while the underlying footprint and fixed cost base remain mostly intact.

That produces:
- some near-term EBIT relief
- little or no real reduction in structural break-even volume
- limited improvement in utilization
- continued capex drag
- and a return of the same problem in 2–4 years

In other words: it buys peace, not competitiveness.

## Likely comparison by option

### Option A: Plant closure
**Best for:** fastest and clearest fixed-cost reset  
**Economic profile:**
- highest one-off restructuring charge
- strongest long-term fixed-cost and capacity reduction
- usually best for utilization at surviving plants
- strongest cash-flow improvement after the charge

### Option B: Partial footprint reduction without closure
**Best for:** politically and socially less disruptive than closure, if implemented hard enough  
**Economic profile:**
- moderate one-off charge
- savings depend heavily on how much capacity is truly removed
- can approach closure economics if a shift or line is removed permanently
- often less efficient than closure if the site remains heavily underutilized

### Option C: No-closure package based mainly on labour concessions
**Best for:** short-term industrial peace  
**Economic profile:**
- low immediate disruption
- limited structural savings
- weak durability unless concessions are permanent and enforced
- usually inferior on 3–5 year cash flow unless paired with hard capacity cuts

## Quantitative logic: what “equivalent” would require

Without company-specific data, I can only use restructuring logic, but the equivalence test is straightforward.

A no-closure package would need to offset the lost savings from closure in roughly these categories over 3–5 years:

- **fixed cost avoidance:** enough to match plant overhead elimination
- **direct labour reduction:** enough to match the labour-hours no longer needed
- **indirect labour / support reduction:** local engineering, logistics, administration
- **capex avoidance:** reduced sustaining and renewal spend
- **utilization gain at remaining plants:** enough to lower unit costs meaningfully

If the non-closure package cannot remove at least a substantial share of these costs, then it will not be economically equivalent.

A useful rule of thumb in large automotive manufacturing is:
- if the site remains open but loses only some flexibility, savings are often **material but incomplete**
- if it loses a full shift or a complete line, savings can become **closure-like**
- if it retains most of its footprint and output, savings are usually **insufficient**

## 3–5 year cash-flow view

From a turnaround perspective, the relevant question is not just annual savings, but the **net present value of cash flow** over 3–5 years.

### Closure
- large upfront cost
- strong recurring savings
- usually positive 3–5 year cumulative cash effect if the plant is truly overcapacity

### No-closure package
- lower upfront cost
- weaker recurring savings unless hard capacity comes out
- can outperform closure in year 1 if restructuring charges are much lower
- but often underperforms by year 3–5 if it leaves too much fixed cost in place

So the comparison is often:
- **closure wins on structural economics**
- **non-closure can win only if it is almost as hard as closure**

## What I would test in an independent assessment

To validate whether the no-closure package is truly equivalent, I would quantify:

1. **Current and target utilization by plant**
2. **Fixed cost per unit before and after restructuring**
3. **Direct labour hours removed**
4. **Indirect labour and overhead reduction**
5. **Capex reduction or deferral**
6. **One-off restructuring charges**
7. **EBIT and operating cash flow over 3–5 years**
8. **Break-even volume after restructuring**
9. **Sensitivity to demand downside**
10. **Durability of labour concessions**

If the post-deal break-even volume remains too high, the package is not durable.

## My expert conclusion

A **no-closure package can match closure-level savings only if it is effectively a hard capacity-reduction package in disguise**:
- shift removal
- line consolidation
- permanent labour-cost reset
- overhead cuts
- reduced capex burden
- and credible utilization improvement

If instead it is built around:
- guarantees to 2030,
- voluntary measures only,
- temporary concessions,
- and keeping the footprint broadly intact,

then it will **not** match closure-level savings in fixed cost, utilization, or cash flow over 3–5 years.

### In plain terms:
- **No closure is not automatically weak**
- **But no closure plus no real footprint reduction is weak**
- **Economic equivalence requires hard capacity removal somewhere**
