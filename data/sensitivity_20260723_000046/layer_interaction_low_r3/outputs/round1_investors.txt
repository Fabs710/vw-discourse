We are aligned on one thing: this has to restore competitiveness, not just delay the problem.

From the capital markets side, that is the only test that matters. If the final package leaves the cost base structurally uncompetitive or margins chronically under pressure, then we will regard it as value-destructive, even if it is politically easier to sell. In that sense, I agree with management, and partly with the family owner, that cosmetic change is not enough.

But I do disagree with any framing that starts from “closures and layoffs are therefore the default answer.” They may be one route to savings, but they are not the objective. The objective is a durable return to competitive margins and a credible investment case. If you can get there through a negotiated package that avoids the worst disruption, that is better value than a prolonged confrontation with strikes, reputational damage, and execution risk.

So my position is:

- **No support for a settlement that preserves the current cost structure.**
- **No support for a cosmetic compromise that looks peaceful but leaves margins impaired.**
- **Yes to a package that delivers real, measurable, durable savings.**
- **Yes to alternatives to plant closures if they are economically equivalent and enforceable.**

To the works council and union: I understand the insistence on preserving employment, but from an investor perspective “no closures, no compulsory redundancies” cannot be a hard red line if it blocks the scale of adjustment the business needs. If you want that protection, you need to show a package that replaces it with comparable economics. Temporary sacrifices, working-time tweaks, or one-off concessions are not enough unless they materially change the cost base over time. Otherwise we are just buying peace at the expense of shareholders.

To management: I support a serious restructuring, but you still have to prove that the proposed savings are real, durable, and not just a blunt-force exercise that creates more risk than value. Prolonged industrial conflict would destroy near-term value, so if there is a negotiated route that achieves equivalent savings, you should take it. But if the alternative is too weak, then you need to be prepared to insist on structural action.

To the state and family owners: I hear the concern about the company as a regional anchor, but value destruction is not prevented by vetoing every hard decision. A company that fails to restore returns will eventually cut investment, lose competitiveness, and damage the region anyway. What investors need is not a promise that nothing painful happens; what we need is a credible path to better margins.

So our bottom line is simple:  
**we will back the restructuring if, and only if, it credibly restores competitive margins and is not just a paper compromise.** If the negotiation produces a weak settlement that avoids the hard choices but leaves the economics broken, we will not support it.
