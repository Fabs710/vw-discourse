Yes. On the industrial-restructuring question, the key point is that **there is no single universal threshold where a non-closure package becomes “economically equivalent” to closure-based restructuring**. The threshold has to be defined against the company’s own cost base, utilization, and cash-flow gap.

That said, in practice, a non-closure package is economically close enough to a closure scenario only if it achieves, on a steady-state basis, **most of the same structural effects**:

## 1) Fixed-cost reduction
A credible equivalence test would usually require the alternative package to remove roughly the same **annual fixed-cost burden** that a closure would remove, after allowing for any offsetting costs.

That means:
- headcount reduction in affected functions,
- overhead and support-function cuts,
- lower maintenance/utility/indirect costs from removed shifts or lines,
- and lower depreciation or underutilization drag if assets are decommissioned or consolidated.

If the non-closure package only reduces variable labor cost but leaves most fixed plant and overhead cost intact, it is **not equivalent**.

## 2) Capacity removal
A closure removes capacity outright. A non-closure package is only comparable if it removes **real, permanent installed capacity** through:
- shift elimination,
- line consolidation,
- platform or variant simplification,
- and permanent volume transfer away from the affected assets.

A key test is whether the remaining footprint can operate at a materially higher utilization rate. If utilization stays low, the company still carries structural overcapacity.

## 3) Break-even volume
This is often the most useful practical metric.

A non-closure package is close to equivalent if it lowers the company’s or plant’s **break-even volume** enough that:
- the remaining German footprint can be profitably loaded at realistic demand levels, and
- the margin improvement is durable, not dependent on a temporary wage concession.

If break-even volume remains materially above expected sales, the restructuring has not solved the problem.

## 4) 3–5 year cash flow
Equivalence should be judged over a medium-term horizon, not just year one.

A serious comparison should include:
- one-time restructuring costs,
- severance and transfer costs,
- retraining/redeployment costs,
- capex required to reconfigure the footprint,
- and the annual savings stream.

A non-closure package can be acceptable if its **net present value over 3–5 years** is broadly comparable to the closure case. If it delivers less savings but is politically easier, that is not equivalence; it is a compromise with weaker economics.

## Practical benchmark
If I were setting a decision rule for the parties, I would say:

A non-closure package is only economically equivalent if it achieves:
- **substantially the same annual fixed-cost reduction** as the closure case,
- **material and permanent capacity removal**,
- **a meaningful reduction in break-even volume**, and
- **a comparable 3–5 year net cash-flow outcome after restructuring costs**.

In plain terms: the package must not merely preserve sites; it must **change the economics of the footprint**.

## Important caution
Because I do not have the company’s actual plant-level data, I cannot give a numeric threshold like “X million euros” or “Y percent capacity” with confidence. Any such figure would require:
- plant-by-plant fixed cost data,
- installed and effective capacity,
- utilization by model and line,
- labor cost by site,
- one-time restructuring cost estimates,
- and forecast volumes by product program.

## Bottom line
The right question is not “Can we avoid closures?”  
It is: **Can we remove enough fixed cost and capacity to reach the same steady-state economics as closures would?**

If yes, a non-closure package can be viable.  
If no, then it is not economically equivalent, regardless of how politically attractive it may be.
