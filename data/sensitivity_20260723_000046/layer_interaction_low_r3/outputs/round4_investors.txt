We are aligned with the expert on the central issue: **this must be judged by economics, not by symbolism**.

## Where we agree
- **Restructuring is necessary.** The current cost base is not competitive enough, and that is visible in margins, share performance, and the return-on-sales gap.
- **Negotiation is preferable to a prolonged strike.** Industrial conflict would destroy value.
- **A non-closure package can be acceptable in principle.** We do not require closures for their own sake.
- **Management, labour, owners, and the state all need to contribute.** A one-sided burden on employees would not be durable.

## Where we disagree
### To the works council and union
Your insistence on **“every German plant stays open”** and **“no compulsory redundancies”** as absolute principles is not enough for us.

That may be a legitimate political position, but it does **not** answer the shareholder question:  
**How is the fixed-cost base reset enough to restore competitiveness?**

The expert has already made the key point: if a no-closure package is to be acceptable, it must remove **roughly the same capacity, fixed cost, and break-even volume** as a closure-based restructuring. If it does not, then it is just a softer compromise and a delay mechanism.

So far, we have heard a lot about internal measures, but not enough about **quantified equivalence**. Until that is shown, we cannot support a package that rules out closures and compulsory redundancies up front.

### To the regional state
We understand the concern for jobs and regional stability. But a categorical no-closure stance is not value-preserving if it leaves the company structurally uncompetitive.

A protected but uncompetitive footprint does not help shareholders, and in the medium term it does not help the region either.

### To the controlling family holding
We agree with your core point: a cosmetic settlement would be destructive. But we would caution against using “economic flexibility” as shorthand for preserving every structural option indefinitely if a verified alternative exists.

The right test is still the same:
- permanent capacity reduction,
- durable labor-cost reset,
- lower fixed cost,
- lower break-even volume,
- and a credible 3–5 year cash-flow improvement.

### To management
You are right that this cannot be just optics. But you still need to be precise about what “economically equivalent” means. If the non-closure package does not materially improve the economics, then it is not enough.

## What would move us
We would support a **non-closure** solution only if it is proven to be economically equivalent to closure-based restructuring in steady-state terms. That means:

- **permanent capacity removal**
- **real fixed-cost reduction**
- **durable labor-cost contribution**
- **management and overhead cuts**
- **measurable improvement in break-even volume**
- **credible investment commitments supporting the revised footprint**

If those conditions are met, then closures are not necessary.

If they are **not** met, then a no-closure package is not value-preserving, regardless of how acceptable it may be politically.

## What we need next
At this point, this should not remain an abstract argument.

## Bottom line
We are not here to demand closures. We are here to demand a **credible return to competitive margins**.

If the no-closure package truly delivers closure-level economics, we can support it.  
If it does not, then it is not a solution — it is a postponement of the problem.
