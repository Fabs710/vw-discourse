The expert’s assessment helps, and I take it seriously. But it does **not** justify management’s implication that closures and compulsory redundancies are therefore the default or inevitable answer.

### Where I agree
I agree with the specialist on three important points:
1. **Cosmetic measures are not enough.**
2. **A serious restructuring needs quantified, binding measures.**
3. **Non-closure tools alone work only if the savings package is broad and credible.**

That is exactly why the union came forward with a real counter-proposal instead of just saying “no” and walking away.

### Where I disagree
What I reject is the leap from “non-closure measures may not cover everything in every scenario” to “therefore closures must remain freely available.”

That is too crude, and it ignores the bargaining reality of this company:
- The workforce has already shown willingness to contribute.
- The company has co-determination structures and a blocking minority state shareholder.
- A closure-led solution would trigger severe industrial conflict.
- The cost of conflict, brand damage, and disruption is not a side issue; it is part of the economics.

So if management wants to insist on closures as a standing option, then it also has to show why the social and industrial costs are not destroying the very value it claims to protect.

### On the specialist’s scenarios
The specialist outlined a closure-free package that is economically plausible **if** the savings are strong enough. That is the key point.

We are prepared to negotiate on exactly such a package, but only if it includes all of the following:
- **No compulsory redundancies**
- **No plant closures**
- **Meaningful capacity reductions**
- **Voluntary exits and redeployment**
- **A time-limited employee contribution**
- **Management and owner contributions as well**
- **Binding investment and product commitments in Germany**
- **A clear employment and site security framework**

If management says that is still insufficient, then it should put forward the numbers transparently instead of hiding behind abstract threats of closure.

### What is unacceptable
I need to be explicit here: a deal is **not** acceptable if it means:
- workers give up pay or guarantees,
- but management and owners make no comparable contribution,
- and the company keeps the threat of closure over the workforce as leverage.

That is not shared sacrifice. That is coercion.

### What would move us
A durable settlement becomes possible if management and the owners are prepared to accept:
- a fair labour-cost contribution,
- temporary adjustments rather than permanent wage destruction,
- investment in German sites,
- and binding no-closure / no-forced-layoff commitments for a defined period.

That is the condition for labour peace. Without it, the workforce will not accept being the sole shock absorber for a strategy failure.

### Bottom line
So my position remains:
- **Yes** to quantified restructuring.
- **Yes** to capacity reductions and productivity measures.
- **Yes** to temporary, negotiated employee contributions.
- **No** to compulsory redundancies.
- **No** to plant closures.
- **No** to an “all options on the table” posture that is really just a threat against the workforce.

If management and investors want a viable alternative, they should engage seriously with the closure-free scenario the expert described, not use it as a pretext to reopen the door to irreversible cuts. If needed, we are prepared to continue industrial action to make that clear.
