We agree on the diagnosis: this is a structural crisis, and cosmetic measures will not restore value. We also agree that prolonged conflict is expensive. But from a capital-markets perspective, the only question is whether the final package produces a **credible, durable return to competitive margins**.

## Where we agree
- **Management** is right that the current cost base is too high and the company needs real restructuring.
- **The expert** is right that non-closure measures can help, but may not be enough if the gap is truly structural.
- **The union and works council** are right that trust, industrial peace, and execution matter.
- **The state** is right that a purely destructive solution would damage legitimacy and long-term value.

## Where we disagree
### 1) “No closures, no compulsory redundancies” is not a sufficient answer
That remains the central problem.  
I do **not** accept a package that rules out the very instruments that may be necessary to fix a structurally uncompetitive footprint.

If the business is genuinely overcapacity and still missing its return-on-sales target, then the burden is on labour-side stakeholders to show a **quantified alternative** that closes the gap. So far, I have heard principles and red lines, but not a hard economic replacement for footprint reduction.

### 2) A cosmetic “hybrid” is not enough
I am open to a hybrid solution, but only if it contains **real capacity removal**, measurable cost cuts, and enforceable milestones.  
A hybrid that just re-labels delay, preserves too much footprint, and hopes productivity will magically do the rest is not a compromise I can support.

### 3) Binding guarantees are only acceptable if they are economically funded
I understand the demand for job and site security. But blanket guarantees through 2030 are not credible if they freeze an uncompetitive structure.  
If labour wants protection, it must come with a package that demonstrably restores competitiveness, not merely postpones hard choices.

## What would move us
We would support a deal only if it includes:
- **material and durable cost reduction**
- **real capacity adjustment**
- **measurable labour-cost contribution**
- **voluntary exits and redeployment as first tools**
- **tight execution milestones**
- **a credible path back to target margins**

That could be a hybrid package. It could even be a closure-free package **if** it is truly economically equivalent. But that equivalence has not been demonstrated yet.

## On the facilitator’s options
### Option 1: Time-bound hybrid with review gates
This is the most promising path, but only if the first phase delivers hard savings and the review gate is a real performance test, not a mechanism to endlessly renegotiate while the cost base stays bloated.

### Option 2: No-closure pledge with bounded contingency clause
I would not oppose this in principle, but only if the contingency is objective, narrow, and genuinely protects capital from under-delivery. If it becomes a backdoor to permanent non-action, it is useless.

### Option 3: Plant-by-plant equivalence test
Yes — this is the right next step.  
We need a plant-level comparison of:
- fixed-cost absorption
- utilisation
- conversion cost
- savings from shift reductions and redeployment
- one-time restructuring costs
- timing to run-rate savings
- impact on margins and return on sales

Without that, the debate stays ideological.

## Bottom line
Capital markets will back **whatever credibly restores competitiveness**. But we will not support an outcome that leaves the cost base structurally impaired or turns a turnaround into a symbolic peace deal.

So my position remains:
- **No** to cosmetic compromise.
- **No** to an under-restructured outcome.
- **Yes** to a quantified hybrid if it truly closes the gap.
- **Yes** to deeper structural change, including closures, if it does not.
