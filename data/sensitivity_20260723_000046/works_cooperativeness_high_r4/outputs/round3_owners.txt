I will be direct.

## Where I agree
I agree with management, the expert, the investors, and even the state on one basic point: **this is a structural crisis, and cosmetic measures are not enough**. The company must restore competitiveness, margins, and investment capacity. If it does not, long-term value erodes further.

I also agree that:
- prolonged industrial conflict is value-destructive,
- labour peace matters,
- and any settlement must be **quantified, durable, and enforceable**.

So I am not looking for a feel-good compromise. I am looking for a package that actually fixes the economics.

## Where I disagree
I do **not** accept the works council’s and union’s attempt to make:
- **no plant closures**
- **no compulsory redundancies**
- **all German plants open through 2030**

into hard prior conditions.

That is not a restructuring framework. It is a veto on necessary adjustment.

I also do **not** accept the state’s suggestion that because closures are disruptive, they should therefore be treated as off-limits. Disruption is not the test. **Economic adequacy is**.

And I do **not** accept the investor impulse to treat closure as the default answer either. Closure is a tool, not a virtue. If a negotiated hybrid package can close the gap credibly, then that is preferable to a destructive closure-led conflict. But it must be real, not cosmetic.

## On the expert’s scenarios
The scenario work was helpful because it clarifies the trade-off:

- **Closure-led**: strongest cost reset, highest conflict and execution risk.
- **Closure-free**: least disruptive, but highest risk of under-delivery.
- **Hybrid**: most plausible if it includes **real capacity reduction**, not just softer language.

That last point matters. A “hybrid” that keeps the same footprint and hopes for productivity miracles is not enough. Nor is a closure-free package acceptable if it merely defers the problem and burns value for another cycle.

## What I take from labour’s arguments
I hear the workforce side saying:
- workers should not carry the whole burden,
- trust matters,
- and the social cost of closures is real.

I do not dismiss that. But the response cannot be to freeze the company in place and call that stewardship. If the cost base remains structurally too high, then preserving every site and every guarantee simply transfers the problem into weaker margins, lower investment, and eventually less employment anyway.

That is the hard truth.

## What I require
For me to support any settlement, it must satisfy three tests:

1. **Material and durable cost reduction**
   - not symbolic savings,
   - not temporary optics,
   - but a real improvement in the cost base.

2. **Real capacity adjustment**
   - shift removal, model reallocation, line consolidation, or other footprint changes that actually raise utilisation.

3. **Governance stability**
   - no outcome that permanently impairs enterprise value or governance control,
   - no deal that creates open-ended instability,
   - no permanent erosion of the owners’ ability to steward the asset.

If those conditions are met, I can support a negotiated package. If they are not met, I will not endorse a false peace.

## On the facilitator’s options
### Option 1: Time-bound hybrid with review gates
This is the most workable route **if** the package includes hard numbers and real milestones.

I would support this only if:
- the first phase delivers measurable cost and capacity reduction,
- savings are verified,
- and the review gate is tied to performance, not endless renegotiation.

### Option 2: No-closure pledge with bounded contingency clause
I am cautious. I would not reject it outright, but only if the contingency is **narrow, objective, and formula-based**.

It cannot become a backdoor for permanent paralysis. If the clause is so weak that management can never act when the economics deteriorate, then it is not a serious restructuring tool.

### Option 3: Plant-by-plant equivalence test
This is useful and necessary. I support it.

But I want the test to answer the real question:
- what savings come from non-closure measures,
- what savings come from partial footprint reduction,
- what one-time costs each option carries,
- and what each does to margins and return-on-sales over a realistic horizon.

That is the only way to separate serious restructuring from political positioning.

## My minimum acceptable direction
I am prepared to support a package that combines:
- capacity reductions,
- voluntary exits and redeployment,
- working-time flexibility,
- productivity improvements,
- a time-limited employee contribution,
- and binding investment commitments in Germany,

**if** it closes the gap materially.

What I will **not** support is:
- a blanket no-closure commitment that blocks restructuring,
- a blanket no-compulsory-redundancy rule regardless of economics,
- or a deal that protects every legacy structure while leaving the company uncompetitive.

## Bottom line
My position is simple:

- **Yes** to a serious negotiated restructuring.
- **Yes** to a hybrid package if it is economically sufficient.
- **Yes** to transparency and plant-by-plant comparison.
- **No** to cosmetic compromise.
- **No** to permanent governance or value impairment.
- **No** to pretending that stability can be built on an uncompetitive cost base.

If the parties want me to back a settlement, bring me a package that is **quantified, enforceable, and economically equivalent** to the restructuring task. Otherwise, closures and deeper structural measures remain available because they may be necessary.
