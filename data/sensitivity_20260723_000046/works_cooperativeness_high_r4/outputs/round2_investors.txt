We are clear on the core issue: **the company must restore competitive margins through a restructuring that is real, durable, and measurable**. From the capital market side, that remains the only test that matters.

### Where we agree
- I agree with management, the family holding, and even the labour side on one point: **cosmetic measures are not enough**.
- I agree that prolonged conflict is value-destructive. Strikes, delivery disruption, and brand damage are not a harmless way to defend jobs; they destroy enterprise value.
- I also agree that any package must be **quantified**, not rhetorical. “Shared sacrifice” only counts if it produces hard savings.

### Where I disagree
I do **not** accept the repeated attempt by the works council, union, and state to turn:
- **no plant closures**
- **no compulsory redundancies**
- **all German plants open through 2030**

into hard prior conditions.

That is not a restructuring framework; it is a veto on the adjustment the business may need. If the company truly has structural overcapacity and a cost base above target, then those red lines can only stand if an alternative package is **economically equivalent** in verified terms. So far, I have not seen that level of proof.

### On the expert scenarios
The scenario comparison was helpful, and it actually sharpens the investor view:

- **Closure-led** restructuring is the strongest direct reset to cost and capacity.
- **Closure-free** restructuring is the easiest politically, but the riskiest economically because it may under-deliver and leave margins structurally impaired.
- **Hybrid** is the most credible path *if* it includes real footprint reduction, not just softer wording around the same overcapacity.

That means I am not demanding closures for their own sake. I am saying this: **if the numbers do not close the gap, then preserving every site is not an acceptable outcome**.

### What we will support
We would support a package only if it clearly delivers:
1. **material and durable cost reduction**
2. **real capacity adjustment**
3. **measurable labour-cost savings**
4. **a credible path back to return-on-sales target**
5. **front-loaded execution and enforceable milestones**

That can be through a hybrid package, or through a closure-led package if that is what the economics require. What we will not support is a “peace deal” that leaves the business with the same cost structure and just defers the pain.

### On the regional state and labour
I understand the political and social constraints, but they do not change the economics. The state cannot ask capital markets to fund sentiment. And the workforce cannot demand permanent job and site guarantees if the company cannot demonstrate the savings needed to remain competitive.

If the employee side wants to avoid closures, then it needs to put forward a **hard, plant-by-plant, quantified alternative** showing:
- where the savings come from,
- how much they are,
- when they arrive,
- and why they are sufficient.

Without that, “no closures” is just a refusal to confront the problem.

### On management
Management is right to insist that the restructuring must be durable. But durability is not the same as confrontation. Management still needs to show that it is selecting the **best value solution**, not merely the fastest or most forceful one.

### Bottom line
Our position remains:
- **No** to cosmetic compromise.
- **No** to a settlement that leaves the cost base uncompetitive.
- **Yes** to a quantified hybrid if it truly closes the gap.
- **Yes** to closures or deeper structural change if the alternative does not.
- **No** to open-ended conflict that destroys value while protecting legacy structures.
