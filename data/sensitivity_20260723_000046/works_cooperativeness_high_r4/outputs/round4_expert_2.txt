## Independent assessment: what the scenario implies economically

Based on the information provided, the company appears to be facing a **genuine structural overcapacity and cost-competitiveness problem**, not a short-term cyclical dip. In that setting, the three broad paths have very different economics:

### 1) Closure-led restructuring
**Strengths**
- Fastest way to reduce fixed cost and capacity.
- Highest probability of achieving a durable step-change in cost base.
- Strongest signal to capital markets that the issue is being addressed decisively.

**Weaknesses**
- Highest one-time cost: severance, decommissioning, transfer costs, supply-chain disruption.
- Highest execution risk from industrial action and operational disruption.
- Can damage trust and future productivity if implemented in a highly adversarial way.

**Economic role**
- Most appropriate if the gap is **large** and cannot be closed by internal measures plus voluntary reductions.

---

### 2) Hybrid restructuring
**Strengths**
- Usually the best compromise between economic adequacy and implementability.
- Can deliver meaningful savings through:
  - shift reduction,
  - site footprint consolidation without full closure,
  - voluntary exits,
  - redeployment,
  - working-time changes,
  - wage moderation / temporary contributions,
  - overhead reduction.
- Lower conflict cost than immediate closures.

**Weaknesses**
- Savings may be insufficient if underlying overcapacity is very large.
- Requires disciplined implementation and hard milestones.
- Can fail if it becomes a “delay tactic” without actual capacity reduction.

**Economic role**
- Most plausible if management needs a **large but not extreme** reset.
- Likely the best first-phase solution if the parties want to preserve optionality.

---

### 3) Closure-free, redundancy-free restructuring
**Strengths**
- Lowest social disruption.
- Lowest immediate conflict risk.
- Preserves organizational trust and site continuity.

**Weaknesses**
- Usually the weakest tool for removing structural fixed-cost burden.
- Can preserve excess capacity while moving cost reductions too slowly.
- Risk that savings are temporary or too small relative to the gap.

**Economic role**
- Credible only if the required gap is **moderate** and the package is unusually deep and verifiable.
- If management truly needs a “large, durable” cost reset, this path is often **economically insufficient** on its own.

---

## Likely magnitude of what non-closure measures can do

On a general OEM restructuring basis, a strong closure-free package can sometimes produce roughly **5–10% improvement in operating cost in the affected footprint**, depending on:
- how underutilized the plants are,
- how much indirect labour can be removed,
- whether shifts can be cut,
- how much volume can be consolidated,
- and how large the voluntary-exit uptake is.

That means:

- If the residual gap is in the **mid-single digits**, closure-free or hybrid may be enough.
- If the gap is in the **low-teens or higher**, a closure-free solution becomes much less likely to be sufficient.

Given your description — shrinking European market, high German cost base, missing ROS target, and management seeking a “large, durable reduction” — I would treat a **purely closure-free package as high-risk economically** unless the numbers show the gap is modest.

---

## Most likely economically credible path

The most credible path is usually:

### **Hybrid, phased, with a hard review gate**
A package that combines:
- selected shift removals,
- plant-level volume consolidation,
- voluntary exits,
- redeployment,
- temporary employee contribution,
- productivity and overhead reduction,
- investment commitments in future products,
- and a review gate tied to measured savings and utilisation.

This has the best chance of:
- delivering real savings,
- avoiding immediate full closure decisions,
- and keeping a backstop if the package underdelivers.
