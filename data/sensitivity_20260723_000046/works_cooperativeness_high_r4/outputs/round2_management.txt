Let me respond directly.

### First: what I agree with
I agree with all sides on one basic point: **cosmetic measures are not enough**. This company has a structural cost and capacity problem, and it must be solved in a way that is **durable**, not temporary.

I also agree that:
- **industrial peace matters**,
- **investment in future products matters**,
- and a purely destructive conflict would hurt everyone.

The expert’s assessment is also useful in one respect: it confirms what management has been saying all along — **non-closure measures can help, but they are not automatically enough** when the underlying overcapacity and cost gap are structural.

### Where I disagree
I do **not** accept the works council and union’s attempt to turn “no closures, no compulsory redundancies” into a hard starting point.

That is not a restructuring solution. It is a **constraint** on restructuring.

And it is not credible to say, in advance:
- every German plant must stay open,
- no forced redundancies can ever occur,
- and all historic protections remain intact,
while also demanding that management restore competitiveness, margins, and investment capacity.

Those positions do not add up.

### On the expert’s analysis
The specialist said something important: a closure-free package can work **if** the savings are broad, quantified, and strong enough.

I agree. But that is exactly the point: **if**.

What we have not heard from labour is a package that credibly closes the cost gap at the scale required. We have heard principles, red lines, and political arguments — but not a fully quantified alternative that matches the economics.

That is not sufficient.

### On plant closures
Let me be blunt: I am **not** prepared to rule out plant closures as a matter of principle.

Why?
Because if the company has structural overcapacity, then some footprint adjustment may be necessary to:
- reduce fixed costs,
- improve utilization,
- and restore margins.

If closures are never allowed to be considered, then management is being asked to protect every legacy structure regardless of economics. That would leave the company with an uncompetitive cost base and less money for the future.

Now, I am **not** saying closures are the first choice. I am saying they must remain on the table unless and until an alternative package is proven to be economically equivalent.

### On compulsory redundancies
I am also **not** willing to make an absolute no-compulsory-redundancies commitment.

That does not mean we want mass layoffs. It means we will not preclude the option if the restructuring requires it. The same applies to workforce reduction through attrition, voluntary exits, and redeployment — useful tools, but not a categorical substitute for structural adjustment.

### On the works council’s guarantees
A blanket guarantee that all German plants remain open through 2030 is not credible if it blocks the very restructuring needed to make the business viable.

Likewise, a binding job guarantee with no structural flexibility would amount to freezing the current cost base and calling that security. It isn’t security if it destroys the business’s ability to compete.

### On the union’s “shared sacrifice”
I agree with the union on one point: the workforce should not be the only party making a contribution.

But I reject the implication that any labour contribution must be matched by a permanent no-closure promise. That would make the company pay for concessions with strategic paralysis.

What we need is:
- a **real labour-cost contribution**,
- **capacity reduction**,
- **voluntary exits and redeployment where possible**,
- and a **credible investment commitment** for the future.

That is a restructuring package. What has been proposed by labour so far is still too weak on the central question: **where do the savings come from, and are they enough?**

### On the regional state
I appreciate the state’s concern for regional industrial capacity and legitimacy. But legitimacy is not the same as immunity from adjustment.

If the cost base remains structurally too high, then preserving every site for political reasons simply shifts the burden into weaker margins, lower investment, and longer-term decline. That helps no region.

### On the family holding and investors
I agree with both the family holding and institutional investors on one core issue: **the outcome must be economically adequate**.

I also agree that a purely symbolic deal would be unacceptable. The company does not need a public-relations compromise; it needs a **real restructuring**.

Where I would go further is this: if we cannot get there through voluntary, negotiated measures alone, then management must retain the ability to pursue deeper structural change.

### What management is prepared to consider
I am open to a package that combines:
- capacity reductions,
- shifts and working-time adjustments,
- voluntary exits and internal redeployment,
- productivity gains,
- a material employee contribution,
- and investment commitments into future products and technology.

But I will be equally clear: **that package must be strong enough to restore competitiveness.**

If it does not materially reduce the cost base and overcapacity, then it is not a solution.

### My bottom line
So here is the position, plainly stated:

- **Yes** to serious restructuring.
- **Yes** to negotiated measures where they are sufficient.
- **Yes** to employee contributions.
- **No** to cosmetic compromise.
- **No** to a blanket guarantee against closures or compulsory redundancies.
- **No** to a deal that leaves the company structurally uncompetitive.

If the workforce side wants to avoid closures, it needs to put forward a package that is **quantified, binding, and economically equivalent**. Until then, closures and deeper structural measures cannot be removed from the table.

If needed, I am prepared to ask for a detailed comparison of:
1. a closure-led package,
2. a hybrid package,
3. and a closure-free alternative,
so we can test which one actually closes the gap in hard financial terms.
