I will answer plainly.

## First: where I agree
I agree that we are in a **serious structural crisis**. I agree that cosmetic measures will fail. I agree that the package must be **quantified**, **durable**, and linked to **future investment in Germany**.

I also agree that a plant-by-plant analysis is useful. Transparency is better than vague threats. If management truly believes the gap is so large that closures are necessary, then it should have no problem showing the numbers.

## Where I fundamentally disagree
I do **not** accept the framing that plant closures and compulsory redundancies are “just tools” to be kept available like any other option.

They are not neutral tools. They are the destruction of:
- jobs,
- families,
- local economies,
- trust,
- and the cooperative basis of co-determination.

So my line remains unchanged:

- **No German plant closures**
- **No compulsory redundancies**
- **No weakening of the historic job-security framework**
- **No fait accompli restructuring**

If management wants a serious settlement, it must stop treating those outcomes as acceptable fallback options.

## To management
You keep saying the burden is on labour to prove an equivalent alternative. I reject that as a one-sided power argument.

Management made the strategic decisions that led to this position. Management must now prove, **with plant-level evidence**, why non-closure measures cannot work.

And let me be clear: “we need flexibility” is not proof.

If you want us to consider a restructuring package, then show:
- the cost gap,
- the savings from voluntary exits,
- the savings from shift and capacity changes,
- the savings from redeployment,
- the savings from temporary labour contributions,
- and the savings from productivity measures.

Then compare that against the true cost of closures, including:
- industrial conflict,
- loss of trust,
- execution disruption,
- and long-term damage to the brand and workforce commitment.

Until that is done, closures remain a political preference, not a necessity.

## To investors and the family holding
You are right about one thing: a symbolic deal would fail.

But you are wrong if you think “economic adequacy” automatically means preserving closure options. A company with strong co-determination cannot be run as if labour were just a variable cost to be cut.

You also underestimate the value destruction from confrontation. A deal that forces through closures may look efficient on paper and still destroy execution in reality.

So no, I do not accept your claim that closure flexibility is required to protect value. It may be convenient for you. It is not automatically rational for the company.

## To the regional state
I appreciate your recognition that the company is a regional industrial anchor. But that only matters if you are willing to act like it.

That means supporting:
- all German plants remaining open,
- no forced layoffs,
- binding investment and product commitments,
- and a genuine co-determination role in restructuring.

If you stop at “we prefer a hybrid,” without drawing a clear line against closures and redundancies, then you are not protecting industrial substance. You are just describing it.

## To the union
I agree with you on the need for a serious, negotiated solution and on the need for reciprocal sacrifice.

But I want to sharpen one point: employee contributions are possible only if they are **time-limited**, **fair**, and tied to real guarantees. Workers cannot keep giving while management keeps a closure gun on the table.

So yes to:
- working-time changes,
- shift adjustments,
- voluntary exits,
- redeployment,
- temporary wage moderation,
- and productivity measures.

No to:
- permanent wage damage,
- forced cuts,
- or using employee concessions as a bridge to later site closures.

## On the expert’s scenario work
The expert confirmed what we have been saying all along:  
a closure-free package can work **if** the gap is modest and the package is deep enough.

That is the point management keeps skating past.  
If the gap is modest, then closures are unnecessary.  
If the gap is large, then management must explain why it cannot be closed by the full range of non-closure instruments before reaching for closures.

I am not convinced that case has been made.

## On the facilitator’s proposals
### Proposal A: Two-phase hybrid with protected first phase
This is the only proposal that is even worth discussing, and only under strict conditions:
- no closures in phase 1,
- no compulsory redundancies in phase 1,
- binding investment commitments,
- real co-determination,
- and milestones that measure implementation of the agreed non-closure package.

But I do **not** accept phase 2 as an open backdoor to closures. If phase 2 is just a hidden trapdoor, then the guarantee is meaningless.

### Proposal B: No-closure pledge with a narrow contingency clause
This can only be considered if the contingency is:
- extremely narrow,
- objectively triggered,
- based on extraordinary external shocks,
- and never used as a routine restructuring escape hatch.

My trust level here is low. A “bounded contingency” can very easily become a polished way of breaking the promise later.

### Proposal C: Plant-by-plant equivalence test
I support this in principle. But let me be clear about the burden of proof:
- management must show why non-closure measures fail,
- not labour proving a negative in the abstract.

And the test must not become a spreadsheet ritual that ignores trust, industrial relations, and the social cost of destroying a German site.

## What I will accept
I remain prepared to negotiate a serious restructuring package that includes:
- capacity adjustments,
- working-time flexibility,
- voluntary exits,
- redeployment,
- productivity improvements,
- temporary employee contributions,
- and binding investment commitments in Germany.

But only if the package also includes:
- **no German plant closures**
- **no compulsory redundancies**
- **binding job and site guarantees through 2030**
- **real co-determination in shaping the restructuring**

That is not a decorative demand. That is the minimum for any credible settlement.

## What I will not accept
I will not accept:
- a “hybrid” that is really just closure language with softer wording,
- a contingency clause that reopens the closure threat,
- a package that weakens the collective job-security framework,
- or a restructuring imposed by pressure rather than negotiation.

## My response to the key question posed to me
The moderator asked what additional non-closure measures I would accept if one or two sites still left a material gap.

My answer is: we should not jump to closures at all. First exhaust the real alternatives:
- deeper voluntary exit programs,
- more intensive redeployment,
- stronger shift reductions,
- model transfers,
- work-time adjustments,
- internal efficiency measures,
- and reciprocal management/owner contributions.

Only after a fully transparent plant-by-plant comparison can anyone seriously claim that non-closure measures have failed. We are not there yet.

## Bottom line
I am not retreating.

If management, owners, investors, and the state want a settlement, they need to come back with a **closure-free, redundancy-free, plant-level credible package** backed by numbers and binding commitments.

If they refuse, then they should expect continued resistance — industrial, political, and through our co-determination rights.
