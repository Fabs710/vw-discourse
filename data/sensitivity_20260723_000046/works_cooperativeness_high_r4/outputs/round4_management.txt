I’ll be equally direct.

## Where I agree
I agree with the basic diagnosis from all sides: this is a **structural crisis**, not a temporary dip.

I also agree that:
- cosmetic measures are useless,
- the package must be quantified,
- and a negotiated solution is preferable to an open-ended conflict.

And yes, I agree that industrial peace has value. A company this size cannot be turned around in a constant strike environment.

## Where I disagree
I do **not** accept the premise that a serious restructuring can begin with:

- **no plant closures**
- **no compulsory redundancies**
- **all German plants open through 2030**
- **binding guarantees before the economics are fixed**

That is not a restructuring framework. It is a constraint that may prevent restructuring from happening at all.

### To the works council
You keep framing closures and forced redundancies as if they were illegitimate by definition. They are not. They are painful, yes. They are undesirable, yes. But when a company has structural overcapacity and an uncompetitive cost base, they remain legitimate tools if needed to restore competitiveness.

You say management has not proven necessity. I disagree. We have already shown the core facts:
- the European market is shrinking,
- competition is intensifying,
- German factory costs are far above target,
- and the brand is missing its return-on-sales target.

That is the proof of a problem. The burden is now on the employee side to produce a package that is not just socially preferable, but **economically sufficient**.

You have offered working-time flexibility, voluntary exits, and productivity improvements. I welcome those as components. But I do **not** believe they are enough on their own if the structural gap is large.

### To the union
You are right that workers should not be the only ones contributing. But your response still avoids the central issue: **how much cost and capacity reduction is actually delivered**.

A temporary employee contribution does not buy a permanent immunity from hard decisions. If the package does not close the gap, then the company still has to act.

You want a fair restructuring. Fine. So do I. But fairness does not mean preserving every site, every guarantee, and every legacy structure regardless of economics.

### To the regional state
I understand your political position. But preserving regional industrial substance does not mean freezing the industrial footprint in place.

If a site remains open while utilization stays weak and cost remains uncompetitive, that is not protection. That is postponement.

I am prepared to support a hybrid solution, but I will not commit to a blanket no-closure promise simply because it is politically easier. That would be irresponsible.

### To the family holding and investors
I agree with your central concern: the outcome must be **economically adequate**.

You are right that a cosmetic deal is worthless. You are right that the company cannot fund stagnation. And you are right that future product investment depends on freeing up resources today.

But I also want to be clear: the answer is **not** a reckless closure spree. The answer is a restructuring package that is large enough to matter. If labour produces a package that truly restores competitiveness, I can support it. If not, deeper structural measures remain necessary.

## On the expert’s scenario work
The specialist’s analysis confirms my view more than it undermines it.

What did we hear?

- **Closure-led restructuring**: strongest cost reset, highest conflict and execution risk.
- **Closure-free restructuring**: least disruptive, but least likely to close a serious structural gap.
- **Hybrid restructuring**: most plausible compromise, **if it includes real footprint reduction**.

That last condition is the key.

A “hybrid” that just means:
- fewer slogans,
- voluntary exits,
- some working-time changes,
- and a hope that productivity will magically solve the rest

is **not enough**.

If the package does not materially improve the cost base and utilization, then it is not a solution.

## What management is prepared to consider
Let me be precise. We are prepared to consider a package that includes:
- capacity reductions,
- shift removals,
- model reallocations,
- voluntary exits,
- redeployment,
- working-time flexibility,
- temporary employee contributions,
- and investment commitments for future products and technology in Germany.

But the package must pass one test:

**Does it materially restore competitiveness and get the brand back toward its return-on-sales target?**

If the answer is no, then I will not pretend otherwise.

## On the facilitator’s options
### Option 1: Two-phase hybrid with protected first phase
This is the most workable route **if** it delivers hard savings in phase 1 and not just promises.

But I will not accept a phase 1 that is protected so completely that phase 2 becomes unusable. A review gate must be real. If the package underdelivers, there must be a credible path to deeper adjustment.

### Option 2: No-closure pledge with a narrow contingency clause
I could consider this only if the contingency is:
- objective,
- formula-based,
- narrow,
- and genuinely tied to severe underdelivery or external shocks.

It cannot be a backdoor to permanent paralysis. If management is locked out of action even when the economics deteriorate, then the clause is useless.

### Option 3: Plant-by-plant equivalence test
I support this.

In fact, I think this is the right next step. But let’s be honest about what it means: the test must compare not just political comfort, but:
- fixed-cost absorption,
- utilization,
- conversion cost,
- one-time restructuring costs,
- and the run-rate savings required to hit target margins.

If a site can be restructured without closure, good. If not, then the company cannot be forced to pretend otherwise.

## What I will not accept
I will not accept:
- a blanket no-closure commitment,
- a blanket no-compulsory-redundancies commitment,
- a package that merely defers the problem,
- or an outcome that leaves the company structurally uncompetitive.

And I will not accept the argument that every closure is automatically illegitimate while every labour concession is automatically sufficient. That is not industrial reality.

## My position, plainly
I remain open to a negotiated settlement.

But it has to be a **serious** settlement:
- real capacity reduction,
- real labour-cost contribution,
- real run-rate savings,
- and a real path back to competitiveness.

If the labour side wants to avoid closures, it needs to bring forward a package that is **quantified, binding, and economically equivalent**. Until then, closures and deeper structural measures remain on the table because they may be necessary.
