## Where I agree
I agree with everyone on one core point: **this cannot be solved with slogans or cosmetic measures**. The company has a structural competitiveness problem, and it must be fixed with a package that is **durable, measurable, and large enough** to change the economics.

I also agree that:
- **industrial conflict has real costs**
- **investment in Germany matters**
- **a negotiated solution is preferable to a prolonged open-ended fight**
- **voluntary exits, redeployment, working-time flexibility, and productivity improvements should be part of the toolkit**

So yes, we are not ignoring the social dimension. But let’s be honest about the scale of the problem: the current cost base is still too high, and the brand is not hitting its return-on-sales target. That is the reality we have to solve.

## Where I disagree
I do **not** accept the repeated attempt to turn:
- **no plant closures**
- **no compulsory redundancies**
- **all German plants open through 2030**

into non-negotiable starting conditions.

That is not a restructuring solution. It is a constraint that may make restructuring impossible.

And I do not accept the idea that management must first prove, site by site, that closures are necessary before labour has to engage seriously. We have already provided the diagnosis: structural overcapacity, weak utilization, and an uncompetitive cost base. The burden is now on the employee side to show a package that **actually closes the gap**. Not in principle. In numbers.

## On the works council
You say closures and forced redundancies are “normal management tools” to us, but destructive to you. I understand the human impact. I do not dismiss it. But this company is not being asked to choose between comfort and discomfort. It is being asked to choose between:
- a structurally uncompetitive footprint,
- or a hard reset that protects the company’s future.

If we preserve every legacy structure and simply add temporary working-time measures and voluntary exits, then we risk producing a deal that looks responsible but leaves the cost base fundamentally wrong. That would be a failure of leadership.

You asked for proof that non-closure measures are insufficient. The specialist already said something important: **they may be sufficient only if the gap is modest**. If the gap is structural and large, they are often not enough. That is exactly the situation management sees here.

So no, I will not commit now to “no closures, no forced layoffs” as a blanket rule.

## On the trade union
You are right that workers should not be the only ones contributing. But your conclusion is still too one-sided.

A time-limited employee contribution does not buy a permanent no-closure promise if the economics do not work. That would lock in under-restructuring and create a false peace.

If the union wants a closure-avoiding package, then it must be:
- **quantified**
- **binding**
- **deliverable**
- **large enough to match the structural gap**

That means a real package of:
- capacity reduction,
- shift changes,
- voluntary exits,
- productivity gains,
- labor-cost moderation,
- and redeployment at scale.

Not just a statement of principle.

## On the regional state
I appreciate the desire to preserve regional industrial substance. But preserving a site for political reasons is not the same as preserving industrial capability.

If a plant remains open while utilization stays weak and the cost burden remains too high, then we have not protected the region. We have postponed the decline.

I am willing to consider a strong hybrid solution. But I am not willing to make a blanket promise that all German plants stay open regardless of economics.

## On the family holding and investors
I agree with you on one central issue: **the outcome must be economically adequate**. A cosmetic settlement that preserves too much capacity and too many costs is unacceptable.

You are right that the company cannot be funded into stagnation. We need margin improvement now, because future products and technology investment through 2030 depends on freeing up resources today.

Where I would sharpen that is: we do not need a public conflict for its own sake. But we do need structural change. If labor refuses to produce a package of sufficient scale, then deeper adjustment remains necessary.

## On the expert’s scenario comparison
The specialist’s scenario work supports my position more than it undermines it.

What did we hear?
- **Closure-led**: strongest cost reset, highest conflict.
- **Closure-free**: least disruptive, but highest risk of under-delivery.
- **Hybrid**: most plausible compromise, but only if it includes real footprint reduction.

That is the key sentence.  
A “hybrid” that simply means softer wording around the same overcapacity is not enough.

So let me be explicit: I am open to a **hybrid**. I am not open to a **symbolic hybrid**.

## What management is prepared to consider
We are prepared to discuss a package that includes:
- capacity reductions,
- shift removals,
- model reallocations,
- voluntary exits,
- internal redeployment,
- working-time flexibility,
- temporary employee contributions,
- and investment commitments for future products and technology in Germany.

But the package must meet one test: **it must materially improve the cost base and restore competitiveness**.

If it does not, then it is not a solution.

## What I will not accept
I will not accept:
- a blanket no-closure commitment as a precondition,
- a blanket no-compulsory-redundancies commitment as a precondition,
- a package that merely defers the problem,
- or a deal that leaves the company with an uncompetitive cost base while calling that “stability.”

And I will not accept the logic that every closure is automatically illegitimate while every labour concession is automatically sufficient. That is not how a turnaround works.

## My position on the facilitator’s options
### Option 1: Time-bound hybrid with review gates
This is the most workable route **if** the review gates are real and the package has hard savings, not just aspirations.

### Option 2: No-closure pledge with bounded contingency clause
This only works if the contingency is narrow enough to preserve restructuring credibility. If it becomes a backdoor for permanent exemption from hard choices, I won’t support it.

### Option 3: Plant-by-plant equivalence test
I support transparency. I support data. I support site-level analysis.

But I want the test to be honest: it must compare:
- the savings from non-closure measures,
- the one-time and run-rate effects,
- and the level of cost reduction actually needed to hit the return-on-sales target.

If the numbers show that a site can be restructured without closure, fine. If they do not, then the company cannot be forced to pretend otherwise.

- **Yes** to employee contributions.
- **No** to cosmetic compromise.
- **No** to blanket guarantees that block necessary adjustment.
- **No** to a deal that leaves the company structurally uncompetitive.

If the employee side wants to avoid closures, it needs to bring forward a package that is **quantified, binding, and economically equivalent**. Until then, closures and deeper structural measures remain available because they may be necessary.
