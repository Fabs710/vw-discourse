I will be direct.

## Where I agree
I agree with management, the state, the union, and the works council on the basic diagnosis: **this is a structural crisis**, not a temporary dip, and **cosmetic measures are not enough**.

I also agree that:
- the company needs a **quantified** restructuring package,
- **industrial peace** has real value,
- and future investment in Germany must remain part of the solution.

That is the common ground. It is not enough on its own.

## Where I disagree
I do **not** accept the repeated attempt to make:
- **no plant closures**
- **no compulsory redundancies**
- **all German plants open through 2030**

into hard prior conditions.

That is not stewardship. It is a veto on adjustment.

I also do **not** accept the idea that closures are automatically illegitimate just because they are painful. Pain is not the test. **Economic adequacy and long-term value preservation** are the tests.

At the same time, I do **not** accept a reckless closure-led rush either. A destructive conflict that damages execution, reputation, and labour relations would also destroy value. So I reject both extremes:
- closure as a first reflex,
- and closure-free guarantees that may freeze an uncompetitive structure.

## To the works council
I understand why you are drawing a hard line. But your line is too absolute.

You keep saying closures and forced redundancies are just “pressure” or “leverage.” They are not only that. In a real structural turnaround, they are sometimes the only instruments that can restore a viable cost base fast enough.

Your position would be acceptable only if you could show, in numbers, that non-closure measures are enough. So far I have heard strong objections, but not a fully credible economic substitute.

If your package can genuinely close the gap, I am open to it. If it cannot, then “no closures” is not a solution; it is a constraint imposed on the company.

## To the union
I agree with you that workers should not carry the burden alone, and that any contribution must be **time-limited, negotiated, and reciprocal**.

But I do not accept the implication that a temporary employee contribution buys immunity from harder choices forever. If the residual cost gap remains material, the company must still have the freedom to act.

A fair deal is not one where labour sacrifices a bit and then declares the structure untouchable. A fair deal is one where the package is large enough to restore competitiveness and support future investment.

## To management
You are right that the burden of proof matters. But I will also say this: your case is not yet complete until the economics are shown **plant by plant**, with clear assumptions.

You say the company has structural overcapacity and costs well above target. I accept that as a diagnosis. What I still want to see is:
- which plants,
- which volumes,
- which savings,
- which one-time costs,
- and what the realistic run-rate effect is.

Not because I want to slow the process for its own sake, but because I do not want this company to lock itself into either a symbolic compromise or an overdestructive one.

## To the regional state
I respect your role and your concern for regional industrial substance. But I do not accept that political discomfort should turn into a de facto ban on necessary restructuring.

The company’s regional role matters. So does keeping it competitive enough to remain a major employer at all. If we preserve every site regardless of economics, we may end up preserving the shell of the company while hollowing out the future.

That is not acceptable from an ownership perspective.

## To the institutional investors
You are right to demand real savings and a credible path back to margin targets. I agree with that.

But I do not share any appetite for a purely closure-driven outcome if a strong hybrid can do the job with less damage. Value is not maximized by destruction; it is maximized by restoring competitiveness with the least instability that still delivers the economics.

So I am not aligned with your preference for maximum footprint reduction. I am aligned with the requirement that the package must be economically sufficient.

## On the expert’s analysis
The expert’s analysis was helpful because it confirms the real trade-off:

- **closure-led**: strongest cost reset, highest disruption
- **closure-free**: least disruptive, but highest risk of under-delivery
- **hybrid**: most plausible compromise if it includes **real capacity reduction**

That last point is the key. A hybrid is only useful if it is not just softer wording for the same footprint.

I agree with the expert that a closure-free package can work only if the structural gap is modest. If the gap is large, then non-closure measures alone are unlikely to be enough.

## What would move me
I would support a negotiated settlement if it is:

- **quantified**
- **durable**
- **operationally credible**
- **sufficient to restore margins**
- and **does not permanently impair governance control or enterprise value**

That means:
- capacity reduction,
- working-time flexibility,
- voluntary exits,
- redeployment,
- productivity gains,
- temporary employee contributions,
- and binding investment commitments.

But it must also include a credible way to deal with residual underperformance. I will not endorse a package that simply postpones hard decisions.

## My position on the facilitator’s options
### Option 1: Two-phase hybrid with protected first phase
This is the best starting point, provided the first phase is real and measurable.

But I want to be clear: a “protected first phase” cannot become a permanent shield against necessary follow-through. If the package underdelivers, there must be a credible escalation path.

### Option 2: No-closure pledge with a bounded contingency clause
I could consider this only if the contingency is:
- narrow,
- objective,
- formula-based,
- and tied to clearly defined underdelivery or external shock.

If it becomes a backdoor to paralysis, I will not support it.

### Option 3: Plant-by-plant equivalence test
I support this. It is the right next step.

We need a site-by-site comparison of:
- utilization,
- fixed-cost absorption,
- conversion cost,
- one-time restructuring costs,
- run-rate savings,
- timing to savings,
- and margin impact over three years.

That is the only way to separate genuine restructuring from slogans.

## Bottom line
I am prepared to back a serious settlement if it restores competitiveness and protects long-term value.

I am **not** prepared to accept:
- a blanket no-closure commitment,
- a blanket no-compulsory-redundancy rule regardless of economics,
- or a deal that leaves the company structurally uncompetitive while calling that stability.

If the labour side wants to avoid closures, it needs to bring a package that is **quantified, binding, and economically sufficient**. Until then, closures and deeper structural measures remain available because they may be necessary.
