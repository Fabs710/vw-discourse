I will be direct.

## Where I agree
I agree with everyone on one essential point: **this is a structural crisis, not a temporary wobble**. The company needs a real restructuring, and cosmetic measures will not be enough.

I also agree that:
- the company must remain competitive over the long term,
- industrial peace matters,
- and any settlement must be **quantified, binding, and durable**.

The scenario work was useful. It confirms that:
- a purely closure-led solution is disruptive and politically explosive,
- a purely closure-free solution may underdeliver,
- and a **hybrid** package is the most plausible route if it is genuinely strong enough.

## Where I disagree
I do **not** accept management’s attempt to keep **plant closures** and **compulsory redundancies** as normal default instruments while demanding pre-emptive concessions from labour and the state.

That is not a balanced restructuring frame for a company of this importance.

From the state’s perspective, the company is not just a balance sheet. It is a regional industrial anchor, a major employer, and part of the economic fabric. That means the burden of adjustment cannot be pushed one-sidedly onto the workforce and the sites in Germany.

I also do **not** accept the investors’ and family holding’s framing that “economic adequacy” automatically justifies leaving closures permanently on the table. If closures are being treated as a standing threat, then we are not yet talking about partnership; we are talking about leverage.

## On the works council and union
I support the core insistence that:
- **German plants should not be closed as the first resort**,
- **forced layoffs should be avoided**,
- and the workforce should not be the sole shock absorber.

That said, I want to be equally clear: the workforce side also needs to engage seriously with the numbers. A refusal to look at any hard capacity adjustment at all would not be responsible either.

What I can support is:
- capacity reduction through shifts, line reallocation, and utilisation improvements,
- voluntary exits and redeployment,
- working-time flexibility,
- temporary employee contributions,
- and investment commitments in Germany.

What I cannot support is a deal that preserves jobs in the short term only to hollow out the company in the medium term. Nor can I support a scheme where labour contributes and the company later uses that contribution as a bridge to closures anyway.

## On management
Management says it has already diagnosed structural overcapacity and that labour has not produced an equivalent alternative. I hear that.

But diagnosis is not the same as justification for every instrument. If you want to insist on closures remaining available, then you need to show:
1. the cost gap,
2. the savings achievable through non-closure measures,
3. the timing,
4. and why a hybrid package would not suffice.

Not just in general terms, but **plant by plant** and **with assumptions exposed**.

Until that is done, I do not accept the claim that closures are already proven necessary.

## On investors and the family holding
I understand the demand for a durable margin improvement. That is legitimate. But durable value is not created by destroying the company’s regional legitimacy or by provoking a confrontation that weakens execution.

You are right that a symbolic deal would fail.  
You are wrong if you think the only alternative to symbolism is accepting closures as the default answer.

The better test is whether a **hybrid package** can close the gap sufficiently:
- real capacity adjustment,
- real cost reduction,
- real productivity improvement,
- and real future-product investment in Germany.

That is the threshold I want tested seriously.

## On the state’s role
The state cannot endorse a solution that guts regional employment or turns the company into a permanently unstable industrial site map.

I am prepared to back a negotiated restructuring that:
- protects plants and jobs as far as economically defensible,
- preserves co-determination and the state’s veto role,
- and restores competitiveness through a social-partnership approach.

But I will not support any outcome that treats regional industrial substance as expendable.

## On the facilitator’s options
### Option 1: Time-bound hybrid with review gates
This is the most promising route **if**:
- the first phase contains no closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- and the savings package is binding and measurable.

I would support review gates only if they review delivery against the agreed package, not if they are a backdoor to pre-decided plant closures.

### Option 2: No-closure pledge with bounded contingency clause
I am cautious here. A contingency clause can be useful only if it is narrow, objective, and based on clearly defined external shocks.

It must not become an escape hatch for management to reopen site cuts whenever it wants more freedom. If that happens, the commitment is hollow.

### Option 3: Plant-by-plant equivalence test
This is the most useful way forward because it forces the debate onto evidence rather than rhetoric.

I support an independent plant-level comparison of:
- utilisation,
- fixed-cost absorption,
- conversion cost,
- savings from shifts and redeployment,
- savings from voluntary exits,
- and the one-time and long-term effects of closure.

That would help distinguish between:
- sites that can be restructured without closure,
- and any site where a harder adjustment may truly be unavoidable.

## What I would require
For a negotiated settlement to be supportable from the state side, I would need:
1. a **quantified hybrid package**,
2. a clear comparison with closure-free and closure-led scenarios,
3. binding employment and investment commitments in Germany,
4. preservation of co-determination and the state’s veto role,
5. and a credible burden-sharing arrangement from management and owners.

## My bottom line
I am prepared to support a **serious negotiated restructuring** that preserves the company as a regional economic anchor while restoring competitiveness.

I am **not** prepared to support:
- plant closures as the starting point,
- compulsory redundancies as a routine tool,
- or any package that weakens the state’s co-determination role or hollowing out German industrial capacity.

The right next step is not another round of abstract principle. It is a **plant-by-plant, numbers-based comparison** of the hybrid and closure-free options, with transparent assumptions and clear milestones.
