From the perspective of the family holding, I see both the seriousness of the crisis and the limits of the positions just stated.

### Where I agree
- **Management is right** that cosmetic measures will not restore competitiveness.
- The company does need a **durable cost and capacity adjustment**.
- A purely symbolic solution would just defer the problem and ultimately weaken the enterprise further.
- I also agree with the workforce and the state that **industrial peace and legitimacy matter**. A restructuring that detonates the company’s internal stability would destroy value, not create it.

### Where I disagree
I cannot support the idea that **all German plants remain open as a prior condition** or that **no compulsory redundancies can ever be considered**.

That is not a credible basis for restoring margins in a business facing:
- a shrinking European market,
- intense low-cost competition,
- and a cost base that management says is structurally too high.

If those red lines are made absolute, then the result is likely to be either:
1. a deal that does not solve the economics, or
2. a prolonged conflict that damages the brand, customers, and ultimately the owners.

Neither is acceptable.

At the same time, I also do **not** support a blunt closure strategy pursued as if legitimacy were irrelevant. A solution that destroys trust, triggers years of industrial conflict, or weakens the company’s long-term productive base would also be value-destructive.

### What the family holding wants
We want a package that does three things at once:
1. **Restores competitiveness and margins**
2. **Preserves labour peace and operational stability**
3. **Protects the company’s long-term industrial base in Germany where that is economically defensible**

That means I am open to a negotiated restructuring, but only if it delivers real, durable savings. The package must be more than a promise of “efficiency” while the underlying overcapacity remains untouched.

### On the workforce’s demands
I understand the insistence on no forced redundancies and site security, but those demands can only stand if there is a credible alternative that closes the cost gap. If there is not, then insisting on them in absolute terms is effectively asking the owners to accept permanent value erosion.

That I cannot accept.

### On management’s approach
Management must also be careful not to confuse “structural change” with “maximum pressure.” If the approach becomes purely confrontational, it risks:
- hardening opposition,
- politicising the issue further,
- and making a bad outcome more likely.

If there is a viable negotiated route with:
- capacity reductions,
- voluntary instruments,
- working-time flexibility,
- a fair labour contribution,
- and targeted investment commitments,

then that deserves serious consideration.

### My bottom line
So my position is:
- **Yes** to real restructuring.
- **No** to a purely symbolic or delay-based solution.
- **No** to an outcome that permanently impairs enterprise value.
- **No** to an open-ended conflict that undermines the company’s governance and reputation.
- **Potentially yes** to a negotiated package, but only if it produces verifiable, durable competitiveness gains.

I am not here to endorse closure for its own sake, but I am equally not prepared to lock the company into a structure that cannot compete. If the employee side and the state want to avoid plant closures, they need to help produce an alternative that actually matches the scale of the problem. Otherwise, they are not offering a solution — they are postponing a crisis.
