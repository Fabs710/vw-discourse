I will be equally direct.

## Where I agree
I agree with the works council, the state, and the expert on one central point: **this is a structural crisis, and cosmetic adjustments are useless**. The company needs a package that is quantified, durable, and operationally credible.

I also agree that:
- **industrial peace matters**
- **investment in Germany matters**
- **voluntary exits, redeployment, working-time flexibility, and productivity measures must be part of the solution**
- **workers should contribute fairly**

So far, that is not in dispute.

## Where I disagree
I do **not** accept the idea that the only serious restructuring is one that keeps **plant closures** and **compulsory redundancies** openly available from the start.

That position is not a neutral technical necessity. It is a choice about who carries the burden.

And I do **not** accept the management/family/investor framing that says labour must first prove a closure-free path “economically equivalent” while management keeps the right to cut sites and jobs if it dislikes the answer. That is not a fair bargaining position; it is an asymmetry of power.

## On the works council
I support your core line:
- no forced redundancies,
- no plant closures,
- binding job and site security,
- and joint design under co-determination.

That is the correct starting point for a co-determined company with a real social responsibility.

Where I would sharpen it is this: if labour is to make a contribution, then it must be **time-limited, negotiated, and matched by contributions from management, owners, and, where relevant, capital**. We will not accept permanent wage destruction to paper over strategic failure.

## On management
You keep saying closures must remain on the table unless an alternative is proven equivalent.

My response is simple: **then prove it properly**.

Not with threats. Not with pressure. Not with abstract references to competitiveness.

If management claims the closure-free or hybrid route cannot work, then it should accept a **transparent, plant-by-plant comparison** under independent scrutiny, including:
- fixed-cost absorption,
- utilisation,
- labour-cost impact,
- one-time restructuring cost,
- and the effect of voluntary reductions and capacity measures.

Until then, “closures remain on the table” is just leverage dressed up as prudence.

## On investors and the family holding
I hear your demand for durability and margin restoration. That is legitimate.

But you cannot reduce a co-determined industrial company to a spreadsheet logic that ignores:
- strike risk,
- execution risk,
- trust,
- and the long-term damage from breaking the collective wage framework.

A deal that forces workers to pay all the pain is not a durable solution. It is a conflict deferred.

And I will say clearly: if the capital side insists on maximizing flexibility while minimizing its own contribution, then it should not be surprised when labour uses the tools it has — including warning strikes and further industrial action.

## On the regional state
Your insistence on preserving industrial substance in Germany is welcome, but it must be operationalized.

That means:
- real investment commitments,
- real product allocation,
- no casual acceptance of closures,
- and no forced layoffs.

If the state wants to be more than a symbolic actor, it should actively support a closure-free or at least closure-avoiding settlement with binding guarantees. General concern is not enough.

## On the expert’s scenarios
The expert basically confirmed what we have been saying:
- a closure-free package can work **if** it is broad enough,
- a hybrid is often the most plausible compromise,
- closure-led restructuring is the hardest and most disruptive path.

That does **not** justify making closure the default. It just means the alternative must be serious.

We are prepared to be serious.

What we are **not** prepared to do is accept a package where:
- employees sacrifice wages and guarantees,
- management keeps closures as a standing threat,
- and owners/investors present that as balance.

That is not balance. That is coercion.

## What a serious labour-side package can include
We are still prepared to negotiate:
- temporary working-time reductions
- shift and capacity adjustments
- voluntary exit programs
- redeployment across plants
- targeted productivity commitments
- temporary labour-cost contributions
- moderation of bonuses and other variable elements where applicable
- a defined contribution period with a clear end date

But this only works if it is tied to:
- **no plant closures**
- **no compulsory redundancies**
- **binding site and job guarantees**
- **investment commitments in Germany**
- **no weakening of the collective wage framework**

## My position on the three facilitator options
### Option 1: Time-bound hybrid with review gates
This is only acceptable if the review gates do **not** reopen closures and forced layoffs by default.

Review should mean:
- checking whether the agreed non-closure measures were implemented,
- whether management delivered its side,
- and whether the savings and investment commitments are on track.

It should **not** mean that labour signs up to a trapdoor for later closure.

### Option 2: No-closure pledge with bounded contingency clause
I am highly skeptical. A contingency clause can easily become a backdoor for future site cuts and layoffs.

If such a clause is to exist at all, it must be extremely narrow, objective, and tied to genuinely extraordinary external shocks — not to management’s normal desire for more freedom.

### Option 3: Plant-by-plant equivalence test
This is the only method I regard as genuinely useful, because it forces the discussion onto evidence.

But let me be clear: the burden should rest on those proposing closures to show that non-closure measures cannot work.

And if the test shows that a site can be restructured without closures, then that site stays open.

## Minimum labour position
My minimum remains:

- **no compulsory redundancies**
- **no German plant closures**
- **collective wage framework preserved**
- **temporary, fair employee contribution**
- **binding investment and future-product commitments**
- **jointly shaped restructuring**

If those conditions are rejected, there is no durable settlement — only confrontation.

## Final word
We are not refusing restructuring. We are refusing to let restructuring become a one-sided exercise in job destruction and wage pressure.

If management wants a settlement, it must come to the table with a **serious closure-free or closure-avoiding package** backed by numbers, not threats.

If it refuses, then the workforce will defend its interests with the full range of legitimate industrial means.
