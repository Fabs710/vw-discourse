## What I can assess
Within my remit, the key question is not whether restructuring is needed — everyone agrees it is — but **whether a closure-free or hybrid package can close the cost gap with enough certainty** to be credible to management and capital-side stakeholders.

For this kind of German automotive turnaround, the main operational levers without plant closures are:

- **capacity reduction via shifts / model allocation / line consolidation**
- **working-time changes** and flexible hour accounts
- **voluntary exits** and retirement bridging
- **internal redeployment** across plants and functions
- **temporary wage or bonus moderation**
- **productivity improvements** in indirects, logistics, changeover, scrap, energy, and maintenance
- **supplier and material-cost savings** where the brand has leverage

## My headline view
A **closure-free package can be credible only if the structural gap is moderate, not severe**. In large German automotive plants, the typical savings from non-closure measures are meaningful but limited unless the company is willing to make deep footprint and staffing adjustments inside the network.

As a rule of thumb:

- **Voluntary exits + redeployment + working-time measures** can often deliver about **5% to 12% of direct labour-related cost** over 1–3 years.
- **Shift removals / line consolidation / utilisation improvement** can add another **3% to 8% of conversion cost** at plant level, but only if volume is actually shifted out or concentrated.
- **Productivity programs** can contribute **2% to 5%** more, usually with diminishing returns after the first year.
- **Temporary wage contribution / concession** can help near-term cash and P&L, but it is rarely enough by itself to solve a structural margin gap.

So a serious closure-free package may realistically deliver something like **mid-single-digit to low-teens percentage improvement on plant conversion economics**, not a full reset if the underlying overcapacity is large.

## What that means in practice
If the company’s German manufacturing base is carrying:
- **major fixed-cost underabsorption**
- **multiple underutilized plants**
- and a **material cost gap vs. target/peer benchmarks**

then a no-closure alternative usually needs one or more of these:
1. **very deep voluntary headcount reduction**
2. **substantial volume transfer to fewer sites**
3. **permanent simplification of shift patterns**
4. **aggressive internal redeployment out of production**
5. **some permanent labor-cost reset**

Without that, the package risks becoming a delay tactic.

## Key distinction: savings type
It is important to separate:

### 1) Run-rate savings
These matter for competitiveness.  
Examples:
- lower headcount
- fewer shifts
- less overtime
- lower indirect staffing
- better utilization

### 2) One-time savings or deferrals
These help cash, but not competitiveness.  
Examples:
- hiring freeze
- temporary wage moderation
- short-term working-time cuts
- delayed capex
- one-off bonus restraint

A package can look large in year 1 and still be too weak in run-rate terms by year 3.

## My estimated scenario ranges
These are **illustrative operational ranges**, not company-specific facts:

### Closure-led restructuring
- **Strongest cost reset**
- Typically delivers the largest fixed-cost reduction
- Highest disruption, highest execution and industrial-relations risk
- Can remove **large structural overhead** and underutilized capacity

### Hybrid package
- Most realistic compromise in a co-determined German setting
- Can work if it includes **real footprint reduction without full closures**
- Usually delivers **moderate** savings if volume is concentrated
- Often the best balance of savings vs. feasibility

### Closure-free package
- Lowest disruption
- Highest risk of insufficient savings unless the company is willing to accept:
  - major voluntary exits
  - deep shift reductions
  - strong labour contributions
  - and tighter network integration

## Bottom line
My operational judgment is:

- **Yes**, a closure-free package can sometimes be made credible.
- **No**, it is unlikely to be sufficient if the company’s overcapacity and labour-cost gap are both material.
- The most plausible middle ground is a **hybrid package with real capacity reduction, voluntary exits, and measurable run-rate savings**.
- If the parties want to avoid closures, they will need to accept **deeper internal footprint rationalization** than is usually implied by a simple “no closures” promise.
