## Institutional Investors — Round 4

We are prepared to be blunt: **we will support the package that most credibly restores durable margins, fixed-cost absorption, and cash generation by 2030**. Not the package that is most comfortable politically, and not the one that merely postpones the adjustment.

### Where we agree

We agree with management, the family, and the expert on the core financial diagnosis:
- the German cost base is too high;
- the footprint is likely oversized;
- temporary measures alone will not solve the problem;
- and a cosmetic settlement would destroy value.

We also agree with labor and the state on one essential point:
- the workforce should not be the only side bearing the burden.

That is the right starting point. But it does **not** get anyone off the hook for delivering a real savings bridge.

---

## Where we disagree

### To the Works Council
You continue to say that the burden of proof lies with management to justify closures or compulsory redundancies. That is only half the equation now.

The expert has been clear: a no-closure path is **possible in principle**, but only if it creates a **large permanent correction**. That means the burden is also on the no-closure camp to show a **fully quantified** alternative that closes the margin gap.

What we do **not** accept:
- preserving every site as a starting assumption;
- relying mainly on temporary concessions;
- calling a structurally weak package “acceptable” because it avoids hard decisions;
- or leaving the company with margins that remain below target.

If you want closures off the table, then show us the numbers:
1. permanent savings,
2. fixed-cost removal,
3. capacity reduction,
4. and 2030 margin impact.

Until then, “no closures” is a preference, not a credible restructuring plan.

### To the Industrial Union
We appreciate your willingness to discuss burden-sharing, but the measures you emphasize still look too reversible:
- working-time changes,
- bonus waivers,
- temporary contributions,
- redeployment,
- qualification.

Those may help with cash and flexibility, but they do not necessarily create durable competitiveness.

What we do **not** accept:
- temporary concessions that expire before the business is healthy;
- a deal that preserves the cost base and then declares victory;
- or a settlement where the collective wage framework is protected so completely that management has no structural levers left.

If the union wants to avoid harsher measures, it must help produce **permanent** savings.

### To the Regional State Government
We respect the state’s role and the regional importance of the company. But a blanket “no closures” principle is not an economically credible governance standard if the network is genuinely overcapacity.

Protecting the region ultimately requires protecting a **viable company**. If the German footprint remains too expensive, then value erodes, investment capacity shrinks, and the industrial base is weakened anyway.

So yes: insist on a serious industrial plan.  
No: do not confuse political discomfort with economic viability.

### To the Controlling Family
You are right to reject a cosmetic compromise. But the family’s role is not just to endorse “real restructuring” in the abstract. It is to support the hard decision if the numbers require it.

That means not drifting into a position where:
- closures are rejected in principle,
- compulsory redundancies are rejected in principle,
- and yet no equally durable cost correction is accepted.

If the company needs permanent capacity reduction, then that reality should be faced openly. If it can be achieved without closures, fine — but that must be shown with numbers, not asserted.

### To Management
You are right that this cannot be solved by slogans. But you still need to do the one thing that turns argument into credibility: **show the savings bridge in detail**.

What we still need from you:
- current cost base,
- target cost base,
- gross gap,
- savings from labor measures,
- savings from headcount/capacity reduction,
- savings from complexity simplification,
- remaining gap,
- and the effect on return on sales by 2030.

Without that, “closure is necessary” remains a claim, not a demonstrated conclusion.

---

## Our position, plainly stated

We are **not** attached to closures as ideology. We are attached to:
- durable savings,
- fixed-cost relief,
- improved capacity utilization,
- and a credible return to competitive margins.

That means we could support a package that avoids closures **if and only if** it delivers:
1. permanent savings, not just temporary relief;
2. a real capacity correction;
3. measurable margin improvement;
4. execution certainty;
5. and no cosmetic preservation of uneconomic structures.

If those conditions cannot be met, then refusing closures becomes a way of preserving value destruction.

---

## What would move us

A credible package would need to show, in numbers, one of two things:

- **No-closure scenario**: enough permanent savings and capacity reduction to restore margins despite keeping all sites open; or
- **Partial consolidation / closure scenario**: better net present value after restructuring costs, with a clear margin uplift and lower structural cost base.

We are open on mechanism. We are **not** open on outcomes that leave the company structurally uncompetitive.

---

## Bottom line

We will support tough restructuring if it is:
- durable,
- measurable,
- and sufficient to restore competitiveness.

We will **not** support a package that mainly protects legacy claims while the business remains stuck with an uncompetitive cost base.

So the next step is not more rhetoric. It is the quantified comparison:
- no-closure,
- partial consolidation,
- and closure/transfer,

with annual savings, one-time costs, employment impacts, capex needs, execution risk, and 2030 return-on-sales effects.
