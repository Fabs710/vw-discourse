I will be very clear: I do **not** accept a restructuring logic that treats German plant closures or compulsory redundancies as the default answer. That would directly threaten regional employment, industrial stability, and the company’s role as a regional anchor — and it would be the wrong signal for long-term trust in this governance model.

## To management
You are right about one thing: the company needs **durable** restructuring, not delay, and temporary measures alone are not enough. I accept that the cost base must come down and that the 2030 margin target cannot be reached by wishful thinking.

But I strongly disagree with the way you are using the expert’s scenarios.

The expert did **not** say closures are inevitable. The expert said:
- a no-closure solution is **mathematically possible**,
- but only if it delivers a **large permanent correction** elsewhere.

That is not a mandate for closures. It is a demand for a serious, quantified alternative.

So my position is:
- show me a **site-by-site savings bridge**,
- show me what can be achieved through working-time changes, capacity adjustments, product reallocation, overhead cuts, and productivity gains,
- and show me why those measures, together with fair burden-sharing, are insufficient.

Until that is shown, I will not accept plant closure as necessary.

## To the works council and union
I agree with you on the core red lines:
- no compulsory redundancies as a first or routine instrument,
- no hollowing out of the job-security framework,
- no one-sided burden on the workforce,
- and no permanent wage rollback disguised as crisis contribution.

Where I want to be careful is this: I can support **temporary, limited, and reversible** employee contributions if they are genuinely part of a broader settlement and if they protect sites and jobs in the state. I cannot support a deal that shifts the adjustment burden onto labor while avoiding structural choices elsewhere.

So I support your insistence on:
- no closure-first logic,
- fair burden-sharing,
- and binding investment commitments.

But I also expect labor to engage with the reality that some **permanent capacity correction** may be needed somewhere in the system. That does **not** automatically mean closure. It does mean the package must be credible in operational terms.

## To the controlling family
I appreciate that you are not calling for cosmetic compromise. That is right. But I do not accept a position that leaves closures and forced redundancies as the “most efficient” fallback if negotiations become difficult.

As a long-standing shareholder, you also have a duty to protect the company’s legitimacy and its industrial base. That means supporting a solution that:
- preserves the German footprint as far as possible,
- maintains social partnership,
- and keeps the company viable for the long term.

If a non-closure package can deliver durable savings and capacity correction, you should back it. If you insist on only the most aggressive instrument, you will damage trust and increase execution risk.

## To the institutional investors
Your emphasis on fixed-cost absorption, margin uplift, and execution credibility is understandable. I do not dismiss it.

But you are flattening a governance reality into a spreadsheet problem. This company is not a normal listed company where the highest theoretical savings automatically win. Co-determination, statutory veto rights, and regional economic responsibility are real constraints — and ignoring them does not make them disappear.

You are right that a cosmetic settlement would be destructive. But you are **not** entitled to conclude from that that closures are therefore the necessary outcome.

If a solution can protect the regional industrial base and still deliver durable savings, that is the better long-term outcome — even if it is more complex.

## My position on the scenarios
From the figures presented:
- **No-closure** is weaker on savings, but it is **not ruled out**.
- **Partial consolidation** is financially stronger, but it becomes unacceptable if it is just closure by another name.
- **One-site closure / transfer** gives the strongest savings, but it is also the most socially disruptive and politically dangerous.

My threshold is not “whatever gives the cleanest model.” My threshold is:
1. protect jobs and industrial substance in the state,
2. preserve the company as a regional anchor,
3. keep the restructuring durable enough to restore competitiveness,
4. and preserve the state’s co-determination and veto role.

Anything that guts regional employment or sidelines the state’s role is off the table.

## What I can support
I am prepared to support a serious package if it includes:
- binding investment commitments,
- credible product allocation for German sites,
- real productivity and overhead correction,
- working-time and flexibility measures,
- redeployment and qualification,
- and a fair, limited employee contribution.

I remain open to **capacity reductions** and to a **negotiated restructuring** — but not to a closure-led solution unless every non-closure option has been genuinely exhausted and proven insufficient.

## What I reject
I reject:
- plant closures in Germany as the default tool,
- compulsory redundancies as a normal restructuring instrument,
- any outcome that weakens the state’s blocking minority or co-determination role,
- and any settlement that destroys trust in the regional economic anchor function of this company.
