From an operational and financial restructuring perspective, the core question is straightforward:

**Can this German footprint be made competitive enough without plant closures or compulsory redundancies, and what would it take to do so?**

## Short answer

**Possibly, but only if the company can achieve a very large permanent cost and capacity correction through other means.**  
If the gap versus target is as large as management suggests, then **temporary measures alone are unlikely to be sufficient**. A credible solution would typically require some combination of:

- permanent capacity reduction,
- major productivity improvement,
- lower fixed labor cost,
- better plant utilization,
- product and volume reallocation,
- and possibly one or more site-specific adjustments short of full closure.

If those measures cannot close the gap, then the remaining arithmetic usually points to **closure, consolidation, or transfer of production** somewhere in the system.

## The basic restructuring logic

In a car manufacturing network, the relevant variables are:

1. **Volume**
2. **Capacity utilization**
3. **Fixed cost absorption**
4. **Direct labor cost per vehicle**
5. **Overhead and support cost**
6. **Logistics and complexity cost**
7. **Capital intensity and depreciation**
8. **Mix effects**: EVs, ICE, premium, low-volume variants

When market demand is shrinking, a plant network built for a higher volume base becomes expensive fast. The problem is not just wages; it is also **under-absorption of fixed cost**. Even a well-run plant can become uncompetitive if it sits at 50–60% utilization while carrying large fixed overhead.

## What “no closures” would require

If every German plant is to remain open, then the company would need to achieve something like the following:

### 1) Permanent capacity reduction without site closure
This can happen through:
- removing shifts,
- ending selected derivatives or variants,
- consolidating platforms,
- moving volume between plants,
- reducing assembly hours,
- eliminating low-margin output.

But if the physical sites stay open, the fixed costs do not disappear fully. So the savings may be meaningful, but usually **less than a true closure**.

### 2) Major labor cost correction
This can include:
- wage moderation,
- temporary concessions,
- bonus deferrals,
- longer working time for the same annual pay,
- loss of legacy premiums,
- lower entry cost for new hires,
- reduced guaranteed payments.

However, **temporary concessions do not fix a structural problem unless they are renewed or converted into permanent savings**. From a financial standpoint, markets and creditors value **durable** cost reductions.

### 3) Productivity gains
This means:
- higher units per labor hour,
- less downtime,
- better line balance,
- fewer indirect staff per direct worker,
- lower rework and scrap,
- more flexible staffing.

Productivity improvements can be real, but in a mature automotive plant they rarely close a very large gap on their own.

### 4) Working-time and flexibility measures
These help with:
- demand volatility,
- shift smoothing,
- avoiding idle labor,
- better utilization.

They are useful, but again, they are not a substitute for structural capacity reduction if the network is oversized.

## What a credible quantified package would usually need to show

Management should be able to produce a **savings bridge** with at least these components:

- current German cost base
- target cost base
- gross gap
- savings from wage/labor concessions
- savings from headcount reduction
- savings from shift or capacity changes
- savings from complexity reduction
- savings from procurement and overhead simplification
- remaining gap

Without that bridge, arguments about necessity remain qualitative.

## Practical scenario ranges

I’ll frame this in generic terms because I do not have verified plant-level operating data here.

### Scenario A: mild restructuring
- 5–10% capacity reduction
- limited labor-cost concessions
- productivity improvements
- no closures

**Likely result:**  
Some improvement in EBITDA and cash, but often **insufficient** if the company is materially below target. This tends to stabilize, not transform.

### Scenario B: medium restructuring
- 10–20% capacity reduction
- stronger labor-cost correction
- shift removal at selected plants
- internal transfer of some production
- targeted headcount reduction
- no full closure, but some sites sharply resized

**Likely result:**  
This can be enough if the competitiveness gap is moderate and if management can execute aggressively. But it usually requires **real sacrifice** and a strong discipline on fixed costs.

### Scenario C: deep restructuring
- 20%+ permanent capacity reduction
- one or more site closures or production exits
- substantial headcount reduction
- structural overhead cuts
- major footprint simplification

**Likely result:**  
This is the most reliable way to restore margins if the cost gap is severe. It is also the most disruptive socially and operationally.

## Why “temporary employee contribution” is limited

A temporary concession can improve short-term cash and EBIT, but it has three limitations:

1. **It expires**
2. **It does not remove excess plant cost**
3. **It can be capitalized by markets only weakly unless accompanied by permanent structural change**

So if the company’s issue is a durable mismatch between cost base and volume, temporary contributions alone are usually a bridge, not a solution.

## The real decision trade-off

From a restructuring lens, the real choice is not:

- “closures or peace”

It is:

- **How much permanent correction is required, and where can it be absorbed with the least total cost?**

Total cost includes:
- severance,
- decommissioning,
- tooling relocation,
- write-downs,
- labor disruption,
- supply-chain disruption,
- lost output,
- and execution risk.

A closure may save more fixed cost, but it carries a heavy one-time burden. A non-closure solution avoids that one-time shock, but may leave too much fixed cost in the system.

## My assessment of feasibility

If management’s diagnosis is correct — that the German footprint is structurally too expensive and overcapacity is material — then:

- **No closures + no compulsory redundancies + no major labor-cost reform** = not financially credible.
- **No closures + strong labor-cost reform + meaningful capacity reduction + productivity gains** = potentially credible, but only if the cost gap is not too large.
- **Closure or consolidation of some kind** becomes likely if the required permanent savings exceed what the remaining plants can absorb through concessions and productivity.

## Bottom line

The key issue is not ideology; it is **whether the numbers close**.

A negotiated solution without plant closures is **not impossible**, but it needs:
- permanent, not just temporary, cost reduction;
- real capacity reduction somewhere;
- measurable productivity gains;
- and a savings bridge that gets the business to a sustainable margin.

If those conditions cannot be met, then from a restructuring standpoint the company will still face the same arithmetic, just delayed.
