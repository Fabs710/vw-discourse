We have now reached the point where words must be matched with a credible industrial solution.

## To management
I agree with you on one thing: this cannot be solved by delay, and temporary measures alone will not be enough if the structural gap is as large as you claim.

But I do **not** accept your attempt to turn that into a blanket argument for plant closures or compulsory redundancies. The expert’s assessment did **not** say closures are inevitable. It said a no-closure solution is possible **if** there is a large permanent correction elsewhere in the system. That is a very different statement.

So here is the position plainly:

- If you want to argue for closures, show the **site-by-site savings bridge** proving that the required savings cannot be achieved through a combination of:
  - working-time changes,
  - capacity reductions short of closure,
  - product and volume reallocation,
  - productivity measures,
  - overhead simplification,
  - and a negotiated employee contribution.

- If you cannot prove that, then closure is not a necessity — it is a choice.

And that choice is unacceptable if it means German jobs and sites are sacrificed while management and owners continue to frame the workforce as the primary adjustment variable.

## To the works council
We are aligned on the fundamentals:
- no plant closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- no dismantling of the collective wage framework,
- no unilateral attack on job security.

I support your insistence that the burden must be shared and that management must present hard numbers instead of slogans.

Where I want to sharpen the point is this: we must not allow “temporary” concessions to become a disguised permanent wage rollback. If the company needs employee contribution, then it must be:
- temporary,
- limited,
- fairly distributed,
- and matched by real contributions from management, ownership, and capital.

We will not accept a settlement where labor gives permanently and everyone else calls it realism.

## To the regional state government
I agree that the state should not defend the status quo blindly. A real restructuring is necessary.

But I disagree with any framing that treats closures as a normal or first-choice instrument. In this governance structure, plant closures are not just operational decisions; they are industrial and social ruptures. If the state wants to protect the regional base, it should use its role to insist that **all non-closure alternatives are exhausted first**.

That means:
- a site-by-site plan,
- a quantified savings bridge,
- binding investment commitments,
- and a solution that keeps all German plants open unless proven impossible.

## To the controlling family
You are right that the company needs a durable competitiveness reset. We do not dispute that.

What we reject is the assumption that this can only be achieved by asking the workforce to absorb the damage through closures or forced layoffs. There are other permanent levers:
- capacity reduction through shifts and product allocation,
- internal redeployment,
- qualification,
- overhead cuts,
- productivity gains,
- and negotiated cost contributions.

If ownership wants credibility, it should support a solution that shares the burden fairly instead of drifting toward management’s harshest option once pressure rises.

## To the institutional investors
I understand your focus on margins, cash flow, and fixed-cost absorption. Those are real metrics.

But your position keeps flattening the governance reality into pure spreadsheet logic. This company is not a simple listed entity where the fastest cost cut automatically wins. Co-determination, statutory vetoes, and industrial peace matter. If you push a closure-heavy solution that ignores that reality, you increase execution risk and industrial conflict.

So yes: we agree that the final package must be durable and financially credible.  
No: we do **not** accept the conclusion that closures are the only credible route.

## What we can support
We are prepared to negotiate a serious package that includes:
- working-time flexibility,
- temporary and limited employee contributions,
- internal redeployment,
- qualification,
- product and volume reallocation,
- shift changes,
- and capacity adjustments.

But the package must satisfy our core conditions:
1. **No plant closures**
2. **No compulsory redundancies**
3. **No unilateral attack on the collective wage agreement**
4. **Fair burden-sharing from all sides**
5. **Binding job and site security through the restructuring horizon**

## What we reject
We reject:
- closure-first logic,
- “targeted” cuts that are really forced layoffs by another name,
- open-ended insecurity,
- and any deal where employees carry the main pain while management and ownership contribute only rhetorically.

## My bottom line
The expert has confirmed that the issue is arithmetic, not ideology. Good. Then let the arithmetic be tested honestly against a **no-closure, no-forced-redundancy** solution before anyone pretends layoffs or closures are unavoidable.

If management cannot prove that all viable alternatives fail, then it does not have the right to impose plant closures or compulsory redundancies.

If it can prove that with hard numbers, we will still insist on:
- fair burden-sharing,
- protection of the collective wage framework,
- and the strongest possible safeguards for jobs and sites.

If that is refused, then the union will continue to defend its members with the means at its disposal.
