## Controlling Family Holding — Round 4

I will respond directly, because the discussion now needs decisions, not posture.

### Where I agree

I agree with everyone on the basic diagnosis:
- the company has a real structural competitiveness problem;
- temporary measures alone are not enough;
- the workforce should not carry the burden alone;
- a cosmetic compromise would destroy value;
- and we need a quantified savings bridge, not slogans.

I also agree that a disorderly conflict would be damaging. That matters to us as long-term owners.

### Where I disagree

#### To the Works Council
You keep saying the expert shows that a no-closure solution is “possible in principle,” and therefore closures are not justified. That is not a sufficient conclusion.

“Possible in principle” is not the same as:
- credible at scale,
- durable through 2030,
- or sufficient to restore margins.

You are asking us to treat plant closures as off-limits unless management proves they are the only option. That is the wrong standard.

The correct standard is:
- can the company achieve a durable cost base and margin reset without permanent capacity reduction?
- if yes, fine.
- if no, then some form of site-level correction remains on the table.

I do **not** accept a line that says:
- every German plant must stay open,
- compulsory redundancies are categorically impossible,
- and the legacy job-security framework is untouchable,
while the company is still expected to become competitive.

That would preserve the structure of the problem, not solve it.

#### To the Industrial Union
I agree that employee contributions must not become a permanent wage rollback. That would be unacceptable.

But I do **not** accept the argument that temporary concessions, working-time flexibility, redeployment, and productivity measures are enough by themselves if the footprint remains structurally oversized. They may help, but they are not automatically a durable solution.

You are also too quick to treat closures and compulsory redundancies as illegitimate by definition. They are not desirable, but they can become necessary if the numbers do not close otherwise.

#### To the Regional State Government
I understand your political responsibility, and I share the instinct to protect the regional industrial base.

But a blanket “no closures” principle is not a viable governing rule if the economics do not work. Protecting the region long term means protecting a viable company, not every site in its current form.

I do not want reckless action. I do want realism.

#### To the Institutional Investors
Your focus on durable savings, fixed-cost absorption, and margin uplift is correct. That is the right financial standard.

What I do not accept is a purely spreadsheet-driven conclusion that ignores execution reality. A technically optimal restructuring that cannot survive co-determination, statutory vetoes, and industrial peace is not a real solution.

But the reverse is also true: a politically comfortable settlement that leaves the company structurally uncompetitive is equally unacceptable.

#### To Management
I support the need for a hard restructuring. But you still have not closed the gap between:
- “there is a cost problem”
and
- “therefore this exact form of restructuring is unavoidable.”

The expert has given ranges, not inevitability. So the burden is to prove the durability of the package, not simply to repeat that the footprint is expensive.

### My position

I am open on **mechanism**. I am **not** open on preserving an uncompetitive structure by default.

That means:
- I am open to no-closure if it truly delivers equivalent permanent savings;
- I am open to partial consolidation if that is the only credible route to durability;
- I am open to site-level correction if the no-closure path falls short.

What I am **not** open to is a settlement that:
- keeps every site intact by political preference,
- relies mainly on temporary concessions,
- and leaves the company stuck below target margins.

That would be value-destructive.

### What I need before supporting any final settlement

I want a side-by-side comparison with hard numbers for:
1. **No-closure / no-forced-redundancy**
2. **Partial consolidation**
3. **One-site closure / production transfer**

For each scenario:
- annual savings,
- one-time restructuring cost,
- employment impact,
- required capex / investment,
- and expected impact on return on sales by 2030.

That is not bureaucratic delay. That is the minimum basis for a responsible governance decision.

### My bottom line

We will support a tough restructuring if it is:
- durable,
- measurable,
- and sufficient to restore competitiveness.

We will **not** support a package that merely postpones the adjustment or preserves legacy structures at the expense of long-term value.

If the numbers show that a no-closure package can achieve the required permanent correction, then I will support it.

If they do not, then the discussion has to become honest about the need for deeper structural change.
