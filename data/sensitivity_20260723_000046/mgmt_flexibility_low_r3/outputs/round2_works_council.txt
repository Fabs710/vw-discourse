We need to move from abstract pressure to concrete responsibility.

Management keeps repeating “competitiveness” as if that alone justifies breaking the social compact. It does not. If the company has a cost problem, then **management, ownership, and capital must also bear the burden**. The workforce will not be the only side asked to pay for strategic failures.

Let me be explicit on the points that remain non-negotiable from the works council side:

- **No plant closures in Germany.**
- **No compulsory redundancies.**
- **No dismantling of the job-security framework by the back door.**
- **No fait accompli presented to the workforce as “the only realistic option.”**

## Where I agree

I agree with everyone on one point: this company needs a credible restructuring, not cosmetic delay.

I also agree that:
- we need a durable solution, not a temporary patch;
- capacity, working time, qualification, redeployment, and negotiated contributions are legitimate instruments;
- prolonged conflict is bad for the company and the workforce.

But agreement ends there if the solution is built on closures and forced job cuts.

## Where I disagree

### To management
You say the German footprint is too expensive. That may be your diagnosis, but your chosen remedy is still politically and socially unacceptable if it destroys sites and jobs that can be protected through other means.

You have **not yet shown** that the required savings can only be achieved through closures or forced redundancies. Until you do, those options remain an assertion, not a necessity.

If you want this to move, then bring:
- a **site-by-site industrial plan**,
- a **quantified savings bridge**,
- a **credible investment commitment**,
- and a package that preserves employment through 2030.

If the plan depends on closure as the first instrument, we will resist it with everything co-determination and the workforce allow us to use.

### To the institutional investors
You keep saying the form matters less than the financial outcome. That is exactly the problem. For you, people become a variable in a spreadsheet. For us, they are livelihoods, communities, and the industrial base of this company.

If your definition of “credible” means “whatever cuts margins fastest,” then you are missing the governance reality of this company. You cannot just demand deep cuts and ignore co-determination, the statutory veto over closures, and the social consequences of breaking job-security promises.

### To the regional state government
I appreciate that you reject reckless closures. But “not reckless” is not enough. The workforce needs binding protection, not political sympathy. If you are serious, then use your shareholder and statutory role to block any proposal that makes plant closure or forced layoffs the starting point.

### To the controlling family
You say you do not support a closures-first approach. Good. Then do not drift into supporting management’s pressure tactics once the numbers get difficult. If you want a serious turnaround, insist on a plan that protects industrial substance in Germany rather than hollowing it out.

## What would actually be acceptable

We are ready to negotiate a serious package if it is based on all of the following:

1. **Every German plant remains open.**
2. **No compulsory redundancies.**
3. **Binding site and job guarantees through 2030.**
4. **Capacity adjustments through working-time, shift, and product allocation measures.**
5. **Internal redeployment and qualification instead of layoffs.**
6. **A fair employee contribution that is temporary, limited, and matched by contributions from owners and management.**
7. **A real co-determination role in shaping the restructuring, not just being informed afterward.**

That is the minimum basis for a settlement.

## What I reject

I reject the idea that the choice is between closures and collapse. That is a management framing tactic, not an economic law.

I also reject:
- “targeted” redundancies if they are in fact forced layoffs by another name;
- “capacity rationalization” if it means shutting a plant in practice;
- “future investment” as a bargaining chip unless it is tied to binding employment and site commitments;
- vague promises about competitiveness with no guarantee for the workforce.

## Bottom line

If management wants a negotiated solution, it must come back with a package that:
- keeps all German plants open,
- rules out compulsory redundancies,
- and gives the workforce real security in return for any contribution.

Without that, there is no basis for an agreement from the works council side. And if the company tries to force the issue, it should expect sustained resistance from the workforce, including escalation beyond warning strikes.
