We have now moved from broad principles to the real issue: **whether the company can restore competitiveness without permanent capacity reduction in Germany, or whether some form of site-level correction is unavoidable.**

My view remains clear.

## Where I agree

I agree with almost everyone on the following:
- this company needs a **durable** restructuring, not delay;
- temporary measures alone are unlikely to solve a structural cost problem;
- the workforce should not carry the burden alone;
- prolonged conflict would damage value, the brand, and the industrial base;
- we need a **quantified savings bridge** and a serious scenario comparison.

Those are all sensible points.

## Where I disagree

### To the Works Council and Union
I do **not** accept the position that:
- every German plant must remain open,
- compulsory redundancies must be ruled out absolutely,
- and legacy guarantees must remain untouched,
while the company is still expected to reach a materially better cost and margin structure.

That is not a restructuring answer. It is a request that the company absorb structural overcapacity without structural correction.

The expert did not say that a no-closure solution is proven. The expert said it is **possible only if** there is a large permanent correction elsewhere. That is the crucial point.

So I reject the claim that management has not made its case unless it proves closure is the only option. That is the wrong standard. The correct standard is: **can the company achieve durable savings sufficient to restore competitiveness?** If the answer is no without site-level change, then site-level change must remain on the table.

I also do not accept the suggestion that any discussion of closures is automatically “closure-first logic.” There is a difference between:
- using closure as a bargaining weapon, which I do not support, and
- acknowledging that a permanently oversized network may require permanent correction, which I do support.

### To the Regional State Government
I understand the desire to protect the regional base. I share that instinct. But a blanket “no closures” stance is not a viable governing principle if the economics do not work.

Protecting the region long term means protecting a **viable company**, not preserving every site in its current form regardless of cost and utilization.

I do not want reckless action. I do want realism.

### To the Institutional Investors
I agree with your focus on durable value creation, but I would caution against treating the form of restructuring as irrelevant. In this governance structure, execution matters. A theoretically optimal solution that cannot survive co-determination and statutory constraints is not a solution.

That said, your core point is right: if the final package does not materially improve fixed-cost absorption, capacity utilization, and margins, then it is value-destructive.

### To Management
I support your insistence that this cannot be solved by cosmetics. But support for restructuring is **not** support for any specific instrument. You still need to show:
- what permanent savings are required,
- where they come from,
- and why the company can or cannot achieve them without permanent site/capacity correction.

Right now, I do not see a fully quantified bridge from “cost gap” to “acceptable package.”

## My position, plainly stated

I am open on the **mechanism**. I am not open on a solution that:
- preserves the current footprint by default,
- relies mainly on temporary concessions,
- and leaves the company structurally uncompetitive.

That would be a poor use of the family holding’s control position and would risk long-term value erosion.

At the same time, I am also **not** endorsing closures as an abstract principle. If there is a credible no-closure package that delivers equivalent permanent savings and a durable margin reset, I would support that. But it must be credible in numbers, not just in intention.

## What I need before supporting any final settlement

I want a **side-by-side scenario comparison** with hard numbers:
1. **No-closure / no-forced-redundancy**
2. **Partial consolidation**
3. **One-site closure / production transfer**

For each scenario, I want:
- annual savings,
- one-time restructuring cost,
- employment impact,
- required capex / investment,
- and expected impact on return on sales by 2030.

## Bottom line

I am prepared to support a tough restructuring if it is:
- durable,
- measurable,
- and sufficient to restore competitiveness.

I will **not** support a package that merely delays the adjustment or preserves legacy structures at the expense of long-term value.

If the numbers show that permanent correction can be achieved without closures, then that deserves serious consideration.  
If they do not, then the debate needs to become honest about the need for deeper structural change.
