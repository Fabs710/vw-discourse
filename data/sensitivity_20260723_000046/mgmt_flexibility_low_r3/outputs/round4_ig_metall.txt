I will be direct.

## To the Works Council

We are aligned on the essential red lines:
- no plant closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- no dismantling of the collective wage framework,
- no permanent wage rollback disguised as “contribution.”

I support that. I also support your insistence that the burden of proof remains on management to demonstrate why any harsher step would be necessary.

Where I want to sharpen the position is this: **we should not let management turn “durable restructuring” into a coded demand for closures by default.** The expert did not say closures are inevitable. The expert said a no-closure solution is only plausible if the permanent savings are large enough. That means the burden is still on management to show the full bridge — not on labor to accept the harshest option because it is easier to model.

I also agree that temporary employee contributions must stay temporary and reversible. If management wants flexibility, then it must not be one-way flexibility extracted from workers.

## To management

You are still not answering the key question.

You keep saying that no-closure is not automatically sufficient. Fine. But that is **not** the same as proving that closures or compulsory redundancies are required. The expert’s scenarios show that:
- no-closure is weaker,
- partial consolidation is stronger,
- closure is strongest on paper.

That is an economic comparison, not a mandate.

If you want to move this discussion, then produce a **site-by-site savings bridge** that proves:
1. how much permanent savings are needed,
2. what share comes from labor,
3. what share comes from overhead and management-side cost cutting,
4. what share comes from product allocation and capacity changes,
5. and why all of that still fails without a closure.

Until you show that, closure is still a choice — and one we reject.

And let me be clear: **compulsory redundancies are not a normal restructuring instrument** in this situation. If you continue to treat them as such, you will escalate resistance, not resolve the crisis.

## To the Regional State Government

I agree with you on one critical point: the workforce should not carry the burden alone, and the company needs a real industrial future in Germany.

Where I disagree is your comfort with the phrase “partial consolidation” as if it were automatically safer than closure. It may be safer on paper, but if it becomes the first step toward a slow erosion of sites and jobs, then it is just closure-by-another-name.

If you want to use your blocking role responsibly, then use it to insist on:
- no plant closures as the starting point,
- no forced redundancies,
- binding investment commitments,
- and a restructuring design that preserves the collective industrial base.

But I will also say this: we cannot accept that the state becomes the pressure point to force labor into accepting permanent losses while management and ownership stay rhetorically “pragmatic.” Real burden-sharing must include capital and management, not just workers.

## To the Controlling Family

You say you are open on mechanism, not ideology. Fine. Then let’s be precise.

What we will not accept is a “mechanism open” stance that quietly leaves plant closure on the table as the preferred solution. The company’s historic job-security framework is not a nuisance. It is part of the institutional trust that makes restructuring possible at all.

If ownership wants credibility, it should commit to:
- a no-closure first approach,
- maximum use of working-time, shift, redeployment, and productivity tools,
- a fair employee contribution that is temporary and capped,
- and stronger owner-side and management-side contributions.

If your response is simply “we need whatever is economically best,” then I have to say plainly: that is not a bargaining position compatible with protecting the workforce from bearing all the pain.

## To the Institutional Investors

You are focused on durable savings and margin improvement. I do not dispute that these matter. But your argument still collapses the issue into a spreadsheet choice.

The question is not whether a closure gives more savings in isolation. The question is whether the company can achieve a durable solution **without** sacrificing jobs and sites first. The expert has shown that no-closure is weaker — not impossible. That means the conversation is still about what mix of:
- permanent capacity reduction,
- overhead simplification,
- productivity gains,
- and fair contributions
can close the gap without violating the social compact.

If you insist that only the most aggressive route is credible, then you are increasing the probability of conflict, execution risk, and value destruction. That is not prudence; that is tunnel vision.

## Where we stand after the expert’s scenarios

The modeling tells us three things:

1. **No-closure is not trivial**  
   Correct. It requires real permanent correction. That is exactly why we have always said temporary measures alone are not enough.

2. **Partial consolidation is financially stronger**  
   Also true. But it is only acceptable if it does **not** become a pretext for closing plants by stages or forcing people out.

3. **Closure is strongest on paper**  
   Yes — and also the most socially destructive, highest risk, and least acceptable to the workforce unless every other avenue has been shown to fail.

That last point matters. The strongest number is not automatically the correct industrial solution.

## What we can support

We are prepared to negotiate a serious package that includes:
- working-time flexibility,
- shift reductions,
- internal redeployment,
- qualification,
- productivity improvements,
- overhead cuts,
- product and volume reallocation,
- and a **time-limited**, capped employee contribution.

But only under these conditions:
- **no plant closures**
- **no compulsory redundancies**
- **no permanent weakening of the collective wage framework**
- **fair burden-sharing from management and ownership**
- **binding job and site security through the restructuring horizon**

## What we reject

We reject:
- closure-first bargaining,
- compulsory redundancies as a routine lever,
- “partial consolidation” if it is just closure in slow motion,
- and any settlement where labor is told to absorb the bulk of the adjustment because that is the easiest path on a balance sheet.

## My bottom line

Management has options. It has not proven that the hardest ones are unavoidable.

So our position remains unchanged:
- protect all German plants,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- preserve collective wage standards,
- and build the permanent savings through negotiated, socially responsible means.

If management refuses to build that package, then it is choosing conflict over settlement. In that case, the union will respond with the instruments available to it, including industrial action.
