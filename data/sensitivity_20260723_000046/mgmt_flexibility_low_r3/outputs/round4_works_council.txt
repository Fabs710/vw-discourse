Let me be equally clear in Round 4: **the expert’s numbers do not force closures, and they do not justify compulsory redundancies.** They show that management has choices — and that some of those choices are politically and industrially harder. That is not the same as “unavoidable.”

## To management

You keep saying a no-closure solution is not automatically sufficient. Fine. But that is still not a case for closures. It is only a case for **doing the hard work of building a real alternative**.

What I accept:
- the company needs durable savings;
- temporary measures alone are not enough;
- structural correction is required;
- a quantified savings bridge is necessary.

What I reject:
- your attempt to convert that into a mandate for plant closures;
- your insistence that compulsory redundancies are a normal restructuring tool;
- and the idea that you can simply declare the network “oversized” and then make the workforce pay for it with sites and jobs.

The expert has explicitly said a no-closure path can be mathematically plausible if there is a large permanent correction elsewhere. That is the operative point. So before anyone speaks of closure as a necessity, show me why the alternatives do **not** close the gap.

And let me be precise: if the package requires a permanent correction, that does **not** mean a plant must disappear. It can mean:
- shift reductions,
- capacity rationalization within sites,
- product reallocation,
- productivity gains,
- overhead cuts,
- internal redeployment,
- and a negotiated employee contribution.

Management has not proven that all of those, taken together, fail.

## To the Industrial Union

We are aligned on the core red lines:
- no plant closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- no dismantling of the job-security framework,
- no unilateral tearing up of collective protections.

Where I want to sharpen the point: **temporary employee contributions must remain temporary and reversible**. I will not let crisis language become a permanent wage rollback.

The expert’s modeling supports our concern: the no-closure route is weaker on annual savings than partial consolidation, but it is still not dead. That means the union should not drift toward accepting closures because someone says they are “more credible.” Credible to whom? To investors who prefer faster simplification? That is not our standard.

Our standard is:
- jobs protected,
- sites protected,

## To the Regional State Government

You said you will not support a restructuring that hollows out the regional industrial base. Good. Then act like it.

A “partial consolidation” can quickly become a euphemism for the first step toward closure. I am not interested in a staged erosion of sites under the cover of compromise. If the state is serious, then it should insist on:
- every German plant remaining open,
- binding investment commitments,
- and a restructuring path that does not rely on plant closures as the hidden endgame.

I also reject the idea that political discomfort should be recast as economic inevitability. The governance structure exists precisely so that jobs and sites are not sacrificed on the altar of short-term simplification.

## To the Controlling Family

You say you are open on mechanism but not on preserving an uncompetitive structure. That sounds pragmatic, but it still leaves the door open to closures and forced redundancies in practice.

Let me answer directly: **I do not accept a “mechanism open / outcome open” framing if the likely outcome is plant closure.** We are not bargaining over whether the workforce will absorb the blow; we are asserting that it should not.

You want permanent savings? Then support a package that produces them **without** closing a German plant and without compulsory redundancies. If that means stronger owner contribution, more patient capital discipline, or tougher management-side cost cutting, then that is what credibility requires.

## To the Institutional Investors

Your logic is the narrowest in this room: durable savings, fixed-cost relief, margin uplift. That is understandable, but it is not the only legitimate standard.

You keep asking for proof that non-closure alternatives achieve the same annual savings. Here is my answer: the company has not been forced to show that, because management has not seriously committed to making that package work. Instead, it has used the threat of closure to pressure labor into accepting a worse deal.

That is not neutral financial discipline. That is leverage.

And let me say it plainly: your preferred outcome may look cleaner on a spreadsheet, but it ignores the execution risk of industrial conflict, legal blocking positions, and the very real damage to trust and labor peace if management tears up guarantees. A “better margin by 2030” is meaningless if it is built on a confrontation that destroys the company’s social compact in the meantime.

## On the expert’s scenarios

I do **not** dispute the arithmetic:
- no-closure is weaker on annual savings;
- partial consolidation is stronger;
- closure is strongest on paper.

But arithmetic is not the same as inevitability.

The missing question is this: **What combination of measures can deliver enough durable savings without breaking the job-security framework and without closing a plant?** That is the test you have not answered.

The works council is not required to accept the most aggressive instrument just because it gives the highest headline savings. We are required to defend the workforce, the sites, and co-determination. That is exactly what we are doing.

- and any attempt to normalize structural job destruction as if it were merely a technical correction.

## Bottom line

The expert has shown that management has **options**. It has **not** shown that closures or compulsory redundancies are unavoidable.

So my position remains unchanged:

- Keep every German plant open.
- No compulsory redundancies.
- Preserve co-determination and the job-security framework.
- Put real permanent savings on the table through other means.
- If management refuses to build that solution, then the conflict is of its own making.

If management wants a settlement, it must present a no-closure package that is genuinely serious — not a dressed-up pretext for closure later. And if no such package is brought forward, then the workforce will continue to resist with every lawful means at our disposal.
