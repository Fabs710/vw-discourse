We are now at the point where the discussion has to be judged by one criterion: **does the package credibly restore competitive margins on a durable basis, or does it merely postpone the problem?**

From the perspective of dispersed capital-market shareholders, that is the only standard that matters.

## Where we agree

We agree with management, the family, and the expert on the core diagnosis:
- the German cost base is too high;
- the footprint appears oversized relative to current demand;
- temporary measures alone are unlikely to be enough;
- and a cosmetic compromise would destroy value.

We also agree with labor and the state on one important point:
- the workforce cannot be the only side asked to pay.

So far, so good. But agreement on the diagnosis does **not** mean we accept any solution that avoids difficult structural decisions.

## Where we disagree

### To the Works Council
You keep saying the burden of proof remains on management to justify closures or compulsory redundancies. That is only partly true.

At this stage, the burden is also on those rejecting permanent site/capacity reduction to show a **fully quantified alternative** that gets the company back to competitive margins. A general insistence on:
- no plant closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- no weakening of guarantees,

is **not** enough if the company still ends up with an uncompetitive cost structure.

The expert did **not** say a no-closure solution is automatically sufficient. The expert said it is only possible if there is a **large permanent correction elsewhere**. That is exactly the point you still have not answered convincingly.

What we do **not** accept:
- preserving every site regardless of economics;
- relying mainly on temporary employee contributions;
- defining “socially acceptable” in a way that leaves margins structurally impaired;
- or treating site protection as a substitute for industrial competitiveness.

If you want closures off the table, then show how the same permanent savings are achieved by other means. Until then, “no closures” is a preference, not a solution.

### To the Industrial Union
We appreciate your willingness to discuss burden-sharing. But your position still relies too heavily on reversible measures:
- working-time changes,
- bonus waivers,
- temporary contributions,
- redeployment,
- qualification.

Those can help, but they do not reliably solve a structural overcapacity and fixed-cost problem.

What we do **not** accept:
- temporary concessions that expire before the company is healthy;
- a settlement that preserves the cost base and calls that a turnaround;
- or a deal that protects the collective wage framework so completely that management is left without durable correction.

If the union wants to avoid harsher measures, it must help produce **permanent** savings, not just postpone the cost.

### To the Regional State Government
We respect the governance role and the regional stake. But “no closures” as a principle is not a financially credible position if the network is genuinely overcapacity.

Protecting the region ultimately requires protecting a viable company. If the domestic footprint remains too expensive, then the company weakens, investment capacity falls, and the long-term industrial base erodes anyway.

So yes, insist on a serious industrial plan. But do not confuse political discomfort with economic viability.

### To the Controlling Family
We agree that a cosmetic settlement would be value-destructive. But the family’s role is not just to say “real restructuring” in the abstract. It is to support the hard decision if the numbers require it.

That means not drifting toward a position where:
- closures are rejected in principle,
- compulsory redundancies are rejected in principle,
- and yet no equally durable cost correction is accepted.

If the company needs permanent capacity reduction, then that reality should be faced openly. If it can be achieved without closures, fine — but that must be shown with numbers, not asserted.

### To Management
You are right that the situation cannot be solved by slogans. But you still need to do the one thing that turns argument into credibility: **show the savings bridge in detail**.

What we still need from you:
- current cost base,
- target cost base,
- gross gap,
- savings from labor measures,
- savings from headcount/capacity reduction,
- savings from complexity simplification,
- remaining gap,
- and the effect on return on sales by 2030.

Without that, “closure is necessary” is still a claim, not a demonstrated conclusion.

## Our position, plainly stated

We are **not** attached to closures as an ideology. We are attached to:
- durable savings,
- fixed-cost relief,
- improved capacity utilization,
- and a credible return to competitive margins.

That means we could support a package that avoids closures **if and only if** it delivers:
1. **permanent** savings, not just temporary relief;
2. a **real** capacity correction;
3. a **measurable** margin improvement;
4. execution certainty;
5. and no cosmetic preservation of uneconomic structures.

If those conditions cannot be met, then refusing closures becomes a way of preserving value destruction.

## What would move us

A credible package would need to show, in numbers, one of two things:

- **No-closure scenario**: enough permanent savings and capacity reduction to restore margins despite keeping all sites open; or
- **Partial consolidation / closure scenario**: better net present value after restructuring costs, with a clear margin uplift and lower structural cost base.

We are open on mechanism. We are **not** open on outcomes that leave the company structurally uncompetitive.

## Bottom line

We will support tough restructuring if it is:
- durable,
- measurable,
- and sufficient to restore competitiveness.

We will **not** support a package that mainly protects legacy claims while the business remains stuck with an uncompetitive cost base.

So the next step is not more rhetoric. It is the quantified comparison:
- no-closure,
- partial consolidation,
- and closure/transfer,

with annual savings, one-time costs, employment impacts, capex needs, and 2030 margin effects.

Without that, no one can credibly claim that the most difficult measures are avoidable.
