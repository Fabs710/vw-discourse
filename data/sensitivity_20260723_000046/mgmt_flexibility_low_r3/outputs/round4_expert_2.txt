## Interpretation

### 1) No-closure package
This is only credible if the company’s gap is **moderate** and can be closed with:
- shift reductions
- higher line efficiency
- overhead cuts
- redeployment
- product reallocation
- temporary employee contribution

If the company’s structural cost gap is large, this option often **under-delivers on durable fixed-cost relief**.

### 2) Partial consolidation
This is usually the best compromise **economically** when:
- one plant or one major sub-operation is clearly redundant or underutilized,
- but full closure would be disproportionately disruptive,
- and the company needs a material permanent cost reset.

This is often the strongest balance between:
- savings,
- feasibility,
- and governance acceptability.

### 3) One-site closure / transfer
This is the most powerful lever on paper, but it can lose part of its theoretical value if:
- industrial conflict delays implementation,
- the receiving plants need expensive capex,
- or launch/quality issues occur during transfer.

So the headline savings must be judged **net of disruption and ramp-up cost**, not only by gross annual savings.

---

## Decision test

A prudent board-level test would be:

- If **no-closure** closes at least **~70–80%** of the required gap with manageable capex and low execution risk, it may be sufficient.
- If it closes only **~50–70%** of the gap, then **partial consolidation** is the more credible middle path.
- If the gap remains large after partial consolidation, then **closure/transfer** becomes the economically stronger option despite the higher social and execution cost.

---

## Bottom line

Within the assumptions here:

- **No-closure** is plausible, but only for a **smaller** required correction.
- **Partial consolidation** is the most balanced option if management needs a real structural reset without going straight to closure.
- **One-site closure** gives the strongest cost relief, but also the highest one-time cost and execution risk.
