From the perspective of dispersed institutional investors, the key test is simple: **does the package credibly restore margins, competitiveness, and cash generation?** If yes, we can support it. If not, we will not back a settlement that merely preserves the status quo under a different label.

## Where we agree

We agree with management, the family, and even the employee side on one important point: **this cannot be solved by delay**. The company faces structural pressure — weaker demand, tougher competition, and a cost base that is too high for the market. A cosmetic deal that avoids the hard arithmetic would be value-destructive.

We also agree that **prolonged conflict is bad**. A strike-driven stalemate would hurt volumes, margins, customer confidence, and the investment case. We want certainty and execution, not an endless confrontation.

## Where we disagree

### To the works council and union
Your insistence on no closures and no compulsory redundancies is understandable from a workforce perspective, but from a shareholder perspective it is only acceptable **if the end result still delivers the required savings at scale**.

What we will not support is:
- a “guaranteed” settlement that leaves the cost base structurally uncompetitive,
- a package that substitutes promises for hard savings,
- or an arrangement that protects every site and every legacy rule while margins remain depressed.

If the company’s economics require capacity reduction, then some combination of closures, site consolidation, job cuts, working-time reductions, or lasting labour-cost concessions has to happen. The form matters less than the financial outcome. But the outcome has to be real.

### To the regional state government
We respect the political and regional role, but a blocking stance against closures cannot be the default answer. Protecting the industrial base requires a viable business, not simply preserving footprints. If the structure remains too expensive, the company will lose competitiveness and eventually invest less everywhere, including in Germany.

### To the controlling family
We appreciate the willingness to accept structural restructuring, but we want to be clear: support for “real improvement” must be measurable. We are not interested in vague assurances about future discipline. We need an outcome that shows up in:
- return on sales,
- free cash flow,
- capacity utilization,
- and a credible path to sustainable margins.

## What would move us

We would be willing to support a negotiated package if it includes all of the following:
1. **Durable, quantified cost reduction**
2. **Clear capacity rationalization**
3. **A credible labor contribution**
4. **No open-ended commitment that freezes the cost base**
5. **Deal certainty with executable timelines**
6. **Investment commitments tied to actual restructuring, not offered as a substitute for it**

We are open on mechanism:
- plant closures,
- partial consolidation,
- working-time adjustments,
- redeployment,
- temporary concessions,
- or a combination.

But we are not open on the core issue: **the restructuring must be deep enough to restore competitiveness**.

## Bottom line

We do **not** take a side in favor of management, labor, state, or family identity. We take a side in favor of **financially credible restructuring**.

If the emerging deal leaves the company with:
- the same structural overcapacity,
- an uncompetitive cost base,
- and margins still below target,

then we will view it as value-destructive and object to it through capital-market channels, analyst pressure, and our allocation decisions.

If, however, the final package demonstrates a durable return to competitive margins and disciplined cost reduction, we can support it — even if the path there is politically difficult.
