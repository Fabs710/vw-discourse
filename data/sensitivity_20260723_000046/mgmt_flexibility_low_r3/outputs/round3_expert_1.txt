Below is a quantified, operational comparison of the three broad restructuring paths, using a German automotive footprint with several plants and a structurally high fixed-cost base.

I will stay within my remit: capacity, cost, cash, margin, and execution tradeoffs. I will not opine on governance or labor strategy beyond the operational consequences.

## Executive view

**Core finding:**  
A **no-closure** path can be made *mathematically plausible* only if it delivers a very large, durable cost reduction through some combination of:
- major labor-cost concessions,
- shorter working time / fewer shifts,
- higher utilization,
- significant productivity gains,
- overhead simplification,
- product transfer,
- and possibly selective asset rationalization inside sites.

But in most realistic German OEM structures, **a true no-closure / no-forced-redundancy outcome is usually the weakest on durable savings and the most execution-sensitive**.

A **partial consolidation** scenario often gives the best balance of:
- durable fixed-cost relief,
- manageable one-time costs,
- lower industrial disruption than full closure,
- and better 2030 margin restoration than “no closure.”

A **full closure / production transfer** gives the strongest structural savings, but also:
- the highest one-time restructuring cost,
- the highest execution risk,
- the largest employment shock,
- and the greatest supply-chain and launch-risk exposure.

## Assumptions used

Because no actual site data were provided, I use a stylized German carmaker footprint:
- 4–6 German vehicle / major component plants
- total German direct industrial workforce in the low tens of thousands
- current operating margin below target
- significant fixed-cost absorption issue
- labor cost materially above benchmark peers
- high complexity from overlapping product / platform allocation

I assume a **baseline structural gap** versus target of roughly:
- **€1.5bn to €2.5bn annual EBIT improvement needed** at the brand level to restore acceptable margin by 2030
- with **€0.8bn to €1.4bn of that needing to come from German footprint correction**
- and the rest from mix, pricing, product cadence, overhead, and group-level actions

These are estimates, not facts.

# 1) Scenario comparison

## A. No-closure / no-forced-redundancy scenario

### What it typically contains
- all German plants remain open
- headcount reduced only by natural attrition, voluntary exits, retirement, redeployment
- working-time reductions or shift cuts
- temporary or semi-permanent wage concessions
- productivity and overhead programs
- product allocation optimized within existing network
- some capacity reduction through lower shifts and longer downtime, not site exits

### Annual savings potential
**Estimated durable annual savings:** **€300m to €700m**

Typical breakdown:
- labor concessions: €100m to €250m
- shift / working-time reductions: €50m to €150m
- overhead simplification: €75m to €150m
- productivity / scrap / logistics improvements: €75m to €150m
- product transfer / allocation benefits: €0m to €100m

### One-time restructuring cost
**Estimated one-time cost:** **€150m to €500m**
- mainly severance for voluntary exits
- retraining / redeployment
- system and process changes
- modest asset write-downs if any lines are idled

### Employment impact
- **Direct job reduction:** likely **1,500 to 4,000** equivalent positions over time
- mostly through attrition and voluntary measures
- minimal involuntary job loss if the package is very generous and the timeline is long

### Capacity impact
- **Capacity reduction:** roughly **5% to 12%**
- usually via fewer shifts, lower weekend build, delayed launches, or selective line idling
- little true structural network simplification

### Margin effect by 2030
- likely **+0.5 to +1.2 percentage points** EBIT margin improvement at the brand level versus baseline
- enough to help, but often **not enough alone** if the gap is severe

### Assessment
This is the **lowest-disruption** path, but also the one with the greatest risk of:
- savings dilution
- reversion after temporary concessions expire
- residual overcapacity
- underinvestment if cash relief is too small

**Operational verdict:** viable only if the true gap is modest. If the structural gap is large, this scenario is usually **insufficient**.

---

## B. Partial consolidation scenario

### What it typically contains
- one major site or a major sub-operation is reduced or consolidated
- another site absorbs volume or a product line
- some shifts are removed across the network
- job reductions are larger, but not all plants are closed
- more permanent capacity removal than in the no-closure scenario

### Annual savings potential
**Estimated durable annual savings:** **€700m to €1.3bn**

Typical breakdown:
- fixed-cost absorption improvement: €200m to €400m
- plant overhead and indirect labor reduction: €150m to €300m
- logistics / complexity simplification: €50m to €150m
- productivity and line utilization gains: €100m to €200m
- labor cost correction: €150m to €250m
- inventory / quality / disruption benefits: €50m to €100m

### One-time restructuring cost
**Estimated one-time cost:** **€500m to €1.2bn**
- severance and social plan cost
- asset write-downs
- transfer / relocation logistics
- launch disruption
- capacity retooling at receiving sites
- temporary inefficiency during ramp-up

### Employment impact
- **Direct job reduction:** roughly **4,000 to 8,000** positions
- if done partly via transfers, the net involuntary loss can be lower
- but the footprint becomes materially leaner

### Capacity impact
- **Capacity reduction:** roughly **12% to 20%**
- removes genuine structural excess
- improves utilization at surviving plants

### Margin effect by 2030
- likely **+1.2 to +2.0 percentage points** EBIT margin improvement
- often enough to move a weak brand materially closer to target if execution is disciplined

### Assessment
This is frequently the **best tradeoff**:
- meaningful permanent savings
- manageable one-time cost
- less risk than full closure
- clearer credibility with investors

**Operational verdict:** often the most realistic solution when the network is clearly oversized but the governance structure makes full closure hard.

---

## C. One-site closure / production transfer scenario

### What it typically contains
- closure of one German plant or one complete production footprint
- production shifted to another German site or abroad
- major simplification of the network
- substantial permanent reduction in fixed costs
- likely larger headcount reduction

### Annual savings potential
**Estimated durable annual savings:** **€1.0bn to €1.8bn**

Typical breakdown:
- site fixed-cost elimination: €250m to €450m
- indirect labor and overhead cuts: €200m to €350m
- logistics and complexity savings: €100m to €200m
- improved utilization at remaining sites: €200m to €350m
- labor cost improvement: €150m to €250m
- procurement / manufacturing standardization benefits: €100m to €200m

### One-time restructuring cost
**Estimated one-time cost:** **€900m to €2.0bn**
- severance and closure-related costs
- asset impairments
- transfer and duplicate-running cost
- ramp-up losses at receiving plant
- supply chain changes
- potential customer service / quality disruption

### Employment impact
- **Direct job reduction:** roughly **5,000 to 10,000+** positions depending on site size
- some roles may transfer, but not all
- local labor-market and political impacts are severe

### Capacity impact
- **Capacity reduction:** roughly **15% to 25%**
- strongest structural correction
- highest improvement in utilization of remaining footprint

### Margin effect by 2030
- likely **+1.8 to +3.0 percentage points** EBIT margin improvement
- best chance of reaching a high-confidence return-on-sales target

### Assessment
This is the **strongest financial reset**, but also:
- highest one-time cost
- highest execution risk
- biggest industrial-relations shock
- greatest risk of launch delay and reputational damage if the transfer is mishandled

**Operational verdict:** best on paper for margins and fixed cost, but not always the best net outcome once disruption and execution risk are fully priced.

# 3) What matters most economically

## A. Fixed-cost absorption
If the company is underutilized, the key value driver is not just labor cost. It is **fixed-cost absorption**:
- plant overhead
- maintenance
- utilities
- indirect labor
- logistics
- quality / rework burden
- depreciation drag on low-output sites

If volume is spread across too many sites, the cost curve is structurally too high. That is why **capacity reduction is often more important than wage cuts**.

## B. Temporary vs permanent savings
A central distinction:
- **temporary concessions** help cash and breathing space
- **permanent network simplification** improves margin durability

The market will generally discount temporary labor concessions unless they are tied to structural capacity reduction or permanent productivity change.

## C. One-time cost versus net present value
A closure-heavy option can look attractive on annual savings, but it only wins if:
- transfer execution is clean,
- launch losses are contained,
- customer quality does not suffer,
- and the NPV of savings exceeds the one-time cost and disruption.

This is where many restructurings fail in practice.

# 4) Likely conclusion for a German OEM like this

If the company truly has:
- a materially oversized German footprint,
- structurally elevated labor and overhead costs,
- and a return-on-sales gap that must be closed by 2030,

then:

### Most plausible operational outcome:
**Partial consolidation** is often the most realistic compromise between:
- financial necessity,
- execution feasibility,
- and political / co-determination constraints.

### When no-closure can work:
Only if the cost gap is **moderate**, and if the employee contribution plus productivity gains are unusually large and durable. In practice, that is difficult.

### When closure becomes economically necessary:
If the required annual savings exceed roughly **€1.0bn** from the German footprint alone and the network cannot generate that through non-closure measures, then **some site-level reduction becomes hard to avoid** from a financial perspective.

---

# 5) Practical model for decision-making

A useful test is this:

## If the package can deliver all four:
1. **€800m+ durable annual savings**
2. **at least 10%–15% capacity reduction**
3. **clear fixed-cost elimination**
4. **margin uplift of 1.5 points or more by 2030**

then it is likely financially credible.

If not, the company risks:
- under-resolving overcapacity,
- paying restructuring costs without enough benefit,
- and returning to the same problem in 24–36 months.

---

# 6) Bottom line

From a turnaround perspective:

- **No-closure** is the least disruptive, but often too weak unless the cost gap is small.
- **Partial consolidation** is usually the best balance of savings, speed, and feasibility.
- **One-site closure / transfer** gives the strongest financial reset, but with the highest social and execution cost.
