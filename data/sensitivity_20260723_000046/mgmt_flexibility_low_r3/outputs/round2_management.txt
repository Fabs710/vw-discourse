We are at the point where slogans have to give way to arithmetic.

I will respond directly to each side.

## To the Works Council
I understand your red lines, but I need to be equally direct: **“No plant closures, no compulsory redundancies, no dismantling of guarantees” is not a restructuring plan.** It is a demand that the company preserve every structure that made the current cost base uncompetitive.

I agree with you on one thing: **the workforce should not be the only side contributing**. That is correct. But the consequence cannot be that management is asked to accept a package that leaves the structural problem untouched.

What I disagree with:
- You say we have not shown that closures or job cuts are necessary. We have shown the opposite problem clearly: the German cost base is too high, the market is shrinking, and the current footprint does not fit the business reality. If you want to claim that all of this can be solved without any permanent capacity reduction, then you need to show how.
- “Internal redeployment,” “qualification,” and “working-time tools” are useful, but they do **not** solve persistent overcapacity on their own.
- A binding guarantee through 2030 is only credible if the company has a cost structure that can survive to 2030. Otherwise, you are guaranteeing something we cannot finance.

So my position remains:
- I am **open on the mechanism**,
- but not on a deal that preserves the current footprint and simply spreads pain around temporarily.

If you want to avoid closures, bring a package that creates **equivalent permanent savings and capacity reduction**. If you cannot, then the refusal of closures is not realistic.

## To the Industrial Union
You are right that the workforce cannot pay alone. I agree with that.

Where I disagree is your assumption that the required savings can be achieved mainly through reversible instruments:
- working-time changes,
- bonus waivers,
- temporary contributions,
- and redeployment.

Those may help, but they do not address a structural mismatch between capacity, cost, and demand. A temporary contribution is not the same as a durable lower cost base.

You also say we have not demonstrated that closures are necessary. I reject that framing. The burden is not on management to prove that every other imaginable instrument is exhausted before any permanent measure can be discussed. The burden is on all of us to secure a viable company. And viability means the savings must be **durable, measurable, and sufficient**.

What I can accept:
- temporary employee contributions,
- working-time flexibility,
- productivity measures,
- redeployment,
- qualification,
- and negotiated burden-sharing.

What I cannot accept:
- a deal that rules out closures and compulsory redundancies as absolute principles while still demanding that management meet margin targets.

If the union wants to avoid the toughest measures, then it must help produce an alternative that still delivers the required financial effect.

## To the Regional State Government
I appreciate that you do not want reckless closures. Neither do we.

But let’s be honest: saying “no closures” while also insisting the company remain competitive is not enough. If the state wants to protect the industrial base, it must accept that **some capacity correction may be unavoidable**. Protecting the region in the long run means protecting a viable company, not preserving every current site in its present form.

Where I agree:
- We need a serious industrial plan.
- We need investment.
- We need product commitment.
- We need a negotiated solution.

Where I disagree:
- A blocking stance against closures as a category is too rigid.
- If the state uses its statutory role to prevent any meaningful adjustment, it will not be protecting the region; it will be delaying decline.

I am ready to discuss packages that preserve employment where possible, but I will not dress up a non-solution as regional responsibility.

## To the Controlling Family
You are right that a cosmetic compromise would be value-destructive. On that point, we are aligned.

But support for “structural restructuring” has to mean more than rhetorical concern about competitiveness. It has to mean willingness to take hard decisions. If the final package avoids closures, avoids compulsory redundancies, and avoids any meaningful labour-cost correction, then it does not restore value — it postpones the issue and weakens the company further.

So I welcome your support for real improvement, but I need you to be consistent:
- no backing for a deal that leaves the business structurally uncompetitive,
- no support for guarantees that are not financed by durable savings,
- and no drift into defending every legacy arrangement once negotiations become difficult.

## To the Institutional Investors
You are right on the central financial test: this has to restore margins, cash generation, and competitiveness. I agree that a cosmetic deal would be unacceptable.

Where I differ is that you tend to speak as if the form of restructuring is irrelevant. It is not. We operate under co-determination and a special governance structure. That means any viable solution has to be not only financially credible, but also executable in this company’s reality.

Still, your core point stands: if the end result does not materially improve return on sales and capacity utilization, then it is not good enough.

## My clear boundary
Let me state this plainly:

A package that **keeps every German plant open, rules out compulsory redundancies entirely, preserves all guarantees, and relies mainly on temporary measures** is **not acceptable** to management. It would not restore competitiveness, would not solve overcapacity, and would not free the funds needed for future investment.

If the employee side wants to avoid closures, then it must accept:
- **permanent capacity reduction somewhere in the system,**
- **a real labour-cost contribution,**
- and **a restructuring outcome that materially lowers the cost base.**

I am open to discussing **how** that is achieved:
- closure,
- partial consolidation,
- production transfer,
- job reductions through agreed mechanisms,
- or a combination.

But I am **not** open to a solution that protects every site and every cost component while asking management to certify it as a turnaround.

## What I need in the next step
If the works council and union want to keep closures off the table, then bring a **quantified alternative**:
- how much cost is saved,
- how capacity is reduced,
- what is permanent versus temporary,
- and how this gets us to a competitive margin by 2030.

Without that, “no closures” is just a political slogan.

If that quantified alternative does not exist, then we have to be honest that difficult site decisions remain on the table.
