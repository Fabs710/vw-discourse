We have now heard enough numbers, and I want to be equally clear: **the burden of proof remains on management, not on the workforce.**

The expert has confirmed what we said from the beginning: **a no-closure solution is possible in principle** if the company is prepared to make a **large permanent correction elsewhere**. That is exactly why the debate cannot be reduced to “closures or nothing.” Management is trying to turn one possible route into inevitability. I reject that.

## To management

You keep saying the German footprint is structurally too expensive. That may be true. But you still have **not shown** that the only way to fix it is to close a German plant or impose compulsory redundancies.

The expert’s model does **not** support your attempt to make closures the default answer. It says:

- no-closure solutions may be credible,
- temporary concessions alone are not enough,
- and a **permanent** correction is needed.

That is a very different conclusion from “therefore close plants.”

So let me be precise:

- If you want to argue for closures, then show the **quantified savings bridge** proving that **no combination** of working-time changes, capacity reductions, productivity gains, product transfers, and negotiated contributions can preserve all German sites.
- If you cannot show that, then your closure-first approach is a choice, not a necessity.

And we are not accepting a choice that sacrifices German plants and jobs when alternatives still exist.

## To the Industrial Union

We are aligned on the core red lines:
- **no plant closures**
- **no compulsory redundancies**
- **no dismantling of the job-security framework**
- **no unilateral attack on the wage agreement**

That remains the basis.

Where I want to sharpen the point is this: a “fair contribution” by employees must never become a permanent wage rollback hidden behind crisis language. Temporary, limited, and reversible measures are one thing. A structural transfer of the adjustment burden onto labor is another. We will not allow that.

## To the Regional State Government

You are right to reject reckless closures. But I need to be frank: “not reckless” is too weak if it leaves the door open to plant closure as a bargaining threat.

Your statutory role and political weight must be used to **block** any proposal that turns German sites into sacrificial assets. If you are serious about protecting the region, then you should insist on:

- a site-by-site industrial plan,
- binding investment commitments,
- and a restructuring path that keeps every German plant open.

Not “if possible.” **As the starting point.**

## To the Controlling Family

You say you want a durable competitiveness reset. Fine. Then use your governance power to reject management’s habit of reaching first for closures and layoffs.

You are right about one thing: the company needs permanent savings. But permanent savings do **not automatically mean** closing a plant. They can come from:

- capacity adjustments,
- shift reductions,
- product allocation,
- overhead cuts,
- productivity improvements,
- and a negotiated employee contribution.

What they cannot come from is simply breaking promises and calling it restructuring.

If ownership wants credibility, it must show that it will not support a deal built on closures and forced layoffs as the first resort.

## To the Capital Markets / Institutional Investors

You are focused on margin, cash flow, and fixed-cost absorption. That is understandable. But your argument keeps flattening governance reality into spreadsheet logic.

This is not a simple listed-company optimization exercise. We have co-determination, statutory blocking positions, and a workforce that will defend its sites. If you push for a solution that ignores that reality, you are not accelerating value creation — you are increasing execution risk, industrial conflict, and the probability of a worse outcome.

So yes, we understand the need for durable savings. But no, we do **not** accept your conclusion that only closure-heavy restructuring is “credible.”

## Where I agree

I agree with the following:
- the company needs real restructuring;
- temporary measures alone are not sufficient;
- a quantified savings bridge is necessary;
- all sides must contribute;
- prolonged conflict would hurt everyone;
- capacity must be addressed somewhere.

But agreement ends where you try to make those points justify closures or forced layoffs.

## Where I disagree

I disagree with the idea that:
- preserving all German plants is unrealistic by definition;
- compulsory redundancies are somehow a normal part of “serious” restructuring;
- or that management can first declare a crisis and then use that declaration to tear up job-security guarantees.

That is not responsible turnaround. That is a unilateral attempt to shift risk onto the workforce.

## What we can support

We are prepared to discuss:
- working-time flexibility,
- temporary and limited employee contributions,
- internal redeployment,
- qualification,
- product and volume reallocation,
- shift changes,
- and capacity adjustments.

But these must be designed to **preserve every German plant and prevent forced layoffs**.

That remains our line.

## What we reject

We reject:
- plant closures in Germany,
- compulsory redundancies,
- “targeted” cuts that are really forced layoffs,
- and any plan that treats the historic job-security framework as negotiable collateral damage.

The expert has confirmed that the restructuring can only be judged by whether the numbers close. Good. Then let the numbers be tested against a **no-closure, no-forced-layoff** alternative before anyone pretends closure is inevitable.

If management cannot prove that all viable alternatives fail, then it does not have a mandate to shut plants or force people out.

And if anyone tries to turn this into a fait accompli, they should expect sustained resistance from the works council and the workforce, using every co-determination right and every lawful means available.
