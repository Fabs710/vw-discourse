I’ll respond directly.

## Where I agree

I agree with management, investors, and the state on the **diagnosis**: this company has a real structural cost and capacity problem, and a cosmetic settlement would simply defer value erosion. I also agree that:
- future products and investment are essential,
- voluntary exits, redeployment, qualification, and working-time flexibility should be part of the toolkit,
- and a prolonged conflict would damage the company, its workforce, and the family asset.

I also agree with the state that the company’s regional footprint and co-determination are part of the value model. That is not irrelevant sentiment; it is part of how this business preserves legitimacy and industrial strength.

## Where I disagree

### To the works council and union
Your position is internally coherent, but it is not yet a workable basis for a settlement.

You continue to insist on:
- no closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- and binding guarantees through 2030  
before meaningful concessions are made.

I understand why you want security first. But from the shareholder side, I cannot accept a framework that removes all meaningful restructuring tools up front while the cost gap remains unresolved. That would freeze an uncompetitive structure and shift the entire adjustment burden onto owners and future cash flow.

That is not stewardship. It is postponement.

And I need to say this plainly: **I do not accept an unconditional blanket guarantee through 2030** if it prevents the company from restoring competitiveness. Nor do I accept a position where closures and involuntary measures are treated as permanently off-limits regardless of economics.

I am not saying closures are desirable. They are not. I am saying they cannot be ruled out categorically if no equivalent savings path exists.

### To management
I agree with your underlying mandate, but you still need to be more concrete.

“Structural adjustment” is not enough. If you want capital-side support, you need to present:
- a quantified savings target,
- the capacity reduction implied,
- the timeline,
- and the link to margin recovery.

You are right that the company cannot buy security with an unaffordable cost base. But that argument becomes far more credible when it is tied to numbers, not just general urgency.

### To the state government
I respect your role and your constraints. But I do not fully share the view that closures and involuntary redundancies should be taken off the table as a matter of principle.

If the business case requires stronger tools, then governance must preserve them. Long-term regional stability depends on a viable company, not on preserving every site regardless of economics.

### To institutional investors
I agree that a deal must be economically credible and not merely politically comfortable. But credibility also depends on durable labour peace and the avoidance of endless confrontation. A settlement that creates years of instability is not value-maximizing either.

## On the bridge options

### 1) Time-bound “protected restructuring” package
This is the only bridge that deserves real consideration.

I could support a **time-bound no-closure / no-forced-layoff period** only if it is:
- tied to a quantified savings and transformation package,
- limited in duration,
- and paired with objective review points.

But it must not become a disguised permanent veto. If the package underdelivers materially, the agreement must preserve a real route to stronger action. I will not support a structure that allows a one-sided freeze of all restructuring tools.

### 2) Stepwise trigger model with earned security
This is more promising than an absolute guarantee.

A phased approach can work if:
- the initial concessions are real and measurable,
- management also commits upfront to investment and product allocation,
- and the review mechanism is objective.

What I will not accept is a model where management gives up all flexibility first and then waits passively for the workforce to decide whether to contribute. That would simply recreate the same sequencing problem in a different form.

### 3) Company-wide framework plus site-specific implementation
This is sensible in principle.

I am open to site-specific implementation if it is embedded in a company-wide restructuring framework. That may be the best way to align investment, products, and capacity with economics. But it cannot become a way to isolate sites, weaken solidarity, or preserve inefficient structures by political pressure.

## What I would support

I could support a package built around:
- material capacity reduction,
- voluntary exits and redeployment first,
- working-time flexibility,
- qualification and productivity measures,
- a measurable employee contribution,
- binding investment commitments for future products and technology,
- and conditional security tied to delivery.

That would be a serious package.

## What I will not support

I will not support:
- a blanket no-closure commitment,
- a blanket no-forced-redundancy commitment,
- or an unconditional 2030 job guarantee.

Those positions would lock in the very value erosion we are trying to stop.

## My bottom line

The family holding’s responsibility is to protect the long-term value of the core asset. That means restoring competitiveness and margins, not preserving every option for every side indefinitely.

So my line is:
- **yes** to a negotiated restructuring,
- **yes** to a substantial workforce contribution,
- **yes** to conditional and time-bound protection,
- **no** to unconditional guarantees,
- **no** to removing all hard tools from governance before the economics are fixed.

If labour can bring forward an **equivalent, quantified alternative** that closes the gap without closures, I will listen. If not, then the company must retain the tools needed to correct the cost base.

That is where I stand.
