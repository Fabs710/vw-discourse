I will speak plainly.

The state government agrees that this company needs a **real restructuring**, not a cosmetic one. We also agree that future products, investment, and a credible path back to competitiveness are essential. On that point, I am with management, the family holding, and the investors.

But I do **not** accept the conclusion that plant closures and compulsory redundancies must stay on the table as ordinary fallback options. And I do **not** accept a restructuring logic that treats the regional footprint as the first and easiest adjustment variable.

## Where I agree
- The cost and capacity problem is real.
- The company needs a durable industrial plan through 2030.
- Voluntary exits, redeployment, qualification, and working-time flexibility should all be part of the package.
- A prolonged confrontation would damage value and jobs.
- The workforce should make a **measurable contribution** if the company also makes binding commitments.

## Where I disagree
### To management
You are right that the company cannot carry structural overcapacity forever. But you are wrong to present closures and forced redundancies as if they were normal instruments that can simply stay live until the last moment.

For this company, in this state, **closures are not a neutral efficiency tool**. They are a major break with the social and industrial balance that has made this firm viable for decades. If you want support from the state, then do not begin from the assumption that German sites are disposable.

Your argument also ignores the political and economic damage of a closure: supplier chains, regional employment, trust, and the company’s legitimacy as an anchor employer. That damage is not a side issue.

### To the family holding and investors
I understand the concern for margins and competitiveness. But value protection is not served by a strategy that destroys industrial peace and weakens the company’s long-term social foundation.

A settlement that provokes prolonged conflict or permanently damages the regional base is not stewardship. It is short-sighted. If you want long-term value, then you have to accept that **site protection and co-determination are part of the value model**, not obstacles to it.

### To labour
I also need to be direct with the works council and union: a pure “no closure, no compulsory redundancies, full guarantees first” position is politically understandable, but it is not enough by itself to close the economics.

If you want the state to defend the footprint, you must also help produce a package that is economically credible:
- real productivity gains,
- real flexibility,
- measurable cost reduction,
- and a workforce contribution that is more than symbolic.

You are right to insist on security. But security has to be tied to a serious restructuring path, not just declared as a precondition.

## What I can support
The state is prepared to back a package that includes:
1. **No plant closures in Germany as the default restructuring path**
2. **No compulsory redundancies as the first instrument**
3. **A substantial negotiated workforce contribution**
4. **Capacity reduction through alternatives to closure**
5. **Binding investment and product commitments for German sites**
6. **Preservation of co-determination and the state’s blocking role**
7. **Conditional security linked to measurable performance**

That is the line I can defend publicly and politically.

## What I cannot support
I will not support:
- closures being used as the preferred answer,
- compulsory redundancies becoming the normal adjustment tool,
- a deal that shifts the burden mainly onto workers and the region,
- or any arrangement that weakens the state’s co-determination and veto rights.

Those rights are not decorative. They are part of the institutional balance that prevents exactly the kind of one-sided restructuring that would hollow out the company’s role in the region.

## On the moderator’s bridge options
### 1) Conditional no-closure / no-forced-layoff package with triggers
This is the only bridge worth serious discussion, but only if the commitment is genuine.

I could support a **time-bound no-closure and no-forced-layoff framework** if:
- it is paired with quantified savings,
- it includes a credible transformation plan,
- and it has a jointly monitored review mechanism that does not simply become a backdoor to immediate plant closure.

In other words: the trigger mechanism must protect the integrity of the agreement, not undermine it.

### 2) Site-specific agreements instead of a blanket 2030 guarantee
I am open to site-specific planning if it serves investment and future products. But site-specific treatment must not become a divide-and-conquer tool.

A company-wide framework for security and co-determination still has to exist. Site differentiation can be added on top of that, not replace it.

### 3) “No involuntary redundancies first” plus jointly monitored adjustment corridor
This is sensible if it is genuine. If management commits to:
- voluntary exits,
- retirement transitions,
- redeployment,
- qualification,
- working-time solutions,
- and internal mobility,
then the state can support that direction.

But I will not endorse a so-called “last resort” clause if it is just a disguised path to closures or mass forced layoffs later.

## My bottom line
The state is prepared to support a **negotiated restructuring that protects German sites, protects employment as far as possible, and restores competitiveness**.

We are **not** prepared to support:
- plant closures as the default response,
- compulsory redundancies as a normal tool,
- or any solution that damages the regional economy beyond repair.

So my message is:
- management must bring a **quantified non-closure alternative**,
- labour must engage on **real savings and flexibility**,
- and shareholders must accept that **regional industrial stability is part of long-term enterprise value**.

If that is the basis, the state will broker and back it. If not, then you will find the state using its rights to prevent a solution that sacrifices the region for speed.
