We agree with the diagnosis, and we agree that a cosmetic settlement would destroy value. What we do **not** agree with is any package that leaves the company’s cost base structurally uncompetitive or preserves margins only on paper.

## Where we agree
- The competitiveness problem is real.
- A prolonged conflict would be value-destructive.
- The solution must be durable, not a short-term patch.
- Future-product investment matters, and it has to be funded by a real restructuring.
- Voluntary exits, redeployment, qualification, and working-time flexibility should be used first where they are effective.

## Where we disagree
### To the works council and union
Your insistence on **no closures, no compulsory redundancies, and binding guarantees through 2030 before meaningful concessions** is not acceptable from a capital perspective.

That sequencing protects employment security first, but it does **not** prove that the business will become competitive. If the cost gap is not closed, then shareholders are left with the downside: weak margins, lower returns, and continued valuation pressure.

We are not willing to support a deal that simply locks in the current structure and calls it a solution. If closures or involuntary measures are avoidable through a quantified alternative, fine — but the alternative must actually deliver the savings.

### To management
We support structural action, but you still need to be more precise. “Closures remain available” is not enough by itself. Investors need:
- a quantified savings target,
- a timeline,
- the capacity reduction implied,
- and a clear bridge from restructuring to margin recovery.

If those numbers are not credible, then the market will treat the whole process as theatre. That would be unacceptable.

### To the state government
We respect the regional and political constraints, but preserving every site as a matter of principle is not a strategy. Regional stability matters, but so does enterprise value. If the company remains uncompetitive, eventually everyone loses: workers, the region, and shareholders.

### To the family holding
We agree with the need for stewardship, but stewardship means restoring competitiveness, not just retaining optionality. A package that avoids difficult decisions while leaving margins impaired would be a failure of governance.

## What would move us
We would back a tough restructuring if it includes:
- **material and durable cost reduction**
- **real capacity rationalisation**
- **measurable workforce contribution**
- **voluntary-first workforce adjustment**
- **a credible investment plan for future products**
- **a clearly modeled return to competitive margins**

We can support conditional security, but only if it is tied to measurable delivery. We cannot support blanket guarantees that freeze the company into an uncompetitive cost structure.

## Our bottom line
We are prepared to support the strongest credible path to competitiveness, not the most politically comfortable one.

If labour wants to avoid closures and forced redundancies, it needs to present an **equivalent, quantified package** that closes the gap. If management wants capital-market support, it needs to show the numbers and execution path clearly enough that margin recovery is believable.

Until then, our position remains:
- no cosmetic compromise,
- no structurally impaired cost base,
- and no settlement that sacrifices risk-adjusted returns for the appearance of peace.
