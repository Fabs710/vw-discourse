I’ll be equally direct.

## Where I agree
I agree with everyone on one point: **this cannot be a cosmetic deal**. If we sign something that leaves the cost base structurally uncompetitive, we will be back here in a few months with worse numbers, weaker margins, and even less room to invest. That would be a failure of responsibility.

I also agree that:
- future products and technology investment are essential,
- a prolonged conflict would destroy value,
- voluntary exits, redeployment, qualification, and working-time measures should be part of the toolkit,
- and a negotiated settlement is preferable to open-ended escalation.

## Where I disagree — firmly

### 1) “No closures” cannot be an absolute precondition
You keep asking us to remove closures from the table before you make any serious contribution. That sequencing does not solve the problem; it preserves overcapacity and shifts the adjustment entirely onto the balance sheet.

I will not commit to a blank no-closure guarantee in a situation where the company’s German cost base is structurally above target. If equivalent savings are not achieved through other means, closures remain a necessary instrument. Not desirable first choice — necessary fallback.

### 2) “No compulsory redundancies” cannot be an absolute either
I prefer negotiated reduction over forced exits. But I will not sign a framework that makes meaningful structural adjustment impossible.

If the workforce footprint must come down materially, management needs credible tools. A blanket ban on compulsory redundancies while also ruling out closures is not a restructuring plan; it is a promise that the current cost structure will somehow stop being a problem by itself.

### 3) A blanket 2030 guarantee is too rigid
A universal job and site guarantee through 2030 sounds attractive, but it freezes the company exactly when it needs flexibility to recover competitiveness.

What I can discuss is **conditional security** tied to measurable performance, savings delivery, and investment commitments. What I cannot accept is unconditional security that prevents us from correcting the cost base if the business case demands it.

## On the sequencing
The labour side says: security first, then sacrifice.  
Management says: sacrifice first, then security.

My position is simpler: **we need a package that delivers real, quantified savings and real, durable security where it is earned**. Security cannot be a shield against necessary change. And sacrifice cannot be one-sided.

## On your “trigger” objections
I heard the moderator’s bridge options. A review mechanism is only useful if it is tied to objective milestones and gives us a real route to adjust if the package underdelivers.

I do **not** accept a trigger system that becomes a hidden veto on restructuring. But I also do not accept a system where management can promise everything and then, once concessions are banked, reopen closures at will.

So yes: if we are to discuss conditionality, it must be:
- measurable,
- time-bound,
- and linked to actual delivery on cost, capacity, and margin improvement.

## On labour’s contribution
You say you are prepared to contribute only if closures, forced layoffs, and insecurity are removed first. That is not a balanced offer.

If the workforce wants to avoid the strongest restructuring tools, then the workforce contribution must be substantial enough to close the gap. That means real savings on:
- pay moderation,
- working time,
- flexibility,
- productivity,
- and, where necessary, headcount.

A symbolic or temporary concession will not be enough. We need a measurable, durable contribution that actually improves the brand’s return on sales.

## On the state and the family holding
I respect the regional concern and the history of co-determination. But stewardship also means not protecting structures that are no longer affordable.

To the state: preserving every site regardless of economics is not industrial policy; it is postponement.  
To the family holding: value protection requires hard decisions, not just rhetoric about stewardship.

I am not interested in a deal that looks socially neat but leaves the company strategically weak.

## What I will put on the table
I remain open to a negotiated package that includes:
- material capacity reduction,
- a substantial labour-cost contribution,
- voluntary and negotiated workforce reduction first,
- redeployment and qualification,
- binding investment commitments for future products and technology,
- and conditional site security tied to performance.

## What I will not put on the table
I will not commit to:
- a blanket no-closure promise,
- a blanket no-compulsory-redundancy promise,
- or a blanket job/site guarantee through 2030.

Those would be fundamentally inconsistent with restoring competitiveness.

## The core question
The question is not whether change happens. The question is whether the package is **sufficient**.

If the employee side can deliver an alternative that produces **equivalent, quantified savings** without closures, I will listen. But until that exists, you are asking management to give up its restructuring tools while the cost problem remains unsolved. That is not acceptable.

If the goal is to protect the company, the brand, and future investment to 2030, then the package must actually fix the economics — not just freeze the conflict.
