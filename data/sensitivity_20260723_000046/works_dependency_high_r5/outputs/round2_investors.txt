We agree with the **diagnosis** from management, the family holding, and the state: the company has a real competitiveness problem, and a cosmetic settlement would be value-destructive. If the cost base stays structurally too high, margins stay impaired and shareholders will continue to bear the downside. That is not acceptable.

We also agree that a drawn-out conflict is harmful. Prolonged strikes, uncertainty, and customer disruption would destroy value fast. So we are not looking for confrontation for its own sake.

Where we **do not** agree is on the labour side’s insistence on absolute red lines before any real savings are shown.

## Our view on the main points

### 1) Plant closures
We do **not** treat closures as desirable. But we also will not support a package that rules them out in advance if no equivalent savings are delivered.

If closures are avoidable through a credible alternative, fine — but that alternative must be quantified, durable, and sufficient. “No closures” is not a plan. It is only acceptable if it is matched by equally strong and credible cost reduction elsewhere.

### 2) Compulsory redundancies
Again, we prefer negotiated instruments first. Voluntary exits, early retirement, redeployment, working-time changes, and qualification should be the first choices.

But we will not support a framework that makes meaningful headcount reduction impossible. If the workforce footprint has to come down materially, management needs real tools, not a promise that adjustment can be avoided indefinitely.

### 3) Job and site guarantees through 2030
A blanket guarantee through 2030 is too rigid if it prevents the company from restoring competitiveness. We can support **conditional security**, but only if it is tied to:
- measurable savings,
- capacity reduction,
- and a credible transformation plan.

Unconditional guarantees without a hard economic package would just postpone the problem.

### 4) Employee contribution
We agree with management that there must be a real workforce contribution. But it must be **measurable**, not symbolic, and it must actually improve the cost base.

A limited, time-bound contribution is acceptable if it is part of a broader package that restores margins and supports future investment. What we will not back is a deal where labour contributes little while the company remains uncompetitive.

## What would move us
We would support a package only if it includes all of the following:
- **material and durable cost reduction**
- **real capacity rationalisation**
- **meaningful labour-cost improvement**
- **a quantified workforce contribution**
- **a credible investment plan for future products and technology**
- **a clear path back to competitive margins**

And crucially: the package must be sufficiently concrete that investors can model the return to competitiveness. We need numbers, not generalities.

## What we will not support
We will not support:
- a cosmetic settlement,
- a “wait and see” approach,
- a deal that preserves all structures while margins remain weak,
- or prolonged industrial conflict that destroys value.

## To management
You need to put real numbers on the table:
- how much cost reduction is required,
- how much capacity is coming out,
- what the execution timeline is,
- and how the savings translate into margin recovery.

## To labour
If you want to avoid closures, forced layoffs, and harsher measures, then bring forward an **equivalent package** that actually closes the gap. Not principles. Numbers.

## Bottom line
We are prepared to back a tough restructuring if it is **credible, durable, and sufficient**. We are not prepared to back either:
- a politically comfortable but economically weak compromise, or
- a conflict strategy that erodes enterprise value.

The decisive question remains the same:  
**What package actually restores competitive margins on a risk-adjusted basis?**  
Until that is answered, our support goes to the strongest credible path to competitiveness — not to any stakeholder’s preferred taboos.
