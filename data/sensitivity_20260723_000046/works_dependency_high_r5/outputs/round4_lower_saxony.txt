I will be equally direct.

## Where I agree
I agree with management, the family holding, and investors that this company needs a **real restructuring**, not a cosmetic one.  
I also agree that:
- the cost base is too high,
- future products and investment matter,
- voluntary exits, redeployment, qualification, and working-time flexibility should be used,
- and a prolonged conflict would damage value and jobs.

I further agree with the works council and union on one point: **the company must not be allowed to solve this crisis by simply gutting the regional industrial base**. That would be a strategic failure, not a recovery.

## Where I disagree — firmly

### To management
You keep saying closures and compulsory redundancies must remain available as fallback tools. For the state, that is not an abstract question of flexibility. It is a question of **whether the company treats German sites as expendable**.

I do **not** accept plant closures in the state as a normal restructuring instrument.  
I do **not** accept forced redundancies as the first or default response.  
And I do **not** accept a process in which the workforce is asked to concede first while the most destructive tools stay fully live.

If you want our support, then you need to bring forward a **quantified non-closure alternative** that actually closes the gap. Not slogans. Numbers.

### To the works council and union
I understand your insistence on security first. And I agree that workers should not be asked to carry the burden alone. But I need to be equally clear: a blanket position of “no closures, no compulsory redundancies, full guarantees through 2030” is only sustainable if there is a **serious, measurable restructuring path** behind it.

The state will defend jobs and sites. But we cannot defend a package that freezes the economics and postpones the problem. If you want us to use our leverage, then help produce a package that is **economically credible and socially fair**.

### To the family holding
Stewardship does not mean preserving maximum flexibility at the expense of the regional footprint. It means protecting long-term enterprise value **and** the industrial base that creates it.

If closures are treated as the easy option, then the company risks losing legitimacy, trust, and skilled capacity that cannot be rebuilt quickly. That is not good stewardship.

### To institutional investors
I hear your concern about margins and cash generation. But you also need to understand that **regional stability, labor peace, and co-determination are not side constraints**. They are part of the value model in this company.

A settlement that destroys trust may look “efficient” on paper and still fail in practice.

## What I can support
The state can support a package built around:
1. **No plant closures in Germany as the default restructuring path**
2. **No compulsory redundancies as the first instrument**
3. **Material capacity reduction through negotiated alternatives**
4. **A measurable workforce contribution**
5. **Binding investment and future-product commitments**
6. **Preservation of co-determination and the state’s blocking role**
7. **A time-bound, jointly monitored implementation framework**

That is the line I can defend politically and economically.

## What I cannot support
I will not support:
- plant closures being used as the preferred tool,
- compulsory redundancies becoming normal practice,
- a deal that shifts the burden mainly onto workers and the region,
- or any arrangement that weakens the state’s co-determination and veto rights.

Those rights are not decorative. They are part of the balance that keeps this company anchored in the region.

## On the bridge options

### 1) Time-bound “protected restructuring” package
This is the only bridge that deserves serious discussion, but only if protection is real.

I could support a **time-bound no-closure and no-forced-layoff framework** if:
- it is paired with quantified savings,
- it includes a credible transformation plan,
- and it has a jointly monitored review mechanism.

That review mechanism must **not** be a backdoor to immediate closures. It must protect the agreement’s integrity.

### 2) Stepwise trigger model with earned security
I am open to phasing, but not to a model where workers must give up security first while existential threats remain live.

A stepwise model is only acceptable if:
- initial concessions are matched by immediate management commitments,
- milestones are objective,
- and no one can unilaterally reinterpret the deal to reopen closures.

### 3) Company-wide framework plus site-specific implementation
This is sensible if it strengthens the regional footprint, not if it divides sites against each other.

Site-specific arrangements can make sense for investment and product allocation. They cannot become a tool to isolate one plant after another.

## What would move me
What would genuinely move the state government is:
- a **credible, quantified alternative to closures**,
- clear commitments on **investment and future products**,
- a **measurable employee contribution** that is shared fairly,
- and a framework that keeps **jobs, sites, and co-determination** intact.

If management brings that, we will support it.  
If it does not, then I will use the state’s rights to prevent a solution that hollowes out the company’s role in the region.

## Bottom line
I am not here to broker agreement for its own sake. I am here to protect the company’s long-term health **and** its role as a regional economic anchor.

So my position is simple:
- **No closures as the default answer**
- **No forced redundancies as the default answer**
- **Yes to a negotiated restructuring**
- **Yes to measurable workforce contribution**
- **Yes to binding investment and co-determination**
- **No to any solution that sacrifices the region to restore short-term flexibility**

If that is the basis, we can work. If not, I will not soften my line.
