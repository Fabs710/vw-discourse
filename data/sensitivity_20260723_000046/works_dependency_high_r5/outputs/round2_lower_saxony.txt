I will be frank: I agree with management on the **diagnosis** that the company needs a serious competitiveness response. I also agree with the shareholders and investors that a purely cosmetic settlement would be irresponsible. But I do **not** accept the conclusion that plant closures and compulsory redundancies must remain normal fallback options.

From the state’s perspective, this is not just a cost exercise. It is about the company’s role as a regional economic anchor, the stability of skilled employment, and the credibility of the social partnership model that has helped this business endure for decades.

## Where I agree
- The company does need a **durable industrial plan**.
- There must be **real productivity and cost improvement**.
- There should be **investment in future products and technologies**.
- Workforce contributions can be part of the solution, if they are fair and tied to guarantees.

## Where I disagree
### On plant closures
I cannot support treating German plant closures as an ordinary restructuring instrument. For this company, in this region, a closure is not a technical adjustment — it is a severe break with long-term industrial responsibility.

If management wants my support, it must first show that the cost gap can be closed **without gutting the German footprint**. “Closures if needed” is too blunt and too destructive to be the starting point.

### On compulsory redundancies
I also cannot support compulsory redundancies as a default mechanism. If headcount must come down, then the path must be:
- voluntary exits,
- retirement transitions,
- redeployment,
- qualification,
- and working-time solutions.

That is the only approach consistent with regional stability and social responsibility.

### On blank guarantees
At the same time, I am not endorsing a frozen status quo. A blanket “nothing changes” position is not credible either. The company does need flexibility, and the workforce should not pretend otherwise. But flexibility must be negotiated and balanced, not imposed through threats.

## What I can support
I remain prepared to support a package that includes:
1. **No plant closures in Germany**
2. **No compulsory redundancies**
3. **A substantial but negotiated workforce contribution**
4. **Capacity reduction through alternatives to closure**
5. **Binding investment commitments for future products**
6. **Preservation of co-determination and the state’s veto role**
7. **Conditional security linked to measurable performance, not open-ended insecurity**

That is a serious restructuring path. It is not a refusal to adapt.

## What I will not support
I will not back:
- a restructuring built around closure threats,
- a plan that shifts the adjustment burden almost entirely onto employees and regions,
- or any arrangement that weakens the state’s co-determination and blocking rights.

Those rights are part of the institutional balance here, and they matter precisely because this company is too important to be treated like a disposable asset.

## My challenge to management
If you want a credible deal, bring forward a **quantified alternative**:
- how much capacity must come out,
- how much cost must be reduced,
- what instruments can deliver that without closures,
- and what concrete investment and site commitments you will make in return.

Then we can judge whether the package is economically sufficient and socially acceptable.

## My challenge to labour
I also say to the works council and union: you will need to engage on real numbers, not only principles. If you want to avoid closures, then a serious package must still deliver genuine savings and flexibility. A pure no-change position will not hold politically or economically.

But let me be equally clear: the burden must be shared. The workforce cannot be asked to surrender protections while management keeps the right to close plants later. That is not a balanced settlement.

## Bottom line
The state is prepared to back a **negotiated restructuring that protects sites, jobs, and future investment**. We are **not** prepared to support closures as the preferred answer or accept a solution that damages the regional economy beyond repair.

So my position remains:
- **Yes** to restructuring,
- **yes** to shared contributions,
- **yes** to competitiveness and investment,
- **no** to closures as the default,
- **no** to compulsory redundancies,
- **no** to sidelining the state’s co-determination role.

If management wants support, it must come back with a package that proves competitiveness can be restored **without sacrificing the company’s role as a regional anchor**.
