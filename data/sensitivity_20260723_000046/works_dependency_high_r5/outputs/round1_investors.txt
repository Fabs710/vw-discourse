From the perspective of dispersed institutional investors, the key test is not who wins the confrontation, but whether the outcome credibly restores competitive margins and protects enterprise value on a risk-adjusted basis.

### Where we agree
We agree with management, the family holding, and even the state on the core diagnosis: **the current cost base is not competitive** and the company cannot simply preserve the status quo. If the German operations remain structurally overstaffed or burdened by legacy costs, then margins will stay under pressure and so will the valuation.

We also agree that a **durable industrial plan** matters. A purely symbolic settlement, or one that buys short-term peace while leaving the economics unchanged, would be negative for shareholders. That kind of outcome would simply defer the problem and likely make the eventual adjustment worse.

### Where we disagree
We do **not** support a confrontation strategy that relies on open-ended threats, prolonged strikes, or a value-destructive standoff. That would be costly in the near term, damage customer confidence, and create uncertainty that markets will punish.

We also do **not** support a “no change” position from the labour side. If every site, every guarantee, and every legacy arrangement is treated as untouchable, then the restructuring will fail economically. That is not a credible path to improved returns.

At the same time, we are not impressed by rhetoric around closures if it is not paired with a clear, quantified plan. Closing plants may be a legitimate lever in principle, but investors will only support it if management can show:
- durable savings,
- manageable execution risk,
- and a path to restored margins without simply substituting one cost problem for another.

### What we will support
We would support a package that includes:
- **material and durable cost reduction**,
- **meaningful capacity rationalisation**,
- **measurable labour-cost improvement**,
- **workforce contributions that are real, not cosmetic**,
- and **investment commitments tied to competitiveness and future products**.

We are open to alternatives to outright closures if they genuinely deliver equivalent savings. But those alternatives must be more than delay mechanisms. They need to reduce the cost base enough to get the company back to competitive returns.

### Our red lines
We will not back an outcome that:
- leaves the cost base structurally uncompetitive,
- preserves all guarantees without real concessions,
- or results in prolonged industrial conflict that destroys value.

We are also wary of a politically neat but economically weak compromise. If the final package is built mainly to avoid headlines, while margins remain impaired, shareholders will bear the cost.

### Bottom line
We are prepared to support a tough restructuring, including difficult measures, **if** it is credible, durable, and sufficient. We are **not** prepared to support a cosmetic settlement or an outcome that trades short-term peace for long-term underperformance.

So the question for the other stakeholders is simple:  
**What exact combination of capacity reduction, labour-cost change, and investment will actually restore competitiveness?**  
Without a clear answer to that, we will side with the strongest credible path to margin recovery — not with any particular institution or red line.
