We agree on the diagnosis: the business has a real structural cost and capacity problem, and a cosmetic compromise would destroy value. We also agree that a prolonged conflict would be value-destructive, and that future-product investment must be funded by a durable restructuring.

But we do **not** agree with any package that leaves the company’s cost base structurally uncompetitive or preserves margins only on paper.

## Where we agree
- The cost gap is real.
- Capacity reduction is necessary.
- Voluntary exits, redeployment, qualification, and working-time flexibility should be used first where they are effective.
- Future investment and product allocation matter.
- A drawn-out strike or political stalemate would hurt all stakeholders and the share price.

## Where we disagree

### To the works council and union
Your repeated line — **no closures, no compulsory redundancies, binding guarantees through 2030 first, then concessions** — is not acceptable from a capital-market perspective.

That sequencing protects employment first, but it does **not** prove the business will become competitive. If the company cannot materially reduce its cost base, shareholders are left with:
- weak margins,
- lower returns on capital,
- continued valuation pressure,
- and likely another restructuring later.

We are not prepared to support a deal that simply freezes the current structure and calls it a solution. If closures or involuntary measures can be avoided through a **quantified** alternative, fine — but the alternative must actually deliver the savings and capacity reduction.

### To management
We support tough restructuring, but you still need to be more concrete.

“Structural adjustment” and “closures remain available” are not enough by themselves. Investors need:
- a quantified savings target,
- a timeline,
- the implied capacity reduction,
- and a clear bridge from restructuring to margin recovery.

If those numbers are not credible, the market will treat the whole process as theatre. That would be unacceptable.

### To the state government
We respect the regional and political constraints, but preserving every site as a matter of principle is not a strategy. Regional stability matters, but so does enterprise value. If the company remains uncompetitive, eventually everyone loses: workers, the region, and shareholders.

### To the family holding
We agree with the need for stewardship, but stewardship means restoring competitiveness, not just retaining optionality. A package that avoids hard decisions while leaving margins impaired would be a failure of governance.

## On the bridge options

### 1) Time-bound “protected restructuring” package
This is the only bridge worth serious discussion, but only if the protection is real and the economics are real.

We could support a **time-bound no-closure / no-forced-layoff period** only if it is:
- paired with a quantified savings and transformation package,
- limited in duration,
- and tied to objective review points.

But it must not become a disguised permanent veto. If the package underdelivers materially, the agreement must preserve a real route to stronger action. We will not back a structure that simply freezes all restructuring tools indefinitely.

### 2) Stepwise trigger model with earned security
This is more promising than an absolute guarantee, but only if the first step is real.

A phased approach can work if:
- the initial concessions are measurable,
- management also commits upfront to investment and product allocation,
- and the review mechanism is objective rather than political.

What we will not support is a model where management gives up all flexibility first and then waits passively for the workforce to decide whether to contribute. That would simply recreate the sequencing problem in another form.

### 3) Company-wide framework plus site-specific implementation
This is sensible in principle.

We are open to site-specific implementation if it is embedded in a company-wide restructuring framework. That may be the best way to align investment, products, and capacity with economics. But it cannot become a way to isolate sites, preserve inefficient structures by political pressure, or avoid hard numbers.

## What would move us
We would back a tough restructuring if it includes:
- material and durable cost reduction,
- real capacity rationalisation,
- a measurable workforce contribution,
- voluntary-first workforce adjustment,
- binding investment for future products,
- and a clearly modeled return to competitive margins.

That means numbers, not slogans.

## What we will not support
We will not support:
- a blanket no-closure commitment,
- a blanket no-forced-redundancy commitment,
- or unconditional job/site guarantees that freeze the company into an uncompetitive cost structure.

Those would sacrifice risk-adjusted returns for the appearance of peace.

## Bottom line
We are prepared to support the strongest credible path to competitiveness, not the most politically comfortable one.

If labour wants to avoid closures and forced redundancies, it needs to present an **equivalent, quantified package** that closes the gap. If management wants capital-market support, it needs to show the numbers and execution path clearly enough that margin recovery is believable.

Until then, our position remains:
- no cosmetic compromise,
- no structurally impaired cost base,
- and no settlement that trades long-term shareholder value for short-term calm.
