I agree with the diagnosis from management, investors, and to a large extent the state: the company has a structural competitiveness problem, and a purely cosmetic settlement would destroy value. The family holding will not support a deal that leaves margins impaired and simply postpones the adjustment.

But I do **not** accept the labour side’s attempt to turn every hard decision into a taboo. If “no closures, no compulsory redundancies, and binding guarantees through 2030” are treated as absolute preconditions, then there is no restructuring — only delay. That would be irresponsible and, in the end, worse for jobs.

### Where I agree
- The solution must be **durable**, not a short-term patch.
- It must include a **real industrial plan** with investment in future products and technologies.
- Open-ended confrontation would be value-destructive.
- Voluntary exits, redeployment, qualification, working-time flexibility, and negotiated workforce contributions should all be part of the package.

### Where I disagree
#### 1) Plant closures cannot be ruled out in advance
I understand why this is sensitive, but the company cannot be expected to carry structural overcapacity forever. If all sites are protected regardless of economics, then the burden is simply pushed onto the balance sheet and future investment. That is not stewardship.

I am **not** saying closures should be the first choice. I am saying they must remain available if they are necessary to restore competitiveness and if equivalent savings cannot be achieved otherwise.

#### 2) Compulsory redundancies cannot be excluded absolutely
I would strongly prefer negotiated instruments first. But if the workforce footprint must come down materially, then management must have credible options. A blanket veto on structural adjustment is not compatible with the scale of the problem.

#### 3) Blank job and site guarantees through 2030 are too rigid
I am open to **conditional security**, but not unconditional guarantees that freeze the business. Any security must be tied to a package that genuinely closes the cost gap and supports future investment.

### What I expect from labour
If the workforce wants to avoid closures, then it needs to put forward an alternative that delivers **equivalent, quantified savings**. That means:
- measurable labour-cost reduction,
- real capacity reduction,
- meaningful flexibility,
- and a credible path to lower headcount without damaging the business.

A general refusal is not enough.

### What I expect from management
Management also has to be serious. This cannot be a punishment exercise. If we want labour to contribute, then the company must commit to:
- a convincing product and investment roadmap,
- transparent numbers,
- and a restructuring logic that is economically sound, not merely aggressive.

### What I can support
The family holding can support a package with:
- material capacity reduction,
- a substantial workforce contribution,
- voluntary and negotiated workforce reductions first,
- redeployment and qualification,
- investment commitments tied to the transformation,
- and conditional security where justified.

### What I will not support
I will not support:
- a “no change” outcome,
- a settlement that preserves all structures regardless of economics,
- or a value-destroying standoff that damages the company’s reputation and governability.

### Bottom line
The condition for our support is straightforward: **a credible package that materially improves competitiveness and restores margins, while avoiding unnecessary instability**. If the employee side can deliver that through alternatives to closure, I am open to it. If not, then management must retain the ability to use stronger measures.

So let’s stop talking in absolutes. The question is not whether change happens, but whether it is sufficient. On that point, the burden is on everyone — especially those asking to preserve every current protection without offering equivalent economic relief.
