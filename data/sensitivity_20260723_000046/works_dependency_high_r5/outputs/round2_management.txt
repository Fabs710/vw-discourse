I’ll respond plainly.

First: I agree with one important point from the works council and union — **this cannot be a cosmetic exercise**. If we do nothing structural, we will simply return here in a few months with a worse balance sheet, weaker margins, and even less room to invest in the future. That would be irresponsible.

But I do **not** accept the red lines as stated, because they eliminate the very tools needed to fix the business.

## Where I agree
- **A durable solution must go beyond short-term cuts.**
- **We need a real industrial plan for 2030**, not just savings for this quarter.
- **Investment in future products and technology is non-negotiable** if we want this company to remain competitive.
- **Open-ended confrontation would destroy value** for everyone.

## Where I disagree
### 1) “No plant closures” is not credible as a blanket rule
I understand why this is politically and emotionally difficult. But the company’s German cost base and capacity are structurally too high. If every site is protected in advance, then we are not restructuring — we are preserving overcapacity and postponing the adjustment.

I will not agree to a plan that rules out closures before we have even closed the cost gap. That would be a promise to keep an uncompetitive structure in place.

### 2) “No compulsory redundancies” cannot be an absolute
I would prefer to avoid compulsory redundancies wherever possible. But if the workforce footprint has to come down materially, we need credible instruments to achieve that. Voluntary exits, retirement models, redeployment, qualification, and working-time measures should be the first choice — yes. But I will not sign up to a settlement that makes structural headcount reduction impossible.

### 3) A guaranteed no-change security framework through 2030 is too rigid
A blanket job and site guarantee through 2030 would freeze the company precisely when it needs flexibility to recover. I am prepared to discuss **conditional security**, but only if it is linked to a package that delivers real and durable savings. Guarantees cannot become a shield against necessary restructuring.

## On the workforce contribution
The union says it is prepared to discuss a contribution only with no closures, no compulsory redundancies, and binding security through 2030. That sequence does not work for me.

If the workforce expects management to rule out the main restructuring tools first, while asking owners and management to shoulder the burden alone, that is not balanced burden-sharing. We need a meaningful labour-cost contribution — on pay, working time, flexibility, and productivity. It must be large enough to matter.

## On the state government’s position
I respect the concern for regional stability. But blocking structural change because it is uncomfortable is not a strategy. If we avoid hard decisions now, the company weakens further, and then the risk to jobs becomes bigger, not smaller.

## On the family holding and investors
I agree with the central point from the shareholder side: **the outcome must restore competitiveness and margins**. If we end up with a politically neat but economically weak settlement, we will have failed.

## What I am putting on the table
I am still open to a negotiated package, but it has to be serious. That means:
- **material capacity reduction**
- **substantial labour-cost improvement**
- **voluntary and negotiated workforce reduction instruments first**
- **redeployment and qualification**
- **investment commitments tied to a credible restructuring**
- **conditional site security where justified by economic performance**

## What I am not putting on the table
I will not commit to:
- preserving every site regardless of economics,
- blanket job guarantees through 2030,
- or a restructuring that leaves the cost base structurally uncompetitive.

## A concrete direction
If the employee side wants to avoid closures, then it needs to help us deliver **equivalent savings through other means**. That means a package with real numbers, not general principles. We need to know:
- how much labour cost will come out,
- how much capacity will be removed,
- how headcount will be reduced without damaging competitiveness,
- and what binding contribution the workforce is prepared to make.

If we can get to a package that genuinely closes the gap, then closure is not the only path. But if the answer is simply “protect everything and hope for the best,” then I cannot support it.

### My bottom line
We need a **substantial, structural reduction in cost and capacity**. I am open on the instruments, but not on the outcome. The outcome must restore competitiveness, protect the brand’s return-on-sales target, and free up investment for 2030. Anything less is not enough.
