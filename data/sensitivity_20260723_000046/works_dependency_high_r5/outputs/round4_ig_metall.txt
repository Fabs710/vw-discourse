I will be equally direct.

## Where I agree
I agree with the central diagnosis from several sides:  
- the company has a real competitiveness problem,  
- a durable restructuring is needed,  
- future products and investment matter,  
- and a prolonged conflict would hurt everyone.

I also agree that a settlement can include:
- capacity reduction,
- working-time flexibility,
- redeployment,
- qualification,
- voluntary exits,
- and a fair employee contribution.

So yes: the union is not blocking change for the sake of it.

## Where I disagree — firmly

### To management
Your repeated insistence that closures and compulsory redundancies must remain available as fallback tools is exactly why this deadlock exists.

From our side, that means the workforce is being asked to pay first while management keeps the right to shut plants and impose layoffs later. That is not a balanced restructuring. It is a threat-based one.

I also reject the idea that employee security must wait until savings are “proven” while existential risks remain live. If you want a credible agreement, you must show you are prepared to share risk, not just demand it from the workforce.

Let me be precise: **a package that leaves plant closures in Germany as an ordinary option is not acceptable to the union.** And **compulsory redundancies cannot be treated as a normal restructuring instrument** if we are supposed to negotiate in good faith.

### To the family holding and investors
You keep saying this is about stewardship and value. Then act like stewards.

A deal that destroys trust, triggers industrial conflict, and undermines the collective wage framework is not value protection. It is short-termism dressed up as discipline. If you want long-term value, you need a settlement that protects the industrial base and the workforce that produces it.

### To the state government
I appreciate your support for the regional footprint and co-determination. But “conditional support” is not enough if the result still allows closures later.

If the state wants to defend the region, then it should back a settlement that rules out closures and forced layoffs, not merely postpones the decision.

## On the works council’s position
We are aligned on the essentials:
- no plant closures in Germany,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- and binding protection for jobs and sites.

That said, I want to be clear: the union also needs a **serious, quantifiable restructuring path**. We cannot defend wage standards and jobs by wishful thinking. We need a package that actually delivers savings and gives management a credible path to competitiveness without shredding the workforce.

## On the moderator’s bridge options

### 1) Time-bound “protected restructuring” package
This is the closest thing to a usable framework — but only if the protection is real.

For the union, that means:
- **no plant closures in Germany for the duration of the agreement**
- **no compulsory redundancies for the duration**
- **quantified savings and capacity measures**
- **binding investment commitments**
- **and a fair employee contribution**

If the “time-bound” language is just a backdoor to reopen closures later, then it is not security and not acceptable.

### 2) Stepwise trigger model with earned security
I do **not** accept the logic that workers must first concede while management keeps destructive tools available, and only later “earn” security.

That sequencing is wrong.

What we can discuss is a package where:
- the workforce makes an initial, limited contribution,
- management simultaneously commits to no closures and no forced layoffs,
- and both sides are bound to objective milestones.

But the milestones must not be a mechanism to reintroduce plant closures as a punishment clause.

### 3) Company-wide framework plus site-specific implementation
Yes — if the company-wide framework is strong enough.

Site-specific planning can make sense for investment, product allocation, and capacity adjustment. It cannot become a divide-and-conquer tool that isolates one site after another. We will not accept a model where the company picks off plants one by one.

## What we are prepared to put on the table
The union remains prepared to discuss a fair contribution from employees, including:
- temporary wage moderation or forgoing certain bonuses,
- working-time flexibility,
- internal mobility,
- qualification,
- redeployment,
- and voluntary exit arrangements.

But we will only do that if the overall package includes:
1. **No plant closures in Germany**
2. **No compulsory redundancies**
3. **Binding job and site protection through 2030**
4. **Preservation of collective bargaining and co-determination**
5. **Binding investment commitments for future products and technology**
6. **A measurable, shared-sacrifice cost package**

That is not a maximalist demand. It is the minimum for a fair settlement.

## What I will not accept
I will not accept:
- closures as a fallback,
- forced layoffs as a fallback,
- open-ended insecurity,
- or a settlement where workers carry the pain while management and owners preserve all the hard options.

If that remains the position of management and shareholders, then there is no basis for agreement and we will continue to use our leverage, including industrial action.

## What would move us
We need a concrete package, not general statements:
- a quantified savings target,
- a capacity reduction plan that avoids closures,
- binding no-forced-layoff and no-closure commitments,
- investment for German plants and future products,
- and a fair distribution of sacrifice across all stakeholders.

If management and owners are serious about a durable solution, then bring that package forward. Otherwise, you are asking the workforce to finance uncertainty they did not create.

I remain ready to negotiate seriously — but only on the basis of real protection, shared sacrifice, and no one-sided restructuring.
