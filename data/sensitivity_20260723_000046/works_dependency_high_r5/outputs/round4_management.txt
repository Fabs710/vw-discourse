## Where I agree
I agree with everyone on the basic diagnosis:
- this company has a **real competitiveness problem**,
- the current cost base is **not sustainable**,
- we need a **durable industrial plan**, not a cosmetic fix,
- and future products, technology, and investment to 2030 must be protected.

I also agree that a prolonged, destructive conflict would hurt the company badly. That is precisely why I am insisting on a settlement that actually changes the economics.

## Where I disagree — firmly

### To the works council and union
You keep repeating the same line: **no closures, no compulsory redundancies, binding guarantees through 2030 first, then concessions**.

That sequencing is exactly what I cannot accept.

If we remove closures and involuntary measures from the toolset up front, while the cost gap remains unresolved, then we are not restructuring — we are freezing an uncompetitive structure and hoping it gets better on its own. It won’t.

Let me be explicit:
- **I will not commit to a blanket no-closure guarantee.**
- **I will not commit to a blanket no-compulsory-redundancy guarantee.**
- **I will not accept an unconditional, company-wide job and site guarantee through 2030.**

Those positions are incompatible with restoring competitiveness.

You say “security first.” I say the company cannot buy security with a cost base it cannot afford. If the workforce wants to avoid harder tools, it has to help close the gap in a way that is **measurable, durable, and sufficient**.

### To the regional state government
I respect your role, and I understand the regional and political consequences. But I disagree with the idea that the state can or should effectively veto structural adjustment by treating every German site as equally protected regardless of economics.

That is not long-term industrial policy. That is delay.

If the state wants to preserve the regional footprint, then it should support a package that actually makes the footprint viable — not one that preserves all options for labour and removes the company’s ability to adapt.

### To the controlling family holding
You are right that management needs credibility and that cosmetic change is unacceptable. But stewardship also means being honest about the scale of the problem.

If the company’s German operations remain structurally too expensive, then optionality alone is not value creation. A deal that avoids hard choices while leaving margins impaired is not stewardship; it is deferral.

I agree that closures are not the first choice. I do **not** agree that they can be ruled out categorically.

### To institutional investors
I agree with your point that a deal must be financially credible and durable. I also agree that value is destroyed by prolonged conflict. But investors also need to understand something very simple: **a non-closure solution only works if it delivers equivalent savings**.

If the labor-side alternative does not produce the same structural effect on cost and capacity, then it is not an alternative. It is a delay mechanism.

## On the moderator’s bridge options

### 1) Time-bound “protected restructuring” package
This is only acceptable if it is genuinely protected and genuinely restructuring.

What does not work is:
- a soft promise,
- vague language,
- or a “review” mechanism that simply postpones hard decisions.

If we discuss a time-bound package, then it must include:
- a **clear, quantified savings target**,
- a **clear capacity reduction target**,
- **binding employee contributions**,
- and **objective milestones**.

But I will not accept a time-bound package that functions as a de facto no-closure veto without proving the economics.

### 2) Stepwise trigger model with earned security
This is closer, but only if the first step produces **real and immediate** savings.

I can discuss phasing. I cannot accept a model where management gives up all restructuring tools first and then waits to see whether the workforce feels like contributing later.

If we do stepwise, then:
- phase one must deliver verifiable savings,
- phase two can grant stronger security,
- and phase three must preserve a real fallback if the targets are missed.

But let me be clear: the fallback cannot be stripped away in advance.

### 3) Company-wide framework plus site-specific implementation
This is the most sensible direction so far, but only if site-specific planning reflects business reality.

Site-specific agreements can work if they help allocate investment, products, and capacity rationally. They do **not** work if they become a way to preserve every site by political preference and block any hard adjustment.

A company-wide framework can exist, yes — but it must be a framework for restructuring, not a framework for freezing the status quo.

## What I am putting on the table
I remain prepared to discuss a serious package that includes:
- material capacity reduction,
- negotiated workforce contribution,
- voluntary exits before involuntary measures,
- redeployment and qualification,
- working-time flexibility,
- and binding investment commitments for future products and technology.

That is a real offer.

But it is conditional on the package being strong enough to restore the economics. I will not sign up to a deal that merely redistributes pain without solving the underlying cost problem.

## What I am not putting on the table
I am not putting on the table:
- a blanket no-closure commitment,
- a blanket no-forced-redundancy commitment,
- or an unconditional 2030 job guarantee.

If those are the preconditions, then there is no basis for agreement from management’s side.

## My challenge back to labour
You say you are prepared to contribute, but only after protection is locked in. I am telling you plainly: **protection that removes all structural tools up front is not available unless you can show an equivalent, quantified alternative.**

So here is the real question:
- What exact savings do you deliver?
- What exact capacity reduction do you accept?
- What exact labour-cost contribution is durable enough to restore the brand’s return on sales?

If you cannot answer those with numbers, then your proposal is not a restructuring proposal. It is a demand for preservation.

## Bottom line
I am not retreating from the core mandate:
- restore competitiveness,
- cut overcapacity,
- reduce labour costs structurally,
- and free funds for investment to 2030.

If the other sides want a deal, they need to stop asking for absolute security while refusing equivalent structural contributions. We need a package that actually fixes the business.

If no such package is forthcoming, then management must retain the tools necessary to do so.
