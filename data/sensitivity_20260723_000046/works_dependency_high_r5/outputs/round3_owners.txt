I will speak plainly, because the discussion has now reached the point where ambiguity would be unhelpful.

## Where I agree

I agree with management on the central diagnosis: **the business is structurally uncompetitive on cost and capacity, and a cosmetic compromise would destroy value**. I also agree with the investors that prolonged conflict is value-destructive, and with the state that a durable industrial plan and future investment are essential.

I further agree that the solution should be built from:
- voluntary exits first,
- redeployment,
- qualification,
- working-time flexibility,
- and a measurable workforce contribution.

That is the right direction.

## Where I disagree

### 1) Labour’s sequencing is not acceptable
The works council and union continue to demand:
- no closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- and binding guarantees through 2030  
**before** meaningful concessions are made.

That sequencing does not solve the problem. It simply shifts all risk to the owners and management while leaving the structural cost gap unresolved.

I understand the desire for security, but I do not accept the premise that the company should commit to a frozen structure before it knows whether the labour-side package can close the gap.

### 2) “No closures” cannot be an absolute
I am not saying closures are the preferred path. They are not. But I will not support a framework that removes them entirely from the universe of options regardless of economics.

If equivalent savings can be achieved through other means, fine. But if they cannot, then closures must remain available as a last resort. That is basic stewardship, not aggression.

### 3) Blanket guarantees through 2030 are too rigid
A universal job-and-site guarantee through 2030 is not compatible with restoring competitiveness if the business needs flexibility to adapt.

I can consider **conditional security**, but only if it is tied to:
- quantified savings,
- capacity reduction,
- and a credible industrial plan.

Security must be earned by the package, not declared in advance as a veto over restructuring.

### 4) Compulsory redundancies cannot be excluded absolutely
Again, I would strongly prefer negotiated instruments first. But I will not agree to a deal that makes structural headcount reduction impossible if the business case requires it.

A blanket prohibition would simply lock in overcapacity and weaken the company’s ability to invest.

## What I take from the other parties

### To management
You need to be more concrete. “Structural change” is not yet a package. Put real numbers on the table:
- exact savings required,
- capacity to be removed,
- timeline,
- and how the savings translate into margin recovery.

You also need to show what you are offering on future products and investment. If labour is asked to contribute, management cannot be vague about the industrial roadmap.

### To the works council and union
You are right that trust matters. But trust cannot be built by insisting that the company keep all adjustment tools off the table while the economics remain weak.

If you want to avoid closures and forced layoffs, then show how the alternative delivers **equivalent, durable savings**. Not principles. Numbers.

### To the state government
I respect your political role and the importance of the regional footprint. But preserving every site regardless of performance is not long-term industrial policy. It can easily become a way of postponing adjustment until the problem is worse.

### To investors
I agree that this cannot become a politically neat but economically weak compromise. But value also depends on stable governance and labour peace. A settlement that creates years of disruption is not value-maximising either.

## My position on the bridge options

### 1) Conditional no-closure / no-forced-layoff package with triggers
This is the only bridge worth discussing, but only if the conditions are serious:
- a defined period of protection,
- verified savings milestones,
- and a real review mechanism.

What I do **not** accept is a trigger system that becomes a hidden veto on restructuring. The triggers must be objective and tied to delivery.

### 2) Site-specific agreements
I am open to site-specific commitments if they are anchored in a company-wide restructuring logic. That may be the best way to align investment with economics. But it cannot become a way to preserve every site by default.

### 3) No involuntary redundancies first
This is the most realistic labor principle on the table. I support it **as a first-order approach**, not as an absolute ban. Involuntary measures should be a last resort after all negotiated options are exhausted.

## What I would support

I could support a package with:
- material capacity reduction,
- meaningful but measurable workforce contribution,
- voluntary exits and redeployment first,
- qualification and working-time measures,
- binding investment commitments for future products and technology,
- and **conditional security** tied to performance.

That would be a serious package.

## What I will not support

I will not support:
- a blank no-closure commitment,
- a blanket ban on compulsory redundancies,
- or unconditional guarantees through 2030 that freeze the business.

Those would undermine the very objective we all claim to share: restoring competitiveness and protecting long-term enterprise value.

## Bottom line

The question is not whether the company changes. It must.  
The question is whether the package is sufficient to restore margins and competitiveness.

If labour can present an **equivalent, quantified alternative** that closes the gap without closures, I am prepared to consider it. If not, then management must retain the ability to use stronger tools.

That is my line.
