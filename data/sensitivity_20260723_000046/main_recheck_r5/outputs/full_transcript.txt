[Works Council (Betriebsrat) (Plant-level employee representation under co-determination) — Round 1]
As Works Council, I will be very clear from the outset:

We will not accept plant closures in Germany and we will not accept compulsory redundancies. That is the line.

The workforce has already carried a great deal of the burden, and management cannot now solve strategic mistakes by destroying jobs, breaking trust, and hollowing out the German sites that built this company. We are not here to rubber-stamp a fait accompli.

Our priorities are:

- Keep every German plant open
- Secure all jobs through 2030
- Preserve co-determination and the existing job-security framework
- Find restructuring measures that are fair, reversible, and socially responsible
- Ensure that any transformation comes with binding investment and future product commitments for the sites

If management wants a contribution from the workforce, then it must be negotiated fairly and only as part of a package that guarantees site security. We are prepared to discuss working-time flexibility, internal redeployment, qualification, voluntary instruments, and productivity measures. Let me also say this plainly: if this is presented as a unilateral closure plan or as an attack on the historic protections of the workforce, there will be resistance. We have the legal means, the supervisory-board leverage, and the support of the workforce to defend these sites.

So my position is: no closures, no forced layoffs, and a binding agreement on jobs and sites to 2030 before any meaningful discussion of sacrifices by the workforce.

[Industrial Union (Trade union and collective-bargaining counterpart) — Round 1]

[Expert: independent industrial restructuring exp — Round 3]
From an industrial-restructuring perspective, the key issue is not whether the company needs adjustment — it clearly does — but **which measures can credibly deliver the required savings without becoming unenforceable or operationally self-defeating**.

## 1) Are the claimed savings likely to require plant closures and compulsory redundancies?
**Possibly, but not proven from the facts given.**  
Management’s position is directionally plausible if:
- German fixed costs are materially above peer level,
- the company has excess installed capacity,
- utilization is structurally low,
- and the product mix cannot be shifted fast enough.

In that case, **capacity removal is usually the fastest and most durable source of savings**. If the cost gap is large enough, then a package relying only on wage restraint, working-time flexibility, and voluntary exits may be **insufficient**.

However, the critical missing information is quantitative:
- current and target capacity by plant,
- utilization rates,
- fixed-cost base by site,
- the size of the annual savings gap,
- and how much of that gap is labor-related versus footprint-related.

Without those numbers, it is not credible to say closures are unavoidable. It is only credible to say they may be one of the few instruments with enough magnitude.

## 2) Are “no closures / no compulsory redundancies” alternatives operationally realistic?
**Only if the savings target is moderate and the company has enough internal flexibility.**  
Such alternatives can work when the gap can be closed by a combination of:
- natural attrition,
- voluntary severance,
- redeployment across plants,
- short-time or working-time reductions,
- wage moderation,
- bonus restraint,
- and productivity gains.

But there are limits:
- **Redeployment only works if there are receiving sites or functions.**
- **Voluntary exits only work if the number of leavers is sufficiently high.**
- **Working-time measures only work if they produce real FTE and cost reduction, not just accounting flexibility.**
- **Wage restraint alone rarely closes a major structural gap.**

So a “no closure” solution is realistic only if management’s required saving is not too large, or if there is substantial slack in employment that can be removed without site closures.

## 3) Can site-by-site differentiation be a viable compromise?
**Yes, and in practice it is often the most workable industrial compromise.**  
A blanket closure ban across all German sites is usually too rigid. But a pure closure-first strategy is politically and industrially difficult.

A more credible middle path is:
- protect strategically important or highly utilized sites,
- convert weaker sites to narrower roles,
- remove capacity selectively,
- and use voluntary instruments plus internal transfers to the maximum extent possible.

This is operationally realistic **if** the company can define which sites have:
- future product allocation,
- competitive cost structure,
- logistics advantages,
- and sufficient scale.

Without such site-level differentiation, the compromise risks becoming either:
- too vague to implement, or
- too broad to achieve savings.

## 4) Are trigger clauses and conditional no-closure frameworks enforceable?
**Only partly.** They are enforceable if drafted very carefully, but they are inherently fragile.

### What is enforceable
A workable trigger-based package can include:
- objective financial and operational milestones,
- predefined measurement dates,
- independent audit/review,
- automatic escalation of voluntary measures,
- defined management and owner contributions,
- and pre-agreed fallback steps.

### What is not robust
What is usually not robust is a clause that says:
- “no closures unless targets are missed,”
without specifying:
- who determines failure,
- how failure is measured,
- what remedial steps happen first,
- and whether closures are truly the only fallback.

In German industrial-relations practice, these arrangements are often **negotiable and documentable**, but they can become contested if the triggers are vague or politically loaded.

## 5) Are the employee-side “no closure” commitments likely to be credible?
**Only if they are matched with real, measurable contributions.**  
If the workforce side wants durable site and job security, then it must typically accept some combination of:
- wage restraint,
- longer working time or less paid time off,
- redeployment obligations,
- training commitments,
- flexibility in shift patterns,
- and limits on some allowances or bonuses.

What is not credible is a package that preserves all sites, all jobs, and all guarantees while asking management to accept a large structural cost reduction with no footprint adjustment. That usually does not close a real gap.

## 6) Can management and owners “share the burden” in a meaningful way?
**Yes, but their contribution is usually supplementary, not substitutive.**  
Visible top-side burden-sharing can include:
- management pay restraint,
- bonus deferral,
- capital allocation to German sites,
- product commitment,
- and possibly dividend moderation at group level if the governance structure allows it.

But these measures rarely substitute for plant-level restructuring if overcapacity is the main issue. They can improve legitimacy and bargaining feasibility, but they do not by themselves solve a serious fixed-cost problem.

## 7) Practical assessment of the three broad options

### Option A: Deep cuts with closures and forced redundancies
- **Most likely to deliver the fastest structural savings**
- **Highest industrial-relations conflict**
- **Hardest to enforce politically**
- **Risky if conflict disrupts production or governance**

### Option B: No closures, no forced redundancies, with flexible labor and voluntary exits
- **Potentially workable only if the savings gap is moderate**
- **Higher chance of industrial peace**
- **Risk of insufficient savings**
- **Depends heavily on voluntary uptake and real productivity gains**

### Option C: Differentiated site strategy with negotiated protection for stronger sites and conversion/reduction of weaker sites
- **Most realistic compromise**
- **Can be economically credible if site-level economics are clear**
- **Still requires some capacity reduction**
- **Easier to enforce than a blanket no-closure promise**
- **Politically more defensible than open-ended closure threats**

## 8) Bottom line
My assessment is:

- **Management’s demand for structural savings is credible.**
- **The claim that closures and compulsory redundancies are strictly unavoidable is not yet proven on the facts provided.**
- **A pure no-closure / no-forced-layoff package is only realistic if the required savings are materially smaller than implied or if there is substantial hidden flexibility in the footprint.**
- **The most realistic compromise is a differentiated site-by-site package with quantified labor contributions, binding investment commitments, and a clearly defined fallback mechanism.**

## 9) What I would need to validate any package properly
To assess credibility, I would need:
1. savings target in euros per year,
2. capacity by plant and utilization rates,
3. labor cost per vehicle or per unit by site,
4. expected voluntary exit capacity,
5. redeployment options within Germany,
6. product allocation by plant,
7. investment commitments by model and platform,
8. enforcement terms and trigger definitions.

Without that, any side claiming “our alternative is fully sufficient” is making an assertion, not a validated restructuring case.

If you want, I can next translate this into a **practical compromise package**: minimum savings, acceptable instruments, and an enforceable German settlement structure.

[Expert: independent industrial finance and opera — Round 4]
Here is the industrial-finance assessment, focusing only on feasibility, savings math, and capacity adjustment.

## Executive view

A **credible 2030 return-on-sales recovery** for a German passenger-car business with structurally high factory costs usually requires a combination of:

1. **durable fixed-cost reduction**,
2. **material capacity reduction or load balancing**,
3. **lower labor cost per vehicle**,
4. **productivity gains / simplification**,
5. **and product mix improvement**.

A **pure no-closure package** can work only if the savings gap is **modest** and if the company has enough hidden flexibility in:
- headcount,
- overtime / working time,
- model allocation,
- internal logistics,
- and capex deferral.

If the German operations are truly **materially above competitor cost**, then a no-closure package is usually **not sufficient by itself** unless it includes either:
- a very large employee contribution, or
- substantial volume transfer / downsizing of weak sites, even if formal closures are avoided.

## 1) What return-on-sales recovery typically needs

For a mature premium/mass-market automotive brand, moving from weak profitability to a defensible 2030 ROS usually requires roughly:

- **+3 to +5 percentage points** improvement in operating margin from the trough case, if the current position is around break-even to low-single-digit margin.
- If the brand is in a more severe position, the required swing can be larger.

That improvement typically comes from some mix of:
- **€800m to €1.5bn+ annual structural cost reduction** for a large German passenger-car footprint,
- depending on volume, plant count, and current labor/capacity inefficiency.

If the company has multiple German plants and significant overcapacity, I would treat **~€1bn annual recurring savings** as the **minimum credible order of magnitude** for a serious 2030 recovery plan, with **€1.2bn–€1.8bn** not unusual if the cost base is truly uncompetitive.

## 2) How that savings usually breaks down

A realistic structural package usually needs some combination of:

### A. Workforce and labor-cost reduction
Typical contribution:
- **20% to 40%** of total required savings

For a large German site network, this might mean:
- headcount reduction,
- lower entry wages / progression changes,
- bonus restraint,
- working-time increases or flexibility,
- reduced guarantees,
- more variable labor deployment.

Order of magnitude:
- often **€200m to €600m per year** if done meaningfully.

### B. Capacity reduction / footprint rationalization
Typical contribution:
- **25% to 45%** of total required savings

This includes:
- closing one site,
- downsizing one or more sites,
- shifting volume to fewer lines,
- eliminating underloaded shifts,
- reducing fixed overhead, maintenance, utilities, and indirect labor.

Order of magnitude:
- often **€250m to €700m per year** in recurring fixed-cost relief if a meaningful amount of underutilized capacity is removed.

### C. Productivity / overhead simplification
Typical contribution:
- **15% to 30%** of total required savings

Includes:
- procurement simplification,
- logistics efficiency,
- engineering complexity reduction,
- lower variant count,
- faster changeovers,
- indirect function cuts.

Order of magnitude:
- **€150m to €400m per year**.

### D. Product mix / pricing / mix quality
Not a plant-restructuring instrument per se, but essential.
- Often **€200m+** of ROS improvement can come from mix and pricing if the product lineup is stronger.
- This is less controllable and less certain than cost actions.

## 3) Minimum savings needed for a “no-closure” package to be credible

A no-closure package can be credible only if it still delivers roughly:

- **at least €700m–€1bn annual recurring structural savings** for a large German network,
- and preferably more if current utilization is poor.

If it cannot get into that range, it is unlikely to restore competitiveness by 2030 unless market demand and product mix improve sharply.

### A practical threshold
For a no-closure deal to be believable, I would look for:

- **€300m–€500m** labor-cost contribution,
- **€200m–€400m** productivity/overhead improvement,
- **€200m–€500m** through capacity reduction without formal closure, for example:
  - shift elimination,
  - line consolidation,
  - conversion of a site to parts / components / R&D / battery / services,
  - volume transfer out of a weak plant,
  - or very deep load balancing.

If the package cannot reach these kinds of numbers, it is probably **too small**.

## 4) Can “no closure” really work?

### Yes, but only if all of the following are true:
1. The current savings gap is not extreme.
2. The company can permanently reduce capacity **without** legal closure.
3. Employee-side measures are unusually large and durable.
4. Management accepts serious product and investment commitments.
5. There is a hard enforcement mechanism if savings slip.

### In practice, the problem is this:
If all plants remain open and all sites are protected, then the main remaining levers are:
- labor cost,
- working time,
- mix,
- voluntary exits,
- and efficiency.

Those levers often **do not sum to enough** unless the workforce contribution is quite deep.

So a “no-closure” package is feasible only if it is not really a “status quo” package; it must still include **real capacity shedding**, just without the political label of closure.

## 5) What “differentiated site package” would need to include

A differentiated package can be economically credible if it does **one or more** of the following:

### Option 1: Protect some sites, convert others
- Core sites remain and get product investment.
- Weaker sites lose volume, are repurposed, or shifted to lower-cost functions.
- Net savings can be substantial even if a plant is not formally closed.

### Option 2: No closure, but deep line consolidation
- Multiple sites remain legally open.
- One or more sites run at much lower volume or no longer assemble complete vehicles.
- Fixed overhead falls, but only if the headcount and operating model really shrink.

### Option 3: No closure, but major labor-cost reset
- Wages, bonuses, hours, and guarantees are changed enough to generate durable savings.
- This can work only if the savings are large and lasting, not temporary.

### Option 4: Time-bounded protection with automatic fallback
- This is economically more credible than permanent guarantees.
- But it only works if the fallback is real and enforceable.

## 6) My judgment on feasibility

### If the company needs only a moderate savings bridge:
A **no-closure differentiated site package could be feasible**.

### If the company’s German footprint is structurally and materially too expensive:
A **pure no-closure / no-forced-redundancy package is unlikely to be sufficient**.

In that case, the economically necessary actions are usually:
- fewer vehicles built in Germany,
- fewer fixed-cost plants,
- lower labor intensity,
- and some form of footprint adjustment.

That does **not** always require a formal plant closure, but it usually does require **real capacity removal**.

## 7) Bottom-line answer

### Minimum credible target
For a large German carmaker in this situation, I would treat:

- **~€1bn annual recurring structural savings** as a reasonable minimum benchmark for a credible 2030 turnaround,
- with **€1.2bn–€1.8bn** plausible if the current cost gap is severe.

### No-closure test
A no-closure package is only credible if it can deliver, in durable annual savings terms:

- substantial labor-cost reset,
- material productivity gains,
- and enough capacity reduction to remove underutilized fixed cost.

If it cannot, then it is **economically insufficient**, even if politically attractive.

With that, one can calculate whether the no-closure path closes the gap or merely delays it.
