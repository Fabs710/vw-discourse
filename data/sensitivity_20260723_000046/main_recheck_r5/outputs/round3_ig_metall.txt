I will be direct.

## To management, the family holding, and investors
You keep repeating “quantified savings” as if that by itself makes plant closures and forced layoffs legitimate. It does not.

We have already acknowledged the structural crisis. What you have **not** shown is that German closures or compulsory redundancies are the only viable instruments. You are still treating the most damaging measures as the default, then asking the workforce to prove an alternative. That is not a fair burden of proof.

A negotiated solution must start from this reality:
- workers did **not** create the whole problem,
- workers will not finance the whole adjustment,
- and workers will not accept being the sole shock absorber while top-side actors stay protected.

## To the Works Council
I agree with your core line:
- no plant closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- no erosion of co-determination,
- no one-sided burden shifting.

That is the correct boundary.

Where I would caution is this: we should not let “quantification” become a trap that quietly normalizes the employer’s preferred end state. We need to be willing to discuss real numbers, but only inside a framework that protects jobs and the collective bargaining framework.

## To the state
Your emphasis on the industrial base and regional stability is right. A hollowed-out German footprint would damage the company and the region.

But the state should be careful not to turn “no closures” into a vague political wish while leaving workers exposed through indirect means. If the state wants stability, it should back a binding industrial settlement with:
- secure jobs,
- secure sites,
- and real top-side contributions.

## On the facilitator’s bridge proposals
### 1) Conditional no-closure / no-forced-layoff with trigger clauses
I do **not** trust this as currently framed.

If “no layoffs” can later be suspended because management says targets were missed, then it is not security — it is deferred pressure. That is exactly the kind of arrangement workers experience as a time bomb.

If there is to be any conditionality at all, then the conditions must not be a backdoor to closures or forced redundancies. They should instead trigger:
- additional management contribution,
- additional owner support,
- additional investment,
- or further voluntary measures.

Not automatic job destruction.

### 2) Site-by-site restructuring with differentiated guarantees
This is dangerous if it becomes a ranking exercise that pits plant against plant.

I can see the logic of site-specific production planning, but I will not accept a framework that creates “safe” and “unsafe” sites and leaves weaker locations permanently vulnerable. Once that dynamic starts, workers everywhere are weaker.

If differentiation is discussed, it must be tied to:
- transparent industrial criteria,
- no forced redundancies,
- no closure path,
- and guaranteed redeployment options.

### 3) Burden-sharing package with enforceable top-side contributions
This is the only serious path on the table — but only if the burden-sharing is genuine.

That means:
- worker contribution,
- management contribution,
- owner contribution,
- and investment commitment.

Not “employees first, others later.”

## What I can support
I am prepared to support a package with the following elements:

1. **No compulsory redundancies**
   - Workforce reduction only through voluntary and socially responsible instruments.

2. **No plant closures**
   - German sites remain open; the restructuring must work without closure threats.

3. **A fair labor contribution**
   - Temporary wage restraint, bonus restraint, working-time flexibility, or similar collective measures may be negotiable.

4. **Internal capacity adjustment**
   - Redeployment, qualification, product-mix shifts, and productivity gains.

5. **Binding investment and product commitments**
   - Concrete allocations of future models, technology, and capital spending to German sites.

6. **Top-side burden sharing**
   - Management and owners must make visible contributions, not just demand them from labor.

7. **Durable settlement**
   - A time-bound agreement that protects the collective framework and prevents repeated reopening of the same conflict.

## What I will not accept
Let me be even clearer:

- no plant closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- no open-ended insecurity,
- no erosion of collective bargaining standards,
- no “shared sacrifice” where the workforce sacrifices and everyone else lectures,
- no conditional arrangement that can be turned into layoffs later.

## On the actual savings question
If management wants a real answer, here is mine:

We are willing to discuss a **material but fair employee contribution** if — and only if — it is matched by equally real contributions from management and owners, and if it secures jobs and sites.

That could include:
- temporary wage restraint,
- moderated bonuses,
- working-time flexibility,
- redeployment,
- retraining,
- and productivity measures.

But it must be accompanied by:
- binding site security,
- no forced layoffs,
- and a credible investment plan.

If the savings gap cannot be closed without closures or forced layoffs, then management has not yet presented a convincing industrial plan. It has presented a downsizing strategy.

## Bottom line
I remain open to a serious settlement, but not to a restructuring that shifts the burden onto workers while keeping closures and forced redundancies on the table.

My position is unchanged:
- **protect jobs**
- **protect sites**
- **protect the collective wage framework**
- **share the burden fairly**
- **and make management and owners contribute visibly**

If the next proposal still depends on plant closures or compulsory redundancies, then it is not a compromise. It is a confrontation.
