I will respond plainly.

## Where I agree
I agree with everyone on the diagnosis:
- this is a **structural** competitiveness problem,
- the company needs a **durable** solution,
- productivity, flexibility, redeployment, qualification, and investment all belong in the package,
- and prolonged conflict would be damaging for the company and the region.

I also agree that workers, management, owners, and shareholders must each make a contribution. That is fair, and it is the only way a social settlement can hold.

## Where I disagree
I do **not** accept the repeated framing that plant closures and compulsory redundancies are the default answer, or that the burden of proof sits primarily with the workforce to disprove them.

From the state’s perspective, that is not acceptable as a starting point. German plants are not disposable surplus. They are:
- industrial capacity,
- training capacity,
- supplier anchors,
- and major regional employers.

If you damage that base, you do not just “optimize” the company — you weaken the company’s long-term resilience and the region’s economic stability.

So let me be clear:

- **No blanket mandate for German plant closures**
- **No compulsory redundancies as the preferred route**
- **No erosion of co-determination or the state’s blocking role**
- **No solution that shifts the adjustment mainly onto workers and the region**

## To management
You have not yet convinced me that closures or forced layoffs are the only way to close the gap. You keep saying the package must be economically equivalent — I agree with that standard. But then you must actually demonstrate the economics of your preferred path.

What I need is not broad claims about structural pressure. I need a **quantified comparison**:
- savings from working-time flexibility,
- internal redeployment,
- voluntary exits,
- product-mix changes,
- qualification and productivity measures,
- management restraint,
- owner contributions,
- and targeted capacity adjustments that do **not** require plant destruction.

If you cannot show that less damaging instruments fail, then pushing closures first is a choice, not a necessity.

## To the Works Council and Union
I support your core line:
- no closures,
- no forced layoffs,
- no hollowing out of the collective framework,
- and no one-sided burden shifting.

That said, I also have to be honest with you: a viable alternative must be more than a political refusal. If you want to keep sites open and jobs secure, the package must be **economically credible and measurable**.

That means:
- real productivity gains,
- real flexibility,
- real redeployment,
- real training and re-skilling,
- and a real labor contribution.

But it must happen within a framework of binding site and job protection. Workers cannot be asked to pay for a restructuring that destroys the very industrial base they are supposed to help preserve.

## To the family holding
I understand your insistence on competitiveness and durable margin restoration. I do not dispute the need for change. But I cannot support an approach that treats the German footprint as freely disposable in the name of abstract value creation.

You want structural adjustment. Fine. Then show me that structural adjustment can be achieved **without** making German employment the sacrificial zone.

If you want burden-sharing, it must be visible at the top too:
- management restraint,
- owner contribution,
- and binding investment in the German sites.

## To investors
You are right to demand credible savings and an enforceable timeline. But I do not accept a narrow shareholder logic that ignores the company’s role as a regional anchor and its co-determination architecture.

A “value-preserving” restructuring that destroys industrial capacity, politicizes the conflict, and undermines trust is not value-preserving. It is a risk to the business model.

## What I can support
I can support a package that includes:

1. **Binding site and job protection for a defined horizon**
   - not vague promises, but real guarantees.

2. **A negotiated worker contribution**
   - working-time flexibility,
   - temporary restraint,
   - redeployment,
   - retraining,
   - productivity improvements.

3. **Visible management and owner contributions**
   - burden-sharing must not stop at the workforce.

4. **Concrete investment and product commitments**
   - future models and technology allocated to German sites.

5. **Measured capacity adjustment**
   - but only if it can be done without destroying the company’s regional industrial base.

## What I cannot support
I cannot support:
- a plan that begins from closures as the default solution,
- a plan that makes compulsory redundancies routine,
- a plan that weakens co-determination,
- or a settlement that leaves the region with the downsides and the company with only short-term relief.

## My line
If management can bring a **quantified, enforceable** restructuring that preserves the German industrial base, protects jobs to a meaningful degree, and restores competitiveness, then I will engage seriously.

If the proposal still depends on treating closures and forced layoffs as normal tools, then I will not move. That crosses the line for the state.

So my message is simple:
- **Protect the industrial base**
- **Share the burden fairly**
- **Invest in Germany**
- **And do not ask us to endorse a solution that damages the region to save face in the short term**
