I will respond directly.

I agree with all parties on one essential point: this is a **structural** competitiveness problem, not a temporary wobble. The company needs a durable solution, and short-term cosmetics will not be enough.

But I need to be equally clear about the boundaries from the state’s perspective:

## Where I agree
- The company must restore competitiveness and margins.
- Productivity, flexibility, redeployment, qualification, and investment commitments are all necessary parts of the package.
- Management, owners, and workforce will all need to contribute.
- Prolonged conflict would be damaging for everyone, especially the regional economy.

## Where I disagree
I do **not** accept that the only credible restructuring route is plant closures in Germany or compulsory redundancies.

That is not a neutral economic conclusion; it is a choice with severe regional consequences. For this company, German sites are not just lines on a cost sheet. They are an industrial base, a training base, a supplier anchor, and a regional employer. If you destroy that footprint, you do not just “adjust capacity” — you damage the company’s long-term backbone and the region’s economic stability.

So my position remains:
- **No blanket mandate for German plant closures**
- **No compulsory redundancies as the preferred route**
- **No settlement that sidelines co-determination or the state’s blocking role**
- **No burden-sharing that falls mainly on workers and regions**

## On management’s argument
Management says alternatives are only acceptable if they are economically equivalent. I agree with the principle. But that cuts both ways: you have not yet shown that closures are the only way to achieve the required savings.

Before moving to irreversible site decisions, I expect a serious quantified comparison of:
- savings from working-time flexibility,
- internal redeployment,
- product-mix changes,
- voluntary instruments,
- qualification and productivity measures,
- management and owner contributions,
- and targeted capacity adjustments that do not require plant destruction.

If management wants the state to consider more far-reaching measures, then it must first prove that less damaging instruments cannot close the gap.

## On the workforce side
I also want to be constructive here: I hear the clear rejection of closures and forced redundancies, and I support the demand that this company not solve its problems by hollowing out the German industrial base.

But I would also say to the workforce side: a viable alternative must be more than a refusal. If you want to keep sites open and jobs secure, then the package must be economically credible and measurable. That means real numbers, real productivity effects, and real commitments — not only a political line.

## What I can support
The state could support a package that includes:
1. **Binding site and job protection through a defined horizon**
   - not vague assurances, but a real framework.
2. **A negotiated workforce contribution**
   - working-time flexibility, temporary restraint, voluntary instruments, redeployment.
3. **Management and owner contributions**
   - visible burden-sharing at the top, not a one-sided employee sacrifice.
4. **Concrete investment commitments**
   - future products, technology, and capital allocation to German sites.
5. **A measured capacity adjustment**
   - only if it can be done without destroying the regional industrial anchor.

## What I cannot support
I cannot support:
- a plan that starts from closures as the default answer,
- a restructuring that treats regional employment as disposable,
- or a settlement that weakens co-determination and the state’s governance role.

If the company wants our support, it must show a restructuring that preserves the long-term health of the enterprise **and** its role in the region. That is not sentimentality; it is strategic realism.

## Bottom line
I am open to a serious, quantified restructuring package. But I will not move on the core point:
**German plant closures and mass layoffs are not acceptable starting assumptions.**

Bring forward a plan that protects the regional industrial base, secures investment, and delivers credible savings through negotiated means. If that is impossible, then the problem has not been solved — it has merely been shifted onto the workforce and the region, and I will not support that.
