I agree with the Works Council on the core line: **no plant closures and no compulsory redundancies**. That is not a bargaining detail; it is the minimum basis for any serious discussion.

What we also need to be clear about is **why** this matters. The workforce cannot be expected to pay for every strategic error, market setback, and management miscalculation while owners and leadership escape with merely “adjustments.” If the response to a cost problem is to cut jobs, weaken the collective framework, and threaten sites, then the burden is being pushed one-sidedly onto employees. That is neither fair nor sustainable.

At the same time, I want to be constructive in a very specific way: **we are prepared to talk about a shared-sacrifice package** if, and only if, it protects jobs and sites in a binding way. That can include:
- temporary wage restraint or the forgoing of certain bonus elements,
- working-time flexibility,
- internal redeployment,
- qualification and retraining,
- productivity measures,
- capacity reductions through negotiated and socially responsible instruments.

But there are hard limits:
- **No compulsory redundancies**
- **No plant closures**
- **No dismantling of the collective wage framework**
- **No open-ended insecurity for the workforce**

If management wants contributions from employees, then management and owners must also contribute visibly and measurably. That means:
- credible investment commitments,
- a clear product and technology roadmap for the German sites,
- burden-sharing at the top,
- and a binding job-security framework, not vague assurances.

So my position is straightforward: **we are ready for difficult negotiations, but only on the basis of fairness and reciprocity**. If the company wants the workforce to help stabilize the future, it must stop treating German jobs and sites as disposable. If that is the direction, we can work. If not, the conflict will escalate.
