I hear the core concern from the workforce side and from the state: no one wants a value-destroying hammer blow that permanently weakens the company’s German footprint. That concern is legitimate.

But I also need to be equally clear: **a restructuring that leaves the cost base fundamentally untouched is not an option**. If the company cannot restore competitiveness and margins, then the long-term outcome is not “protecting jobs” but steadily eroding the industrial base that supports them.

Where I agree:
- Management is right that this is a structural problem, not a temporary hiccup.
- The workforce is right that any solution must be durable and not just a short-lived cut.
- The state is right that the company is a major regional anchor and that reckless action would be damaging.
- There is value in negotiated solutions that preserve industrial peace and protect the investment case.

Where I disagree:
- I do **not** accept that plant closures are off the table in principle.
- I do **not** accept that every existing site and every current structure must be protected regardless of economics.
- I do **not** accept a settlement that preserves all legacy guarantees while expecting owners to fund the gap indefinitely.

The key issue is this: **if there is genuine overcapacity, it must come out somewhere**. Pretending otherwise just delays the adjustment and makes the eventual correction harsher. The family holding cannot support a plan that protects every site as-is while margins continue to lag and investment capacity is squeezed.

That said, I am not asking for a scorched-earth approach. I would support a path that:
- delivers real, durable savings,
- avoids unnecessary instability,
- prioritizes negotiated instruments over forced ones where they actually work,
- and ties workforce contributions to binding commitments on investment, product allocation, and site development.

But the package must be credible. That means:
- meaningful capacity reduction, not just marginal optimization,
- labor-cost adjustment that is real, not symbolic,
- productivity improvements that are measurable,
- and a clear line of sight to competitiveness.

On the governance side, I want to be explicit: **any solution that threatens the long-term value of the enterprise or undermines the owners’ control structure is unacceptable**. I am not interested in a process that uses crisis pressure to weaken governance or turn the company into a permanently subsidized compromise machine. The family holding will back responsible restructuring, not value erosion.

So my position is:
- **Yes** to a durable turnaround plan.
- **Yes** to negotiation and burden-sharing.
- **Yes** to avoiding instability where possible.
- **No** to a settlement that rules out structurally necessary measures in advance.
- **No** to preserving the status quo under the label of “social responsibility.”

If management can bring forward a package that restores margins credibly and preserves labour peace, I am prepared to support it through governance. If not, I will not support a watered-down agreement that simply postpones the crisis.
