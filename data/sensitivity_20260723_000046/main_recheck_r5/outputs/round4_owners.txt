I will be direct.

## My overall position
I support a restructuring that **materially restores competitiveness and margins**. I do **not** support a cosmetic settlement that preserves the current cost structure and merely postpones the problem. At the same time, I do **not** want a value-destructive confrontation that damages production, reputation, and long-term industrial capability.

So my standard is simple:

- **real savings**
- **real enforceability**
- **real labour peace**
- **no permanent impairment of enterprise value**
- **no weakening of governance control**

## Where I agree

### With management
You are right that this is structural, not cyclical. And the expert’s numbers are sobering. If the required turnaround is in the range of roughly **€1bn recurring annual savings**, then a symbolic package will not do.

### With investors
You are right that the package must be quantified and enforceable. “Goodwill” is not a restructuring plan.

### With the state and employee side
You are also right that prolonged conflict would be damaging. A breakdown into strikes, public escalation, and political trench warfare would destroy value for everyone.

## Where I disagree

### With the Works Council and union
Your absolute **“no closures, no compulsory redundancies, no review sites, no trigger clauses”** line is not a restructuring framework. It is a veto on meaningful adjustment.

I respect the intent to protect jobs and sites. But I do **not** accept that every German site must remain structurally unchanged regardless of economics. If some plants are weak, underloaded, or misaligned with future product logic, then pretending otherwise is not stewardship — it is denial.

Also, a flat rejection of any conditional framework is not credible. If the company gives up flexibility, it must receive something in return: measurable savings, durable contribution, and enforceable delivery. Otherwise the owners are being asked to underwrite stagnation.

### With the state
I understand the political responsibility. But I do **not** accept a position that effectively rules out all serious footprint adjustment before the numbers are even tested. The state is right to reject reckless destruction; it is not right to freeze the industrial map in place.

### With management
I also do **not** accept vague talk of “indispensable” closures without a hard savings bridge. If you want major structural tools, you need to show precisely:
- what the savings target is,
- where the savings come from,
- what capacity is removed,
- and why less damaging measures do not suffice.

You have not yet earned a blank cheque.

## What the expert changed for me
The expert’s assessment matters. A credible turnaround likely needs an annual structural improvement on the order of **€1bn or more**. That makes it clear that:

- a purely voluntary, no-footprint-change solution is probably **too weak** unless the labour contribution is unusually deep;
- but it does **not** automatically prove that plant closures are the only route;
- a **differentiated site strategy** is still the most plausible middle ground if it is economically real.

That is the key point.

## My view on the compromise proposals

### 1) Two-stage settlement with conditional protection
I could consider a **time-limited phase 1** only if it is tied to hard economic delivery and a credible review. But I share the Works Council’s concern: if the trigger structure is just a disguised closure option, then it is not stable.

So: yes to sequencing, no to empty promises. Phase 1 must deliver real savings immediately. Phase 2 must not be an open-ended threat. It must be a tightly defined escalation mechanism.

### 2) Differentiated site strategy
This is the only realistic route that I see.

Not because I enjoy ranking sites — I don’t — but because a blanket “all plants equally protected” approach is not economically credible, and a blanket closure approach is not socially or politically sustainable.

So if there is a workable path, it is this:
- protect and invest in the strongest sites,
- convert or specialize weaker sites,
- reduce capacity where economics demand it,
- maximize voluntary instruments and redeployment,
- avoid forced redundancies if possible,
- but do not rule out footprint change in principle.

### 3) Burden-sharing package
Yes, but only if it is real.

I want to see burden-sharing from:
- labour,
- management,
- owners,
- and the footprint itself.

If the entire burden falls on workers, the deal will be unstable and unjust. If the entire burden falls on owners through capital support without operational change, the deal will be too weak. It must be balanced and durable.

## What I require next
I am not prepared to endorse any final settlement until I see a **management-side savings bridge** with site-level detail. Specifically:

1. **annual savings target**
2. **site-by-site capacity and utilization**
3. **labour contribution by instrument**
4. **management/owner contribution**
5. **product and investment commitments**
6. **what happens if milestones are missed**

Without that, everyone is arguing in abstractions.

## My minimum acceptable direction
I would support a package only if it includes:

- a quantified structural savings target,
- durable labour-cost and productivity contributions,
- binding investment and product allocation for German sites,
- differentiated industrial treatment by site,
- a credible enforcement mechanism,
- and protection against a destructive strike spiral.

## My red lines
I will not support:
- a cosmetic peace that leaves the business uncompetitive,
- a package that permanently weakens governance control,
- a solution that triggers prolonged conflict and value erosion,
- or a settlement that pretends all sites can remain unchanged forever.

And I will also not support:
- a blunt closure-first program without hard numbers,
- or a strategy that uses closures as an ideological first choice rather than a last-resort industrial necessity.

## Bottom line
The family holding is prepared to back a serious restructuring. But seriousness means numbers, not slogans.

So my position is:

- **Yes** to a durable competitiveness reset
- **Yes** to burden-sharing
- **Yes** to differentiated site solutions if justified
- **No** to absolute vetoes on restructuring tools
- **No** to closure-first rhetoric without proof
- **No** to any outcome that permanently impairs enterprise value or governance control
