We are listening closely, and I want to be just as direct as the other parties.

From the perspective of dispersed capital-market shareholders, the only acceptable outcome is one that **credibly restores competitive margins and lowers the cost base on a durable basis**. That is the benchmark. Anything that preserves an uncompetitive structure and merely postpones the adjustment is value-destructive.

Where I agree:
- Management is right that this is a **structural** problem, not a temporary one.
- The family holding is right that **overcapacity has to come out somewhere**.
- The state and workforce are right that a reckless, purely destructive approach would be bad for long-term value if it creates prolonged conflict, operational disruption, or destroys the production base.
- I also agree that a cosmetic settlement is useless. We need something durable.

Where I disagree:
- I do **not** support a settlement that protects every site, every guarantee, and every legacy cost if that leaves the company uncompetitive.
- I do **not** support a package that trades away real restructuring for political comfort.
- I do **not** support “socially responsible” language if it means the cost base remains structurally above competitors.
- I do **not** support prolonged confrontation either, because that destroys margin, cash flow, and investor confidence.

So my position is this:

1. **The end state must be measurable competitiveness**
   - A clear path back to target margins.
   - A durable reduction in fixed labor and site costs.
   - A capacity footprint that matches demand.
   - No ambiguity about when savings arrive.

2. **Substance matters more than symbols**
   - If plant closures are needed to achieve the savings, then they cannot be ruled out in advance.
   - If alternative measures can deliver the same savings with equal certainty, I would support them.
   - But I will not back a package that avoids hard decisions while claiming victory.

3. **Labour contribution must be real**
   - Working-time flexibility, productivity gains, and wage restraint can be part of the answer.
   - But they must be material enough to matter.
   - A marginal or temporary contribution will not fix the problem.

4. **Deal certainty matters**
   - The market will not reward endless brinkmanship.
   - If this turns into a drawn-out strike cycle or a half-baked compromise, capital will discount the company accordingly.
   - We need an agreement that is enforceable and credible.

On the employee side: I understand the refusal of compulsory redundancies and the concern about closures. But those red lines are only defensible if the alternative still delivers the economics. If they do not, then they are not a solution; they are a demand that shareholders absorb ongoing underperformance. That is unacceptable.

On management: you need to show that this is not just a threat tactic. If you want investor support, bring a restructuring that is concrete, quantified, and capable of restoring returns. “Capacity adjustment” is not enough as a phrase; it must translate into real savings and a credible implementation timeline.

On the state and family owners: we understand the governance reality, but that does not change the economics. Blocking a necessary restructuring in the name of local politics would eventually damage all shareholders, including the controlling holders. The company’s long-term value comes first.

So our line is straightforward:
- **Yes** to a durable restructuring with real savings.
- **Yes** to negotiated solutions if they are economically equivalent.
- **No** to a cosmetic compromise.
- **No** to preserving an uncompetitive cost base.
- **No** to indefinite delay.

If the emerging package credibly restores competitiveness, we can support it. If it does not, we will continue to press for stronger measures through capital-market channels.
