I’ll be equally direct.

## To the Works Council and Union
I hear your insistence on:
- no plant closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- no weakening of co-determination,
- and no hidden closure roadmap.

I **understand** why you are drawing that line. But I do **not** accept it as the basis of a viable restructuring.

You keep saying closures and forced layoffs are a choice, not a necessity. That is only true if you can show a **quantitatively sufficient alternative**. So far, you have not. You have listed tools:
- working-time flexibility,
- redeployment,
- retraining,
- productivity improvements,
- voluntary exits,
- temporary restraint.

I agree those belong in the package. But you are still not answering the hard question:

**How does that close a structural cost and capacity gap large enough to restore competitiveness by 2030?**

If your answer is “by guaranteeing every plant and every job,” then you are not solving the problem. You are freezing it.

And let me be clear: a “no closures” line is not credible if it merely shifts the burden into:
- longer working time,
- lower pay,
- weaker margins,
- and delayed investment.

That is not job protection in a durable sense. That is postponing the pain until the company is weaker.

## To the state
I appreciate your emphasis on the industrial base and regional stability. That is legitimate.

But I reject the implication that a serious restructuring is inherently a threat to the region. A company that cannot compete is a worse threat than a company that adjusts its footprint. If we preserve every structure untouched, we may preserve headlines — not competitiveness.

You are right to demand:
- binding investment,
- product commitments,
- and respect for co-determination.

But you are wrong if you think that means a blanket prohibition on site changes. The state cannot demand industrial resilience and simultaneously veto the measures that create it.

## To the family holding
You are right on one point: this is a structural competitiveness problem, not a short-term dip. I agree.

Where I disagree is your tendency to treat “no closures” as automatically unrealistic and then stop there. I am not arguing for closures as an end in themselves. I am arguing for **durable savings**. If you can show a package that delivers them without closures, I will listen.

But I need more than assurances. I need a package that is:
- measurable,
- enforceable,
- and large enough to change the economics.

If that does not materialize, then pretending the footprint can remain unchanged is irresponsible.

## To investors
You are right to demand numbers and enforceability. I agree with that.

But I also need to be blunt: a deal that leaves the cost base structurally above target is **not** a value-preserving compromise. It is value erosion with better optics. So I will not accept a cosmetic peace either.

## Where I agree with the expert
The expert was right on one crucial point: a pure no-closure / no-forced-layoff solution only works if the savings gap is moderate or there is substantial hidden flexibility. That is exactly why I am pressing for a differentiated solution instead of a blanket freeze.

## What I am actually asking for
I am not asking for destruction. I am asking for a **credible, differentiated restructuring** that does all of the following:

1. **Cuts overcapacity materially**
2. **Reduces fixed labor cost durably**
3. **Protects the strongest German sites**
4. **Converts or downsizes weaker sites where needed**
5. **Avoids forced redundancies where possible**
6. **Links employee contributions to management and owner contributions**
7. **Frees funds for product and technology investment to 2030**

That is the only responsible path.

## On the facilitator’s proposals
### 1) Two-stage settlement with conditional protection
I can support the *idea* of sequencing, but not as a fig leaf for permanent blockage.

If we do phases, phase 1 must already contain real structural savings. Otherwise phase 2 becomes endless negotiation. And I will not accept a design where every difficult question is simply deferred.

### 2) Differentiated site strategy with protected core and conversion track
This is the only realistic route that I see.

But let me be precise: differentiation must be **based on industrial logic**, not politics. Sites with strong product relevance, scale, and competitiveness should be protected and invested in. Weaker sites must face conversion, specialization, or reduction. That is not divide-and-rule. That is industrial reality.

A blanket “all sites equal, all sites open, no questions asked” approach is not a plan. It is denial.

### 3) Burden-sharing package with fixed contribution shares
Yes — but only if the shares are meaningful.

That means:
- workers contribute through flexibility, productivity, and labor-cost moderation,
- management contributes through restraint and operational simplification,
- owners contribute through capital discipline and visible support,
- and the footprint contributes through real capacity adjustment.

If the employee contribution is the only real one, then the package is not balanced. If footprint adjustment is forbidden entirely, then the economics are not balanced.

## What I need to see next
If you want to avoid closures and forced layoffs, bring a **quantified alternative** that answers these points:

- annual savings target,
- capacity reduction by site,
- labor-cost contribution,
- voluntary exit volume,
- redeployment capacity,
- investment commitments,
- and what happens if the target is missed.

Without that, “no closures” is not a solution — it is a demand.

## My line
So let me be very clear:

- I do **not** accept absolute vetoes on restructuring tools.
- I do **not** accept a package that leaves the company uncompetitive.
- I do **not** accept a cosmetic deal that protects appearances and weakens the business.
- I do **not** accept that every German plant can remain unchanged through 2030 regardless of economics.

What I **will** accept is a serious, durable package that materially cuts cost and capacity, preserves as much German industrial strength as is economically justified, and creates room to invest in the future.

If that means differentiated site decisions, then that is what we need. If that means no closures anywhere can be proven compatible with the economics, then show me the proof. But until then, I will not retreat from the need for structural change.
