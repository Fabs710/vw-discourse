I will be direct.

I agree with the basic diagnosis from management, investors, and even the employee side: this is a **structural competitiveness problem**, not a temporary dip. I also agree that a cosmetic deal would be damaging, and that prolonged conflict would destroy value. Those points are not in dispute.

Where I disagree is with the attempt by the Works Council and union to make **“no closures”** and **“no compulsory redundancies”** absolute starting conditions. That is not a restructuring framework; it is a veto on structural adjustment. If those are untouchable, then someone else must explain how the company achieves a durable cost and capacity reset.

## To the Works Council
I respect the insistence on social stability, but I do **not** accept your claim that the German footprint must remain unchanged and protected through 2030 regardless of economics.

You say the burden of proof is on management. Partly true. But you are also avoiding the key question: **what is your quantified alternative?**  
If closures and redundancies are off the table, then the alternative must be large enough, durable enough, and fast enough to close the gap. So far I hear principles, not a number.

I also disagree with your rejection of trigger-based frameworks. A conditional protection arrangement is not a “trap” by definition. It is a way to buy certainty now in exchange for measurable delivery. If targets are missed, there must be fallback measures. Otherwise the company is being asked to accept permanent protection without permanent economics.

## To the Union
I agree with you more than with the Works Council on process: you are at least talking about a package, not just a refusal. But I still do **not** see a labor-side package that can replace deeper restructuring tools on its own.

Temporary restraint, bonus moderation, working-time flexibility, retraining, redeployment — all useful, all necessary, but not obviously sufficient. If the labor contribution is too small relative to the structural gap, then it is not a solution. It is delay.

I also reject your assumption that management and owners can simply “contribute visibly” in a way that substitutes for real capacity adjustment. Governance contribution does not magically fix factory economics.

## To Management
I agree with your core point: the business case must be credible, not rhetorical. But I am not yet persuaded that closures are the only economically necessary instrument. If you want the family holding to support deeper restructuring, then bring me a **quantified savings bridge**.

I want to see:
- required annual fixed-cost reduction,
- capacity reduction by site,
- timing of savings,
- share of savings from labor measures,
- share from footprint adjustment,
- and what happens if targets are missed.

Without that, I will not endorse a blunt closure-first approach. But equally, I will not support a “protect everything” outcome that fails the economics.

## To the State
I understand your regional responsibility. I also agree that the industrial base matters. But I do **not** accept the implication that any meaningful restructuring is a threat to the region by definition.

A company that stays uncompetitive is the larger threat. If the package preserves jobs today but weakens margins and investment capacity, then the region loses later. So your position cannot simply be “no closures, no layoffs.” It must also answer how the company gets back to strength.

That said, I do agree with you on one important point: any solution must respect co-determination and avoid a value-destructive showdown. I share that concern.

## To Investors
I agree with your demand for durability and enforceability. The capital markets are right to reject a cosmetic compromise.

But I would caution against assuming that the fastest-looking cut is always the best-value cut. A forced, destabilizing restructuring can destroy more value than it saves if it triggers prolonged conflict, reputational damage, and industrial disruption. That is why I want hard numbers, not ideology.

## What I would support
I would support a package with the following features:

1. **Material, durable savings**
   - large enough to restore competitiveness and margins.

2. **Negotiated workforce contribution**
   - flexibility, redeployment, retraining, productivity gains, and wage restraint where appropriate.

3. **Targeted capacity reduction**
   - not necessarily across-the-board, but real and enforceable.

4. **Binding investment and product commitments**
   - future models and capital allocation tied to Germany.

5. **Top-side burden sharing**
   - management restraint and visible owner support.

6. **A clear enforcement mechanism**
   - milestones, review points, and pre-agreed consequences if delivery fails.

## What I will not support
I will not support:
- a deal that preserves every structure and pretends that is transformation,
- a deal that leaves the cost base structurally above target,
- a deal that destroys enterprise value through drawn-out conflict,
- or a deal that weakens ownership control or governance rights.

And to be equally clear: I also will not support an unnecessarily brutal closure program if an economically equivalent negotiated path exists. The standard is not “close plants at all costs.” The standard is **restore competitiveness durably**.

## My practical position
So the next step is not more slogans. It is numbers.

I want a **quantified alternative package** from management, with clear response from labor:
- how much savings,
- by when,
- through which instruments,
- and what binding protections are exchanged.

If the employee side truly believes closures and redundancies can be avoided, then show the economics. If management truly believes they cannot, show the gap.

That is where I stand:
- open to a serious compromise,
- not open to a cosmetic peace,
- not open to value destruction,
- and not open to permanent impairment of the enterprise or governance control.
