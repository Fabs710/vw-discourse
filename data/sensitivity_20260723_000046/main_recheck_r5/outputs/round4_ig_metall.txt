I will be equally direct.

## To management, the family holding, and investors
You are still trying to normalize **closures and compulsory redundancies** as if they were the obvious default. They are not. They are the **most damaging** instruments, and you have still not demonstrated that they are unavoidable.

You keep saying “quantified savings,” “durability,” and “structural reset.” Fine. But numbers do not justify any instrument by themselves. If you want the workforce to accept sacrifice, then show why you have already exhausted:
- product allocation,
- internal redeployment,
- working-time changes,
- voluntary exits,
- qualification,
- bonus restraint,
- and management/owner contributions.

Until then, closures and forced layoffs remain **a choice**, not a necessity.

## To the Works Council
I agree with your core red lines:
- no plant closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- no weakening of co-determination,
- no treatment of workers as the adjustment reserve.

That is the correct line. Where I would sharpen it is this: we should not let the debate be turned into a “site-ranking” contest or a hidden closure roadmap. A settlement that protects only the “best” sites while leaving others exposed is not acceptable. It would fracture solidarity and create permanent insecurity.

## To the state
Your concern for the industrial base is right, and your support for job and site protection is important. But I want to be very clear: the state should not allow a “two-stage” or “review site” structure if that is just a delayed closure mechanism. That is not stability. That is deferred damage.

If you use your blocking position, use it to prevent:
- plant closures,
- forced redundancies,
- and a restructuring that shifts the burden onto the workforce and the region.

## To the family holding
You say a no-closure stance is a veto on restructuring. I disagree. It is a veto on **destructive restructuring**. There is a difference.

If the ownership side wants credibility, it must be prepared to contribute visibly:
- capital support,
- investment commitments,
- management restraint,
- and an explicit acceptance that workers will not be made the sole shock absorber.

So far, I do not see that burden-sharing seriousness. I see pressure on labor to give up certainty while capital retains all the options.

## To investors
You are right about one thing: a cosmetic deal is worthless. But you are wrong if you think the only alternative to cosmetic peace is closures and forced layoffs.

A real settlement can be economically credible **without** making jobs and sites disposable. It requires hard commitments from all sides, not just from the workforce.

## On the facilitator proposals
### 1) Two-stage settlement with conditional protection
I do **not** support this as currently framed.

A “no closures in phase 1” promise with a later review that can reopen closures is not job security. It is a trapdoor. Workers know that once those fallback clauses exist, pressure will be applied until management gets the result it wants.

If there is any staged approach, the protection must become **more** binding over time, not less.

### 2) Differentiated site strategy with protected core and conversion track
I also reject this.

Once you create categories like “review sites” or “conversion sites,” you have already started the closure process politically. The company’s German footprint is an integrated system. Plant-by-plant ranking is a divide-and-rule tactic dressed up as industrial realism.

### 3) Burden-sharing package with fixed contribution shares
This is the only direction worth discussing — but only if burden-sharing is genuine and **does not** become a pretext for forced reductions.

That means:
- labor contribution,
- management contribution,
- owner contribution,
- and binding job and site guarantees.

Not “workers first, then maybe protection later.”

## What we are prepared to discuss
We are not refusing adjustment. We are refusing to let adjustment be paid for by the workforce alone.

We can discuss:
- temporary working-time flexibility,
- redeployment across German sites,
- retraining and qualification,
- productivity improvements,
- moderation of variable pay,
- voluntary exit instruments,
- and a fair, limited labor-cost contribution.

But only inside a framework with these non-negotiables:
1. **Every German plant remains open**
2. **No compulsory redundancies**
3. **No hidden closure pathway**
4. **No weakening of co-determination**
5. **Binding job and site security through 2030**
6. **Concrete investment and product commitments**

## What we will not accept
I want to say this plainly, because the discussion keeps drifting back toward it:

- no closure logic,
- no forced layoff logic,
- no “last resort” trapdoor,
- no plant-ranking system,
- no open-ended insecurity,
- no settlement where the workforce sacrifices and ownership merely observes.

If the package still depends on any of that, then it is not a compromise. It is a restructuring offensive against the workforce.

## On the “savings gap”
You keep insisting that the gap must be closed. I agree. But you still have not shown that the gap can only be closed by attacking sites and jobs.

The burden of proof is on management and owners to demonstrate that:
- labor measures,
- product allocation,
- internal redeployment,
- and top-side contributions

are insufficient **before** they invoke closures or redundancies. That has not been proven here.

## My position
So my position remains:

- **No plant closures**
- **No compulsory redundancies**
- **Secure German sites**
- **Secure collective bargaining standards**
- **Fair burden-sharing**
- **Management and owners must contribute visibly**
- **Workers will contribute too, but not as the sole adjustment reserve**

If you want a serious settlement, bring a package that respects those boundaries. If you bring another closure-and-layoff scenario, then you should not expect us to pretend that it is a compromise.
