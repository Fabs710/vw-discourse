We are prepared to be blunt.

We agree with management, the family holding, and to an extent the state on the core diagnosis: this is a **structural competitiveness problem**. A cosmetic settlement, or a package that preserves an uncompetitive cost base, would be value-destructive. Prolonged conflict would also be value-destructive. So the goal is not “peace at any price”; it is **credible, durable margin restoration**.

Where we disagree is with the employee side’s attempt to make **no closures** and **no compulsory redundancies** into hard taboos before the economics are demonstrated. That position may be understandable politically, but it is not sufficient for shareholders if it blocks a restructuring that actually restores returns.

### What we need to see
For capital markets, the test is straightforward:

1. **Material, durable savings**
   - not just temporary restraint;
   - not just soft promises;
   - not just productivity language without quantified impact.

2. **A clear capacity adjustment**
   - If plant closures are needed to get there, they cannot be ruled out in advance.
   - If alternatives can deliver the same economics, fine — but show the numbers.

3. **Timing and certainty**
   - Savings must arrive on a defined schedule.
   - A slow, uncertain adjustment will keep margins and valuation under pressure.

4. **Labor contribution that is real**
   - Working-time flexibility, wage restraint, redeployment, and productivity gains can all help.
   - But they must be large enough to materially change the cost base.

5. **No open-ended conflict**
   - A strike cycle or drawn-out standoff would destroy cash flow and investor confidence.
   - We will not support a “deal” that simply defers the fight.

### Where we stand on the current positions
- **Works Council / Union:** We respect the desire to avoid unnecessary damage, but a blanket ban on closures and compulsory redundancies is not acceptable unless you can match it with equally strong, quantified savings. So far, you have not done that.
- **Management:** You are right that the problem is structural, but you still need to prove that your chosen instruments are economically necessary and not just the most confrontational option. Bring hard numbers, not broad claims.
- **State:** We understand the regional and political constraints, but blocking necessary restructuring in the name of local protection would eventually damage enterprise value, including for minority and anchor shareholders.
- **Family holding:** We agree that overcapacity must come out somewhere. But “somewhere” must still be translated into a credible, enforceable package, not just a principled insistence on adjustment.

### Our position
We could support:
- a restructuring with real capacity reduction,
- a meaningful labor-cost contribution,
- binding investment and product commitments,
- and a timeline that credibly restores margins.

We will not support:
- a cosmetic compromise,
- a settlement that leaves the cost base structurally above competitors,
- or a deal that avoids hard decisions and expects shareholders to absorb the shortfall.

### Bottom line
If the emerging package can be shown to restore competitive margins on a durable basis, we can back it. If it cannot, then we will continue to press for stronger measures through analyst scrutiny, capital allocation, and market pressure.

If nobody can present a quantified alternative that closes the gap **without** weakening competitiveness, then the shareholder case is with management on deeper restructuring.
