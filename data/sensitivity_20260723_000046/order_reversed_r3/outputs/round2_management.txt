Let me respond plainly, because we are now at the point where ambiguity becomes avoidance.

## To institutional investors
I agree with you on the essentials:
- this must be **durable**,
- it must be **quantified**,
- and it must restore a **competitive cost base**.

You are right that a cosmetic settlement would simply defer the problem and damage credibility. That is exactly why management is insisting on structural measures, not temporary patches.

Where I disagree is your implied comfort with any outcome as long as it is “economically equivalent” in the abstract. We do **not** need a theoretical model; we need an implementable agreement in a live industrial setting. If the package cannot actually be delivered, it is worthless, no matter how elegant the spreadsheet looks.

So the test is not whether the measures are called closures or not. The test is:
- do they **remove enough capacity**,
- do they create a **real labour-cost contribution**,
- and do they do so **quickly and reliably** enough to restore margins?

If the answer is no, then it is not a restructuring. It is delay.

## To the controlling family holding
I agree with your emphasis on long-term value and on avoiding a value-destroying confrontation. That is precisely why we cannot preserve an uncompetitive structure out of caution.

Where I disagree is your repeated suggestion that closures are merely one option among many and that non-closure alternatives should be assumed equivalent unless proven otherwise. The burden of proof is the other way around. We already know the current structure is too expensive. We already know the market is shrinking and competition is intensifying. We already know the company’s return-on-sales target is at risk.

So if someone proposes to avoid closures, they must show:
- the **same scale** of savings,
- the **same durability**,
- and the **same certainty of implementation**.

That means not just promises, but binding, enforceable measures. Otherwise we are asking investors and owners to finance a continued cost disadvantage.

## To the regional state government
I understand your political constraints. I also understand the regional importance of the company. But I need to be direct: if you rule out closures as a matter of principle, then you are not engaging with the reality of the economics.

You say capacity reduction does not automatically mean closures. Correct. But it does mean that capacity must come out somewhere, and the company cannot keep every site fully intact while also solving the cost problem. That is not how overcapacity is eliminated.

I am prepared to discuss alternatives to plant closure **if** they deliver equivalent structural relief. But I am not prepared to accept a package that:
- preserves too much capacity,
- leaves labour costs too high,
- and then claims success because no site was formally closed.

That would be a political compromise, not an industrial one.

## To the works council and the union
I need to address your position directly, because this is where the gap remains widest.

You both say the status quo is over, and I agree. But then you draw red lines that would make meaningful restructuring impossible:
- no plant closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- binding site guarantees through 2030,
- and limits on how far the company can adjust its workforce.

I have to be honest: if those lines remain fixed, then the savings will not be sufficient. The company cannot restore competitiveness while keeping the full legacy structure intact.

I also reject the framing that management is trying to shift the burden entirely onto employees. That is not what is on the table. What is on the table is a package that must include:
- a **material employee contribution**,
- **capacity adjustment**,
- and **lower structural costs**.

We are not asking for “pain for its own sake.” We are asking for a company that can survive to 2030 and invest in future products. If we fail now, everyone loses more later.

## On job security
Let me be very clear: I am not indifferent to employment. But I cannot accept job security as an absolute that overrides industrial viability. A guarantee that protects all current structures regardless of economics is not a solution; it is a freeze.

If you want job protection, the company must first remain competitive enough to fund it. That is the sequence.

## On strikes
The prospect of industrial action does not change the underlying arithmetic. A prolonged strike would hurt everyone — yes — but it would hurt the company’s ability to invest and recover too. I do not want that outcome, but I also will not accept strike pressure as a substitute for a real solution.

## Where management stands
Our position remains:
1. **Substantial and durable cost reduction is non-negotiable.**
2. **Capacity must be reduced.**
3. **Labour costs must come down structurally.**
4. **The result must restore competitiveness and funding capacity for future products and technology.**

We are open on the instrument mix. We are not open on the outcome.

## What would move us
If the employee side and the state want to avoid closures, then bring something concrete:
- quantified savings that close the cost gap,
- enforceable capacity reduction,
- a genuine labour-cost contribution,
- and a timetable that restores competitiveness quickly.

If you can produce that, we will engage seriously. If you cannot, then we should stop pretending that a non-closure solution is automatically viable.

So let me put the question back to the other side:

**What exactly is the credible, enforceable package that removes enough capacity and cost to restore the brand to target — without plant closures and without compulsory redundancies?**

If that answer is missing, then the conversation has not yet produced an alternative.
