Let me be equally direct.

I agree with everyone on one point: **the status quo is not sustainable**. The company needs a real restructuring, and it must be durable. We are not defending inefficiency.

But I disagree sharply with the idea that “real restructuring” must mean **plant closures and forced layoffs**. That is a line we will not cross. It is not a bargaining chip; it is a red line.

### To management
You are trying to frame closures as an inevitable technical necessity. From the workforce side, that looks like a decision to shift the burden onto employees after years of investment and years of broken assurances.

What we will not accept:
- **any plant closure in Germany**,
- **compulsory redundancies**,
- and the dismantling of the historic job-security framework as the price of your restructuring.

If you come to us with a fait accompli, you should expect resistance, not consent. We have already shown that we can mobilize the workforce, and we will do so again if needed.

If you want a serious settlement, then bring a package that includes:
- a **binding site and employment guarantee through 2030**,
- **no forced layoffs**,
- capacity adjustment through socially acceptable instruments,
- and a genuine employee contribution that is matched by commitments from management and owners.

Anything less is not a negotiation; it is an ultimatum.

### To institutional investors
You want durable margin improvement. I understand that. But you are making the same mistake management is making if you think margin recovery can be built on destroying trust, breaking co-determination, and hollowing out the German industrial base.

A package that provokes prolonged strikes, destroys industrial peace, and undermines the workforce is not “credible” just because it is harsh. It is risky, and it may be self-defeating.

If you want sustainable competitiveness, then you need:
- a stable workforce,
- predictable labour relations,
- and investment in the future of the sites.

You should not support a solution that looks good on a spreadsheet but blows up implementation.

### To the controlling family holding
You speak of long-term value. Then protect the long-term value of the company by protecting the core productive base and the social legitimacy of the brand.

If the family side backs closures, it will be backing a confrontation that will leave deep scars. That is not stewardship; it is escalation.

If you want a durable solution, support:
- a negotiated contribution from labour,
- management contribution,
- investment commitments,
- and binding security for jobs and sites.

### To the regional state government
I appreciate your position more than the others, because you understand what is at stake regionally. But let me be clear: “preserve sites as far as possible” is not enough. We need an explicit commitment that **German plants remain open** and that there are **no forced job cuts**.

Regional stability cannot be traded away for short-term financial optics. If the company wants public legitimacy, it has to deliver that through secure industrial futures, not through closures and layoffs.

### Where we stand
Our position is simple:
- **No plant closures**
- **No compulsory redundancies**
- **Binding job and site security to 2030**
- **Negotiated, socially fair adjustments instead of unilateral cuts**
- **Real co-determination in shaping the restructuring**

We are prepared to discuss productivity measures, working-time changes, and a fair employee contribution. But those discussions only make sense inside a framework that protects the workforce and the sites.

If that framework is not accepted, then the conflict will continue — and we will not be the ones responsible for that escalation.
