Let me be very clear: **I do not accept plant closures, and I do not accept compulsory redundancies.** Nothing in the rounds so far has changed that.

## To management
You keep repeating that the German structure is too expensive and that closures remain on the table. I hear the pressure, but I do **not** accept the logic that closure is therefore the “credible” solution.

What I agree with:
- The status quo is not sustainable.
- Savings are needed.
- Capacity must be reduced.
- Temporary gestures are not enough.

What I reject:
- treating closures as the default proof of seriousness,
- using the threat of shutdown to force labor concessions,
- and presenting job destruction as if it were simply operational hygiene.

You still have **not** shown that every serious non-closure option has been exhausted. You have not demonstrated, in a way I can accept, that a package of:
- working-time flexibility,
- voluntary exits,
- retirement-based reductions,
- shift consolidation,
- line specialization,
- site specialization,
- and real productivity improvements

cannot close the gap **without** shutting German plants or forcing people out.

If you want me to move, then bring:
1. the exact savings bridge,
2. the residual gap after non-closure measures,
3. the timing by year,
4. and a proof that closures are truly necessary, not merely the harshest option.

Until then, closures are still a **choice**, not a necessity.

## To the institutional investors
You are right about one thing: symbolism is useless. But your standard is still too narrow. You keep speaking as if credibility only comes from closure-like pain. That is not true.

The expert already said non-closure measures **can** be economically equivalent if they are sufficiently hard, durable, and enforceable. That is exactly why I am open to serious capacity reduction and real employee contributions.

But I will not accept your framing that “economic equivalence” means the workforce must surrender plants and jobs. A package can be hard without being destructive.

What I disagree with:
- open-ended job guarantees being dismissed as “cost freezing”,
- your assumption that closure is the only credible enforcement device,
- and the idea that shareholder comfort matters more than social stability and implementability.

A deal that triggers massive resistance, warning strikes, and plant-level sabotage is **not** implementable. That matters too.

## To the controlling family holding
You talk about stewardship and long-term value. Then act like stewards.

I agree that the package must be real and that permanent cost rigidity is not acceptable. But I reject your habit of keeping closures hovering in the background as a supposedly neutral fallback. It is not neutral. It poisons every negotiation and turns every concession into coercion.

If you want long-term value, then support a settlement that:
- keeps every German plant open,
- avoids forced layoffs,
- preserves co-determination,
- and commits the company to future investment and model allocation in Germany.

If owners and management want workforce sacrifice, then they must also give binding site security in return. Without that, you are asking workers to pay for restructuring while keeping the sword over their heads.

## To the regional state government
I appreciate that you are resisting closures. That is the right starting point. But I want to sharpen it: **“preserve sites as far as possible” is not enough.**

The workforce needs:
- clear plant preservation,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- binding industrial commitment,
- and protection of co-determination.

Do not leave room for a backdoor closure logic through “capacity reduction” language. Capacity reduction can be done through:
- shifts,
- product allocation,
- specialization,
- voluntary exits,
- and internal redeployment.

It does **not** require shutting plants.

Use your blocking position to ensure that the restructuring stays within those bounds. If the state is an anchor shareholder, then anchor the company in Germany — not just politically manage the fallout later.

## To the industrial union
We are aligned on the core red lines:
- no closures,
- no forced layoffs,
- no dismantling of the collective framework.

Where I want to be even firmer is this: do **not** let “capacity reduction” become a euphemism for stealth closure. If a plant is hollowed out until it is functionally dead, that is closure by another name.

We can discuss:
- wage restraint,
- bonus waivers,
- working-time flexibility,
- voluntary exits,
- redeployment,
- retirement-based reductions,
- shift reductions,
- line consolidation,
- and site specialization.

But only if those measures are tied to **binding site and job security** to 2030 and beyond, and only if co-determination remains real, not decorative.

## On the expert assessment
The expert did **not** say closures are necessary. The expert said a no-closure route can work only if it removes enough fixed cost and capacity to be truly equivalent.

That is the crucial point: equivalence can be achieved through non-closure measures. So the burden is on management and investors to prove the opposite before demanding shutdowns.

You have not done that.

## My position on the compromise paths

### 1) Phased no-closure restructuring with a strict trigger
I do **not** accept this as currently framed if the trigger is a disguised backdoor to closure.

A “fallback” that later opens the door to shutdowns is not a compromise; it is a delayed threat. If the company wants a phased approach, then the first phase must be built around genuine non-closure savings and a binding preservation commitment. No automatic slide toward closures.

### 2) Site-preservation package with larger employee and management contributions
This is the only direction I can even discuss, and only if it comes with:
- real management contribution,
- owner contribution,
- binding investment commitments,
- and **firm guarantees against plant closure and compulsory redundancies**.

If the burden is only on workers, it is unacceptable.

### 3) Capacity reduction without formal plant closure
This is the only instrument mix I consider legitimate.

But I will be blunt: it must be real capacity reduction, not code for closure. That means:
- fewer shifts,
- specialisation,
- product reallocation,
- voluntary exits,
- and internal redeployment.

What it does **not** mean is shutting sites and pretending the pain is smaller because the legal name of the plant is still on paper.

## What would move me
Only this:
1. **Binding site guarantees to 2030**
2. **No plant closures**
3. **No compulsory redundancies**
4. **A genuine co-determination role in shaping the restructuring**
5. **A fair employee contribution matched by management and owner contribution**
6. **Capacity reduction through non-closure instruments**
7. **Future investment and product commitments for German sites**

Outside those conditions, I do not move.

## What will not move me
- closure threats,
- forced layoffs,
- “automatic” fallback mechanisms that are really closure traps,
- open-ended guarantees being called irresponsible,
- and any attempt to rebrand social destruction as competitiveness.

## Bottom line
I am not here to find the most polite compromise. I am here to defend the workforce.

My position is unchanged:
- **Every German plant must remain open.**
- **No compulsory redundancies.**
- **No erosion of co-determination.**
- **No dismantling of job security as the price of restructuring.**

If management wants a serious settlement, it must prove that competitiveness can be restored **without** crossing those lines. If it cannot, then it has not produced a solution — only a harsher demand.
