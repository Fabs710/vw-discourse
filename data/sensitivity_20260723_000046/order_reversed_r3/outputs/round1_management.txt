I’ll be direct.

I agree with one point from everyone so far: **the status quo is not viable**. The German cost base is too high, our capacity is too large for a shrinking European market, and we do not have the luxury of waiting for the problem to solve itself. If we preserve the current structure, we will keep destroying margin and then lose the ability to invest in the future.

But I need to challenge two positions clearly.

### To institutional investors
You are right that we need a **credible, durable return to competitive margins**. That is exactly the objective. Where I agree is on the need for **real structural savings**, not accounting tricks or temporary deferrals.

Where I do not align with you is any assumption that management can achieve this without hard choices. A “credible” package in this situation will require:
- meaningful capacity reduction,
- a genuine labour-cost contribution,
- and a restructuring that can actually be implemented.

If the outcome is merely a softer version of the current cost base, then it will not be credible. That is unacceptable to management and, frankly, to anyone who wants this company to remain investable.

### To the family holding
I appreciate the emphasis on long-term value. We are aligned on the principle that this is about **protecting the core asset**, not staging a symbolic confrontation. But long-term value is not protected by avoiding necessary decisions. If we refuse to address overcapacity head-on, we just postpone the erosion.

I also need to be clear: I am not using closures as theatre. Plant closure is not the only possible instrument, but it is one of the tools on the table because the economics are severe. If an alternative package can deliver the same scale and durability of savings, I will consider it. But it must be equivalent in effect, not merely politically more comfortable.

### To the regional state government
I understand your concern for jobs and regional stability, but I cannot accept the idea that closures are off-limits by definition. If a plant is structurally uncompetitive and perpetuates a cost gap, pretending otherwise is not responsible industrial policy. It is delay.

What I can say is this: we are not pursuing closures as an end in themselves. We are pursuing a **durable reduction in cost and capacity**. If that can be achieved through other enforceable measures, I am open. But if the alternative does not close the structural gap, then it is not sufficient.

### My position
Let me state it plainly:

- **We need a substantial and durable reduction in cost base.**
- **We need capacity to come out of the system.**
- **We need a meaningful employee contribution on labour costs.**
- **We need a result that restores competitiveness through 2030, not just this year.**

I am open on the instrument mix, but not on the outcome. A package that preserves nearly all capacity, protects legacy cost structures, and calls that a solution will not be accepted by management. Nor would it reassure owners, investors, or the market.

### On industrial conflict
I do not want a prolonged strike. It would damage output, reputation, and value. But let me be equally clear: the prospect of disruption cannot be used to freeze necessary restructuring. We are prepared to negotiate seriously, but the negotiation must deliver structural change.

So the next step needs to be concrete:
- quantified savings,
- durable labour-cost measures,
- and capacity adjustments that actually bring the company to a competitive footing.

If those elements are missing, then we do not have a solution — we have postponement.
