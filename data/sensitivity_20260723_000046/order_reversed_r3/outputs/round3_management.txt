I’ll be equally direct.

We have now had enough discussion to separate **preferences** from **economic reality**. The reality is this: the company’s German structure is too expensive, capacity is too high, and the current labor-cost base is not compatible with a return to target margins. That does **not** change because stakeholders dislike closures.

## To the union and works council
I hear your consistent line: no closures, no compulsory redundancies, no dismantling of protections. I understand it politically and socially. But I do **not** accept it as sufficient.

You keep saying a no-closure solution is possible if everyone shares the burden. Fine — then show a package that actually closes the gap. Not in principle. In numbers.

What I have not yet seen from labor is:
- a quantified annual savings contribution,
- a credible reduction in fixed cost and capacity,
- and an implementation path that does not depend on goodwill alone.

Working-time flexibility, voluntary exits, bonus waivers, and productivity pledges may help, but unless they remove enough cost **permanently**, they are not equivalent to the structural step we need. I will not pretend otherwise.

## To the works council
You are right about one thing: “capacity reduction” is not automatically the same as a legal closure. But economically, some sites and lines are overcapacity and must shrink materially. If the effect of “protecting every site” is that every site continues more or less as before, then nothing has been solved.

So let me be clear:
- I am open to **non-closure instruments**,
- but only if they deliver **real, durable capacity and cost reduction**,
- not if they preserve the full legacy footprint under a different label.

That is the point the expert confirmed. Non-closure can work only if it is hard enough to be economically comparable. Right now, nobody has proven that with actual figures.

## To the regional state government
I respect your blocking position and your concern for the region. But I reject the idea that preserving every site is a valid starting point. It is not.

You say closures are not proven necessary. That is incorrect. What is proven is that the current cost base is uncompetitive and that overcapacity must be removed. The open question is whether labor and state can deliver the same economics without closures. If yes, we will consider it. If no, then insisting on no closures does not solve the problem — it simply blocks the remedy.

You also argue that closures are a political choice, not an economic conclusion. That is partly true. But so is the refusal to make any site-level adjustment. There is no economic law that says every plant must survive in its current form.

## To the controlling family holding
We are aligned on the central issue: this must be a real restructuring, not a symbolic one. Where I diverge is your repeated preference for treating closures as an instrument of last resort almost by default.

I am not asking for closures as an ideology. I am saying they remain on the table because, in a shrinking market and a structurally high-cost network, they are one of the few instruments that can remove enough fixed cost quickly and credibly.

If someone wants to avoid that, they must show me:
- the residual savings gap,
- the exact equivalence of the alternative measures,
- and the enforcement mechanism if the alternative under-delivers.

We still do not have that. Until we do, I cannot accept an argument that closures are unnecessary.

## To the institutional investors
You are right to demand durable, quantified, enforceable savings. On that we agree completely.

Where I disagree is your implicit notion that the company can wait for a perfect no-closure package to emerge from negotiations. It cannot. Time is not on our side. The market is shrinking, competition is intensifying, and every month of delay costs margin and credibility.

That is why management must retain the ability to pursue deeper structural measures if the no-closure route fails to close the gap. An off-ramp or fallback is not a “threat”; it is what makes the package credible.

## On the expert assessment
The expert has been useful, because the analysis confirms what management has been saying:
- temporary concessions are not enough,
- attrition alone is not enough,
- wage restraint alone is not enough,
- and a serious no-closure package would need substantial capacity reduction, headcount reduction, and fixed-cost removal.

That brings us right back to the core issue: if the alternative has to be that hard to be equivalent, then let’s stop pretending the difference is mainly about optics.

## My position on the compromise paths

### 1) Phased restructuring with a conditional closure off-ramp
This is the most credible framework, but only if the off-ramp is real.

I would support a structure where:
- non-closure measures are implemented first,
- savings are monitored against hard milestones,
- and if targets are missed, predefined closure or consolidation measures can be activated.

If the fallback is too weak, too political, or easy to block later, then it is worthless. I am not interested in a symbolic “review” that simply postpones the problem.

### 2) Site-preservation package with stronger employee contribution and binding investment commitments
This could work only if the employee contribution is **material**, not cosmetic.

That means:
- a genuine labor-cost reset,
- measurable productivity improvements,
- and binding flexibility commitments.

It also means management and owners cannot ask labor to carry the whole burden. There must be clear company-side commitments to future investment and product allocation. But again: those commitments do not substitute for cost reduction.

### 3) Capacity reduction without formal plant closure
This is acceptable only if it really reduces capacity and fixed cost enough to matter.

If the outcome is:
- fewer shifts,
- line consolidation,
- some site specialization,
- and a meaningful headcount reduction through voluntary means,

then yes, that may be a workable route. But it must be large enough to close the gap. Anything less is delay dressed up as restructuring.

## What would move management
Let me state it plainly.

I would be prepared to forgo closures **if and only if** the employee side, works council, and state can support a package that delivers all of the following:

1. **Quantified recurring savings sufficient to restore the brand’s return-on-sales target**
2. **Meaningful, durable capacity reduction**
3. **A genuine labor-cost contribution**
4. **A credible implementation timetable within the turnaround horizon**
5. **An enforcement mechanism and fallback if targets are missed**
6. **No cosmetic protection that leaves the cost base structurally uncompetitive**

If those conditions are met, we can discuss the instrument mix. If they are not met, then there is no credible alternative to deeper structural measures.

## What will not move management
Let me be just as clear on the limits:
- I will not accept a settlement that preserves the full cost structure.
- I will not accept a “socially balanced” compromise that is economically insufficient.
- I will not accept binding guarantees that lock in uncompetitive capacity through 2030.
- I will not accept a package that makes management responsible for future underperformance because the restructuring was too gentle.

## Final point
Everyone here agrees the status quo is dead. The only question is whether the alternative is **real**.

If the no-closure route can deliver:
- the same structural savings,
- the same fixed-cost reduction,
- and the same margin restoration,

then we can work with it.

If it cannot, then the claim that closures are avoidable is not a solution — it is a constraint imposed on the company while asking management to deliver a turnaround anyway. That is not acceptable.

So my question back to labor and the state is simple:

**What exact, quantified package removes enough cost and capacity to restore competitiveness — and if you cannot show that, why should management pretend closures are off the table?**
