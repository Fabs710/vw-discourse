I appreciate the capital-markets position because it is aligned with the central issue: **this must be a real restructuring, not a postponement**.

That said, I want to be precise about where I agree and where I do not.

### Where I agree
- The company needs **durable, structural savings**.
- The current German cost base is too high.
- A package that merely preserves legacy structures while claiming victory would destroy value.
- We cannot afford a long, open-ended conflict that damages the brand, operations, and market confidence.

### Where I disagree
I do **not** accept the idea that plant closures are the only serious path, or that closures should be treated as the default proof of credibility.

From the controlling shareholder’s perspective, the test is not whether the package is emotionally hard enough; the test is whether it:
1. restores **long-term competitiveness and margins**,
2. is **implementable**,
3. avoids **value-destructive instability**,
4. and does not permanently impair the enterprise.

If an alternative package can deliver the same economics through capacity reduction, working-time changes, labor-cost contribution, productivity measures, and binding commitments, then that is preferable to closures. If it cannot, then I understand management’s position that harder instruments may be necessary. But I will not support a solution that creates a prolonged confrontation simply to demonstrate toughness.

### To the union and works council
Your red lines are clear, but red lines alone do not close the cost gap. If you reject closures and compulsory redundancies outright, then you need to show a package that produces **equivalent savings** in a way that is enforceable and durable. Otherwise, you are asking shareholders to accept a weaker company and call it compromise.

What I can support:
- a negotiated employee contribution,
- site and job protections where they are economically compatible,
- capacity adjustment through socially acceptable means,
- and future investment commitments tied to performance.

What I cannot support:
- a settlement that leaves the company structurally uncompetitive,
- or one that shifts all the burden onto owners and investors while preserving the old cost base.

### To the regional state government
I understand the political and regional stakes. But “preserve sites as far as possible” is only acceptable if it still results in a viable company. The company cannot be managed as a regional employment program if that means permanent underperformance and declining investment capacity.

If closures can be avoided **without** sacrificing competitiveness, fine. If they cannot, then avoiding them at any cost is not stewardship — it is value destruction by another name.

### To management
You need to be more concrete. If you want the governance side to stand behind this, bring:
- the **size of the savings gap**,
- the **exact contribution** expected from labour,
- the **timing** of savings,
- and the **implementation risk** under different scenarios.

That will allow us to judge whether non-closure alternatives are truly equivalent or merely politically easier.

### My bottom line
I am **open to an alternative to closures** only if it is genuinely equivalent in economic effect and durable in implementation.

I am **not** open to:
- cosmetic compromise,
- permanent cost rigidity,
- or a settlement that triggers prolonged industrial conflict and undermines value.

If the next iteration cannot demonstrate credible, quantified, enforceable savings without destabilizing the company, then we should be honest that deeper structural measures remain on the table.
