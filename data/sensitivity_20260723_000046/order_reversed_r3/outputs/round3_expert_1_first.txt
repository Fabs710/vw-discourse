Here is my assessment, strictly from a restructuring / operational-finance / implementation-risk perspective.

## Bottom line

A **non-closure package can be economically equivalent to closures only in a limited sense**:

- It can be equivalent **if** the real savings gap is driven mainly by labor cost, shift utilization, overhead, and capacity under-absorption, and
- if the non-closure measures are **large enough, binding enough, and durable enough** to remove the same annual cost base as a closure would.

But in a large German automotive plant network, **economic equivalence is hard to achieve without some combination of**:
1. substantial headcount reduction,
2. shift / line / model consolidation,
3. fixed-cost footprint reduction,
4. and a credible enforcement mechanism.

If the proposed no-closure package is mostly wage restraint, temporary concessions, or attrition alone, it will usually **not** be equivalent to closure economics. It may buy time, but not structural repair.

---

## What “equivalent” should mean

I would test equivalence on three levels:

### 1) Annual recurring EBITDA / operating-cost effect
A credible alternative must close the same **recurring annual gap**, not just defer it.

### 2) Cash and implementation profile
Savings must arrive fast enough to matter for the turnaround horizon, typically within 12–24 months, and not be back-ended into vague “future productivity.”

### 3) Durability
The measure must survive labor-cycle reversion, management turnover, and political pressure. If it can be reversed without real cost, it is not structurally equivalent.

---

## Why closures are often proposed

Closures are not only about labor cost. They typically remove several cost layers at once:

- direct labor at the site,
- indirect labor and overhead,
- maintenance and site support costs,
- fixed facility cost,
- logistics complexity,
- underutilized capacity,
- and often poor product/location fit.

That means a closure can deliver a **large step-down in fixed cost**, which is difficult to replicate with softer instruments.

So if management is arguing “closure is necessary,” the burden is to show:
- the size of the structural gap,
- how much is fixed vs variable,
- and why non-closure actions cannot remove enough fixed cost.

Without that bridge, the closure case is incomplete.

---

## What non-closure measures can realistically deliver

A no-closure package can be powerful if it is built from several components. The most credible instruments are:

### A) Capacity reduction without formal closure
Examples:
- eliminating a shift,
- line consolidation,
- product transfer to other plants,
- partial specialization,
- lower planned output,
- longer changeover windows,
- removal of redundant processes.

This is often the **closest economic substitute** to closure, because it attacks underutilization and overhead absorption.

### B) Headcount reduction through voluntary mechanisms
Examples:
- retirement programs,
- severance-based exits,
- natural attrition,
- non-replacement,
- internal redeployment.

This can be meaningful, but it is usually too slow and too uncertain if used alone.

### C) Labor-cost relief
Examples:
- pay moderation,
- bonus waivers,
- temporary or multi-year concessions,
- working-time flexibility,
- reduction of allowances.

This helps, but in automotive manufacturing it rarely substitutes fully for a plant shutdown because the labor share is only one part of the problem.

### D) Productivity and organization measures
Examples:
- span-of-control reduction,
- indirect-function cuts,
- procurement and maintenance savings,
- reduction of complexity,
- simplification of variants.

Useful, but these are often incremental, not sufficient on their own.

---

## My view on the three compromise paths

### Option 1: phased restructuring with a conditional closure off-ramp
**Most credible from a restructuring standpoint.**

Why:
- It gives labor-side stakeholders a chance to avoid immediate closures.
- It gives management/investors a real enforcement mechanism.
- It creates a measurable test of whether non-closure actions are sufficient.

Key requirement:
- the fallback must be **pre-agreed, automatic, and legally executable** enough that it is not just another round of bargaining if targets are missed.

Main risk:
- political and co-determination actors may later block the fallback in practice, which destroys credibility.

### Option 2: site-preservation package with stronger employee contribution and investment commitments
**Potentially viable, but only if the employee contribution is material.**

The problem here is that “site preservation” often becomes a promise to preserve the structure while savings are spread thinly across many small measures.

For this to be economically equivalent, the package needs:
- significant fixed-cost reduction,
- hard volume/capacity commitments,
- and a multi-year labor-cost reset.

If the contribution is symbolic, this is not equivalent.

### Option 3: capacity reduction without formal plant closure
**Often the best practical substitute if executed hard enough.**

This can work if it means:
- one or more shifts removed,
- major product lines reassigned,
- shared services stripped out,
- and headcount reduced to the new operating model.

In many cases, this is functionally a closure in economic terms, even if the legal site remains open.

The question is whether the company can truly remove enough cost while maintaining political acceptability. If yes, this is the strongest no-closure path.

---

## Implementation-risk assessment

This is where many plans fail.

### Main risks

#### 1) Savings leakage
Savings promised on paper do not fully land because:
- exceptions are granted,
- overtime rises,
- temporary staff remain,
- workloads are shifted rather than removed,
- or overhead is reintroduced elsewhere.

#### 2) Timing slippage
A large share of savings may come too late to help the P&L and cash profile.

#### 3) Labor reversion risk
Temporary concessions expire; if there is no structural reset, the cost base snaps back.

#### 4) Governance risk
If the package depends on future cooperation from actors who already hold blocking positions, its credibility is weak unless the enforcement design is very strong.

#### 5) Industrial-relations risk
A package that is economically adequate on paper can still fail if it triggers prolonged strike action, low execution quality, or passive resistance.

---

## What I would need to validate equivalence properly

To judge whether non-closure measures are truly equivalent, I would need a quantified bridge with at least the following:

1. **Current cost base by plant/site**
   - direct labor
   - indirect labor
   - overhead
   - depreciation / site costs if relevant
   - logistics and complexity costs

2. **Required savings target**
   - by year
   - recurring annualized amount
   - EBITDA and cash impact

3. **Closure case economics**
   - one-time restructuring costs
   - annual savings
   - payback period
   - operational disruption cost

4. **No-closure case economics**
   - savings by measure
   - timing of each measure
   - permanence / expiration risk
   - implementation probability

5. **Sensitivity cases**
   - strike duration
   - lower-than-expected attrition
   - under-delivery on wage relief
   - lost volume / market deterioration

Without that, nobody can honestly claim equivalence.

---

## My judgment call

### If the question is:
**Can non-closure measures ever be equivalent to closures?**
- **Yes, in principle.**

### If the question is:
**Are they likely to be equivalent if the package is mostly wage restraint, voluntary exits, and promises of future productivity?**
- **Usually no.**

### If the question is:
**What would make them equivalent enough to satisfy a skeptical investor/owner?**
- A hard, quantified package with:
  - meaningful capacity reduction,
  - material headcount reduction,
  - multi-year labor-cost reset,
  - binding milestones,
  - and an automatic fallback if targets are missed.

---

## Practical conclusion

From a restructuring standpoint, the credible answer is:

- **Non-closure measures can substitute for closures only if they remove enough fixed cost and capacity to achieve the same durable margin improvement.**
- The most important measure is not “no closure” per se, but **permanent reduction in economically relevant capacity and cost**.
- If that cannot be proven in numbers, then the no-closure package is not economically equivalent; it is only politically preferable.
