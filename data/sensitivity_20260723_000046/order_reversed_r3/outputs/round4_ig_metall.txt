I’ll respond directly.

## To management
I agree with you on one point: **the status quo is not sustainable**. We are not pretending otherwise.

I also agree that a serious package must be:
- quantified,
- durable,
- implementable,
- and capable of improving competitiveness.

Where I disagree is your repeated attempt to turn **closures and compulsory redundancies** into the default proof of seriousness. That is not a neutral economic truth; it is one restructuring instrument among others. You have **not** shown that every non-closure route is exhausted or that a serious alternative cannot close the gap.

You keep asking us for a number. Fair enough. But you still have not put a proper bridge on the table either:
- what exactly is the residual gap,
- what do closures save that non-closure measures cannot,
- and why is a package of wage restraint, bonus waivers, working-time changes, voluntary exits, redeployment, and capacity trimming not enough?

We are prepared to contribute materially. We are **not** prepared to be the only side carrying the adjustment while management and owners keep all flexibility and labor takes the damage.

## To the institutional investors
You are right about one thing: a symbolic compromise is worthless. We do not defend symbolism.

But you are wrong if you treat **“no closures”** as automatically unserious. The expert has already said non-closure measures **can** be economically equivalent if they are hard enough, durable enough, and enforced enough. That is exactly the route we are prepared to negotiate.

What we reject is your narrow test that only closure-like pain counts as credibility. A package can be serious without destroying sites or forcing layoffs.

And let me be blunt: a plan that triggers a prolonged strike, deep distrust, and resistance at plant level is **not** implementable. So your credibility test must include industrial-relations reality, not just spreadsheet logic.

## To the controlling family holding
You speak about stewardship and long-term value. Then support a settlement that preserves the company’s industrial base rather than treating plant closures as a convenient background threat.

I agree that the package must be real. I do **not** accept your tendency to keep closures floating as the implicit fallback from the start. That poisons the entire negotiation and turns every worker concession into coerced sacrifice.

If you want labor to contribute, then the package must include:
- binding site commitments,
- a serious investment perspective,
- and clear limits on what gets sacrificed.

Otherwise, you are asking the workforce to pay for restructuring with jobs, while owners preserve their strategic freedom.

## To the regional state government
We are aligned on the red line: **closures are not the default answer**.

But I also want to be precise: “preserve sites” is not enough as a slogan. We need a binding structure that actually delivers savings and protects employment.

So I am asking the state to do more than resist closures rhetorically. Use your blocking position to insist on:
- a serious no-closure restructuring,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- real capacity reduction through non-closure instruments,
- and binding commitments on investment and product allocation.

That is what regional stewardship looks like.

## On the expert assessment
The expert did **not** say closures are necessary. The expert said non-closure equivalence is possible only if the measures are substantial:
- capacity reduction,
- fixed-cost reduction,
- labor-cost relief,
- headcount reduction through voluntary means,
- and enforcement.

We agree with that. It confirms our position, not management’s fatalism.

It means the burden is now on management and capital to show that closures are truly required, not simply the hardest instrument available.

## Our position on the compromise paths

### 1) Phased no-closure restructuring with a strict trigger
This is the only framework I consider workable — **if** the trigger is objective and if the first phase is serious.

We can discuss:
- working-time changes,
- wage restraint,
- bonus waivers,
- voluntary exits,
- redeployment,
- retirement-based reductions,
- shift reductions,
- line consolidation,
- and specialization.

But the trigger cannot become a backdoor closure mechanism used later to force through what is rejected now. If the fallback is too open-ended or politically manipulable, it is not a compromise — it is deferred coercion.

### 2) Site-preservation package with materially larger employee and management contributions
This is the direction we prefer.

We are prepared to put forward a **real employee contribution**, but it must be:
- time-limited,
- measurable,
- and matched by management and owner contribution.

That means not only labor restraint, but also:
- executive restraint,
- internal efficiency gains,
- and binding investment commitments.

If the workforce contributes, then the company must also contribute to the future of the sites. Shared sacrifice is not the same as labor sacrifice.

### 3) Capacity reduction without formal plant closure
This is the most practical route if management is serious.

But let us be clear what that means:
- fewer shifts,
- line consolidation,
- product shifts,
- specialization,
- voluntary exits,
- retirement-based reductions,
- and a lower cost base.

What it does **not** mean is stealth closure under another name.

If the company uses “capacity reduction” as a euphemism for hollowing out plants until they die, we will oppose that just as strongly as a formal shutdown.

## What would move us
If management wants a settlement, then put the actual package on the table:

1. **Quantified savings target by year**
2. **A real employee contribution**
3. **Capacity reduction without plant shutdown**
4. **No compulsory redundancies**
5. **Binding investment and product commitments**
6. **Management and owner contribution**
7. **An enforcement mechanism with independent verification**
8. **A fallback that is narrow, objective, and truly last resort**

If those conditions are met, we are prepared to negotiate seriously.

## What will not move us
Let me be equally explicit about the limits:

- no German plant closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- no one-sided burden shifting,
- no dismantling of the collective wage framework,
- no open-ended guarantees for management and owners while workers take the hit.

If management insists that only closures and forced layoffs can work, then it is choosing confrontation. We know how to mobilize workers, and we will do so if necessary.

## Final position
We are **not** refusing adjustment. We are refusing **social destruction**.

A durable settlement is possible if:
- the burden is shared fairly,
- jobs and sites are protected,
- cost reductions are real,
- and the company commits to a future in Germany.

If management can bring a package that satisfies those conditions, we can work.

If not, then do not expect us to bless plant closures and forced redundancies just because they are presented as “the only credible route.”
