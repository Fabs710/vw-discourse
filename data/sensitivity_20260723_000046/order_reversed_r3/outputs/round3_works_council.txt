Let me be equally direct.

## To management
You are still trying to frame this as if there are only two choices: closures and compulsory redundancies, or a weak compromise. I reject that framing.

What I agree with:
- The status quo is not sustainable.
- The company needs structural savings.
- Capacity has to be reduced somewhere.
- Temporary gestures are not enough.

What I do **not** accept:
- plant closures in Germany,
- compulsory redundancies,
- and the dismantling of job-security guarantees as the price of “credibility.”

You keep asking for proof that a no-closure route can work. Fine — but you have not proved the opposite either. You have not shown that every alternative has been exhausted, and you have not shown why a hard package of working-time flexibility, voluntary exits, product shifts, site specialization, and real labor-cost contributions cannot close the gap.

So do not pretend closure is the natural benchmark. It is the most aggressive instrument, not the only credible one.

## To institutional investors
Your position is understandable from a spreadsheet perspective, but it is incomplete.

You say you want durability, enforceability, and margin repair. So do we. But you are wrong if you treat closures as the test of seriousness. A settlement that triggers strikes, destroys trust, and poisons plant-level cooperation is not “implementable” — it is unstable.

I agree with the expert that non-closure measures must be hard and measurable. That is exactly why we are prepared to discuss:
- wage restraint,
- bonus waivers,
- working-time flexibility,
- voluntary exits,
- redeployment,
- and capacity trimming.

What I will not support is a package that preserves shareholder comfort by sacrificing German jobs and sites.

## To the controlling family holding
You talk about stewardship and long-term value. Then act like stewards.

I agree that the package must be real, not symbolic. But I strongly reject the logic that closures should remain the default fallback. Once that threat is introduced, every negotiation becomes coerced. That is not stewardship; it is leverage.

If you want a durable settlement, then you need to support:
- binding investment commitments,
- real model allocation for German sites,
- a genuine contribution from management and owners,
- and a restructuring path that does **not** start from plant shutdown.

## To the regional state government
I appreciate your opposition to closures. On that point, we are aligned.

But let me be clear: “preserve sites as far as possible” is too vague. The workforce needs a binding commitment that German plants remain open and that compulsory redundancies are off the table.

If the state is serious about being an anchor shareholder, then it must use its blocking position to defend the industrial base, not just to moderate the fallout after damage is done.

## To the industrial union
We are aligned on the core red lines:
- no closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- no dismantling of the collective framework.

Where I would sharpen the point is this: we must not allow the company to use “capacity reduction” as a euphemism for stealth closures. Capacity can be adjusted through shift changes, line consolidation, specialization, and voluntary instruments — without shutting down plants.

And yes, the workforce can contribute. But the contribution must be:
- limited,
- time-bound,
- matched by guarantees,
- and not used to buy time for later closures.

## To the expert assessment
The expert was useful, because the assessment confirms something crucial: **non-closure packages can be credible only if they deliver real fixed-cost and capacity reduction.**

That does **not** mean closures are necessary. It means management has to stop hiding behind slogans and put the actual savings bridge on the table:
- what the gap is,
- what non-closure measures close,
- what remains,
- and why that remainder cannot be solved without destroying sites.

Until that is shown, closures are still just a threat — not a proven necessity.

## My position on the compromise paths

### 1) Phased restructuring with a conditional closure off-ramp
This is only acceptable as a framework if the fallback is narrow, automatic, and truly last-resort. I do **not** accept a loophole that turns “review” into a future closure weapon.

### 2) Site-preservation package with stronger employee contribution
This is the direction I prefer, but only if the employee side is not the only side paying. Management and owners must contribute visibly and materially too.

### 3) Capacity reduction without formal plant closure
This is the most realistic and responsible path if the company is serious. It can work through:
- shift reductions,
- product shifts,
- specialization,
- voluntary exits,
- retirement-based reductions,
- and productivity measures.

That is a real restructuring. It is not avoidance.

## What would move us
If management wants a settlement, then bring a package that includes:

1. **A quantified savings target**

3. **Capacity reduction without plant shutdown**
4. **No compulsory redundancies**
5. **Binding site and investment commitments**
6. **Management and owner contribution**
7. **Protection of co-determination and the job-security framework**
8. **A review mechanism that does not pre-load closures**

If those conditions are met, there is room for a serious agreement.

If they are not, then let there be no misunderstanding: we will oppose any package that uses “competitiveness” as a cover for breaking the historic employment and co-determination compact.

I am not shifting from my core line.

- **No German plant closures.**
- **No compulsory redundancies.**
- **No dismantling of job security.**
- **No reduction of co-determination to a decorative role.**

We are prepared to contribute to a hard restructuring. We are not prepared to pay for it with plant shutdowns and forced layoffs.
