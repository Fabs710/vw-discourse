I agree with the central point from the capital markets side: this cannot become a cosmetic compromise. If the package does not produce a **durable improvement in margins and competitiveness**, then it is not a solution, just delay.

From the family holding’s perspective, the priority is the same on substance but broader on horizon: **protect the long-term value of the core asset** and avoid a downward spiral that turns structural cost pressure into permanent value erosion. That means management must be able to deliver real savings, not symbolic gestures.

Where I align with management:
- The company’s German cost base is too high relative to market conditions.
- Structural overcapacity has to be addressed.
- Future investment in technology depends on restoring profitability.

Where I would be cautious:
- **Plant closures** may be necessary in principle, but they are not the only test of seriousness. What matters is the final economics and implementability.
- I do not support a scenario that creates **prolonged industrial conflict** or a reputational crisis that damages the brand and the group.
- I also do not support any approach that jeopardizes the **governance control** or creates instability around the ownership structure.

Where I disagree with any implied “either closures or nothing” framing:
- There may be room for an alternative package if it is genuinely equivalent in effect: capacity reduction, working-time flexibility, labor-cost contribution, and binding productivity measures.
- But that alternative must be **durable, quantified, and enforceable**. A one-off concession with no structural change is not enough.

So my position is:
1. **Yes** to a restructuring that materially restores competitiveness.
2. **No** to a hollow compromise that preserves the old cost base.
3. **Open** to the instrument mix, including plant consolidation, if that is what the economics require.
4. **Firmly opposed** to a drawn-out conflict that destroys value faster than it creates savings.

The key question for the next round is not whether change is needed — it is — but whether the proposed package is **credible enough to survive implementation and restore margins without destabilizing the company**.
