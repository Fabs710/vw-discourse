We agree on one thing: **the status quo is over**. The company cannot keep the current cost base and capacity and still expect a viable future. We are not defending stagnation.

But I need to be equally clear on what **cannot** be the price of restructuring.

### Where we agree
- There must be **durable savings**.
- The company needs to regain **competitiveness**.
- Capacity has to be adjusted somehow.
- A purely symbolic compromise is not acceptable.

We are prepared to talk about:
- working-time flexibility,
- capacity reductions,
- a negotiated employee contribution,
- and other measures that reduce cost without destroying the company’s social and industrial base.

That is exactly why we already submitted a counter-proposal.

### Where we disagree
#### 1. Plant closures are not a neutral “tool”
Management keeps presenting closures as just another option on the table. For us, that is not true. **Closing plants means irreversible damage to jobs, regions, and trust.** It is not a routine lever to be used first and justified later.

If management wants support for deep restructuring, it must show that it has exhausted alternatives that preserve sites and avoid compulsory redundancies. Otherwise this is not a restructuring plan; it is a transfer of the burden onto the workforce.

#### 2. Workers will not bear all the pain
We will not accept a package in which:
- employees give up pay,
- employees accept insecurity,
- employees sacrifice bonuses or guarantees,
- and management and owners contribute little or nothing.

A fair settlement requires **shared sacrifice**. If the company needs labor-cost relief, then yes, we can discuss it. But it must be matched by:
- site security,
- job security,
- investment commitments,
- and visible contributions from management and ownership.

#### 3. “Durable” must not mean permanent one-sided concessions
We hear the demand for durable savings. But durable cannot mean locking in permanent wage erosion while the company keeps all strategic options open. The collective wage framework matters. If it is broken here, it sets a precedent far beyond one plant or one brand.

### Our limit is clear
Let me say it plainly: **no compulsory redundancies, no plant closures.**

If the restructuring depends on forcing workers out or shutting sites, then we are not close to agreement. And if management insists on that path, it should not expect labor peace.

### What would move us
A serious agreement would need to include:
- a **binding job and site security framework**,
- **no forced layoffs**,
- a **negotiated employee contribution** instead of unilateral cuts,
- capacity adjustment through **socially acceptable instruments**,
- and a credible commitment that the company will invest in the future of the German sites.

That is the basis for a durable settlement. Not workers paying while others lecture about competitiveness.

### To investors and owners
You say you want credibility. Then judge credibility fairly:
- A solution that destroys labour peace and weakens the workforce is not credible.
- A solution that protects margins by permanently hollowing out the industrial base is not sustainable.
- A company with no workforce buy-in will face strikes, delay, and lasting conflict — which destroys value too.

### To management
If you want us to engage constructively, bring a package that shares the burden:
- real employee contribution, yes,
- but also a real commitment from the company side to protect jobs and sites,
- and no ultimatum built around closures.

We are ready for hard bargaining. We are **not** ready to sign off on a restructuring where the workforce pays the full bill.
