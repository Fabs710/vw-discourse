We appreciate the insistence on durability and real savings. On that point, I agree: **a cosmetic compromise would be irresponsible**.

But I need to be equally clear that I do **not** accept the implication that the only credible restructuring is one that puts **plant closures in Germany** and **forced redundancies** back on the table.

### Where I agree
- The status quo is not viable.
- The company needs **structural** cost relief.
- Any solution must be **quantified, durable, and implementable**.
- A prolonged industrial conflict would damage the company and the region.

### Where I disagree

#### To institutional investors
You are right that savings must be real and not temporary. But you are wrong if you treat closures as the benchmark of seriousness.

From the state’s perspective, the relevant question is not whether the package looks harsh enough. It is whether it:
1. restores competitiveness,
2. preserves the company as a regional anchor,
3. protects a meaningful share of jobs and sites in the state,
4. and remains implementable without detonating industrial peace.

If non-closure measures can achieve equivalent savings, then they are not a weaker option — they are the better option. Your own argument about deal certainty should lead you to that conclusion: a shutdown-driven package that triggers strikes, legal conflict, and political backlash is not “certain” at all.

#### To the controlling family holding
I understand the long-term value argument. We are aligned on avoiding a hollowed-out company. But long-term value is not protected by treating regional employment as collateral damage.

I do **not** accept a logic in which every alternative must be judged against the most aggressive cost-cutting tool available. The test must be whether there is a **socially and economically durable** solution. As a shareholder, we are prepared to support a hard restructuring — but not one that sacrifices core sites as a first resort.

#### To management
You keep saying capacity must come out of the system. That may be true. But “capacity reduction” does **not automatically mean closures**. There are other instruments:
- targeted production shifts,
- working-time adjustments,
- negotiated labor-cost contributions,
- productivity measures,
- redeployment and attrition,
- and investment commitments tied to future model allocation.

If you want our support, bring a package that shows:
- the savings gap,
- the exact savings by measure,
- what job losses are unavoidable,
- and how many can be avoided through negotiated alternatives.

What I will not support is a package that starts from closure and then asks everyone else to justify why their communities should pay for it.

### Our position
The state is open to a **tough negotiated restructuring**, but not to a “closure first” approach.

What we can support:
- durable cost reductions,
- capacity adjustment,
- a fair employee contribution,
- productivity improvements,
- and a credible future investment plan for the German sites.

What we cannot support:
- plant closures in the state as the preferred instrument,
- a restructuring that guts regional employment,
- or any move that sidelines the state’s co-determination and blocking role.

### On what would move us
If management and the owners can show a package that:
- delivers quantified savings,
- protects core sites and a substantial share of employment in the state,
- avoids forced redundancies,
- and secures future investment and model allocation,

then we are prepared to engage constructively.

But if the answer is simply “closures are necessary,” then let me be explicit: **that is not a position we can accept**. We have the statutory leverage to block it, and we will use it if needed.

### Bottom line
We are ready for a serious restructuring. We are **not** ready to trade the region’s industrial base for a cleaner spreadsheet. If the company wants support from the state, it must bring a solution that is both economically credible **and** regionally responsible.
