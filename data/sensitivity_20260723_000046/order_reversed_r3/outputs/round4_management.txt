I’ll respond plainly.

## First, what I agree with

I agree with everyone on one point: **the status quo is not sustainable**.

I also agree with the expert and the capital side that any serious package must be:
- **quantified**
- **durable**
- **implementable**
- and **material enough to restore competitiveness**

And I agree with the state, union, and works council on one more point: a deal that detonates into a prolonged strike or cannot be implemented would destroy value.

So yes — we need a package that works in the real world, not a press release.

## Where I disagree

### To the union and works council
I do **not** accept the idea that “no closures” is a solution in itself.

It is a preference, not a restructuring plan.

You keep asking us to prove closures are necessary. Fair. But the burden also runs the other way: **show me a no-closure package that actually closes the structural gap**. Not in principle. In numbers. With recurring effect. With enforcement. With a timeline that fits the turnaround, not 2030 wishful thinking.

What I have heard so far is:
- wage restraint,
- bonus waivers,
- flexibility,
- voluntary exits,
- redeployment,
- site specialization,
- and capacity trimming.

That may help. But unless it removes enough **fixed cost** and **overcapacity**, it is not equivalent. It is delay dressed up as compromise.

I will be blunt: **I will not take a package that leaves the German cost base still uncompetitive and call it a restructuring.**

### To the regional state government
You say closures are not proven necessary. I say the opposite: **what is proven is that the current structure is too expensive**.

You cannot defend every site as a principle and still expect the business to recover margins. If preserving every current footprint means preserving underperformance, then the company does not get healthier — it gets slower.

I respect the state’s blocking role. But that role does not change the economics.

### To the controlling family holding
I agree that closures are not an ideology. They are a tool. But when the cost base is structurally out of line and the market is shrinking, that tool remains on the table.

I am not asking for closures because I like them. I am saying they remain necessary unless someone can show an alternative with the **same recurring savings, same speed, and same durability**.

That proof has not been delivered.

### To investors
You are right to demand enforceability. You are wrong if you think every no-closure route is automatically credible just because it sounds less harsh.

A compromise without a real enforcement mechanism is not a restructuring. It is a promise to argue again in 18 months.

## On the expert’s assessment
The expert said something important: non-closure measures can be equivalent **only if** they remove enough fixed cost, capacity, and risk.

Exactly. That is the key.

But that also means we must stop pretending that small, politically easy measures are enough. If the no-closure package has to be that hard to work, then it is no longer a soft alternative — it is closure-equivalent in all but name.

So I ask labor and the state directly:

**Are you prepared to accept a package that is economically equivalent to closures, even if it avoids the legal act of closure?**

If yes, then we can talk.

If no, then you are not offering an equivalent alternative. You are offering a constraint.

## My position on the compromise paths

### 1) Phased no-closure restructuring with a strict trigger
This is the only framework I will treat seriously.

Why?
- It gives labor and the state a chance to avoid immediate closures.
- It gives us a genuine backstop if savings fail to materialize.
- It makes the package testable instead of rhetorical.

But let me be clear: the trigger must be **objective and automatic**. It cannot be a political rerun where everyone reopens the whole deal when the numbers disappoint. If the fallback is negotiable later, it is worthless now.

### 2) Site-preservation package with stronger employee and management contributions
Potentially acceptable — but only if the employee contribution is **material**, not symbolic.

That means:
- real labor-cost relief,
- real productivity gains,
- real capacity reduction,
- and real changes to the footprint and utilization.

It also means management and owners must contribute. I do not accept one-sided sacrifice. But company-side contributions do not replace the need for structurally lower costs.

### 3) Capacity reduction without formal plant closure
This is acceptable **if it is real**.

If it means:
- line consolidation,
- shift elimination,
- specialization,
- volume reallocation,
- indirect-function cuts,
- voluntary exits,
- and a lower fixed-cost base,

then yes, that can be workable.

If it means “keep everything open and hope for better morale,” then no.

## What would move management

I remain open to a no-closure solution **only if** it includes all of the following:

1. **A quantified savings bridge**
   - by year
   - recurring
   - and sufficient to restore the brand’s return-on-sales target

2. **Meaningful capacity reduction**
   - not just announcements

3. **A material labor-cost contribution**
   - not just temporary symbolism

4. **A credible implementation timetable**
   - within the turnaround horizon

5. **An enforcement mechanism**
   - with independent verification

6. **A fallback if targets are missed**
   - real, predefined, and not politically soft

If those conditions are not met, then closures or closure-equivalent measures remain on the table because they are the only instruments that can close the gap fast enough and hard enough.

## What will not move me
I will not support:
- cosmetic compromise,
- permanent cost rigidity,
- open-ended protections that freeze the structure,
- or a settlement that avoids conflict by postponing the crisis.

I will not let the company drift into 2030 with an uncompetitive German cost base because people wanted the least painful headline.

## My answer to the central question
Can a no-closure package be acceptable?

**Yes — if it is economically equivalent, enforceable, and durable.**

Has anyone shown that yet?

**No.**

And until that proof exists, I will keep closures and closure-equivalent measures on the table.

So here is my direct challenge back to labor and the state:

**Bring the numbers. Show the recurring savings. Show the capacity reduction. Show the enforcement. Show that the package truly restores competitiveness.**

If you can do that, we have a route.

If you cannot, then you are asking the company to accept a weaker restructuring just because it is politically easier. And that, for me, is not acceptable.
