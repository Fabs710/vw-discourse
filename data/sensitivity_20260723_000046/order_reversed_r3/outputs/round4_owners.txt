We have moved from slogans to the real issue, and that is useful.

## Where I agree
I agree with management, investors, and the expert on one central point: **the company needs a durable structural reset, not a cosmetic compromise**.

I also agree with the union, works council, and state on one important point: **a prolonged destructive conflict would destroy value** and must be avoided.

So there is common ground, but only at the level of the objective. The disagreement is about the instrument.

## Where I disagree
### 1) I do not accept “no closures” as a sufficient answer
The union and state keep presenting “no closures” as if it were itself a solution. It is not.

It may be a preferred outcome, but it is only credible if it produces:
- enough fixed-cost reduction,
- enough capacity reduction,
- enough labor-cost relief,
- and enough implementation certainty to restore margins.

If it does not, then it is not stewardship. It is delay.

### 2) I do not accept closures as the default first move either
Management and investors are right to demand a hard savings bridge. But I am not prepared to treat plant closure as the starting assumption unless the numbers show there is no other credible route.

A closure is a serious step. It must be justified by:
- a quantified residual gap,
- a comparison with non-closure options,
- and a realistic view of execution risk.

A threat is not the same as proof.

### 3) I do not accept open-ended guarantees
The union and works council want site and job protection, which I understand. But any guarantee that freezes the cost base or blocks adjustment for years would be value-destructive.

The family holding cannot support a settlement that preserves all legacy structures while eroding competitiveness.

## My view on the three compromise paths
### Option 1: phased no-closure restructuring with a strict trigger
This is the most promising framework.

Why?
- It gives labor and the state a real chance to avoid closures.
- It gives management and investors a real backstop.
- It makes the package testable instead of rhetorical.

But the trigger must be:
- objective,
- pre-agreed,
- and not subject to endless political renegotiation.

If the fallback cannot actually happen, then it has no credibility.

### Option 2: site-preservation package with materially larger employee and management contributions
Potentially acceptable, but only if the contributions are genuinely material.

That means:
- real employee contribution,
- visible management restraint,
- and owner-level support where appropriate.

If it simply spreads pain without changing the economics, I would not support it.

### Option 3: capacity reduction without formal plant closure
This is acceptable if it is real.

Not symbolic. Not just shift reshuffling. It must remove:
- capacity,
- overhead,
- and fixed cost,
in a way that is economically comparable to closure, even if sites remain legally open.

That is the test.

## What would move me
I could support a no-closure settlement only if it includes all of the following:

1. **A quantified savings bridge**
   - by year
   - recurring
   - and sufficient to restore margins

2. **Meaningful capacity reduction**
   - real volume and footprint reduction, not just language

3. **A material employee contribution**
   - wage restraint, bonus waivers, flexibility, voluntary exits

4. **A credible implementation mechanism**
   - milestones and independent verification

5. **A genuine fallback**
   - automatic enough to be believable

6. **Proof that long-term value is protected**
   - not merely that conflict is postponed

## What I will not support
I will not support:
- cosmetic compromise,
- permanent rigidity,
- open-ended job-security language,
- or a settlement that leaves the enterprise structurally uncompetitive.

I will also not support a closure-first posture unless it is genuinely necessary. The burden of proof is on those who want the harsher instrument.

## To management
Bring me the actual bridge:
- what savings are required,
- what savings non-closure measures deliver,
- what gap remains,
- and what execution risk attaches to each variant.

If the no-closure path closes the gap, then I can support it.

If it cannot, then we need to say plainly that deeper structural measures remain unavoidable.

## To investors
Your demand for durability is reasonable. I support it.

What I do not support is turning “economic equivalence” into an excuse for insisting on the harshest option regardless of whether it is implementable. A theoretically superior restructuring that triggers industrial war and political blockage is not value-maximizing.

## To the union and works council
Your rejection of closures and forced redundancies is understandable, but it is not enough on its own.

If you want support for a site-preserving solution, then show a package that is:
- hard,
- quantified,
- and genuinely capable of restoring competitiveness.

Shared sacrifice is not a slogan. It must be measured in the economics.

## To the state government
I respect your blocking position and your regional responsibility. But preserving every site in current form is not a valid benchmark.

What matters is whether the package:
- preserves the industrial base where it matters,
- reduces capacity and cost enough,
- and remains durable.

If you can support that, good. If not, then you are protecting structure over substance.

## My bottom line
The family holding will support a **hard, quantified, enforceable restructuring** that restores competitiveness and protects long-term enterprise value.

We are open to a no-closure outcome.

But only if it is proven to be economically credible, operationally enforceable, and durable.

If that proof is not there, then I will not pretend that sentiment can substitute for restructuring.
