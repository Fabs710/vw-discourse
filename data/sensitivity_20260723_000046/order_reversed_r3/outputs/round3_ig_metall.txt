Let me answer directly, because we are now beyond abstractions.

## To management
You keep saying “show the numbers,” as if the absence of a closure plan means the absence of a restructuring plan. That is simply not true.

What I agree with:
- The company needs durable savings.
- Capacity has to come down.
- Temporary, cosmetic measures are not enough.
- A package must be implementable.

What I disagree with:
- Your claim that **closures and compulsory redundancies are the only credible route**.
- Your attempt to make job destruction the default proof of seriousness.
- Your refusal to acknowledge that the workforce is already prepared to contribute materially.

We have already put a serious counter-proposal on the table: labor-cost contribution, capacity reduction, productivity measures, and flexibility — **without plant closures and without forced layoffs**. That is not a slogan. It is a negotiation offer.

But I will be equally clear: if you demand an “equivalent” package, then you must also accept that equivalence is not the same as permanent job cuts disguised through a closure logic. We are prepared to discuss:
- wage restraint,
- bonus waivers,
- working-time adjustments,
- voluntary exits,
- redeployment,
- retirement-based reductions,
- and site specialization,

**if and only if** they come with binding job and site security and a fair contribution from management and owners.

What I will not accept:
- compulsory redundancies,
- plant closures,
- or a settlement that permanently breaks the collective wage framework while asking workers to carry the whole adjustment.

## To investors
You say you want a durable margin reset. Fine. So do we — because a company without a future means no jobs and no wages.

But your benchmark for seriousness is too narrow. You keep treating closures as the measure of whether the restructuring is “real.” That is wrong.

The expert has now confirmed what we have been saying all along:
- non-closure measures **can** be economically credible,
- but only if they are hard, quantified, and durable,
- and only if they remove enough fixed cost and capacity.

That is exactly why we are willing to negotiate a substantial employee contribution. What we are **not** willing to do is accept that the only path to credibility is to sacrifice sites and labor rights.

A package can be serious without being socially destructive. If you are serious about implementation risk, then you should support the path with the **highest chance of actually being carried by the workforce**, not the one that provokes strikes and resistance.

## To the controlling family holding
You speak about stewardship and long-term value. Then act like stewards of a productive industrial company, not enforcers of a shortcut.

I agree with you on one point: the package must be real, not symbolic.

I disagree with you on this:
- You are too quick to leave closures on the table as if they were merely a neutral fallback.
- In reality, keeping closures as a credible threat from the start poisons the entire process and turns every other concession into a coerced one.

If you want a settlement that lasts, then the workforce must be able to trust that sacrifices made now will not simply be followed by plant shutdowns later.

That means:
- no closure-first logic,
- no forced redundancies,
- and binding commitments on future investment and model allocation.

If you want labor to give something up, then owners and management must also give something up in a measurable way.

## To the regional state government
We are aligned on the core point: closures are not acceptable as the starting point. I welcome that.

But I also want to be honest with you: “preserve sites as far as possible” is not enough by itself. We need a binding package, not just a political preference.

What I support:
- durable capacity reduction without plant closure,
- a fair employee contribution,
- investment and product commitments,
- and no forced layoffs.

What I need from the state:
- use your blocking position to protect the industrial base,
- insist on a serious no-closure restructuring,
- and do not let “temporary” pressure become a back door to closures.

If the state is truly an anchor shareholder, then it should anchor the company in Germany — not merely manage the political fallout after a closure decision.

## On the expert assessment
The expert did **not** say closures are necessary. The expert said:
- equivalence is possible in principle,
- but only with substantial capacity reduction, headcount reduction through voluntary means, labor-cost relief, and enforcement.

That supports our position, not management’s fatalism.

It means the real question is not whether closures are the only credible route. The real question is whether management is prepared to negotiate a package that is hard enough to satisfy markets **without** breaking the workforce.

We are saying yes — if the burden is shared fairly.

## Our concrete position
Let me make this unmistakable:

### We can discuss:
- a temporary and measurable employee contribution,
- bonus waivers,
- wage moderation,
- working-time flexibility,
- voluntary exit programs,
- redeployment,
- retirement-based reductions,
- shift reductions and capacity trimming,
- site specialization,
- and future productivity commitments.

### We cannot accept:
- plant closures in Germany,
- compulsory redundancies,
- open-ended job insecurity,
- dismantling the collective wage framework,
- or a restructuring where workers bear the pain and management/owners do not.

## What would make a settlement possible
If management wants a serious agreement, then put a concrete package on the table with all of the following:

1. **A quantified savings target**
   - by year, with recurring effect.

2. **A labor contribution that is real but limited**
   - for example, a time-bound wage restraint / bonus waiver / flexibility package.

3. **Capacity reduction without site shutdown**
   - shift consolidation, line specialization, product reallocation, and output reduction.

4. **No compulsory redundancies**
   - reductions through voluntary instruments only.

5. **Binding investment and product commitments**
   - future allocation to German sites.

6. **Management and owner contribution**
   - executive restraint, internal efficiency gains, and visible shared sacrifice.

7. **A monitoring mechanism**
   - so that if targets are missed, the sides review the package before anything irreversible is imposed.

That is a credible restructuring. It is not the absence of restructuring.

## What will not move us
Let me be equally explicit about the line we will defend:
- no closures,
- no forced layoffs,
- no one-sided burden shifting,
- no conversion of a bargaining process into a threat campaign.

If management insists that only closures and compulsory redundancies can work, then it is choosing confrontation, not solutions. We know how to mobilize workers, and we will do so if necessary. But I still prefer a negotiated settlement — provided it is fair.

## Final answer
We are not refusing adjustment. We are refusing **social destruction**.

If the company wants a durable turnaround, then it must accept that the workforce will contribute — but not by surrendering its jobs, its sites, and its collective protections while owners and management preserve theirs.

Bring a package that is:
- quantified,
- enforceable,
- durable,
- and fairly shared.

If that package avoids closures and forced redundancies, we are ready to negotiate seriously.

If it does not, then we will resist it with all legitimate means available to us.
