From a restructuring and implementation-risk perspective, the conversation is now at the key test: **can the parties produce a no-closure package that is economically equivalent to closures, or not?**

## My assessment, based on the material so far

### 1) The parties are not yet discussing a verified savings bridge
Management and capital repeatedly assert that closures remain necessary unless disproven. Labor and the state repeatedly assert that non-closure options are still viable. But **neither side has yet provided a quantified bridge** showing:

- the current structural cost gap,
- the recurring savings from each measure,
- one-time costs and timing,
- the residual gap after non-closure measures,
- and the execution risk by measure.

Without that, this is still a **claims contest**, not a validated restructuring design.

### 2) The labor-side proposals can produce real savings, but equivalence is not automatic
The non-closure toolkit listed across the rounds is directionally credible:

- wage restraint / bonus waivers,
- working-time flexibility,
- voluntary exits,
- retirement-based reductions,
- shift elimination,
- line consolidation,
- product specialization,
- indirect-function cuts,
- redeployment.

These can absolutely remove cost. But whether they are **closure-equivalent** depends on scale and enforcement.

In automotive manufacturing, the hard truth is:

- **capacity reduction without site closure can be real**,  
- but it often leaves **more fixed cost structure** than a closure if not designed aggressively,
- and it can be diluted by hidden “keep everything alive” compromises.

So the burden is on the no-closure side to show that it removes enough:
- direct labor cost,
- indirect cost,
- plant overhead,
- maintenance and utilities,
- logistics complexity,
- and underutilized capacity.

### 3) The main implementation risk is not concept; it is enforceability
The proposed compromise paths differ mainly in **credibility of enforcement**.

#### A. Phased no-closure with trigger
This is the most credible concept **if**:
- milestones are objective,
- verification is independent,
- trigger mechanics are automatic,
- and the fallback is genuinely pre-committed.

But I would flag a major execution risk:  
if the trigger is politically renegotiable later, it will be viewed by capital as worthless and by labor as a disguised closure threat. That makes the mechanism unstable.

#### B. Site-preservation with larger employee/management contributions
This is viable only if the contributions are:
- material,
- recurring,
- and shared across stakeholders.

If the package relies mainly on temporary wage restraint and voluntary exits, it may improve liquidity and near-term costs, but it may **not** reset the fixed-cost base enough to restore competitiveness.

#### C. Capacity reduction without formal plant closure
This is economically plausible, but only if it is not just a shift shuffle.

For equivalence, it must include:
- genuine line removal,
- specialization that reduces duplication,
- headcount reduction in support functions,
- procurement/logistics simplification,
- and a lower site overhead base.

If the plants stay open but functionally overloaded with legacy cost, the market will treat it as delay.

## What is missing to judge equivalence properly
To assess whether non-closure measures can match closures, I would need at minimum:

1. **Baseline economics**
   - cost per vehicle by site,
   - fixed versus variable cost split,
   - overhead allocation,
   - utilization rates,
   - current margin contribution by plant.

2. **Closure case economics**
   - expected recurring savings from a closure or consolidation,
   - one-time restructuring costs,
   - logistics and transfer costs,
   - impairment / pension / severance effects.

3. **Non-closure package economics**
   - quantified savings from each lever,
   - duration and legal durability,
   - wage cost impact,
   - FTE reduction by mechanism,
   - capacity reduction in units/shift/hours,
   - indirect cost reduction.

4. **Implementation feasibility**
   - collective bargaining constraints,
   - works council rights,
   - strike risk,
   - state and shareholder veto risk,
   - timing to effect.

Without those, no one can credibly say whether the no-closure package is truly equivalent.

## My judgment on the stakeholder positions

### Management
Management is right to insist on a **hard savings bridge**. But it has not yet demonstrated, in the information provided, that closures are necessary rather than simply preferred. The proof standard is not satisfied by general statements.

### Institutional investors
They are correctly demanding:
- quantified recurring savings,
- enforceable milestones,
- and a fallback.

That is the right financial standard. Their weakness is that they appear to treat closure-like pain as the only sign of seriousness. That is not economically rigorous.

### Controlling family holding
The family’s position is internally coherent: it wants a durable reset but not closures as the first move. The risk is that it may underweight how much real operating change is needed if it wants to avoid closures.

### Regional state government
The state is consistent politically, but economically it cannot substitute “preserve sites” for a valid restructuring. If it wants no closures, it must accept a package that genuinely removes fixed cost and overcapacity.

### Union and works council
They are right that non-closure measures can work in principle. But their current position still lacks a quantified demonstration that these measures will fully close the gap. “No closures” is a boundary condition, not a savings bridge.

---

## Bottom line

### Can a no-closure package be economically equivalent?
**Yes, in principle.**  
But only if it produces:
- comparable recurring savings,
- comparable fixed-cost reduction,
- real capacity reduction,
- and enforceable implementation.

### Is that proven here?
**No.**  
The current discussion does not yet show equivalence.

### What is the biggest risk?
That the parties settle on a politically acceptable package that is **not economically sufficient**, then reopen the conflict in 12–24 months after underperformance and loss of trust.

### My skeptical conclusion
At present, the no-closure path is **plausible but unproven**.  
Closures are **not yet justified by evidence in the material provided**, but neither is a closure-free settlement yet shown to be fully closure-equivalent.
