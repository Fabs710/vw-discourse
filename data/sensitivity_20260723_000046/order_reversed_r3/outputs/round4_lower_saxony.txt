We are at the point where the discussion has become sufficiently concrete to be useful. I will respond directly.

## Where I agree

I agree with almost everyone on the following points:
- The status quo is not sustainable.
- The company needs durable, structural cost reduction.
- Capacity must come down somehow.
- Temporary gestures, cosmetic savings, or open-ended promises are not enough.
- A destructive strike or a settlement that cannot be implemented would damage the company and the region.

I also agree with the expert that **non-closure measures only count if they remove enough fixed cost, capacity, and risk to be economically equivalent**. That is the right standard.

## Where I disagree

### 1) I do not accept closures as the default instrument
Management, investors, and the family are still speaking as if closures are the natural benchmark of seriousness. I do **not** accept that.

Plant closures in Germany are not just another operational measure. They have regional, social, and political consequences that cannot be treated as a routine cost item. If closures are to be discussed at all, they must be justified by a clearly proven residual gap after all serious alternatives are exhausted.

So far, I do not see that proof.

### 2) I do not accept a settlement that guts jobs or regional industrial capacity
I want to be equally clear: I will not support an outcome that destroys substantial employment in the state or turns the company away from its role as a regional anchor.

A restructuring that protects shareholder optics while hollowing out the region is not a solution. It is a transfer of cost to workers, communities, and public institutions.

### 3) I do not accept that “no closures” is enough by itself
This is where I partly agree with the capital side’s criticism. Saying “no closures” is not a complete plan. It must be backed by:
- real capacity reduction,
- real labor-cost contribution,
- measurable savings,
- and implementation discipline.

So I am not defending the status quo. I am defending a **serious non-closure restructuring**.

## On the expert assessment

The expert has made the key point: a no-closure package can work only if it is hard enough to remove fixed cost and overcapacity in a durable way.

That supports a negotiated restructuring, but it also sets a high bar. The bar is not “accept closures.” The bar is:

- show the savings bridge,
- show the recurring effect,
- show the implementation risk,
- and show whether non-closure measures close the gap.

If they do, closures are unnecessary. If they do not, then we still have not exhausted the alternatives.

## My position on the compromise paths

### 1) Phased no-closure restructuring with a strict trigger
This is the only framework that is even remotely serious.

I could support it **if**:
- the first phase contains real capacity reduction,
- the savings milestones are objective and measurable,
- the fallback is narrow and truly last-resort,
- and no one uses the trigger as a backdoor to force plant shutdowns later.

I will not accept a vague “off-ramp” that becomes a closure weapon after labor has already conceded.

### 2) Site-preservation package with materially larger employee and management contributions
This is the path I prefer.

But it must be balanced. I will not ask the workforce or the state alone to carry the adjustment. A serious package must include:
- employee contribution,
- management restraint,
- owner contribution,
- and binding investment commitments for German sites.

If the company wants flexibility from labor, it must also offer a credible future for the sites.

### 3) Capacity reduction without formal plant closure
This is the most realistic route if management is prepared to be disciplined.

It can include:
- shift reductions,
- line consolidation,
- product specialization,
- voluntary exits,
- retirement-based headcount reduction,
- and working-time changes.

But it must be real. If “capacity reduction” is simply a euphemism for gradual site hollowing-out, I will not support it.

## To management

You still owe everyone the **actual savings bridge**.

Not rhetoric. Not broad claims. The bridge:
- current gap,
- savings from non-closure measures,
- remaining gap,
- one-time costs,
- recurring annual effect,
- and implementation risk.

If you can show that the no-closure route truly closes the gap, then closures are not necessary.

If you cannot, then you have not yet made the case for the harsher option.

## To institutional investors

You are right that a cosmetic deal is unacceptable. But you are wrong if you treat closures as the only way to prove seriousness.

What you need to show is whether the non-closure package can deliver:
- enough recurring savings,
- enough capacity reduction,
- and enough durability.

If it can, it should be supported. If not, then we are still short of a credible restructuring.

I also need to be blunt: a package that triggers prolonged industrial conflict can destroy more value than it saves. So your “credibility” standard must include implementation reality, not only spreadsheet logic.

## To the controlling family holding

I understand your concern for long-term value and governance discipline. On that, we are not far apart.

Where I disagree is your tendency to keep closures too readily in the background as if they were a neutral fallback. They are not neutral. They alter the company’s regional footprint and social contract.

I can support a hard restructuring, but not one that makes closure the default bargaining stick.

If you want a settlement that lasts, then back a package that:
- preserves major sites,
- reduces cost materially,
- and does not force the region to absorb the adjustment alone.

## To the industrial union and works council

I support your rejection of plant closures and compulsory redundancies.

But I also need to say this clearly: your position will only remain credible if you are prepared to back a package that actually reduces cost and capacity. “No closures” must not become a shield for preserving the full legacy cost structure.

What I can support from the labor side:
- wage restraint,
- bonus waivers,
- flexibility,
- voluntary exits,
- redeployment,
- retirement-based reductions,
- shift and line consolidation,
- site specialization.

What I cannot support:
- closures,
- forced layoffs,
- or a package that offers workers sacrifices without a credible future for the sites.

## On the regional state government’s role

I appreciate the state’s position, and I do not say that tactically. It matters substantively.

The state’s role is not to rubber-stamp cuts. Its role is to ensure that the company remains an industrial anchor in the region while becoming competitive again.

That means I will use the state’s blocking position to insist on:
- a serious no-closure restructuring,
- protection of core sites,
- meaningful employment preservation,
- and binding investment commitments.

I will not use that position to protect inefficiency. I will use it to prevent a one-sided restructuring that damages the regional economy irreversibly.

## My bottom line

I am prepared to support a tough restructuring only if it meets these conditions:

1. **Quantified recurring savings**
2. **Real capacity reduction**
3. **A material but fair employee contribution**
4. **Management and owner contribution**
5. **Binding investment and model commitments for German sites**
6. **No compulsory redundancies as the default tool**
7. **A fallback that is genuinely last-resort, not a disguised closure trap**
8. **Protection of the company’s role as a regional economic anchor**

If those conditions are met, then I can support a negotiated compromise.

If they are not met, then I will not endorse a deal that trades away jobs, plants, and co-determination for the illusion of competitiveness.

## My direct answer to the central question

Can a no-closure package be economically equivalent?

**Yes, in principle.**

Is it already proven that it is impossible without closures?

**No.**

Is it acceptable to force closures before that question is answered with hard numbers?

**No.**

So the next step is not ideological escalation. It is a proper bridge:
- what the savings gap is,
- what non-closure measures close,
- what remains,
- and whether the company can still be competitive without destroying German sites.

That is the standard I will apply.
