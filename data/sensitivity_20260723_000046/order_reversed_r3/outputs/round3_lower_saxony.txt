I will be direct.

The expert has helped clarify the central issue: **non-closure measures can only count as equivalent if they remove enough fixed cost, capacity, and risk to deliver the same durable savings as a closure case.** That is the right standard. But it also means something important for this discussion: **no one has yet proven that closures are necessary**. They are one instrument, not the benchmark of seriousness.

## Where I agree
I agree with management, investors, and the family on three points:
- the status quo is not sustainable;
- the restructuring must be durable and quantified;
- and a cosmetic compromise would be irresponsible.

I also agree with the expert that **temporary wage restraint or attrition alone will not be enough**. If the package is weak, it will fail later and then everyone pays more.

## Where I disagree
I do **not** accept the repeated attempt to make **German plant closures** the default proof of credibility.

That is not a neutral economic conclusion. It is a choice with major regional and social consequences. From our standpoint, it is unacceptable to treat sites and jobs in the state as the first adjustment item when there are still other instruments to exhaust.

I also do **not** accept compulsory redundancies as a normal part of this settlement. If management cannot do the restructuring through voluntary exits, redeployment, working-time changes, specialization, and capacity trimming, then it has not earned the right to demand irreversible job destruction.

## On the expert assessment
The expert’s point about capacity reduction is important. If capacity must come out, then let us be honest about that. But capacity reduction does **not automatically mean plant closure**.

What I take from the expert is:
- a serious no-closure package must reduce **fixed cost** and **overcapacity**;
- it must be **measurable** and **durable**;
- and it must have a real enforcement mechanism.

That is exactly why I am prepared to support a **hard negotiated restructuring** — but not a closure-first approach.

## My position on the compromise paths

### 1) Phased restructuring with a conditional off-ramp
This is the only path that is even remotely acceptable as a framework, because it allows non-closure measures first and keeps an enforcement backstop.

But I want to be explicit: the fallback cannot become a political trapdoor that is triggered too easily or used as leverage to force closures later. If it is to exist, it must be:
- narrowly defined,
- based on missed savings milestones,
- and subject to board-level scrutiny, including the state’s veto role where applicable.

I can consider this only as a last-resort safeguard, not as a disguised closure program.

### 2) Site-preservation package with stronger employee contribution and binding investment commitments
This is the direction I prefer.

If the company wants support for a no-closure solution, then it must include:
- a **real employee contribution**,
- management and owner contributions,
- binding investment and product commitments for the German sites,
- and hard milestones on savings.

That is a legitimate bargain. What I will not support is asking the workforce to make sacrifices while giving no credible future for the plants and no meaningful commitment to the region.

### 3) Capacity reduction without formal plant closure
This is the most realistic path if management is serious about preserving the industrial base.

I can support:
- shift reductions,
- line consolidation,
- specialization of sites,
- redeployment,
- retirement-based reductions,
- productivity improvements,
- and a time-limited labor-cost contribution.

But it must be clear that this is not a euphemism for closing plants by stealth. If the effect is to gut a site while formally leaving it open, then the company is simply renaming the closure. That would not be acceptable.

## What would move us
A package could gain our support only if it includes all of the following:
1. **Quantified savings by year**
2. **A clearly defined savings gap and how it will be closed**
3. **Real capacity reduction without immediate plant shutdowns**
4. **No compulsory redundancies as the default tool**
5. **A binding employee contribution matched by management and owner contribution**
6. **Investment and model commitments for German sites**
7. **Protection of the state’s co-determination and blocking role**
8. **A credible fallback only if targets are demonstrably missed**

## What will not move us
Let me be equally clear about what we cannot accept:
- plant closures in the state as the preferred instrument;
- a restructuring that guts regional employment;
- compulsory redundancies as a first-line measure;
- or any attempt to sideline the state through a “market logic” argument.

The state is not here to rubber-stamp an erosion of the company’s regional anchor role.

## To management
If you want our support, bring a **full savings bridge**:
- what the gap is,
- what non-closure measures achieve,
- what remains,
- and why that remainder cannot be closed without site destruction.

Until then, closures are still only a threat, not a proven necessity.

## To investors and the family holding
Your insistence on durability is understandable. But if you push too hard for closures, you increase the risk of industrial conflict, political backlash, and implementation failure. That is not prudence; that is escalation.

If you want value protection, then support a package that is hard, binding, and socially sustainable. That is more likely to last than an outcome imposed against the region and workforce.

## To the union and works council
I support your rejection of closures and forced layoffs. But I also expect seriousness on your side. If you say “no closures,” then you must be prepared to back a package that actually delivers savings and is not just a defense of the status quo.

That means:
- real flexibility,
- measurable labor-cost contribution,
- and acceptance that some capacity will be reduced.

What I will defend is jobs, sites, and co-determination — not inefficiency as such.

## Bottom line
We are prepared to support a **tough, negotiated restructuring** that:
- protects core sites,
- preserves a substantial share of employment in the state,
- avoids compulsory redundancies,
- and restores competitiveness through real, measurable measures.

We are **not** prepared to support closures as the starting point or as the benchmark of seriousness.

If management cannot demonstrate that non-closure measures are insufficient, then it has not made the case. And if it can demonstrate that they are sufficient, then closures are unnecessary.

That is the test.
