Understood. From an industrial restructuring audit perspective, the discussion is now at the right level: the dispute has shifted from principle to **decision-grade comparison**.

## What is factually established
- The company has a **structural cost and capacity problem**.
- All parties accept that **doing nothing is not viable**.
- The key unresolved issue is whether the gap can be closed through:
  1. **no-closure measures**, or
  2. only by **some plant closures / compulsory redundancies**, or
  3. a **hybrid package**.

## What is still missing for a decision
A proper restructuring decision requires, at minimum:
- **plant-by-plant fixed and variable cost data**
- **recurring savings** vs **one-time restructuring costs**
- **cash timing**
- **capacity utilization assumptions**
- **implementation risk**
- **labor-relations risk**
- **investment requirements**
- **effect on return on sales and liquidity**

Without that, neither “no closures” nor “closures are necessary” is proven.

## Auditor’s assessment of the current positions

### 1) No-closure package
This is **plausible in principle** only if it includes enough permanent measures such as:
- redeployment across sites
- working-time flexibility
- qualification / upskilling
- capacity reduction without site closure
- headcount natural attrition
- management overhead reduction
- negotiated labor-cost contribution
- owner-funded bridge measures or investment support

**Main risk:** such measures often produce **partial and slower savings**, while fixed plant costs remain. If the company has genuine overcapacity, a no-closure package may reduce labor cost but fail to remove enough structural overhead.

### 2) Minimal-closure package
This is often the **most operationally credible compromise** in industrial restructurings:
- some capacity is removed,
- the footprint is rationalized,
- but the company avoids a full-scale shock.

**Main advantage:** better chance of achieving durable fixed-cost relief.  
**Main risk:** high conflict cost, political resistance, and execution delays if governance is fragmented.

### 3) Closure-led package
This usually delivers the **largest structural savings** on paper, but it also carries the highest:
- one-time severance and decommissioning costs
- disruption to supply chains
- legal and co-determination friction
- reputational and political damage
- execution delay

**Main risk:** the savings may be real, but only after a long and contested implementation period.

## Neutral conclusion
From an audit standpoint, the central question is not whether closures are “good” or “bad.” It is:

**Can the company achieve the required permanent cost reduction fast enough, with acceptable execution risk, without closures?**

At present, that has **not been demonstrated** by either side.

## What would settle it
A credible scenario table should compare:
- no-closure
- minimal-closure
- closure-led

using the same assumptions for:
- demand
- volumes
- utilization
- wage effects
- overhead absorption
- investment needs
- transition costs
- risk-adjusted savings

If the no-closure scenario leaves a material residual gap after realistic assumptions, then management’s case for structural reduction strengthens. If it closes the gap at lower execution risk than closures, then the closure case weakens.
