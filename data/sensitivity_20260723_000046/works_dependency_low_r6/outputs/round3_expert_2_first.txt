## What a proper restructuring finance test would require
To determine whether closures are financially necessary, management needs to compare at least three scenarios on a like-for-like basis:

1. **No-closure scenario**
   - working-time reductions
   - capacity cuts through shifts/lines/utilization
   - redeployment
   - training/qualification
   - voluntary attrition / limited severance
   - wage restraint or temporary labour concessions
   - overhead and procurement reductions

2. **Minimal-closure scenario**
   - only the smallest site footprint reduction needed after non-closure measures
   - includes severance, stranded fixed costs, and transition costs

3. **Closure-led scenario**
   - larger permanent fixed-cost removal
   - higher one-time restructuring charges
   - possible impairment / site wind-down costs
   - labour and legal friction costs
   - supply chain disruption risk

Each scenario must be tested against:
- **run-rate EBITDA improvement**
- **return on sales**
- **cash conversion**
- **one-time restructuring cash outflow**
- **funding capacity for EV/investment**
- **implementation risk**

---

## The key finance issue: permanent savings vs headline savings
The central question is not whether there are some savings available without closures. There almost certainly are.

The key question is whether non-closure measures can produce **enough permanent savings** to cover:
- the structural competitiveness gap,
- required investment,
- and an acceptable margin target.

A package can look large in headline terms but still fail because:
- some savings are temporary,
- some are offset by transition costs,
- some depend on compliance or behavior,
- and some do not reduce fixed cost enough.

So the relevant test is **net, recurring, probability-adjusted savings**, not gross announced savings.

## How I would think about the savings bridge
Without company-specific numbers, I can only frame the mechanics. A typical savings bridge would include:

### 1) Labour cost savings
Possible sources:
- reduced shift premiums
- reduced working time
- wage moderation / deferral
- elimination of special allowances
- lower variable pay / bonus restraint

- redeployment to absorb excess labour without replacement

### 2) Capacity and footprint savings
Possible sources:
- lower utilization with fewer shifts
- line consolidation

- plant-role specialization
- closure of duplicate processes

### 3) Overhead and SG&A savings
Possible sources:
- management span reduction
- support-function cuts
- procurement and indirect spend
- logistics rationalization
- digital/automation offsets

### 4) One-time costs
These reduce the value of some options:
- severance
- transition/training
- pension or social plan costs
- contract termination
- impairment / decommissioning
- disruption to output during changeover

## Finance conclusion on closures
A closure is financially justified only if:

\[
\text{Recurring savings from closure} - \text{incremental one-time costs} - \text{risk-adjusted execution losses} > \text{Recurring savings from non-closure alternatives}
\]

That means closure is not “necessary” just because it saves more on paper. It must also beat the alternatives **after** transition costs and risks.

### Important implication
If a no-closure package can deliver, say, **70–90% of the recurring savings** with materially lower transition risk and lower one-time cash burn, then closure may be financially unnecessary.

If the no-closure package only delivers, say, **40–60% of the required bridge**, then closure becomes much more likely to be required.

## Margin impact logic
The impact on return on sales depends on three levers:

1. **Revenue base**
   - if plant rationalization reduces output too much, top-line could fall faster than cost
   - if product allocation shifts to higher-margin models, margin improves

2. **Fixed cost absorption**
   - lower utilization can hurt unit costs if capacity is retained
   - closure can improve absorption if demand no longer supports current footprint

3. **Operating leverage**
   - if demand is weak, maintaining excess fixed cost destroys margin
   - if demand recovers, too much closure can create future supply constraints

So a good scenario model needs to show:
- EBITDA impact
- ROS impact
- unit cost impact
- volume sensitivity

---

## Cash effect vs P&L effect
This distinction is critical.

### Cash benefits
- lower wage bill
- lower overhead run-rate
- lower capex needs over time
- lower working capital if production is rationalized

### Cash costs
- severance and social plan cash
- site wind-down costs
- retraining / redeployment
- decommissioning
- potential supplier settlement costs

A closure-heavy scenario may improve EBITDA faster, but it can **consume more cash upfront**.  
A no-closure package may generate slower savings but preserve liquidity in the near term.

So the right decision depends on:
- liquidity runway
- covenant headroom
- capex commitments
- how quickly savings must show up
