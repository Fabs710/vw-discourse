## What a proper restructuring finance test would require
To determine whether closures are financially necessary, management needs to compare at least three scenarios on a like-for-like basis:

1. **No-closure scenario**

2. **Minimal-closure scenario**
   - only the smallest site footprint reduction needed after non-closure measures
   - includes severance, stranded fixed costs, and transition costs

3. **Closure-led scenario**
   - larger permanent fixed-cost removal
   - higher one-time restructuring charges
   - possible impairment / site wind-down costs
   - labour and legal friction costs
   - supply chain disruption risk

Each scenario must be tested against:

- **return on sales**
- **cash conversion**
- **one-time restructuring cash outflow**
- **funding capacity for EV/investment**
- **implementation risk**

## The key finance issue: permanent savings vs headline savings
The central question is not whether there are some savings available without closures. There almost certainly are.

The key question is whether non-closure measures can produce **enough permanent savings** to cover:
- the structural competitiveness gap,
- required investment,
- and an acceptable margin target.

A package can look large in headline terms but still fail because:
- some savings are temporary,
- some are offset by transition costs,
- some depend on compliance or behavior,
- and some do not reduce fixed cost enough.

So the relevant test is **net, recurring, probability-adjusted savings**, not gross announced savings.

---

## How I would think about the savings bridge
Without company-specific numbers, I can only frame the mechanics. A typical savings bridge would include:

### 1) Labour cost savings
Possible sources:
- reduced shift premiums
- reduced working time
- wage moderation / deferral
- elimination of special allowances
- lower variable pay / bonus restraint
- natural attrition
- redeployment to absorb excess labour without replacement

### 2) Capacity and footprint savings
Possible sources:
- lower utilization with fewer shifts
- line consolidation
- product allocation changes
- plant-role specialization
- closure of duplicate processes

### 3) Overhead and SG&A savings
Possible sources:
- management span reduction
- support-function cuts
- procurement and indirect spend
- logistics rationalization
- digital/automation offsets

### 4) One-time costs
These reduce the value of some options:
- severance
- transition/training
- pension or social plan costs
- contract termination
- impairment / decommissioning
- disruption to output during changeover

---

## Finance conclusion on closures
A closure is financially justified only if:

\[
\text{Recurring savings from closure} - \text{incremental one-time costs} - \text{risk-adjusted execution losses} > \text{Recurring savings from non-closure alternatives}
\]

That means closure is not “necessary” just because it saves more on paper. It must also beat the alternatives **after** transition costs and risks.

### Important implication
If a no-closure package can deliver, say, **70–90% of the recurring savings** with materially lower transition risk and lower one-time cash burn, then closure may be financially unnecessary.

If the no-closure package only delivers, say, **40–60% of the required bridge**, then closure becomes much more likely to be required.

---

## Margin impact logic
The impact on return on sales depends on three levers:

1. **Revenue base**
   - if plant rationalization reduces output too much, top-line could fall faster than cost
   - if product allocation shifts to higher-margin models, margin improves

2. **Fixed cost absorption**
   - lower utilization can hurt unit costs if capacity is retained
   - closure can improve absorption if demand no longer supports current footprint

3. **Operating leverage**
   - if demand is weak, maintaining excess fixed cost destroys margin
   - if demand recovers, too much closure can create future supply constraints

So a good scenario model needs to show:
- EBITDA impact
- ROS impact
- unit cost impact
- volume sensitivity

---

## Cash effect vs P&L effect
This distinction is critical.

### Cash benefits
- lower wage bill
- lower overhead run-rate
- lower capex needs over time
- lower working capital if production is rationalized

### Cash costs
- severance and social plan cash
- site wind-down costs
- retraining / redeployment
- decommissioning
- potential supplier settlement costs

A closure-heavy scenario may improve EBITDA faster, but it can **consume more cash upfront**.  
A no-closure package may generate slower savings but preserve liquidity in the near term.

So the right decision depends on:
- liquidity runway
- covenant headroom
- capex commitments
- how quickly savings must show up

## 1. Decision context
The company faces a structural competitiveness problem: overcapacity, high German cost levels, weak European demand, and pressure from lower-cost competitors. Management seeks a durable restructuring that reduces labour and capacity costs, potentially including plant closures and compulsory redundancies; employee-side stakeholders reject that as the default path. Governance is unusually constraining: co-determination gives the workforce effective veto power, the controlling family has majority voting influence, the regional state holds blocking leverage, and investors want credible value restoration.

## 3. Final positions
### Group & brand management
Management insists that the company must structurally reduce costs and capacity, and that closures must remain available if the numbers require them. It accepts that a cosmetic compromise is worthless and that burden-sharing should not fall only on workers, but it will not endorse a no-closure principle before a decision-grade comparison shows it can close the gap.

### Works council
The works council takes the hardest line against closures and compulsory redundancies, and demands binding site/job guarantees through 2030. It accepts working-time, redeployment, qualification, and limited employee contributions, but only inside a framework that preserves all German sites and co-determination protections.

### Industrial union
The union aligns with the no-compulsory-redundancies, no-one-sided-burden line and wants a quantified no-closure package tested first. It is more process-focused than the works council: it wants hard numbers and a credible industrial package, but still rejects forced layoffs as a first instrument.

### Regional state government
The state strongly resists closures and forced redundancies as first-line measures and wants the no-closure route tested thoroughly. It will support restructuring only if burden-sharing is visible, the regional base is protected as far as economically possible, and the process has a clear deadline rather than indefinite delay.

### Controlling family holding
The family shareholding agrees that the company needs durable restructuring and cannot preserve the status quo by default. However, it will not endorse freezing the structure regardless of economics; closures are possible if proven necessary, but not yet justified without a plant-by-plant case and quantified savings bridge.

### Institutional investors
Investors support material, permanent cost reduction and reject any cosmetic settlement that leaves the company overcosted. They do not want closures as a reflex, but they also do not support locking in the current footprint; their support hinges on a credible, risk-adjusted savings bridge and improved return on sales.

### Independent auditor
The auditor concludes that neither side has yet proven its case: management has not demonstrated that closures are unavoidable, and employee-side actors have not shown that non-closure measures can fully close the gap. The decisive issue is the risk-adjusted savings bridge, not the slogan of “save the plants” versus “close the plants.”

### Finance modeler
The finance modeler formalizes the comparison requirement: no-closure, minimal-closure, and closure-led scenarios should be tested on the same baseline, with recurring savings, one-time costs, cash timing, NPV, payback, margin impact, and downside sensitivity. The modeler implicitly strengthens management’s and investors’ insistence on hard evidence, while also validating the employee-side demand for a real no-closure test.

## 4. Genuine conflicts
1. **Closure legitimacy**
   - Management/investors/family: closures must remain available if economically required.
   - Works council/union/state: closures are not legitimate unless no-closure options are proven insufficient.

2. **Compulsory redundancies**
   - Management and capital-side actors refuse to rule them out categorically.
   - Employee-side stakeholders and state reject them as a first or default measure.

3. **Benchmark for success**
   - Management/investors benchmark against competitiveness, margin, and return on sales.
   - Employee/state stakeholders benchmark against preserving sites, jobs, and co-determination.

4. **Proof standard**
   - Management wants opponents of closures to prove viability of no-closure.
   - Others want management to prove necessity of closures.

5. **Freedom to act vs. precommitment**
   - Management and investors want fallback flexibility.
   - Works council and union want binding guarantees that reduce management discretion.

## 6. Key trade-offs for the decision-maker
1. **Site preservation vs. durable cost reduction**
   - Preserving all sites may be socially and politically valuable, but could leave structural costs too high.

2. **Immediate industrial peace vs. future enforcement**
   - A negotiated no-closure deal may reduce conflict now, but needs credible triggers if savings are missed.

3. **Job security vs. strategic flexibility**
   - Strong guarantees stabilize the workforce, but reduce management’s ability to respond if markets worsen.

4. **One-time pain vs. long-run competitiveness**
   - Closures impose severe upfront costs and conflict, but may produce stronger fixed-cost relief.

5. **Governance legitimacy vs. economic efficiency**
   - Co-determination and blocking-minority rights mean legitimacy matters as much as economics.

## 7. Integrative pathways
### A. Time-limited no-closure first test
A short, audited phase to quantify whether a no-closure package can fully close the gap, using the same assumptions as closure scenarios. This satisfies the employee/state demand for proof while preserving management’s fallback options.

### B. Conditional restructuring with triggers
Immediate non-closure measures are implemented, while pre-agreed fallback measures activate only if savings, utilization, or cash milestones are missed. This creates discipline without immediate irreversible closure decisions.

### C. Shared-sacrifice package
Workforce contributions are explicitly matched by management cuts, owner contributions, and investment commitments, with a protected implementation window. This addresses the fairness objection and can build coalition support.

### D. Minimal-closure as compromise fallback
If the audit shows the no-closure gap is too large, a limited closure or partial footprint reduction becomes the least-disruptive structural option. This is the most plausible bridge if the no-closure scenario fails.

- What trigger thresholds would justify fallback closures?
- What investment commitments are credible and enforceable?
- How much job security can be guaranteed without undermining restructuring?
- What decision rule will the supervisory board accept if scenarios are close?
