I will be direct.

## To management
You are still asking us to accept the logic of closures before you have shown, in a decision-grade way, that they are unavoidable. I do **not** accept that.

Yes, the company has a structural competitiveness problem. Yes, the cost base must come down. Yes, the solution must be durable. On those points, we are aligned.

But your position still rests on a **claim**, not a proven case:
- that no-closure measures are insufficient,
- that compulsory redundancies are unavoidable,
- and that plant closures are the necessary instrument.

That has **not** been demonstrated.

Until you produce a transparent, site-by-site comparison with real numbers, closures remain a **choice**. And as the state shareholder with a blocking minority and a responsibility for the regional economy, I am not prepared to treat that choice as acceptable by default.

## To the works council and union
I agree with your core line: the workforce must not be made the shock absorber for management’s strategic errors. No forced redundancies as a first instrument, no one-sided burden, and no hollowing out of co-determination.

Where I want to be careful is in how we frame the next step. I support a **no-closure-first test** because it is the only responsible way to establish whether the company can be restructured without lasting damage to German sites and jobs.

But I also need a serious answer from the employee side: a no-closure position is not enough by itself. It must be translated into a **credible industrial package**:
- capacity reduction through non-closure means,
- working-time flexibility,
- redeployment,
- qualification,
- limited and negotiated contributions,
- and management/owner restraint.

If that package can truly close the gap, then it deserves support. If it cannot, then we are not being honest with the workforce either.

So I support the red lines:
- no plant closures as the default,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- no weakening of co-determination,
- no dismantling of site security.

But I will not endorse a package that merely preserves the status quo while leaving the company structurally weak.

## To the controlling family and institutional investors
I understand your insistence on economic credibility. I share that concern.

What I do **not** accept is using “credibility” as a euphemism for making closures and job cuts the easy answer. That is not long-term value creation if it destroys the company’s domestic anchor, industrial skills, and social partnership.

Long-term value requires:
- a viable cost structure,
- yes,
- but also stable industrial relations,
- retained skills,
- credible investment in future products,
- and regional legitimacy.

If you want the state to support a deep restructuring, then the burden of adjustment must be shared visibly and fairly. That means management, owners, and capital also contributing materially — not just asking the workforce to absorb the pain.

## On the experts’ point
The independent auditor and finance modeler are right about one thing: we need a **single audited scenario table**.

I support that fully. But it must be used correctly.

The table must compare:
1. no-closure scenario,
2. minimal-closure scenario,
3. closure-led scenario,

with:
- recurring savings,
- one-time costs,
- cash timing,
- margin impact,
- implementation risk,
- and investment implications.

And crucially: the comparison must also show the implications for:
- regional employment,
- supplier networks,
- co-determination,
- and the company’s role as an industrial anchor.

If a closure-led scenario is only marginally better on paper but causes major regional damage and governance conflict, then it is not automatically the better option. That is the whole point of having anchor shareholders and co-determination in the first place.

## My position
I am prepared to support a serious restructuring if it meets these conditions:
- the savings gap is presented transparently,
- no-closure and minimal-closure options are tested honestly,
- management and owners contribute visibly,
- workers contribute in a limited, negotiated, and fair way,
- investment in future products is secured,
- and German sites are preserved wherever economically possible.

What I am **not** prepared to support:
- closures as a first resort,
- compulsory redundancies as an assumed default,
- a deal that shifts the burden mainly onto workers,
- or any outcome that weakens the state’s co-determination and veto role.

## What I want next
Before anyone talks about irreversible steps, bring me:
- the exact savings gap,
- plant-by-plant economics,
- the no-closure bridge,
- the minimal-closure bridge,
- management and owner contribution,
- workforce contribution,
- investment commitments,
- and the likely effect on jobs, sites, and margins through 2030.

If the no-closure route fails that test, then we can have a harder discussion.  
But until then, I will not lend political or shareholder support to closures being treated as inevitable.

## Final word
We are not defending the status quo for its own sake. We are defending the company’s future **without sacrificing its domestic industrial base unnecessarily**.

That is the line I will hold.
