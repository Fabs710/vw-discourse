I appreciate the union and works council making clear that you want a viable industrial future. I agree on one point: the company needs a **durable** solution, not a symbolic one.

But let me be equally direct: **a solution that rules out closures, compulsory redundancies, and meaningful labour-cost adjustment from the outset is not a serious answer to the current crisis**.

We are not dealing with a normal cycle. We are facing:
- structurally too high German factory costs,
- shrinking European demand,
- intensified price pressure from low-cost and Chinese competitors,
- and a brand that is still below its return-on-sales target.

In that situation, “efficiency measures” alone will not restore competitiveness. If the cost base remains unchanged, we simply postpone the problem and weaken the company further. That is not responsible management.

So my position is clear:

1. **We need a structural reduction in capacity and cost.**
   That may include plant closures if necessary. I am not treating closures as a rhetorical threat; they are on the table because the current footprint is oversized for the market reality.

2. **We need a genuine labour-cost contribution.**
   Not cosmetic savings. Not a temporary pause. A measurable and durable contribution that improves the economics of the German operations.

3. **We need room to invest in future products and technology.**
   If the current cost base consumes all available cash, there is no future industrial strategy to protect.

Where I do agree with you:
- A negotiated solution is preferable to unmanaged conflict.
- Internal redeployment, qualification, and targeted capacity adjustments should be part of the package.
- Future investment commitments matter.

Where I disagree:
- Binding 2030 guarantees that freeze the current structure are not compatible with restoring competitiveness.
- No compulsory redundancies cannot be an absolute line if the scale of overcapacity is real.
- Plant closures cannot be ruled out in advance by principle, because that would preserve inefficiency and shift the burden onto the company’s future.

I am open to alternatives **if** they deliver the same economic result:
- materially lower fixed costs,
- lower production capacity,
- and a credible path back to target margins.

But if the proposal is “keep everything open and search for savings at the edges,” then I have to reject it. That would be a cosmetic deal, and we cannot afford that.

So my message back to both of you is simple: bring me a package that matches the scale of the crisis. If you want to avoid closures, it must still produce the same structural savings. Otherwise, the company will have to pursue the hard options.
