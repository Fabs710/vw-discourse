I will be equally clear.

I agree with one part of the discussion: **the company cannot be allowed to drift into permanent underperformance**. The cost problem is real, the competitive pressure is real, and management is right that the group needs a restructuring that restores margins and frees up room for investment.

But I do **not** accept a framing that treats **plant closures and compulsory redundancies as the default answer**. That would be a serious step with long-term consequences for the company’s industrial base, social stability, and ultimately brand value. As the controlling family shareholder, my concern is not only this year’s cost gap, but whether we preserve a viable, competitive enterprise for the next decade and beyond.

### Where I agree
- The current German cost base has to come down.
- The restructuring must be durable, not cosmetic.
- The company needs space to invest in future products and technology.
- A negotiated solution is preferable to a prolonged confrontation.

### Where I disagree
- I do **not** support a strategy that starts from “closures first” and forces everyone else to react.
- I do **not** believe that permanently weakening the company’s German footprint is consistent with long-term value creation.
- I do **not** think “no compulsory redundancies” can be treated as an absolute if no other route delivers equivalent savings, but equally management cannot present redundancies and closures as the only serious instrument before all alternative structural measures are exhausted.

### To management
If you want owner support, you need to show more than a demand for cuts. You need a **credible operational plan** that actually closes the gap:
- what capacity comes out,
- what fixed costs fall,
- what labour-cost contribution is permanent,
- what investment follows,
- and how competitiveness improves without creating a destructive conflict.

If the plan depends on closing plants, then that must be justified against clearly demonstrated alternatives, not assumed as the first move. And if you want board and owner backing, you need to show that the long-term value of the core asset is being protected, not sacrificed for short-term accounting relief.

### To the works council and union
I hear your refusal of compulsory redundancies and plant closures. I understand why that is your starting point. But I need to be plain: **a blanket refusal of all structural change is not workable**. If the cost base is too high, then some combination of capacity adjustment, work-time flexibility, internal redeployment, and labour-cost contribution will be necessary.

What I would consider serious is:
- capacity reductions that avoid outright closures where possible,
- negotiated working-time and flexibility measures,
- targeted redeployment and qualification,
- a fair employee contribution that is limited but real,
- and binding commitments on future products and investment.

What I would not support is a settlement that preserves every existing structure while merely postponing the adjustment. That would erode competitiveness and, in time, threaten more jobs than any disciplined restructuring would.

### To the regional state
I agree that the company’s German base matters materially for the region and for the industrial ecosystem. A solution that destroys confidence and triggers a long conflict would be value-destructive. Your insistence on social responsibility is reasonable.

But I would also caution against turning political pressure into an implicit veto over any hard decision. The company cannot be managed by symbolism. If the economics require difficult adjustments, then the governance bodies need to be able to act. The test is whether the final package restores viability while preserving as much of the industrial base as possible — not whether every painful measure is avoided at all costs.

### My position
I am prepared to back a **serious restructuring package** if it meets three conditions:
1. It delivers a durable improvement in margins and competitiveness.
2. It avoids unnecessary instability and preserves labour peace.
3. It protects the long-term value and governability of the enterprise.

What I will **not** back is a solution that leaves the company structurally weak while pretending that compromise alone is a strategy.

So my message is: bring forward a package that is economically credible, not ideologically comfortable. If there is a way to achieve the required savings without closures, I will listen. If there is not, then the company must have the freedom to take the necessary steps.
