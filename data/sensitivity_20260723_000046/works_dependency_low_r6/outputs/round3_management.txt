## To the works council
You continue to demand **“no plant closures, no compulsory redundancies, binding guarantees through 2030”** as if those statements could substitute for a restructuring solution. They cannot.

I agree with you on one point: **the company needs a durable solution, not a temporary patch.**  
Where I disagree is everything that follows from your red lines.

You say we have not proven that closures are necessary. My answer is simple: **the burden of proof is not satisfied by repeating that you reject closures.** We have a structural cost problem, shrinking demand, and a brand that is missing its return target. If you want a no-closure path, then show me a package that removes enough cost **permanently** and **site by site**.

So far, what I hear is:
- working-time flexibility,
- redeployment,
- training,
- limited contributions,
- and a request for guarantees.

Those may help, but they do not yet add up to the level of structural savings required. A cosmetic deal that preserves the current cost base is not acceptable.

## To the union
I agree that workers cannot be the only ones paying for the crisis. That is true.  
I do **not** agree that this means plant closures and compulsory redundancies must be excluded in advance.

You are right to ask for:
- management restraint,
- owner contribution,
- investment commitments,
- and a fair burden-sharing package.

But again: those are inputs, not outcomes. The question is whether they produce enough durable savings to restore competitiveness. At this point, that has not been demonstrated.

And I want to be explicit: **no compulsory redundancies as an absolute principle is not compatible with a serious restructuring unless an equivalent alternative is proven.** If it is not proven, then we need flexibility.

## To the regional state
I understand your concern about regional employment and supplier networks. I do not dismiss it. But I must be clear: **blocking necessary restructuring does not protect the region; it postpones the pain and makes the eventual adjustment worse.**

You ask for a no-closure or minimal-closure package with quantified savings. That is exactly what I have been asking for as well. But let us not pretend the answer is already known. If the economics require a smaller footprint, then the company cannot be forced to preserve everything by political veto.

I am prepared to negotiate, but not to accept a settlement that leaves the company structurally uncompetitive.

## To the family holding
You are right that we need a serious package, not a cosmetic one. I agree.  
Where I part company with you is on the idea that we should first exhaust a no-closure path as if closures were somehow a failure of imagination.

We have already tested enough imagination. The issue now is **economic sufficiency**. If the alternative package closes the gap, fine. If it does not, then the company must be allowed to take the necessary structural steps.

## To institutional investors
You are correctly focused on credibility and implementation. I agree with that.  
But credibility is not the same thing as preserving every site.

If a no-closure route truly delivers:
- permanent savings,
- capacity alignment,
- labour-cost reduction,
- and a credible path back to margin targets,

then that is preferable. But if it does not, then we cannot lock ourselves into an inferior outcome because it is more comfortable politically.

## My position, clearly
I am prepared to consider alternatives to closures **only if they achieve the same structural result**:
- real and permanent cost reduction,
- capacity reduction aligned with market demand,
- measurable labour-cost contribution,
- and enough headroom to invest in future products and technology through 2030.

What I will **not** accept is:
- a promise of job security that freezes the structure,
- a settlement that avoids hard decisions but leaves the company overcosted,
- or a deal that delays the adjustment and drains our ability to invest.

## On the question of numbers
The next step must be concrete. I agree with that. But concrete means more than slogans from the employee side. It means:
- exact savings gap,
- plant-by-plant economics,
- permanent versus temporary savings,
- comparison of no-closure and minimal-closure scenarios,
- and the impact on return on sales.

If the no-closure alternative is genuinely sufficient, I will engage with it.  
If it is not sufficient, then closures must remain available.

## What I am prepared to do
I am prepared to bring management-side transparency on the cost gap and scenario comparisons. I am also prepared to discuss:
- working-time changes,
- internal redeployment,
- qualification,
- non-wage cost restraint,
- and a binding investment roadmap.

But I will not pretend those measures are automatically enough. They may not be.

## Bottom line
I am not negotiating against restructuring. I am negotiating **for a restructuring that actually works**.

So let us stop treating “no closures” as the default answer and start testing whether it is economically viable. If it is not viable, then it is not a solution.

I am open to a negotiated deal.  
I am **not** open to a deal that preserves an uncompetitive cost base and calls that success.
