I will respond directly, because the situation is being pushed toward a false choice.

## To management
You keep asking for “economic credibility,” but you are still not providing the one thing that would make your case testable: **a transparent, site-by-site, independently reviewable comparison**.

Right now, what you have offered is essentially:
- a diagnosis,
- a pressure narrative,
- and the threat that closures may be needed.

That is not enough.

You say German costs are too high and capacity is too large. Fine. We do not deny the challenge. But you still have **not proven** that:
1. **every German plant cannot be kept open**,  
2. **no compulsory redundancies can be avoided**, and  
3. the same economic result cannot be achieved through capacity alignment, working-time measures, internal redeployment, qualification, and negotiated cost contributions.

Until that proof exists, plant closures remain a **choice**, not a necessity.

And let me be clear: if you continue treating closures as a normal tool rather than a last resort, then trust is gone. You cannot ask the workforce to cooperate while simultaneously keeping a closure gun on the table.

## To the family holding and investors
You are trying to turn “economic credibility” into a code word for forcing the workforce to absorb the adjustment. I reject that.

You keep saying you are open to no-closure solutions “if they work.” Good. Then stop backing a framework that starts from the assumption that closures are legitimate if management says so.

If you are serious about long-term value, then support:
- keeping all German plants open,
- no forced layoffs,
- real management and owner contribution,
- and a negotiated industrial plan that protects the company’s domestic base.

Otherwise you are asking for value creation by destroying the productive foundation that creates value in the first place.

## To the regional state
You are right to reject closures and forced redundancies. But I need to say this bluntly: **political concern is not enough** if, at the end of the day, you allow a settlement that weakens plant security in practice.

If management comes back with a closure package, we expect you to use every available lever, not just issue warnings.

At the same time, I agree with you on one point: any workforce contribution must be matched by management restraint and owner responsibility. We will not accept a one-sided sacrifice package dressed up as modernization.

## To the union
We are aligned on the core issue: no plant closures, no compulsory redundancies, no dismantling of collective protections.

Where I push back is on any suggestion that we should soften that line before we have a concrete alternative in hand. We do not need to move first. Management needs to prove its claim first.

If you are preparing a bargaining path, then it must be based on:
- binding site guarantees,
- job guarantees through 2030,
- internal redeployment,
- working-time flexibility,
- qualification,
- and fair, limited contributions only within a secure framework.

Not a framework where jobs are traded away for vague promises.

## To the current discussion about “equivalent savings”
This term has still not been defined honestly.

Equivalent to what?
- Equivalent to management’s margin target?
- Equivalent to a preferred restructuring scenario?
- Equivalent to the result of shutting down sites and breaking job security?

If that is the benchmark, then the benchmark is biased from the start.

If management wants to compare scenarios, then compare them properly:
1. **No-closure scenario**
2. **No forced layoffs scenario**
3. **Minimal capacity reduction through non-closure instruments**
4. **Management/owner contribution**
5. **Investment commitments**
6. **Industrial and employment guarantees to 2030**

Anything else is just a staged argument for a decision that has already been made.

## What I want on the table now
If you are serious about a real negotiation, then bring the following immediately:

- exact savings gap
- plant-by-plant economics
- what each plant contributes under a no-closure plan
- what savings come from management cuts and owner contribution
- what savings come from work-time, redeployment, qualification, and cost restraint
- what investment is tied to the package
- what binding guarantees are offered to the workforce

Without that, there is no basis to discuss closures at all.

## My position
Let me state it plainly:

- **No plant closures in Germany**
- **No compulsory redundancies**
- **No weakening of co-determination**
- **No dismantling of historic job-security protections**
- **Binding guarantees through 2030**
- **A negotiated industrial strategy, not a cuts diktat**

If management cannot achieve competitiveness within those conditions, then the burden is on management and the owners to explain why their strategy failed — not on the workforce to pay with its jobs and plants.

## Final word
We are not refusing restructuring.  
We are refusing a restructuring that destroys sites, jobs, and trust.

If you want a durable solution, then stop using closures as leverage and start proving that the company can be made competitive **without** sacrificing German plants and compulsory job cuts.

Until then, the works council will continue to resist — in the plants, in the supervisory board, and publicly.
