I will respond directly.

First, I appreciate that the works council and the union are at least acknowledging the scale of the competitiveness problem. And I note the regional state’s and family holding’s recognition that this cannot be allowed to drift. That matters.

But let me be equally clear: **none of the responses so far is yet sufficient to solve the actual problem**.

## Where I agree
I agree on four points:
- the company needs a **durable** solution, not a temporary patch;
- we need **future investment** in products and technology;
- any restructuring should, if possible, be **negotiated** rather than imposed;
- and a solution that produces prolonged industrial conflict would damage everyone.

## Where I disagree
I do **not** agree with the attempt to make “no plant closures” and “no compulsory redundancies” into absolute starting points. That position may be understandable politically, but economically it does not answer the crisis.

I also do **not** accept the idea that management has simply “asserted” the need for closures without evidence. We have a clear structural problem:
- German factory costs are far above target;
- European demand is shrinking;
- competitive pressure is intensifying;
- and the brand is missing its return-on-sales target.

In that context, preserving the current footprint by principle is not a plan. It is a refusal to confront reality.

## To the works council
You say closures are a threat, not a tool. I disagree. They are a tool of last resort, but they remain a necessary tool if the footprint is too large for the market.

You demand:
- every German plant stays open,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- binding guarantees through 2030.

That is not compatible with the scale of restructuring required unless you can show equivalent, permanent savings elsewhere. So far, I have not heard that.

What I hear instead is:
- working-time flexibility,
- redeployment,
- training,
- a fair contribution,
- and vague calls for management and owner sacrifice.

Those can be part of a package, but they do **not** yet add up to the deep, structural reduction in cost and capacity that the company needs.

If you want to avoid closures, then show a **site-by-site, quantified alternative** that closes the cost gap permanently. Not aspirational language. Numbers.

## To the union
You are right that the burden cannot fall only on workers. I agree with that. But I reject the conclusion that therefore plant closures and redundancies must be taken off the table.

You say no compulsory redundancies, no plant closures, no dismantling of the collective framework. That combination is too restrictive unless you are able to deliver the same economic outcome through other means.

I am open to:
- working-time changes,
- capacity reductions,
- internal redeployment,
- qualification,
- limited and temporary employee contributions,
- bonus restraint,
- and a binding investment roadmap.

But I will not pretend those automatically solve the cost problem. They might reduce the pain, but they may not reduce the structural burden enough.

## To the regional state
I hear your political concern. But I must also say this plainly: a blocking posture that prevents necessary restructuring would not protect the region in the long run. It would merely delay the adjustment until the company is weaker and options are worse.

If the state wants German industrial capacity to remain strong, then it must support a package that actually restores competitiveness. That means accepting that some sites, functions, or capacity may have to be reduced if that is what the economics require.

“Socially responsible” cannot mean “economically insufficient.”

## To the family holding
I agree with you that long-term value creation matters, and that closures should not be the first reflex. But let me push back on one point: support for a restructuring package cannot mean backing a process that is so constrained by job guarantees and political caution that it fails to fix the business.

If the company keeps an oversized cost base in order to avoid confrontation, then we will all pay later in weaker margins, weaker investment capacity, and weaker competitiveness.

## To institutional investors
You are right to demand durability and implementation certainty. That is exactly why I am insisting on structural action. Temporary concessions or soft promises will not restore market confidence. We need a package that actually changes the economics.

## What I am prepared to consider
I am **not** insisting on closures as an ideological end in themselves.

I am prepared to consider alternatives **if and only if** they deliver equivalent, durable savings:
- capacity reductions that are real, not cosmetic;
- workforce adjustments that materially reduce fixed costs;
- labour-cost contributions that are measurable and lasting;
- internal redeployment where feasible;
- and investment commitments tied to the restructuring.

But I will not sign up to a package that:
- preserves all plants by default,
- rules out compulsory redundancies absolutely,
- and hopes that a mix of soft measures will somehow close a hard cost gap.

That would be a cosmetic deal, and I will not accept it.

## My challenge back to you
If you reject closures, then put forward a package that answers three questions clearly:

1. **How much structural cost comes out?**
2. **From where, specifically?**
3. **Why is that sufficient to restore the return-on-sales target by 2030?**

If you cannot answer those questions, then your position is not a restructuring proposal. It is a defensive stance.

I remain willing to negotiate a serious deal. But I will only accept a package that delivers:
- durable cost reduction,
- capacity alignment,
- and a credible path back to competitiveness.

Anything less leaves the company exposed, and I will not endorse that.
