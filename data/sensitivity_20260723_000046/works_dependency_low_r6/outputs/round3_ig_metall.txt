I will respond plainly.

## To the works council
We are aligned on the essentials:
- no compulsory redundancies,
- no one-sided burden on workers,
- no hollowing out of co-determination,
- and a negotiated industrial future instead of a diktat.

But I need to push back on one thing: **“every German plant stays open” as an absolute line is politically powerful, but it is not yet an economic answer.** If we lock that in before the numbers are tested, we risk weakening our own position later.

That does **not** mean I accept closures. It means I will not let management turn the debate into a yes/no ritual without evidence. If they want to argue for closures, they must prove why the no-closure path fails — site by site, with numbers, and with an honest comparison.

So I stand with you on the red lines, but I want the next step to be a **quantified no-closure package**, not just a repeat of the slogan.

## To management
You keep saying “economic credibility,” but you still have not given us a credible, testable comparison.

Your argument remains:
- costs are too high,
- demand is weaker,
- margins are under pressure,
- therefore closures may be necessary.

That is not yet proof. It is a preference for a hard restructuring path.

If you want us to engage seriously, then put forward:
- the exact savings gap,
- site-by-site economics,
- what savings come from management and owner contribution,
- what savings come from work-time changes, redeployment, qualification, and capacity reduction,
- and what future investment comes with the package.

Without that, your closure talk remains leverage, not necessity.

And let me be very clear: **compulsory redundancies are not an acceptable first instrument.** If you come with a package that assumes forced layoffs as the default, we will fight it.

## To the regional state
I appreciate that you reject closures and forced redundancies and that you are willing to use your influence. That matters.

But I also need to say this: if the state wants to be taken seriously as a counterweight, it must not merely ask for “social responsibility.” It must insist on a package where:
- workers contribute in a limited and negotiated way,
- management contributes visibly,
- owners contribute visibly,
- and sites and jobs are protected.

If that balance is not there, then the burden is being shifted onto the workforce, and we will not accept that.

## To the family holding
You say you want long-term value and are open to no-closure solutions if they work. Fine. Then stop treating closures as a normal fallback before the alternatives have been fully tested.

Long-term value is not protected by breaking the wage framework and forcing insecurity onto the workforce. It is protected by a settlement that:
- preserves industrial capability,
- keeps skills in-house,
- and shares the adjustment fairly.

I agree that cosmetic deals are bad. But a “serious” deal is not one that uses the workforce as the adjustment buffer.

## To institutional investors
You are asking for implementation certainty and durable savings. I understand that.

But from our side, durability cannot mean permanent job insecurity. A settlement that destroys trust, triggers conflict, and weakens the collective framework is not durable — it is unstable.

If you want a credible, market-facing solution, then back a package that includes:
- real capacity adjustment,
- limited employee contribution,
- management restraint,
- owner contribution,
- and clear investment commitments.

Do not back a settlement that simply re-labels layoffs as “economic credibility.”

## Our position
Let me state it without ambiguity:

We are prepared to negotiate a serious package, including:
- working-time adjustments,
- capacity reductions,
- internal redeployment,
- qualification and training,
- bonus restraint and other limited employee contributions,
- and a binding industrial roadmap.

But our limits are equally clear:
- **no compulsory redundancies**
- **no plant closures as the default answer**
- **no one-sided transfer of crisis costs onto employees**
- **no dismantling of the collective wage framework**

If management wants more from workers, then management, owners, and capital must also put more on the table.

## What we need next
If this is to move forward, the next round must be about **numbers**, not pressure:
1. exact savings gap,
2. no-closure scenario,
3. minimal-closure scenario,
4. management and owner contribution,
5. investment and product commitments,
6. job and site security to 2030.

If management cannot show that the no-closure route is insufficient, then it should stop threatening closures and start negotiating seriously.

## Final word
We are not going to surrender jobs, sites, and wage standards because management prefers a harsher route.

If there is a fair, shared-sacrifice settlement that protects employment and gives the company a future, we are ready to negotiate it. If there is not, then we will defend the workforce with the means available to us.
