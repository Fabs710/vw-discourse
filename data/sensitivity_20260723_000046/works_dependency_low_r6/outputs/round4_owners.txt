I will respond plainly.

## To management
I agree with you on the diagnosis: the company has a structural cost and competitiveness problem, and a cosmetic deal would be value-destructive. I also agree that the restructuring must be durable and not just defer the problem.

Where I disagree is with the way you are still treating closures as a near-default legitimacy point before the evidence is decision-grade.

You are right that flexibility must remain on the table. But flexibility is not the same as justification. So far, I have not seen:
- a plant-by-plant economics case,
- a quantified savings bridge,
- a clear split between recurring and temporary savings,
- or a credible demonstration that non-closure measures cannot close the gap.

Until that is shown, closures are **possible**, not **proven necessary**.

And I want to be explicit: I will not back an outcome that permanently weakens the enterprise’s value or the owners’ governance control through a rushed, conflict-driven decision. Value destruction through prolonged confrontation is also unacceptable.

## To the works council and union
I understand and largely share your resistance to a closure-first logic and to making the workforce the sole shock absorber. That part of your case is legitimate.

I also agree with you that workers cannot be asked to surrender protections while management and ownership stay passive. There must be visible contribution from management and some sacrifice from capital as well.

Where I do **not** agree is in treating:
- “no plant closures in Germany,”
- “no compulsory redundancies,”
- and “binding guarantees through 2030”

as settled outcomes before the economics are tested.

That is too rigid as a governance position. If the independent analysis shows a no-closure package really closes the gap permanently, I will support that strongly. If it does not, then refusing all structural adjustment in advance would be irresponsible stewardship.

So I support:
- an audited no-closure scenario,
- a minimal-closure scenario as fallback,
- and a serious burden-sharing package.

I do **not** support a pledge that freezes the structure regardless of the numbers.

## To the regional state
You are right to insist on transparency, regional stability, and real burden-sharing. I respect the state’s role and the special governance constraints.

I agree that closures should not be used casually or as a political threat. I also agree that the domestic industrial base matters.

But I disagree with any reading of the state’s role as a veto against necessary restructuring if the economics genuinely require it. Protecting the region means securing a viable company, not preserving every structure indefinitely.

I would support a no-closure-first test, but only if it is real and not a rhetorical shield. If the no-closure path fails the audit, then the state should not block necessary change purely to avoid difficult headlines.

## To institutional investors
You are right to demand return-on-sales improvement, implementation certainty, and durable savings. I share that focus.

I also agree that a cosmetic compromise would be a mistake. But I do not accept “shareholder value” as a shorthand for forcing through closures before the evidence is complete. Long-term value depends on competitiveness, yes — but also on stable labour relations, preserved capability, and avoiding self-inflicted conflict.

So my position remains:
- show the numbers,
- test the scenarios,
- and prove that the chosen path restores margins sustainably.

If a no-closure package is enough, good. If not, then we must be honest about that. What I will not accept is a half-measure that produces neither stability nor competitiveness.

## On the expert and modeler input
I support the call for a single audited scenario table. That is the right next step.

But let me be clear on the decision logic I expect:
1. **No-closure scenario first**
2. **Minimal-closure scenario as fallback**
3. **Closure-led scenario only if the first two fail**

And for each:
- recurring savings,
- one-time costs,
- cash timing,
- margin impact,
- implementation risk,
- and investment implications.

That comparison must be decision-grade, not a negotiating prop.

## My position
I am open to a negotiated restructuring package if it does all of the following:
- restores competitiveness materially and permanently,
- protects long-term value,
- avoids value-destructive instability,
- and shares the burden fairly among workers, management, and owners.

I am **not** open to:
- a cosmetic compromise,
- a settlement that preserves an uncompetitive cost base,
- or a process that turns closures into leverage rather than a last-resort tool.

So the next step is not another round of slogans. It is the audited comparison.
