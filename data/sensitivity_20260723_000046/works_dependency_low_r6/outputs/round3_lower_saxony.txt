I will be direct.

We all agree the company has a serious competitiveness problem and needs a durable solution. But I cannot support a restructuring logic that treats **German plant closures** and **compulsory redundancies** as normal instruments before the alternatives have been fully, transparently tested. For the state, that is not a neutral efficiency choice — it means lasting damage to regional employment, supplier networks, and the company’s role as an industrial anchor.

## To management
I agree with you on the diagnosis: the cost base is too high, the market is tougher, and we need a real structural answer. I also agree that a purely cosmetic deal would be irresponsible.

Where I disagree is your leap from diagnosis to closure pressure.

You keep saying the burden of proof is not on you. I disagree. If you want the state to accept a course that may remove jobs and capacity from Germany, then you must show, clearly and credibly:
- the exact savings gap,
- what can be achieved without closures,
- what is permanent versus temporary,
- and why no-closure or minimal-closure alternatives are insufficient.

Until that is on the table, closures are not “necessary”; they are one option among several, and for us the least acceptable one.

## To the works council
I support your insistence that the workforce must not pay for management’s strategic failure through plant closures and forced redundancies. That is legitimate.

I also agree that co-determination and the state’s blocking rights are not decorative. They exist to prevent exactly this kind of one-sided restructuring.

But I want to be equally frank: repeating “every German plant stays open” is not enough on its own. If you want broad support, you need to help define a credible industrial alternative that protects sites and jobs **and** closes the cost gap permanently. That means:
- capacity adjustment through non-closure instruments,
- working-time flexibility,
- internal redeployment,
- qualification,
- and a fair, limited workforce contribution.

What I will not accept is a package where the workforce contributes and management simply keeps the same structure intact. That would be asking for sacrifice without a serious restructuring outcome.

## To the union
I agree with you that the burden cannot fall only on workers. Management, owners, and the company itself must also contribute. And I agree that any settlement must preserve industrial peace and future investment.

Where I do not agree is your implicit suggestion that “no closures” is only a political line. For the state, it is also an economic and regional-policy line. We are not prepared to endorse a course that hollowes out the domestic industrial base unless there is no credible alternative.

So yes:
- no compulsory redundancies as the first instrument,
- no one-sided burden shifting,
- binding investment commitments,
- and a fair burden-sharing package.

But if the numbers truly show that a smaller footprint is unavoidable, then I expect the same honesty from all sides. What I will not support is using that possibility as a threat before alternatives are exhausted.

## To the family holding
You are right that this cannot be a cosmetic settlement. I agree long-term value matters.

But long-term value is not created by forcing through a restructuring that damages the state’s industrial base and social partnership model. A deal that may look efficient in a spreadsheet but destabilizes plants, trust, and skills is not sustainable.

If you are serious about long-term value, then support a negotiated package that:
- preserves core German sites where possible,
- avoids forced layoffs,
- delivers real cost reduction,
- and backs future products and investment.

I am open to burden sharing. I am not open to treating the regional economy as expendable collateral.

## To institutional investors
You are right to ask for implementation certainty and durable savings. That is fair.

But economic credibility is not the same as maximizing immediate restructuring severity. If a no-closure or minimal-closure path can genuinely deliver the required savings, that is preferable. If it cannot, then show that transparently.

What I will not accept is a market logic that says: “if it boosts confidence, closures are fine.” Confidence built on regional industrial damage is not stable value creation.

## What I am prepared to support
I am prepared to support a serious restructuring if it has all of the following:
- a transparent, quantified savings gap,
- a real no-closure scenario and a minimal-closure scenario compared honestly,
- capacity adjustment through non-closure instruments wherever possible,
- management and owner contribution,
- limited and negotiated employee contribution,
- binding investment and product commitments,
- and protection of co-determination and the state’s shareholder rights.

## What I cannot accept
I cannot accept:
- plant closures as the default answer,
- compulsory redundancies as the first instrument,
- a package that shifts the burden mainly onto workers and the region,
- or any outcome that weakens the state’s co-determination and veto role.

If management comes back with a closure-driven package without having shown that alternatives fail, then the state will use its rights accordingly. That should be understood clearly.

## [SUMMON: independent industrial restructuring auditor]
We need an independent, site-by-site economic comparison of:
1. no-closure scenario,
2. minimal-closure scenario,
3. management/owner contribution,
4. labour-cost contribution,
5. investment and product commitments,
6. and the resulting impact on margins and return on sales.

Without that, we are still arguing preferences, not evidence.

My bottom line is simple: **we are open to restructuring, but not to a restructuring that sacrifices German sites and jobs before the alternatives are proven insufficient.**
