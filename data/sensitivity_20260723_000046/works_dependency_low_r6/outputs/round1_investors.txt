From the perspective of capital-market shareholders, the key issue is not who “wins” the industrial argument. It is whether the final package **credibly restores competitive margins** and does so with **durable cost reduction**, not a temporary truce that leaves the company structurally weakened.

### Where I agree
I agree with management, the family, and even parts of the political side on one central point: **the current cost base is too high**. If German operations remain materially above peer cost levels, then the equity story remains broken. That means continued margin pressure, weak returns, and a discount in the market. We will not support cosmetic measures that preserve the same economics.

I also agree that the company needs room to invest in future products and technology. But that only matters if the balance sheet and operating margin can actually support those investments.

### Where I disagree
I do **not** agree that the only credible path is plant closures and compulsory redundancies. Those may be necessary if no alternative produces equivalent savings, but they are not an end in themselves. From a shareholder standpoint, the question is simple: **does the package close the competitiveness gap, yes or no?**

If the answer is no, then preserving every site is just value destruction in slow motion.

I also do **not** accept a deal that relies on indefinite labour peace while leaving the structural cost problem unresolved. A prolonged conflict, with no credible savings, would be worse than a hard but clean settlement. Markets will punish uncertainty and drift.

### What we need to see
Any solution we could back must have the following features:

- **Material and durable cost reduction**
- **A credible path back to target margins**
- **Capacity aligned to demand**
- **No open-ended guarantees that freeze the business model**
- **Clear implementation certainty**
- **No settlement that merely shifts costs into the future**

If there is a negotiated path without closures that still delivers the same economics, that is acceptable and likely preferable. But if the alternatives only generate partial or temporary relief, then we would expect management to pursue the deeper structural measures.

### To the works council and union
Your refusal of closures and compulsory redundancies is understandable, but from our standpoint it is **not sufficient** unless you can also deliver equivalent savings. We will not support a package that protects all current structures while leaving shareholders with structurally impaired returns.

If you want our backing, show us:
- quantified savings,
- durability beyond a short-term wage concession,
- and a believable link to future competitiveness.

### To management
We will support a serious restructuring, but not vagueness. We need specifics:
- how much cost comes out,
- on what timeline,
- from which sites or functions,
- and how this improves return on sales.

If you come back with a vague “transformation” story, the market will treat it as weak. If you come back with a credible and enforceable plan, even if it is painful, we can support it.

### Bottom line
We are **not** committed to closures as such. We are committed to **restoring competitive economics**. If that can be achieved through negotiated capacity reduction, work-time adjustment, and meaningful labour-cost contributions, fine. If not, then management should not be boxed in from using the necessary structural tools.

We will judge the outcome by one standard: **does it restore margin discipline and protect long-term value, or does it merely postpone the problem?**
