I will respond directly.

## To the works council
We are aligned on the core outcome:
- no compulsory redundancies,
- no one-sided burden on workers,
- no weakening of co-determination,
- and no settlement that turns the workforce into the shock absorber for management’s mistakes.

Where I differ is in framing. I do **not** want us to make the debate look like a simple constitutional slogan contest. The crucial question is still whether a **quantified, no-closure package** can close the gap **permanently**. If it can, then closures are unnecessary. If it cannot, then management has to explain why — with numbers, not threats.

So I support your red lines, but I do **not** support drifting into an argument that is only “close the plants” versus “save the plants” without a hard economic comparison. We need to force management to show the bridge.

## To management
You are still not answering the central question.

You keep saying:
- costs are too high,
- demand is weak,
- margins are under pressure,
- therefore closures must remain possible.

That is not proof. That is a preferred restructuring path.

If you want us to consider anything beyond non-closure measures, then put the real numbers on the table:
- exact savings gap,
- plant-by-plant economics,
- recurring versus one-time savings,
- impact of redeployment and working-time changes,
- management overhead cuts,
- owner contribution,
- and the investment link.

Until then, closure talk is leverage, not necessity.

And let me be explicit: **compulsory redundancies are not an acceptable first-line instrument.** If your model assumes forced layoffs as the default, we will oppose it.

## To the family holding and investors
You are right about one thing: a cosmetic compromise would be bad. We do not want a fake settlement that leaves the company uncompetitive.

But you are wrong if you think that means the workforce must accept closure pressure or forced layoffs in advance. A serious deal has to prove that the burden is shared fairly:
- management restraint,
- owner contribution,
- worker contribution in a limited and negotiated form,
- and protection of jobs and sites.

If you want long-term value, then stop treating labour as the first and easiest adjustment variable.

## To the regional state
I appreciate that you reject closures and forced redundancies as first instruments. That matters.

But I need to be blunt: if the state supports a package that weakens site security in practice, then all the rhetoric about regional responsibility is meaningless. We need you to insist on:
- a transparent comparison,
- no-closure first,
- no forced layoffs,
- and a real burden-sharing deal.

That means management and owners must also give something material, not just ask for “social peace.”

## On the experts’ scenario work
I support the call for a single audited scenario table. But it has to be used correctly.

The benchmark is **not** “whatever gets management nearest its preferred margin target.”  
The benchmark must also test whether the company can remain competitive **without** sacrificing:
- German plants,
- collective bargaining standards,
- and job security.

If the no-closure scenario only works by gutting those protections, then it is not a fair solution. A durable settlement must survive industrially and socially, not just on a spreadsheet.

## My position
I want to state this very clearly:

We are prepared to negotiate a serious package, including:
- working-time adjustments,
- redeployment,
- qualification,
- capacity reductions through non-closure measures,
- limited employee contributions,
- and a binding industrial roadmap.

But our limits remain:
- **no compulsory redundancies**
- **no plant closures as the default answer**
- **no one-sided transfer of crisis costs onto workers**
- **no dismantling of the collective wage framework**

If management wants more from the workforce, then management, owners, and shareholders must also put more on the table.

## What must happen next
If this is to move forward, then the next round must finally answer:
1. exact savings gap,
2. no-closure scenario,
3. management and owner contribution,
4. employee contribution,
5. investment commitments,
6. job and site security to 2030.

If management cannot show that the no-closure route is insufficient, then it should stop using closures as pressure and negotiate a real industrial compromise.

## Final word
We are not refusing restructuring.  
We are refusing a restructuring that forces workers to pay with their jobs, wages, and plant security while management and owners keep their fallback options intact.

If there is a fair, shared-sacrifice settlement that protects employment and gives the company a future, we are ready to negotiate it. If there is not, then we will defend the workforce with the means available to us.
