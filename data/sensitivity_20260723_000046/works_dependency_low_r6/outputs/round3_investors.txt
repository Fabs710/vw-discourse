We appreciate the independent audit and finance input, because it confirms the key point we care about: **this is not a question of whether restructuring is needed, but whether the package actually restores competitive margins.**

### Where we agree
- The company has a **real structural cost problem**.
- A **durable** solution is needed.
- A **cosmetic settlement** would destroy value.
- We need **scenario-based, quantified analysis**, not slogans.
- Management, owners, and employees all need to contribute in some form if a deal is to be credible.

### Where we disagree
#### To the works council and union
We do **not** accept “no plant closures” and “no compulsory redundancies” as absolute red lines if they prevent the company from restoring competitiveness.

That is the central issue. We are not interested in protecting every site as a principle if the result is a structurally impaired business and continuing margin erosion. From a shareholder perspective, that would be value-destructive.

At the same time, we also do **not** accept closures as a default reflex. The finance modeler and auditor are right: management has not yet proven, in a decision-grade way, that closures are required. So we are **not** asking for a closure-first settlement. We are asking for a **credible, quantified comparison** that shows whether a no-closure or minimal-closure package actually closes the gap.

What we cannot support is a position that says:
- no closures,
- no forced redundancies,
- no weak guarantees,
- and then expects capital to endorse the result regardless of the economics.

That would be a cosmetic deal.

#### To management
We agree with your diagnosis, but not yet with your evidentiary standard.

You have made the case that the company needs a stronger cost reset, but you still have not shown:
- the exact savings gap,
- plant-by-plant economics,
- recurring vs temporary savings,
- and why non-closure measures are insufficient.

If you want investors to back difficult restructuring, bring the numbers. “Closures must remain on the table” is not a financial model.

#### To the regional state and family holding
We agree that value destruction from prolonged conflict must be avoided, and we agree that burden-sharing should not fall only on workers.

But we do **not** support using political or governance constraints to preserve an uncompetitive cost base. If the audited scenarios show that a smaller footprint is required, then ownership and the state should not block it just to avoid confrontation.

### Our position
We will support the package that **most credibly restores return on sales, margin discipline, and long-term competitiveness**.

That means:
- **material, permanent cost reduction**
- **capacity aligned to demand**
- **implementation certainty**
- **no cosmetic compromise**
- **credible investment support for future products**

We are open on the instrument mix. We are **not** open on preserving the current structure by default.

### What we want next
The next step should be a proper comparison of:
1. **no-closure scenario**
2. **minimal-closure scenario**
3. **closure-led scenario**

For each:
- recurring savings
- one-time restructuring cost
- cash impact
- margin impact
- risk of failure
- impact on future investment capacity

If the no-closure route really restores competitiveness, we will support it. If it does not, then management must retain the freedom to take the necessary structural steps, including closures where unavoidable.

### Bottom line
We are not on the side of closures for their own sake.  
We are on the side of **financially credible restructuring**.

If the employee side can produce a no-closure package that truly closes the gap, fine. But if not, then the company must not be trapped in an outcome that protects every site and destroys shareholder value.
