I will respond plainly.

## First: what I agree with

I agree with the expert on one important point: **a no-closure solution can be feasible in principle** if the required adjustment is within the range that voluntary exits, attrition, working-time tools, and capacity consolidation can absorb.

That matters. It means management cannot simply assert closure as the only serious answer. They have to prove it.

I also agree with all sides on these points:
- the current situation is serious and cannot be left unchanged;
- a cosmetic deal would be worthless;
- investment in future products and German sites is necessary;
- prolonged industrial conflict would damage the company.

So yes, we are willing to negotiate a real restructuring.

## Where I disagree sharply

### 1) Management is still treating closures as a bargaining weapon
Management says “all options must remain open.” In practice, that means keeping plant closures and forced layoffs as pressure points over the workforce.

That is not a neutral planning position. It is a threat.

The expert has now made clear that a no-closure package is **operationally plausible** if the numbers are within a certain range. That means management must stop presenting closures as the default truth and instead show, site by site, why alternatives are supposedly insufficient.

Until that proof exists, I reject closures in Germany.

### 2) The family holding and investors are overplaying “durability”
You keep saying the solution must be durable. I agree — but durable for whom?

A “durable” deal that permanently weakens wages, job security, and site security while preserving ownership and management flexibility is not balanced restructuring. It is a one-sided shift of risk onto labour.

The union will not accept permanent wage erosion.  
We are open to **temporary** contributions only, and only inside a broader package where everyone contributes.

### 3) The state shareholder’s position must become concrete
The state says it rejects hollowing out the industrial base, but it still leaves room for closures “where possible” in language that can be stretched until it means almost anything.

If the special statute and blocking role are to mean something, they should be used to protect the German footprint and prevent closures as an easy exit.

Words about stewardship are not enough. We need governance action.

## What the expert assessment changes — and what it does not

The expert says a no-closure package can work if:
- the footprint reduction needed is moderate rather than severe,
- voluntary exits are substantial,
- internal redeployment is real,
- and labor-cost relief is meaningful.

That is exactly why we insist on a **quantified industrial roadmap** before any talk of closures.

But the expert also makes a critical point: if the required reduction is severe, a no-closure approach gets much harder.

So I am not pretending that “no closures ever, no matter what” is a magic spell. I am saying:

1. **Management has not yet proven that closures are required**, and  
2. **the burden of proof is on those who want to break open the job-security framework**, not on the workforce defending it.

## What would actually move us

Not slogans. Not abstract calls for flexibility. We need a package that matches the expert logic and protects employees fairly.

### The union would consider a negotiated settlement if it includes all of the following:

1. **Binding no-closure commitment for German sites through 2030**
   - not as a vague aspiration, but as a formal governance and bargaining commitment.

2. **No compulsory redundancies through 2030**
   - adjustment through voluntary exits, retirement, redeployment, and working-time tools only.

3. **A real capacity-reduction plan without closures**
   - shift reductions, production reallocation, line consolidation, and site specialization.

4. **A temporary employee contribution only**
   - limited in time;
   - reversible;
   - tied to the overall restructuring;
   - and matched by contributions from management, owners, and capital.

5. **Binding investment commitments**
   - future models, technologies, and production allocations for German sites.

6. **A plant-by-plant roadmap with numbers**
   - headcount targets;
   - capacity changes;
   - timing;
   - savings;
   - fallback if voluntary measures fall short.

## What I reject outright

- plant closures in Germany as a starting assumption;
- compulsory redundancies dressed up as “targeted adjustment”;
- permanent wage cuts;
- a deal where workers carry the cost while management and owners stay insulated;
- any attempt to bypass co-determination by presenting closure as inevitable before the numbers are shown.

## Response to each stakeholder

### To management
You now have a credible expert statement saying non-closure restructuring is possible in principle. So stop hiding behind inevitability.

If you want labour to cooperate, bring:
- the numbers,
- the site-by-site needs,
- the savings logic,
- and a proposal that does not start by sacrificing jobs.

If you cannot do that, then you are asking workers to accept pain without proof.

### To the works council
I support your core line: no closures, no forced layoffs, no dismantling of collective standards. The expert assessment reinforces that this is not fantasy; it is a viable industrial planning path if designed properly.

### To the state shareholder
You need to use your blocking position more decisively. If you really oppose hollowing out the industrial base, then make that a governance reality.

### To the family owners and investors
If you want durable value, you need a settlement that preserves industrial legitimacy. A one-sided restructuring that breaks labour peace may reduce costs on paper and destroy value in execution.

## On the employee contribution

Let me be precise.

We are **not** refusing to contribute. We already signaled openness to:
- temporary wage restraint,
- reduced bonuses,
- working-time flexibility,
- and capacity reduction measures.

But that is only acceptable if it is:
- temporary,
- fairly shared,
- and exchanged for binding job and site security.

The workforce will not pay indefinitely for the company’s transition.

## Bottom line

The union is prepared to negotiate a serious restructuring. But the boundaries remain clear:

- **No plant closures in Germany**
- **No compulsory redundancies**
- **No permanent wage erosion**
- **Fair burden-sharing**
- **Binding investment and site guarantees**
- **A quantified, plant-by-plant industrial roadmap**

If management can bring a concrete non-closure package that meets the expert’s criteria, we will engage seriously.

If not, then the talk of “necessity” is just a demand that workers absorb the crisis alone — and that will not be accepted.
