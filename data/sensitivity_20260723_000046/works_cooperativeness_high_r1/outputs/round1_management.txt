I hear the red lines you are setting, but I need to be equally clear: **we cannot secure the future of this company with promises that preserve the current cost base.**

The reality is simple:
- German factory costs are structurally too high.
- Our European market is shrinking.
- Competitors are faster, leaner, and in many cases lower cost.
- The brand is not meeting its return-on-sales target.
- If we do not act decisively now, we will lose the room to invest in the next product cycle, software, battery technology, and the 2030 portfolio.

So I do **agree** with one point you both make: a solution has to be negotiated, durable, and socially responsible. A cosmetic deal or a temporary patch is not enough. But I **disagree** with the idea that “no closures” can be treated as an absolute starting point. If the numbers do not work, then all options must be on the table.

Let me be direct on the main issues:

### 1) Plant closures cannot be ruled out
I understand the concern about German industrial sites, but the current overcapacity cannot simply be absorbed by attrition and goodwill. If a site is structurally uncompetitive, we need the ability to close or materially repurpose it. Otherwise, savings are too small and too slow.

### 2) No compulsory redundancies is not a basis for a viable turnaround by itself
Voluntary departures, retirement schemes, and natural fluctuation will help, but they will not deliver enough. We need a **substantial and durable reduction in headcount and capacity**. How that is achieved can be negotiated, but the end result must be real.

### 3) A temporary wage contribution is not enough unless it materially changes the cost base
I am open to a negotiated employee contribution, but it must be more than symbolic. If labor costs remain essentially unchanged, then the company still carries an uncompetitive structure. We need measurable and lasting relief.

### 4) Investment commitments must be earned by competitiveness
I am not opposed to commitments on future products and technologies in Germany. In fact, that is exactly why we need this restructuring. But we cannot responsibly promise investment into a structure that still drains cash and fails to compete.

### What I can accept
I am prepared to discuss:
- a negotiated package that avoids unnecessary conflict,
- voluntary departure programs and retirement measures,
- working-time flexibility,
- targeted conversion or consolidation of production,
- binding investment into future products where the business case is sound,
- and a structured transition with clear milestones.

### What I cannot accept
I cannot accept:
- a deal that leaves all German sites effectively protected regardless of economics,
- a solution that preserves excess capacity,
- a purely temporary cost measure with no structural effect,
- or an agreement that postpones the problem until the next crisis.

The message from management is not “we want confrontation for its own sake.” The message is: **we need a settlement that actually restores competitiveness.** If the employee side is willing to engage on a package that delivers real and durable cost and capacity reductions, then we can negotiate. If not, we will have to pursue the restructuring path much more forcefully.

So my position is this:  
**I am ready to negotiate, but only on the basis of structural change, not a defensive freeze of the current model.**
