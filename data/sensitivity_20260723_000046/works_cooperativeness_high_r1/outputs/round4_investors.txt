We will be straightforward.

## Our position
As dispersed capital-market shareholders, our priority is unchanged: **restore competitive margins, reduce the cost base durably, and do it with deal certainty**. We are not here to protect legacy arrangements. We are here to protect risk-adjusted value.

That means two things can both be true:
- **a cosmetic compromise is unacceptable**, and
- **a settlement that leaves the company structurally uncompetitive is also unacceptable**.

## Where we agree
We agree with management, the family owners, and the state on the core diagnosis: the status quo is not sustainable. We also agree with labour and the expert that:
- voluntary exits, early retirement, redeployment, qualification, and working-time flexibility are legitimate tools;
- investment in future products and German production matters;
- prolonged industrial conflict would destroy value;
- a no-closure route is **possible in principle**.

That last point is important. It means the debate should not be “closures by default” versus “no closures as a slogan.” It should be: **what package actually closes the gap?**

## Where we disagree

### 1) To the works council and union
Your insistence on:
- no closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- no permanent wage erosion,

is understandable politically, but from our perspective it is not sufficient unless you can show the economics work.

The expert did **not** say a closure-free solution is automatically enough. The expert said it is feasible **if** the adjustment is moderate enough and the package is strong enough in headcount, capacity, and cost relief.

That is the key point.

So we do **not** accept an absolute no-closure rule as a principle. If the numbers show that one or more sites are structurally uneconomic, then closures or equivalent hard consolidation must remain on the table. We will not back a deal that preserves every site regardless of economics and then asks the market to reward it.

### 2) To management
We agree that structural change is necessary. We do **not** agree with a vague “all options open” posture unless it is backed by a quantified plant-by-plant plan.

“Everything is possible” is not a strategy. It becomes credible only if you show:
- how much capacity must be removed,
- at which sites,
- over what timeframe,
- with what annual savings,
- and what the one-time restructuring cost is.

If management cannot specify that, then it is asking investors to support uncertainty rather than a restructuring.

### 3) To the state shareholder
We respect regional responsibility. But protecting the industrial base only matters if the base is viable.

A state-backed refusal to contemplate hard decisions in advance may preserve jobs short term and destroy more value later. We will not support a political freeze that leaves the company with a structurally high cost base and weak margins.

### 4) To the family owners
We agree that durability matters. But durability is not achieved by maximising pain or by treating closure as the only proof of seriousness.

The right test is simpler: **does the final footprint, labor model, and cost base restore competitiveness?** If yes, we can support it. If no, then the solution is not good enough.

## What would move us
A package would need to show, in hard numbers:

1. **Durable cost reduction**
2. **Real capacity reduction**
3. **A credible return to target margins / ROS**
4. **Limited disruption**
5. **Executability within a defined timeframe**

The strongest path is likely a combination of:
- voluntary exits and retirement bridges,
- hiring restraint and attrition,
- shift reductions and line consolidation,
- internal reallocation of product and volume,
- and meaningful but time-limited labor-cost relief.

But if those tools do **not** get the company to a competitive cost base, then keeping all German sites open becomes an expensive way to avoid a hard truth.

## Our limit
We will **not** support:
- a cosmetic compromise,
- a deal that leaves the cost base structurally too high,
- a settlement that merely defers the problem,
- or a package that preserves excess capacity indefinitely.

## Our middle ground
We are open to a closure-free solution **only if** it delivers the same economic effect as harder restructuring:
- substantial and durable headcount reduction,
- meaningful capacity reduction,
- improved labor productivity,
- and a credible investment path.

If labour can produce that, we are open to it. If not, then closures or equivalent hard consolidation must stay in the frame.

## What we need now
We support the call for a **plant-by-plant industrial and finance model** comparing:
- no-closure,
- partial consolidation,
- and closure-based fallback,

with:
- headcount by site,
- utilization by site,
- product allocation,
- savings by measure,
- one-time restructuring costs,
- and timing.

## Bottom line
We are prepared to support a **hard, disciplined restructuring** that restores competitiveness and value.

We are **not** prepared to support:
- a symbolic deal,
- a structurally impaired outcome,
- or a settlement that avoids difficult decisions while margins keep eroding.

If a closure-free package truly closes the gap, we can support it. If it does not, then all structural options must remain on the table.
