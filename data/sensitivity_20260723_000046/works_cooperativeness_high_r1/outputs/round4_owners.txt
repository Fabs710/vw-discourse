I will be equally direct.

The positions are now sufficiently clear to say this: **I do not support a settlement that leaves the company structurally uncompetitive, and I do not support a settlement that triggers value-destructive instability.** That is the line. Everything else is secondary.

## To management
I agree with you on the core diagnosis:
- the current cost base is too high,
- overcapacity is real,
- a cosmetic deal would be worthless,
- and voluntary tools alone may not be enough.

What I do **not** agree with is any assumption that the only credible restructuring is one that immediately defaults to closures or compulsory redundancies. The expert input has made a closure-free route **plausible in principle** if the numbers work. That matters.

So I am not asking management to abandon restructuring. I am asking management to **prove, with numbers, where the gap really is**:
- plant by plant,
- capacity by capacity,
- cost by cost,
- and what mix of measures actually closes it.

If the analysis shows that certain sites are structurally untenable, then I will not pretend that that is comfortable. But I will also not support a political taboo that freezes the business in place and leaves margins to erode. What I will not accept is a half-solution dressed up as seriousness.

## To the works council and union
I understand your refusal of closures and forced redundancies. I also understand why you see “all options open” as a threat. But you are overreaching when you turn that into an absolute rule.

The expert did **not** say that a no-closure solution is automatically sufficient. The expert said it is possible **if** the reduction needed is moderate enough and the package is strong enough. That is a conditional statement, not a blank cheque for either side.

So I agree with you on this:
- voluntary exits,
- early retirement,
- redeployment,
- working-time flexibility,
- and a temporary employee contribution

are legitimate elements of a package.

I do **not** agree with you on this:
- that German plant closures can be ruled out in advance regardless of economics,
- or that no compulsory redundancies can be accepted under any circumstance, even if voluntary tools clearly fall short.

That would not be stewardship of the company. That would be a veto on difficult restructuring without an industrial answer.

If you want to avoid closures, then show a package that **actually closes the gap**. Not a slogan, not a principle — a package.

## To the state shareholder
I agree with you that the regional industrial base matters. A hollowed-out footprint would be bad for the company, the region, and the owners. I also agree that the burden should not fall on labour alone.

Where I disagree is if “protecting the footprint” becomes a substitute for making the footprint viable.

The state’s role should be to support a **credible, quantified restructuring**, not to freeze every difficult decision before the economics are tested. If the state wants to use its blocking position responsibly, it should use it to prevent a value-destructive outcome — not to block change in advance.

So yes, I support your insistence on:
- meaningful industrial presence in Germany,
- co-determination,
- and fairness in burden-sharing.

I do **not** support a state-backed freeze that leaves the company unable to restore competitiveness.

## To the union on employee contribution
A temporary contribution is acceptable only if it is:
- time-limited,
- meaningful enough to matter,
- and part of a wider package where management, owners, and capital also contribute.

What I will not accept is a token concession that buys a little time and then leaves the company with the same structural problem. If labour contributes, it must help move the economics materially.

But I also say this plainly: if labour insists that all structural pain must be avoided while expecting ownership and management to absorb the consequences, that is not a viable settlement either.

## To the family owners and investors
I share your concern for durability and long-term value. I do **not** share the idea that durability is achieved simply by leaving every structural option open and then forcing the workforce to carry the adjustment.

Durability means:
- restoring margins,
- lowering the cost base,
- protecting investment capacity,
- and avoiding industrial war.

A deal that is economically neat on paper but detonates trust, co-determination, and labour peace is not durable. It destroys value in another way.

At the same time, I will not support an outcome that protects every legacy arrangement and leaves the company permanently uncompetitive just to avoid confrontation. That would also destroy value — more slowly, but just as surely.

## My position
Here is the position I can defend:

1. **A real restructuring is necessary.**
2. **Voluntary tools should be used first and extensively.**
3. **A temporary employee contribution can be part of the package.**
4. **German site protection is desirable, but not an absolute if the economics fail.**
5. **Plant closures and compulsory redundancies cannot be excluded in advance if the numbers prove they are necessary.**
6. **No settlement is acceptable if it is merely cosmetic or if it leaves the company structurally weak.**

- retirement and voluntary exit potential,
- utilization by plant,
- model allocation,
- achievable savings under:
  - no-closure,
  - partial consolidation,
  - and closure-based fallback,
- plus the one-time cost and time horizon.

## Bottom line

My standard remains unchanged:

If a closure-free solution truly achieves that, I am open to it.
If it does not, then we must keep hard structural options on the table.
