I will be equally direct in this round.

## What I agree with

I agree on the following points:
- the company needs a **real restructuring**;
- a cosmetic settlement would be a waste of time;
- voluntary instruments are legitimate and should be used first;
- investment in future products and German production must be part of any solution;
- a prolonged conflict would damage everyone.

So there is no misunderstanding: **the works council is not refusing restructuring**.

## What I still reject

I reject the attempt by management, owners, and investors to keep **plant closures** and **compulsory redundancies** as routine pressure points while asking the workforce to trust that this is somehow only “planning flexibility.”

It is not neutral flexibility. It is leverage against jobs and sites.

And I reject the idea that the burden of proving a closure-free solution lies primarily with labour. The burden lies with those who want to break historic protections.

The expert has already confirmed something important: a **no-closure package is feasible in principle** if the adjustment is properly designed. That means closures are **not** the starting truth. They are one possible outcome, not the default.

## To management

You keep saying the package must be “hard enough to matter.” I do not dispute that. What I dispute is your implication that “hard enough” means German plant closures are acceptable from the outset.

If you want to argue that a non-closure solution is insufficient, then bring the plant-by-plant figures:
- how much capacity must be removed,
- at which site,
- with what utilization after restructuring,
- and why voluntary tools, shift reductions, redeployment, and temporary labor-cost relief cannot do the job.

Until then, “all structural options remain open” reads as a threat, not a strategy.

### What I can accept
- voluntary departures,
- retirement pathways,
- redeployment,
- qualification,
- working-time flexibility,
- temporary employee contribution,

**if and only if** they are embedded in:
- binding no-closure guarantees,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- and real investment commitments.

## To the family owners

I understand your concern about durable value. But durable value is not created by forcing through site closures as proof of seriousness.

A governance strategy that breaks trust with the workforce may reduce costs briefly and destroy operational stability for years. That is not stewardship; that is shortsightedness.

If you want long-term competitiveness, then support a solution that preserves the industrial base rather than treating it as disposable.

## To investors

You keep repeating “competitiveness” and “durability.” Fine. But durability also requires industrial peace, workforce commitment, and planning legitimacy.

A deal that keeps every site open, uses voluntary tools, and secures future products is not weak if it actually closes the gap.  
A deal that triggers open conflict, legal blockage, and social rupture may look cleaner on a spreadsheet and be far worse in execution.

So no: we will not accept a restructuring that treats the workforce as the easiest variable to cut.

## To the state shareholder

You have a blocking role and a regional responsibility. Use both.

If the state truly wants to protect the industrial footprint, then it should not talk in generalities. It should insist on:
- no German plant closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- and a binding investment roadmap for German sites.

Anything less is not leverage; it is commentary.

## What would actually move us

The works council is prepared to engage seriously on a package, but only inside firm boundaries.

### Our non-negotiable limits remain:
- **No plant closures in Germany**
- **No compulsory redundancies**
- **No dismantling of the job-security framework**
- **No bypassing of co-determination**

### We can negotiate on:
- the mix of voluntary instruments,
- working-time changes,
- temporary employee contributions,
- redeployment and qualification,
- the precise investment sequence,
- and operational consolidation **within sites**.

## The key missing piece: numbers, not pressure

The discussion keeps circling around “needed adjustment” without pinning it down.

So here is the practical next step:

### [SUMMON: independent plant-by-plant industrial and workforce model]
We need a **site-by-site model** showing:
- current headcount by plant and function,
- retirement eligibility and voluntary exit potential,
- current and projected utilization,
- model allocation and shift structure,
- cost savings from:
  - voluntary exits,
  - attrition,
  - working-time changes,
  - labor-cost contributions,
  - capacity consolidation without closures,
- and a comparison against any closure-based fallback.

If management believes closures are unavoidable, then let that be tested against a transparent alternative model. If the numbers truly force that conclusion, show it. But do not ask the workforce to surrender guarantees before the analysis is on the table.

## My position on the current trade-off

I will be blunt: there is no trade-off for us between “jobs and sites” on the one hand and “competitiveness” on the other if the only way to gain competitiveness is to destroy the basis of the company’s social compact.

A company this size, with this governance structure, does not have a legitimacy problem because workers are too protected.  
It has a legitimacy problem when management asks for trust while preparing to tear up guarantees that were part of the social settlement of the company.

## Bottom line

We are ready to negotiate a serious restructuring.

But the deal must be built on these principles:
- **all German plants remain open**,
- **no compulsory redundancies**,
- **co-determination respected**,
- **burdens shared fairly**,
- **investment secured**,
- **restructuring proven by numbers, not threats**.

If management wants our cooperation, it must bring a closure-free, redundancy-free plan that actually works economically.  
If it cannot do that, then it is not presenting a solution — it is presenting a demand that workers pay for management’s strategic failure.
