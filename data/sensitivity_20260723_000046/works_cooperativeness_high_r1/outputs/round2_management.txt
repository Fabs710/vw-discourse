I appreciate the clarity, but I need to be equally clear: **the positions just stated by labour do not solve the company’s competitiveness problem. They preserve the symptoms and block the cure.**

## Where I agree

I agree on three points:

1. **A cosmetic deal is useless.**  
   We do need a durable, negotiated restructuring.

2. **Voluntary instruments matter.**  
   Early retirement, natural attrition, redeployment, and working-time flexibility are all part of the toolbox.

3. **Investment in Germany matters.**  
   But investment must be earned by a cost base that can sustain it.

So yes, there is common ground on the need for a real solution. But that is where the agreement ends.

## Where I disagree — fundamentally

### 1) “No plant closures in Germany” is not a viable starting line
I reject the attempt to make plant closures taboo before the economics are even tested.

If a site is structurally uncompetitive, then refusing to discuss closure or consolidation is not responsible industrial policy. It is a guarantee that the company keeps carrying excess cost indefinitely.

You keep saying “show us the numbers.” The numbers are precisely why management is acting. German factory costs are too high, capacity is too high, and the brand’s return-on-sales target is not being met. In that situation, **all structural options must remain open**.

That does not mean we are eager to close plants. It means we will not pre-empt the outcome with an absolute prohibition that makes a viable turnaround impossible.

### 2) “No compulsory redundancies” alone does not deliver the scale needed
I hear the insistence on voluntary measures only. I do not accept that as sufficient.

Natural attrition and retirement schemes help, but they do **not** deliver the scale or speed required to close the gap. We need a **substantial and durable reduction in headcount and capacity**. If the employee side refuses to accept that fact, then it is refusing the core economic challenge.

I am not saying every reduction must be forced redundancies. I am saying the end result must be real, and if voluntary tools cannot achieve it, then we cannot pretend they are enough.

### 3) A temporary employee contribution is not acceptable if it leaves the cost base structurally uncompetitive
A time-limited contribution can be part of a transition. But I will not agree to a deal where labour contributes a little, headlines are produced, and then the company continues with the same cost structure.

We need **durable labor-cost relief**. That can take different forms — working-time changes, pay-related measures, flexibility, benefit adjustments, or other negotiated mechanisms — but it must materially improve the cost position.

A purely temporary or symbolic contribution is not a restructuring. It is a delay.

### 4) Binding guarantees through 2030 cannot come first if they lock in the problem
I understand the desire for site and job guarantees. But guarantees cannot be used to freeze an uncompetitive structure in place.

If we lock in every site, every job, and every legacy condition up front, then we are not restructuring. We are simply deferring the crisis to the next balance sheet.

## On the specific arguments from labour

### To the works council
You say management is treating the workforce as the adjustment variable. That is not accurate. We are saying the current operating model is not sustainable and must be changed.

You also say no alternatives have been shown. That is incorrect. We have already made clear that we are prepared to discuss:
- voluntary departures,
- retirement pathways,
- working-time flexibility,
- production consolidation,
- and negotiated employee contributions.

But if the response is “no closures, no compulsory redundancies, no dismantling, no deep cost relief,” then what remains is not a solution. It is a refusal to confront reality.

### To the union
You say closures are a bargaining weapon. I say they are a legitimate consequence of economic underperformance if no other path closes the gap.

You also say permanent wage erosion is unacceptable. Fine — but permanent uncompetitiveness is also unacceptable. If labor costs remain above target, then the company cannot invest, cannot modernize, and cannot protect the broader industrial base.

You cannot insist that employees be shielded from adjustment while asking the company to fund its future out of a structurally inflated cost base.

## To the state government
You speak of stewardship and regional responsibility. I respect that. But stewardship also means not protecting every site regardless of economics.

If the state’s position is that closures are effectively off-limits, then the state is not balancing interests — it is subsidizing rigidity. That may be politically understandable, but it does not solve the company’s problem.

A company that cannot adapt will eventually shrink more than a negotiated restructuring would require. That is the hard truth.

## To the family owners and investors
I agree with the broad direction you set: the end state must be competitive, and the package must be durable. But I want to be clear that credibility will be lost if governance accepts a plan that leaves the cost base untouched while calling it a compromise.

## What management can accept

We can be flexible on **how** the structural reduction is achieved, but not on **whether** it happens.

That means I am prepared to discuss:
- targeted site consolidation where economics justify it,
- voluntary programs and retirement measures,
- working-time adjustments,
- productivity and process changes,
- and a negotiated employee contribution that is meaningful, not symbolic.

I am also prepared to discuss binding investment commitments — but only as part of a package that demonstrably restores competitiveness.

## What I will not accept

I will not accept:
- an absolute no-closure rule,
- a no-forced-redundancy rule that makes meaningful adjustment impossible,
- a temporary contribution that does not materially improve the cost base,
- or a deal that preserves excess capacity and then calls the outcome a success.

## Bottom line

Management is not demanding sacrifice for its own sake. We are demanding a **structural, sustainable reduction in cost and capacity** so the brand can return to profitability and invest in 2030 products and technology.

If the employee side wants a settlement, it must stop treating every hard option as unacceptable from the outset. A real restructuring cannot be built around the promise that nothing essential will change.

So my position remains:

- **No cosmetic deal**
- **No preservation of uncompetitive capacity**
- **No agreement that leaves the cost base structurally too high**
- **Yes to negotiated tools that deliver durable savings**
- **Yes to investment, but only on a viable industrial foundation**

If labour wants to avoid closures, it must help produce a package that makes them unnecessary. So far, I have not heard such a package.
