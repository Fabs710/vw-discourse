We agree with the direction of the discussion in one important respect: **the status quo is not acceptable**. The current cost base is too high, the margin gap is real, and a cosmetic settlement would destroy value. From a capital-markets standpoint, that is now beyond dispute.

But we are also drawing a clear line: **a restructuring that leaves the company structurally uncompetitive is not acceptable either**. We will not back a deal that preserves excess capacity, protects all legacy arrangements, and then calls that a success.

## Where we agree

### To management
We agree that:
- the company needs a real restructuring, not a symbolic one;
- voluntary exits, early retirement, redeployment, and working-time flexibility are legitimate tools;
- investment in future products and technology is essential;
- prolonged industrial conflict would be value-destructive.

You are right that a cosmetic compromise is useless.

### To the expert
The expert assessment is helpful because it removes absolutes from the table. It confirms that:
- a **no-closure package can be feasible in principle**,
- but only if the adjustment is **large enough** in headcount, capacity, and labor-cost relief,
- and only if the resulting footprint is still economically sound.

That is the right standard.

## Where we disagree

### 1) To the works council and union: “no closures” cannot be an absolute red line
We understand the political and social force of your position. But from our side, a categorical no-closure rule is not a solution if the economics genuinely require capacity exit.

If the company can hit the target through voluntary tools, shift reductions, and internal reallocation, then fine — that is preferable.

But if the numbers do not work, then insisting that all German plants stay open regardless of economics means preserving an uncompetitive cost structure. We will not support that.

So our position is:
- **closures are not the goal**,
- but **they must remain possible if they are the only credible way to restore competitiveness**.

### 2) To the works council and union: no compulsory redundancies is not enough by itself
We understand why you reject forced layoffs. But from a shareholder perspective, what matters is not the label of the tool; it is whether the final cost base is competitive.

If voluntary exits and redeployment can deliver enough reduction, great. If they cannot, then a blanket refusal of compulsory measures may simply delay the adjustment and preserve margin erosion.

We are not asking for mass dismissals. We are saying the company cannot accept an adjustment that is too small or too slow.

### 3) To management: “all options open” is only credible if you show the numbers
We are sympathetic to the need for flexibility. But “all options open” is not enough on its own. It becomes credible only if it is backed by a quantified plant-by-plant case showing:
- what capacity must come out,
- at which sites,
- by what mechanism,
- over what timeframe,
- and what the annual savings are.

Without that, the phrase sounds like leverage rather than strategy.

### 4) To the state shareholder and family owners: value protection must be measured by the end state, not the politics of the process
We agree that a deal that detonates industrial peace would damage value. But the reverse is also true: a deal that preserves every site, keeps the cost base inflated, and postpones the problem will also destroy value — just more slowly.

A weak company eventually destroys more jobs, more value, and more optionality than a disciplined restructuring would.

## What would move us

A package would need to show, in hard terms:

1. **Substantial and durable cost reduction**
2. **Real capacity reduction**
3. **A credible return to return-on-sales target**
4. **Limited disruption**
5. **Executability within a defined timeframe**

That can be achieved by a mix of:
- voluntary departure programs,
- retirement bridges,
- natural attrition,
- working-time adjustments,
- product and volume reallocation,
- targeted labor-cost relief,
- and, if needed, selective site consolidation.

But the package must be **credible**, not just politically palatable.

## Our current limit

We will not support:
- a deal that leaves the cost base structurally too high;
- a compromise that preserves excess capacity indefinitely;
- a settlement where labour contributes temporarily while nothing durable changes;
- or a plan that avoids hard decisions and then expects the market to reward it.

## Our middle ground

We are open to a negotiated solution that avoids closures **if and only if** it delivers the same economic effect:
- meaningful headcount reduction,
- meaningful capacity reduction,
- durable labor-cost improvement,
- and a clear investment path supported by a viable footprint.

That is the only middle ground we will consider.

## To each stakeholder

### To management
Bring the numbers. If you want the market to support the deal, show us how it restores margins, not just how it avoids conflict.

### To the works council and union
If you want to avoid closures, you need to produce a package that makes closures unnecessary economically, not just unacceptable politically.

### To the state shareholder
Use your leverage to secure a viable industrial plan, not a freeze on all difficult decisions.

### To the family owners
Stewardship means preserving value, not preserving every legacy arrangement.

## Bottom line

We are prepared to support a **hard, disciplined restructuring** that:
- restores competitiveness,
- reduces costs and capacity durably,
- avoids value-destructive conflict,
- and funds future investment.

We will **not** support:
- a cosmetic compromise,
- a structurally impaired outcome,
- or a settlement that simply defers the crisis.

If a closure-free package can truly close the gap, we can support it. If it cannot, then the company must keep all structural options available.
