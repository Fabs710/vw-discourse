I will respond directly.

## Where I agree
I agree with the works council on the core point: **the company needs real restructuring, not a cosmetic exercise**.  
I also agree that:
- voluntary exits, retirement pathways, redeployment, qualification, and working-time flexibility belong in the package;
- investment in future products and German sites is necessary;
- prolonged industrial conflict would hurt the company and the workforce;
- a no-closure route is **not impossible** in principle.

That last point matters. The expert assessment has made clear that management cannot simply declare closures inevitable. They must prove it.

## Where I disagree with management, owners, and investors

### 1) Plant closures cannot be the starting assumption
Management keeps repeating “all structural options must remain open.” In practice, that means keeping closures as a permanent threat over the workforce.

I do not accept that as a neutral planning stance.

If the company wants labour to cooperate, it must first show:
- the size of the overcapacity,
- where it exists,
- what alternatives were tested,
- and why those alternatives cannot work.

Until then, closures are not a fact. They are a demand.

### 2) Compulsory redundancies are not an acceptable default
I will say this clearly: **we do not accept forced layoffs as the normal tool of restructuring**.

If voluntary exits, early retirement, attrition, redeployment, and working-time changes are insufficient, then management must come back with a better industrial plan — not jump straight to compulsory redundancies.

### 3) “Durability” cannot mean permanent worker sacrifice
To the family holding and investors: I understand your insistence on a durable solution. But durable for whom?

A settlement that permanently weakens wages, job security, and collective standards while leaving ownership and management insulated is not balance. It is risk transfer.

We are open to a **temporary** employee contribution. We are **not** open to a permanent erosion of the collective wage framework.

### 4) Guarantees cannot be treated as a reward after the fact
To management: you want flexibility first and guarantees later.  
That is the wrong sequence.

If employees are being asked to contribute, they need binding security in return:
- site security,
- employment protection,
- and investment commitments.

Otherwise the “temporary” contribution just becomes the first step in a downward spiral.

### 5) The state shareholder must use its leverage concretely
The state cannot just speak about regional responsibility and then leave the same closure logic on the table. If it has a blocking role, it should use it to protect the industrial base, not merely comment on it.

## On the expert assessment
The expert’s conclusion is very useful, but it cuts both ways.

Yes: a no-closure package can work **if** the adjustment is moderate enough and the package is strong enough.  
No: that does **not** mean closures are the right default, or that labour should accept them without proof.

So the burden now is straightforward:
- management must quantify the gap,
- and demonstrate why voluntary instruments plus capacity consolidation cannot close it.

If they can prove that a closure-free solution does not work, then we will have to discuss the consequences honestly. But we are not there yet.

## What would actually move us

We are prepared to negotiate a serious settlement if it contains all of the following:

1. **No German plant closures**
2. **No compulsory redundancies**
3. **A real capacity-reduction plan inside the existing footprint**
   - shift reductions,
   - line consolidation,
   - product reallocation,
   - internal redeployment
4. **A temporary, reversible employee contribution**
   - limited in time,
   - meaningful but not permanent,
   - and matched by sacrifices from management, owners, and capital
5. **Binding investment commitments**
   - future products,
   - technologies,
   - and allocations for German sites
6. **A plant-by-plant roadmap with numbers**
   - headcount,
   - capacity,
   - savings,
   - timing,
   - fallback if voluntary measures underperform

## To the works council
Your core line is right: the workforce must not be the sole shock absorber.

But we should be careful not to turn “no closures” into a slogan without an industrial plan behind it. That would be weak negotiating ground. The right position is: **no closures unless management can prove no closure is impossible**. That keeps the burden where it belongs.

## To management
If you want credibility, stop speaking in abstractions. Put the numbers on the table:
- how much capacity must come out,
- at which sites,
- through which measures,
- and why the no-closure route cannot work.

Until then, closures remain a threatened option, not a justified one.

## To the family owners and investors
If you want durable value, you need a settlement that the workforce can actually live with. A deal built on coercion, distrust, and job insecurity will not be durable in practice.

## Bottom line
The union is ready to negotiate a real restructuring.

But our boundaries remain clear:
- **no plant closures in Germany**
- **no compulsory redundancies**
- **no permanent wage erosion**
- **fair burden-sharing**
- **binding investment and job security**
- **numbers, not threats**

If management can present a closure-free package that genuinely closes the gap, we will engage seriously.  
If not, then the burden is still on them to justify why the workforce should pay with jobs and rights.
