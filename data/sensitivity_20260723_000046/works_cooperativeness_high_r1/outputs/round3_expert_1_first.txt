Below is a neutral industrial-restructuring assessment of the specific question raised by the works council: **can a non-closure restructuring package plausibly achieve the required cost and capacity adjustment, and under what conditions?**

## Executive assessment

**Yes, in principle, a no-plant-closure package can sometimes achieve very large savings — but only if the company has enough overcapacity, enough voluntary attrition potential, and enough flexibility to reallocate production across sites.**  

Whether it is **credible here** depends on three hard questions:

1. **How much capacity must actually come out of the German footprint?**
2. **How much of the workforce can be reduced through voluntary instruments alone within 2–4 years?**
3. **Can labor-cost relief and footprint changes together close the competitiveness gap without shutting a site?**

If the required adjustment is modest to moderate, a negotiated no-closure package is often viable.  
If the required adjustment is large and structural — for example, if the company must permanently remove an entire model family, platform, or plant-level cost base — then **avoidance of closures becomes materially harder**, and the package must compensate with deeper labor-cost relief, more severe production consolidation within sites, or major product allocation shifts.

## What a non-closure package can realistically include

A credible alternative to plant closure usually combines several instruments:

- **Voluntary exit programs**
  - severance-based departures
  - phased retirement / early retirement
  - retirement bridge arrangements

- **Natural attrition**
  - hiring freeze or selective hiring
  - replacement rate below 100%

- **Internal redeployment**
  - moving workers from shrinking lines to EV, battery-adjacent, software, logistics, or quality roles

- **Working-time reduction**
  - temporary shorter weeks
  - shift-pattern changes
  - partial short-time use if demand weakens

- **Temporary labor-cost relief**
  - wage restraint
  - suspension or reduction of supplements
  - delayed bonus growth
  - limited concession on guarantees, with sunset clauses

- **Capacity consolidation without closure**
  - remove one shift
  - reduce line count
  - concentrate a model at one site
  - reduce in-house complexity and indirect labor

This can be enough to achieve meaningful savings **without closing a site**, but the savings come from **utilization, headcount, and labor-cost compression**, not from a symbolic agreement alone.

---

## Key operational reality: “no closure” does not mean “no capacity reduction”

This is the critical distinction.

A company can keep all plants open while still doing one or more of the following:

- cutting one or more shifts,
- assigning fewer models to a site,
- moving a model to another German plant,
- reducing plant throughput,
- eliminating indirect roles,
- shrinking support functions,
- using a site at lower utilization.

So the real question is not “closures or no closures,” but:

> **How much structural capacity can be removed while keeping every site technically open and economically acceptable?**

If the company needs, say, a **10–15% footprint adjustment**, a no-closure package is often feasible.  
If it needs a **20–30%+ effective footprint reduction**, then non-closure solutions become much more difficult and usually require a very substantial employee contribution plus major product reallocation.

---

## What would make a no-closure package plausible?

A no-closure solution is more plausible if most of the following are true:

### 1) Overcapacity is spread across sites, not concentrated in one irreducibly weak plant
If the excess capacity can be redistributed through:
- shift reductions,
- platform consolidation,
- model transfers,
- and shared logistics,

then a negotiated footprint reshaping is possible.

### 2) A meaningful share of the workforce is near retirement or eligible for exit programs
This is often decisive. If the workforce profile allows a large number of departures through:
- age-based transition,
- pension bridges,
- voluntary severance,

the company can shrink materially without compulsory redundancies.

### 3) The company can absorb lower utilization for a transition period
This matters because a no-closure settlement usually has a **ramp-down period**:
- year 1: negotiate and design
- years 1–2: exit program launches
- years 2–4: capacity rebalancing
- years 4–5: stabilized footprint

If the business needs an immediate step-change in fixed-cost removal, no-closure packages are harder to execute.

### 4) Labor-cost relief is substantial enough to matter
Temporary gestures are not enough if management believes the structural gap is large.  
A meaningful no-closure package usually needs some combination of:
- wage moderation,
- temporary concessions,
- reduced premiums,
- lower internal complexity,
- possibly changes to legacy guarantees.

### 5) The site network can absorb product and volume shifts
If one site loses volume, another German site must take it, or the saved capacity is not real.  
So a closure-free plan requires a workable **plant-by-plant reallocation map**.

---

## What would make it implausible?

A no-closure solution becomes much harder if any of the following are true:

- the German network is **too large for the expected market volume** even after trimming,
- one plant is **structurally uncompetitive** on cost, logistics, or specialization,
- product volumes are falling faster than voluntary exits can reduce headcount,
- the required savings depend mainly on **fixed-cost elimination** rather than labor-cost moderation,
- there is no credible internal destination for the displaced production,
- the company needs a fast, one-time reset rather than a phased transition.

In those cases, management usually argues that “all sites stay open” but one site becomes effectively uneconomic unless it is closed or radically repurposed.

## Scenario-based assessment

Because no exact numbers were provided, I’ll frame this as scenario logic.

### Scenario A: Moderate restructuring need
- Capacity reduction required: around **5–10%**
- Workforce reduction: mostly through voluntary exit and attrition
- Labor-cost relief: modest to moderate, temporary
- Outcome: **No-closure package is likely feasible**

### Scenario B: Material restructuring need
- Capacity reduction required: around **10–20%**
- Workforce reduction: large voluntary program plus internal redeployment
- Labor-cost relief: meaningful and partially structural
- Outcome: **No-closure package is possible, but only with major concessions and strict execution discipline**

### Scenario C: Severe restructuring need
- Capacity reduction required: **20%+**
- Workforce reduction: large, rapid, and possibly exceeds voluntary exit capacity
- Labor-cost relief: significant and durable
- Outcome: **No-closure package is difficult and may be insufficient unless the company accepts slower adjustment, deeper labor concessions, or major product migration**

These are not forecasts; they are operational planning bands.

## What a credible no-closure package would need to contain

If the goal is to avoid closures, the package should be designed around **measurable replacement mechanisms**:

### A. Headcount reduction target
Specify:
- number of jobs to reduce,
- time horizon,
- voluntary share,
- expected retirements,
- fallback if take-up is too low.

### B. Capacity reduction target
Specify:
- units or shifts removed,
- lines consolidated,
- utilization target by site,
- model allocation changes.

### C. Cost-reduction target
Specify:
- annual gross savings,
- one-time restructuring cost,
- payback period,
- percentage from labor vs non-labor.

### D. Guardrails
Specify:
- no closures for a defined period,
- no compulsory redundancies for a defined period,
- review clause if market volume falls below threshold,
- restart mechanism if savings miss target.

### E. Investment commitments
Specify:
- which models or technologies go to which sites,
- what volume commitments are attached,
- what capital spending follows the restructuring.

Without these elements, “no closure” is not a restructuring plan; it is only a political position.

---

## Practical conclusion

From an industrial-planning standpoint:

- **A no-closure restructuring is feasible if the company’s needed adjustment can be achieved through headcount reduction, shift removal, and internal reallocation without requiring elimination of a whole plant economics.**
- **It becomes much less feasible if the company’s core problem is a permanent excess of German capacity relative to demand and platform volume.**
- **The decisive variable is not the existence of goodwill, but whether the numbers work.**

So the works council’s position is operationally plausible **only if it is backed by a quantified plant-by-plant plan showing that voluntary exits, capacity consolidation, and labor-cost concessions can hit the required cost target without leaving a structurally weak site alive on paper but not in economics.**
