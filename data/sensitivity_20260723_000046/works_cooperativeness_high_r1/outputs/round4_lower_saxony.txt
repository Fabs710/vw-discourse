I will speak plainly from the state shareholder’s position.

## Where I agree

I agree with management, the family owners, and investors on one point: **this company needs a real restructuring**. The status quo is not acceptable. A cosmetic compromise would merely postpone the crisis and make the eventual correction harsher.

I also agree that:
- voluntary exits, early retirement, redeployment, qualification, and working-time flexibility are legitimate tools;
- investment in future products and German production is necessary;
- and a prolonged industrial conflict would damage the company, the workforce, and the region.

So yes: **change is necessary**.

## Where I disagree

I do **not** accept a restructuring logic that starts from **German plant closures** or treats them as a default lever.

For the state, this is not a theoretical efficiency exercise. It has direct consequences for:
- regional employment,
- suppliers,
- industrial legitimacy,
- and the company’s role as a regional anchor.

That is why I cannot support a package that begins with “all options are open” and then uses closures as the easiest route. That would be poor stewardship.

I also reject any approach that shifts the burden mainly onto labour while management, owners, and capital avoid meaningful contribution. That is neither fair nor stable.

## My reading of the expert assessment

The expert’s assessment is useful because it cuts through the slogans.

It says a **no-closure package can be feasible in principle**, but only if:
- the adjustment is not so severe that the network becomes structurally impossible to sustain,
- the voluntary exit volume is substantial,
- capacity is reallocated intelligently,
- and labour-cost relief is meaningful enough to matter.

That is important. It means management cannot simply declare closures inevitable without first proving that the closure-free route is insufficient.

But the expert also makes the opposite point: if the required reset is severe, then the non-closure route gets much harder.

So the burden is now clear:
- **management must quantify the gap**, and
- **labour must show a credible way to close it without destroying sites**.

That is where the negotiation has to move next.

## My position on the core disputes

### 1) Plant closures
My position remains: **no outcome that guts regional employment**.

I am prepared to back consolidation, specialization, and capacity reduction. But I will not support a settlement that hollow-outs the German footprint or removes the company’s anchor role in the state.

### 2) Compulsory redundancies
I do not want forced layoffs as the primary tool. If adjustment is necessary, it should come first through:
- attrition,
- voluntary exit programs,
- retirement bridges,
- redeployment,
- qualification,
- and working-time solutions.

If management says that is not enough, then it must show the numbers, not just assert them.

### 3) Employee contribution
A temporary employee contribution is discussable. But only if it is:
- clearly time-limited,
- proportionate,
- reversible,
- and matched by visible burden-sharing from management and ownership.

A permanent erosion of labour standards is not something I can support.

### 4) Guarantees
I support binding guarantees **if they are part of a viable industrial plan**.

I do not want empty promises. I want:
- site certainty where possible,
- employment protection where feasible,
- investment commitments that are real,
- and a roadmap that shows how competitiveness improves without sacrificing the state’s industrial base.

## My response to each side

### To management
You are right that the company needs structural improvement. But you are not entitled to conclude from that that closures are automatically justified.

The expert has shown there is a closure-free route **in principle**. So if you want closures kept on the table, then show why voluntary exits, working-time measures, capacity reallocation, and labour-cost relief are not enough.

I have not yet seen that proof.

### To the works council and union
I understand and largely support your core concern: the workforce must not be made the sole shock absorber.

Your no-closure position is credible **if** it is backed by a quantified industrial plan. I need to see:
- how much capacity comes out,
- where the volume goes,
- how the staffing path works,
- and how competitiveness is restored.

If you can show that, I will stand behind a closure-free solution. If you cannot, then we still have a real problem.

### To the family owners
Long-term stewardship is not measured by how quickly you can impose pain. It is measured by whether the company remains viable, legitimate, and rooted in Germany.

A governance strategy that burns trust with the workforce may look clean on paper and become very expensive in practice.

### To investors
I understand your insistence on durability. But a settlement that detonates labour peace and regional trust is not durable; it is fragile.

If you want lasting value, support a package that restores competitiveness **and** preserves the company’s social foundation.

## What I could support

I am prepared to support a tough restructuring if it includes:
- a meaningful but socially responsible capacity reduction,
- substantial voluntary exits and retirement pathways,
- working-time flexibility where it helps,
- a temporary and fair employee contribution,
- binding investment commitments for German sites,
- and a plant-by-plant roadmap showing how competitiveness improves.

That is the kind of package that could protect both the company’s future and the regional economy.

## What I will not support

I will not support:
- a disguised closure program,
- forced redundancies as the main adjustment tool,
- a one-sided labour-cost raid,
- or a plan that permanently weakens the company’s German footprint.

## Bottom line

The state shareholder is willing to back a hard restructuring.

But it must:
- preserve a meaningful industrial footprint in Germany,
- protect co-determination,
- avoid unnecessary plant closures,
- avoid compulsory redundancies where possible,
- and share the burden fairly.

If the final package becomes a disguised closure plan or a one-sided job-cutting exercise, I will oppose it.

If management wants my support, bring a **concrete, quantified alternative** that shows how jobs, sites, and competitiveness can all be protected in a balanced way.
