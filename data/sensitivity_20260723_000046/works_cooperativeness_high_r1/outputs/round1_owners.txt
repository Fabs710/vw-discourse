I appreciate the seriousness of the arguments on the table, and I want to be equally direct from the standpoint of the family owners.

We are **aligned on the diagnosis**: the current cost and capacity structure is not sustainable. If the company fails to restore competitiveness, the long-term damage will not be limited to one balance sheet cycle; it will undermine the core asset we are all trying to preserve. So I do **not** support preserving the status quo out of sentiment.

That said, I also want to be clear about what is and is not acceptable from a governance perspective.

## Where I agree

I agree with management on the central point that this must be a **real restructuring**, not a symbolic one. A temporary patch, a small concession, or a delay until the next crisis would be value-destructive.

I also agree with the works council, the union, and the state shareholder on one important point: **a settlement that detonates industrial peace and damages the company’s reputation is not a success**, even if it produces headline savings. We are not interested in “winning” in a way that destroys the enterprise.

And I agree that any employee contribution must be part of a **balanced package**, not a one-sided extraction.

## Where I disagree

### 1) Plant closures cannot be ruled out in principle
I do **not** accept the idea that “no plant closures” can be a binding starting premise. If a site is structurally uncompetitive and cannot be brought into a viable future role, then refusing to address that is not stewardship — it is postponing value destruction.

That does not mean I am asking for closures for their own sake. It means we cannot pre-empt the outcome before the economics are examined honestly. If closures are unnecessary, then fine. But if they are necessary, then governance cannot impose a taboo.

### 2) Forced protection of every current arrangement is not sustainable
I understand the attachment to job guarantees, legacy protections, and historic agreements. But if the result is that cost adjustment becomes impossible, then we end up preserving form while eroding the substance of the company. That is not acceptable to long-term owners.

### 3) A purely temporary employee contribution is not enough if the structural gap remains
I am open to a temporary contribution if it helps bridge the transition and is genuinely shared. But if the labor-cost problem is structural, then a short-lived measure that leaves the underlying economics unchanged is not sufficient. The company needs durable improvement.

## What I would consider a credible path

From our perspective, a viable settlement would need to include most of the following:

- **A substantial and durable reduction in cost**
- **Real capacity adjustment**, not just cosmetic trimming
- **Voluntary instruments first** where they can deliver meaningful reductions
- **Working-time flexibility** where it materially improves competitiveness
- **A negotiated employee contribution**, but only as part of a broader package
- **Binding investment commitments** for future products and technologies in Germany
- **A clear industrial roadmap** showing how the company becomes competitive again

I am not demanding a simplistic “cuts first” approach. I am demanding a package that actually closes the gap.

## What I would not support

I would not support:

- a package that rules out closures regardless of site economics,
- an arrangement that preserves excess capacity indefinitely,
- a deal that shifts the burden almost entirely onto owners or capital while the operating structure remains unchanged,
- or a formula that substitutes slogans for measurable structural change.

## To the works council and union

You are right to insist that employees should not be treated as the sole shock absorber. But if you insist on absolute no-closure and no-forced-redundancy principles without showing how the numbers still work, then you are effectively asking the owners to underwrite a permanently uncompetitive structure. That is not a defensible long-term position.

If you want binding guarantees, then they must be paired with **credible, measurable competitiveness gains**. Guarantees without performance will not protect the company; they will only delay harder decisions.

## To the state government

I understand your responsibility to the regional economy. But blocking every difficult site decision in the name of regional protection would ultimately make the company weaker, not stronger. A company that cannot adapt will eventually shrink more than any negotiated restructuring would require. We should not confuse short-term political comfort with durable industrial stewardship.

## To management

I support a serious restructuring effort, but I would caution against treating confrontation as leverage for its own sake. If the strategy is seen as an attempt to force through a maximalist package without sufficient social durability, it will provoke resistance that may consume the value you are trying to create.

A credible plan must be **economically hard and socially executable**. If it is not executable, it is not credible.

## Bottom line

The family holding is prepared to back a restructuring that restores competitiveness, even if it is painful. But we will **not** back a settlement that permanently preserves an uncompetitive structure or blocks necessary capacity decisions in advance.

So my position is:

- **Yes** to a negotiated, durable restructuring.
- **Yes** to burden-sharing.
- **Yes** to investment commitments tied to a viable industrial plan.
- **No** to categorical taboos on plant closures before the economics are properly tested.
- **No** to a deal that sacrifices long-term enterprise value for the sake of avoiding hard decisions.

If the other stakeholders want our support, the proposal must show — in numbers, not rhetoric — that it restores margins and competitiveness while preserving the company’s ability to invest and remain strong in Germany.
