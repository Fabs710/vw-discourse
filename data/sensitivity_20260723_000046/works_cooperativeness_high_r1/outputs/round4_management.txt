I’ll respond plainly.

## To the works council and union

I hear your position: no closures, no compulsory redundancies, no permanent wage erosion, and strong co-determination. I agree with one part of that: **a cosmetic deal is worthless**. I also agree that voluntary exits, retirement pathways, redeployment, qualification, and working-time flexibility belong in the package.

But I do **not** accept your attempt to turn those preferences into absolute vetoes over structural change.

The expert did **not** say “no closures are the answer.” The expert said a no-closure route may be feasible **only if** the adjustment is moderate and the package is large enough in headcount, capacity, and cost relief. That is exactly the point you keep skipping.

You keep saying management must prove closures are unavoidable. Fine. But the burden does not disappear from labour side just because you repeat “no closures” often enough. If you want to rule out closures and compulsory redundancies, then you must bring a package that actually closes the gap. Not a political line — a workable industrial answer.

### What I agree with
- Voluntary exits are legitimate.
- Early retirement and attrition are legitimate.
- Working-time flexibility can help.
- Investment in future products and German sites is necessary.
- Industrial peace is valuable.

### What I reject
- A blanket ban on closures regardless of economics.
- A blanket ban on compulsory redundancies regardless of whether voluntary tools are sufficient.
- Any arrangement that locks in excess capacity and leaves the cost base uncompetitive.
- Any “temporary” contribution so small that it buys time but does not improve competitiveness.

If the company’s structural gap is large, then “no closures, no redundancies, no hard trade-offs” is not a solution. It is a refusal to solve the problem.

## To the state shareholder

I respect the regional responsibility and the blocking position. But stewardship is not the same as freezing the footprint.

If the state wants to protect the industrial base, it must help make that base viable. That means backing a plan that:
- reduces capacity,
- lowers labour costs meaningfully,
- improves productivity,
- and restores margin.

What I will not accept is using the state role to block difficult decisions in advance while leaving the company with a cost structure that continues to underperform.

So yes, I want the state involved. But I want it involved in securing a **credible restructuring**, not in guaranteeing the status quo.

## To the family owners

I agree with one thing you said: a weak company eventually destroys more jobs and value than a disciplined restructuring would.

Exactly. That is why I am not prepared to sign off on a compromise that is politically comfortable but economically shallow.

But I also reject the implication that closure is automatically a sign of seriousness. It is not. Closure is only justified if the economics require it. If a closure-free package can truly close the gap, I will take that seriously.

What I will not accept is a package that preserves the old structure and then asks everyone to pretend it is durable.

## To institutional investors

You are right that a cosmetic compromise is unacceptable. You are also right that the outcome must be durable and value-accretive.

Where I disagree is your tendency to treat closures as the only credible discipline mechanism. They are not. The question is whether the final footprint, cost base, and labour model are competitive.

If labour can deliver:
- meaningful headcount reduction,
- real capacity reduction,
- temporary but material labour-cost relief,
- and a viable product allocation plan,

then a no-closure solution can be acceptable. But if it cannot, then I will not endorse a settlement that leaves the company structurally too expensive just to avoid conflict.

## What actually moves management

Let me be equally direct about our limit.

I am **not** looking for a symbolic burden-sharing exercise. I am looking for a **durable structural reduction in cost and capacity**. That is non-negotiable.

So here is what would move me:
1. A quantified headcount reduction path.
2. A quantified capacity reduction path.
3. A temporary employee contribution that is large enough to matter.
4. A plant-by-plant roadmap.
5. Binding investment commitments tied to a viable footprint.
6. A fallback if voluntary tools underperform.

Without those things, “no closures” is just a slogan.

## Where the current positions fall short

### The works council’s position
You are defending jobs and sites. That is understandable. But you are still not answering the core economic question: **how does the company get to competitiveness if closures are ruled out from the start and no compulsory redundancy is allowed under any circumstance?**

That is not yet a restructuring plan. That is a boundary.

### The union’s position
A temporary contribution is acceptable only if it materially improves the cost base. If it is too small, too short, or too reversible to make a real difference, then it does not solve the crisis.

### The state’s position
You want to protect the footprint, but you also need to back a plan that makes the footprint viable. Those are not the same thing.

### The family owners’ and investors’ position
You are right to insist on durability. But I will not accept an approach that treats labour as the only adjustable variable while management, ownership, and capital remain untouched.

## My line remains clear

I will support a **serious restructuring**.
I will support **voluntary tools**.
I will support **working-time flexibility**.
I will support **investment in future products and German sites**.

But I will **not** support:
- a cosmetic compromise,
- a settlement that leaves the cost base structurally uncompetitive,
- a package that preserves excess capacity indefinitely,
- or a deal that rules out all hard structural options before the numbers are proven.

I respect the seriousness of the objections. I do not accept the absolutes.

The company needs:
- lower costs,
- less overcapacity,
- better margins,
- and funds for future products and technology to 2030.

That objective does not change because the discussion is difficult.
