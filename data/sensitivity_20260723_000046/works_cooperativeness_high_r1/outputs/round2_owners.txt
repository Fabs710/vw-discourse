I will be direct.

## My position remains unchanged on the essentials

The family holding supports a restructuring that **restores competitiveness, margins, and investment capacity**. We do **not** support preserving an uncompetitive structure out of principle or sentiment.

At the same time, we do **not** support a path that creates prolonged industrial war, destroys trust, and erodes the long-term value of the company. That would also be a failure of stewardship.

So the test is not “who wins the argument.” The test is: **does the package materially close the competitiveness gap in a way that the company can actually execute?**

---

## Where I agree

### To management
I agree that:
- the current cost base is too high,
- temporary patches are not enough,
- capacity and cost must come down materially,
- and the company needs room to invest in the next product cycle.

You are right that a cosmetic deal would be value-destructive.

### To the state shareholder, works council, and union
I also agree that:
- a deal that triggers prolonged strikes and destroys labour peace would damage the company,
- investment commitments matter,
- voluntary exits, retirement schemes, redeployment, and working-time flexibility are legitimate tools,
- and any employee contribution should be part of a balanced package.

---

## Where I disagree

### 1) “No plant closures in Germany” cannot be an absolute rule
I do **not** accept a categorical no-closure rule.

If a site is structurally uncompetitive and cannot be made viable in a future industrial setup, then governance cannot rule out closure in advance. That would be a refusal to confront economic reality.

I am not advocating closures for their own sake. I am saying the economics must decide, not a taboo.

### 2) “No compulsory redundancies” cannot be a veto on meaningful adjustment
I understand the workforce’s red line. But if voluntary measures are insufficient to deliver the necessary scale and speed, then the restructuring design has to be honest about that.

A package that depends entirely on voluntary attrition while leaving excess capacity in place is not a restructuring. It is delay.

### 3) Binding guarantees cannot freeze the current cost structure
I hear the demand for site and job guarantees through 2030. I am open to guarantees **if** they sit inside a credible plan that restores competitiveness.

But guarantees that prevent structural change before the numbers are fixed are not stewardship. They lock in the problem.

### 4) A temporary employee contribution is not enough if the structural gap remains
A time-limited contribution can help bridge the transition. It cannot be the substitute for real cost and capacity change.

If labour contributes and then the company returns to the same structure, nobody has solved anything.

---

## My response to each side

### To the works council
You say German plants are not disposable. I understand that view, but I do not accept it as a governance principle.

You ask for no closures, no compulsory redundancies, no dismantling of the job-security framework, and no fait accompli. If those are absolute preconditions, then you are effectively asking the company to carry excess cost indefinitely.

That is not a defensible position for long-term owners.

What would move us is not rhetoric about protection, but a **credible industrial alternative** that shows how enough cost and capacity can be removed without blocking all structural options. So far, I have not seen that.

### To the union
I agree that a permanent wage cut would be unacceptable. I also agree that employees should not be the sole shock absorber.

But I do **not** accept the conclusion that any deep labour-cost adjustment is illegitimate. If the company remains structurally too expensive, then the burden has to be shared in a way that materially improves competitiveness.

The union’s position is understandable, but it is not yet a solution.

### To management
You are right to insist on structural change, but you should be careful not to confuse firmness with credibility.

If the restructuring is framed as a showdown, you may get resistance that destroys value before savings are realised. I would support a hard package if it is economically coherent and socially executable. I would not support a maximalist approach that provokes a breakdown and then calls that discipline.

### To the state shareholder
I respect the state’s role and the special statute. But I cannot support a governance line that effectively blocks all difficult site decisions in advance.

If the state insists that closures are politically off limits, then it must also explain how the cost base is to be fixed otherwise. Otherwise you are preserving political comfort and passing the economic problem forward.

### To institutional investors
I agree with your demand for a durable, margin-accretive outcome. I also agree that a prolonged conflict would destroy value.

But I would caution against treating closure as an automatic signal of seriousness. A bad closure package can destroy more value than it saves. The end state must be competitive **and** executable.

---

## What I would consider acceptable

A credible package would need to show, in numbers:

- substantial and durable cost reduction,
- real capacity adjustment,
- meaningful productivity improvement,
- voluntary tools used first where feasible,
- working-time flexibility where it materially helps,
- a temporary and fair employee contribution,
- and binding investment commitments tied to a viable industrial roadmap.

I would also expect:
- clear milestones,
- plant-by-plant transparency,
- and a realistic assessment of which sites are strategically essential and which are not.

---

## What I will not support

I will not support:
- a settlement that rules out closures regardless of site economics,
- a deal that preserves excess capacity indefinitely,
- a package that makes labour the sole payer while operational inefficiency remains untouched,
- or a compromise that delays the next crisis.

---

## Bottom line

The family holding is prepared to back a **hard but credible restructuring** that restores competitiveness and preserves the company’s long-term industrial strength.

We are **not** prepared to back:
- a no-closure taboo,
- an adjustment model that avoids meaningful capacity exit,
- or a settlement that sacrifices enterprise value to preserve every legacy arrangement.

If the other stakeholders want our support, bring a plan that clearly demonstrates how margins are restored, how investment is funded, and how the company remains strong in Germany. Without that, there is no basis for us to move.
