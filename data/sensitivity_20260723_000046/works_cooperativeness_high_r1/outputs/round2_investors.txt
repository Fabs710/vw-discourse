We appreciate that the debate is moving from slogans to the actual trade-offs. From the capital markets side, our standard remains unchanged: **we will support the package that most credibly restores competitive margins on a durable basis, with the least value-destructive disruption.**

## Where we agree

We agree with management, the family owners, and to a degree the state that:

- the current cost base is too high,
- the status quo is not sustainable,
- temporary fixes are not enough,
- and the company needs room to invest in future products and technology.

We also agree with labour and the state on one important point:

- **a prolonged strike or an unmanaged industrial conflict would destroy value**.

So we are not looking for confrontation. We are looking for an executable outcome that restores returns.

## Where we disagree

### 1) “No plant closures” cannot be an absolute
We do **not** support a restructuring that rules out closures in advance if the economics genuinely require capacity exit.

If German operations remain structurally overcapacity, then preserving every site as a principle would leave the cost base uncompetitive and margins impaired. That is not acceptable to us as shareholders.

That said, we also do **not** support closures as a headline tactic or as a shortcut to “show seriousness.” A closure that triggers years of disruption, litigation, and operational damage can destroy more value than it creates.

So our position is:

- **closures must remain possible in principle,**
- but **they must only be used if they clearly improve the risk-adjusted value case**.

### 2) “No compulsory redundancies” is understandable, but not decisive by itself
We understand why labour draws a red line here. But from our perspective, the question is not whether job losses are politically painful; it is whether the final operating model is competitive.

If voluntary departures, retirement schemes, redeployment, and working-time tools can deliver enough adjustment, that is preferable.

If they cannot, then insisting on no compulsory redundancies at all may simply preserve excess cost.

We are not endorsing forced layoffs as a first choice. We are saying the company cannot leave the adjustment too small or too slow.

### 3) Temporary employee contributions are acceptable only if the outcome is durable
We could support a temporary contribution from employees if it is:

- time-limited,
- meaningful,
- part of a broader burden-sharing package,
- and tied to measurable structural improvements.

What we will **not** support is a cosmetic settlement where workers give something up for a few years and the company then returns to the same cost structure. That would be value-destructive delay.

### 4) Investment commitments should follow credible restructuring, not substitute for it
We agree that Germany needs investment in future products and technologies. But we will not support investment commitments into a structure that still remains uncompetitive.

Investment is not a substitute for margin recovery. It is a result of it.

## What would move us

A credible deal would need to show, in numbers, that it delivers:

- **substantial and durable cost reduction**
- **real capacity adjustment**
- **a clear path back to the return-on-sales target**
- **limited disruption**
- **a settlement that is executable and not just politically palatable**

That could include a combination of:

- selective consolidation or site conversion where economics justify it,
- voluntary departure and retirement programs,
- working-time flexibility,
- targeted labor-cost relief,
- productivity improvements,
- and binding investment commitments that support the future operating model.

## Our limits

We will not back:

- a deal that leaves the cost base structurally uncompetitive,
- a settlement that preserves excess capacity indefinitely,
- a cosmetic compromise designed to avoid hard decisions,
- or a prolonged conflict with no credible endpoint.

## To management

You are right that the current model cannot remain intact. But you need to be careful not to equate “decisive” with “maximalist.” If the package is too confrontational to be executed, it will fail to deliver value.

We want a restructuring that is hard enough to matter, but credible enough to stick.

## To the works council and union

You are right to reject a cosmetic deal and to insist that labour should not be the only shock absorber. But your position becomes unacceptable to us if it amounts to protecting all sites and all legacy conditions regardless of economics.

That would leave the company structurally impaired.

If you want our support, the employee side must be part of a package that actually reduces the cost base and capacity in a durable way.

## To the state shareholder

We understand your responsibility to the regional economy. But protecting every site at all costs is not stewardship if it leaves the company unable to compete.

A weak company eventually destroys more jobs than a disciplined restructuring would.

## Bottom line

We are prepared to support a **hard, negotiated restructuring** if it:

- restores competitiveness,
- reduces costs and excess capacity durably,
- avoids unnecessary conflict,
- and preserves the company’s ability to invest and earn returns.

What we will **not** support is a deal that merely postpones the problem or keeps the cost base structurally too high.

If the final package is credible on margins and durable on execution, we can support it. If it is a political compromise dressed up as strategy, we will not.
