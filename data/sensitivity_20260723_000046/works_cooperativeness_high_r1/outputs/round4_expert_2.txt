From an industrial restructuring standpoint, the key point is now clear:

## What is technically credible
A **closure-free restructuring can be feasible in principle**, but only if all of the following are true:

- the required capacity reduction is **moderate rather than severe**,
- voluntary exits, early retirement, and attrition can remove **enough headcount**,
- shift reductions and line consolidation can remove **real fixed cost**, not just labor hours,
- product allocation can be simplified enough to raise utilization,
- and any temporary employee contribution is **material enough to move the cost base**.

If those conditions are not met, a no-closure package tends to become **socially attractive but economically incomplete**.

---

## What is still missing
At this stage, nobody has yet presented the data needed to prove or disprove any specific plant decision. The crucial missing items are site-by-site:

- current headcount by function,
- installed and utilized capacity,
- model allocation,
- retirement eligibility,
- voluntary exit potential,
- attrition assumptions,
- fixed overhead by plant,
- and one-time restructuring costs.

Without that, assertions like “no closures are possible” or “closures are unavoidable” are not yet evidence-based.

---

## Scenario logic

### 1) No-closure package
Most credible if:
- overcapacity is not too large,
- at least a meaningful share of workforce reduction can come from voluntary tools,
- and capacity can be cut through shifts, line consolidation, and internal reallocation.

Risk:
- residual excess overhead may remain too high,
- especially if multiple sites stay only partly utilized.

### 2) Partial consolidation
Often the most realistic middle path:
- fewer shifts,
- fewer lines,
- specialization of sites,
- some product transfer,
- stronger utilization at remaining plants.

This can deliver much of the savings of a closure without the full social and governance shock.

### 3) Closure-based fallback
Most powerful for removing fixed cost and overcapacity:
- strongest reset,
- highest one-time disruption,
- highest conflict risk,
- biggest impact on industrial relations.

This becomes more likely if the demand outlook cannot support the footprint even after consolidation.

---

## My assessment of the current bargaining geometry
Based on the positions stated, the likely workable zone — if one exists — is **partial consolidation plus voluntary workforce reduction**, not a pure status quo solution and not an immediate closure program.

A pure no-change outcome is not credible.  
A full closure-based solution is technically stronger on cost, but socially and governance-wise much harder to execute.  
So the center of gravity is usually:

- voluntary exits,
- retirement bridges,
- hiring restraint,
- working-time changes,
- shift and line consolidation,
- targeted investment into fewer, better-utilized sites,
- and a time-limited employee contribution.

---

## What would make a no-closure solution fail
It fails if:
- the company needs a **deep** footprint reduction,
- voluntary programs cannot generate enough separation volume,
- overhead per remaining vehicle stays too high,
- or investment would simply prop up underutilized capacity.

That is the hard industrial test.

---

## Bottom line
A **closure-free solution is possible in theory**, but only if the numbers support it.  
At present, the decisive issue is not ideology; it is whether the plant-by-plant math shows that voluntary exits, working-time flexibility, and consolidation can close the cost and capacity gap.
