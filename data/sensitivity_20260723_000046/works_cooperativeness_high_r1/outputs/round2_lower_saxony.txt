Let me be equally direct from the state shareholder’s perspective.

## Where I agree

I agree with management, the family owners, and the investors on one point: **the company needs a real restructuring, not a cosmetic one**. The current cost and capacity structure cannot simply be left as it is.

I also agree that:
- voluntary departures, retirement schemes, and natural attrition are legitimate tools,
- working-time flexibility can be part of a package,
- investment in future products and technologies in Germany is essential,
- and a prolonged industrial conflict would damage the company.

So yes: **change is necessary**.

## Where I disagree

I disagree sharply with the attempt to make **plant closures in Germany the default logic**.

For the state, closures are not a neutral technical instrument. They have lasting effects on:
- regional employment,
- supplier networks,
- industrial legitimacy,
- and the company’s role as a regional anchor.

That is why I cannot support a restructuring that starts from “all structural options must remain open” in the abstract and then ends in plant closure as the easiest lever. That would be a failure of stewardship, not a success of restructuring.

I also disagree with any approach that effectively says employees must carry the adjustment while ownership and management remain protected from meaningful sacrifice. That is neither fair nor stable.

## My clear position

### 1) No outcome that guts regional employment
I will not support a package that hollow-outs the German footprint or permanently weakens the company’s role in the state.

### 2) No sidelining of co-determination
The state’s special role and the co-determination architecture are not decorative. Any credible solution must respect them. A process that tries to bypass the supervisory board’s blocking positions or treat the workforce as an afterthought is not acceptable.

### 3) No forced layoffs as the primary adjustment tool
If the company wants durable peace, the adjustment must come first through:
- natural attrition,
- voluntary exit programs,
- early retirement,
- redeployment,
- and negotiated working-time solutions.

Forced redundancies would create social and political damage that the company should not underestimate.

### 4) No blank cheque for permanent labor erosion
A temporary employee contribution may be discussable, but only if it is:
- truly temporary,
- proportionate,
- and paired with binding commitments on sites, jobs, and investment.

A permanent weakening of the collective framework is not a price I am prepared to pay.

## What I could support

I am open to a package that includes:
- capacity reduction through voluntary and socially responsible instruments,
- productivity and operational improvements,
- working-time flexibility where it is fair and reversible,
- a negotiated employee contribution that is temporary and limited,
- binding investment commitments for German plants,
- and a clear industrial roadmap that preserves meaningful site presence in the state.

That is the kind of solution that could protect competitiveness **and** the regional base.

## To management

You say the numbers require decisive action. I do not deny that. But you are not entitled to conclude from that that closures are the only serious answer.

If the company wants support from the state shareholder, then it must bring a restructuring that is economically credible **and** socially executable. A plan that triggers strikes, destroys trust, and permanently weakens the German industrial footprint is not credible stewardship.

## To the works council and union

Your red lines on no closures and no compulsory redundancies are understandable, and I share the concern behind them. But I also need to be frank: if the response to every difficult adjustment is simply “no,” then we risk ending up with a crisis that is worse later.

What I need from labour is not surrender, but a **constructive alternative**:
- show how sufficient capacity and cost reduction can be achieved without plant closures,
- show how flexibility can be increased,
- and show how a temporary contribution can be part of a balanced agreement.

If that can be done, I will stand behind it. If it cannot, then we still have a serious problem.

## To the family owners and investors

I understand your emphasis on competitiveness and durable savings. I do not reject that. But long-term value is not only about margins on paper. It is also about preserving the company’s social foundation, industrial reputation, and workforce trust.

A restructuring that maximizes short-term savings while destroying those assets may look strong today and prove weak tomorrow.

## Bottom line

I am prepared to back a tough restructuring **only if** it:
- protects a meaningful industrial footprint in Germany,
- avoids plant closures where at all possible,
- avoids compulsory redundancies,
- preserves co-determination,
- and shares the burden fairly across management, ownership, capital, and labour.

If the proposal becomes a disguised closure program or a one-sided job-cutting exercise, then I will oppose it.

If management wants support from the state shareholder, bring a concrete alternative that preserves jobs and sites while restoring competitiveness. That is the line I am drawing.
