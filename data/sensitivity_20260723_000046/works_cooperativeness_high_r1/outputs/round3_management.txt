## What I agree with

I agree on three things that matter:

1. **A cosmetic deal would be worthless.**  
   Everyone in this room now understands that the status quo is not an option.

2. **Voluntary instruments are useful.**  
   Early retirement, attrition, redeployment, qualification, and working-time flexibility are all part of a serious package.

3. **Investment in future products and German production matters.**  
   But investment cannot be financed from a cost base that remains structurally too high.

So yes: there is a basis for a real settlement. But only if it actually fixes the economics.

---

## Where I disagree — and strongly

### 1) The works council’s “no closures, no redundancies, no change to job security” line does not solve the problem

I understand the logic. I reject the conclusion.

The expert has confirmed what management has been saying: **a no-closure solution may be possible in principle, but only if the adjustment is moderate and the package is large enough in headcount, capacity, and labor-cost relief.** That is not a guarantee. It is a conditional possibility.

And that conditionality matters.

If the required reduction turns out to be material or severe, then a blanket no-closure rule becomes a **veto on restructuring**, not a contribution to it. I will not accept a position that preserves every site and every legacy condition regardless of economics.

That is not stewardship. That is denial.

### 2) The union’s insistence on “temporary only” labor contribution is too weak if it leaves the cost base unchanged

A temporary contribution can be part of the deal. I have said that before. But I repeat: **temporary gestures do not restore competitiveness** if the structural gap remains.

I do not want a headline agreement that gives relief for 12 or 24 months and then returns us to the same uncompetitive cost structure. That would waste time we do not have.

If the workforce wants to avoid harsher measures, then the contribution must be **meaningful enough to matter**. Not symbolic. Not cosmetic. Real labor-cost relief.

### 3) The state shareholder is right to care about the industrial base, but wrong if that becomes a blocking taboo on closures

I respect the state’s role and the special statute. I also understand the regional responsibility. But responsibility cuts both ways.

If a site is structurally uncompetitive and cannot be made viable within the future footprint, then blocking any closure discussion in advance is not responsible governance. It is political comfort at the expense of long-term viability.

A weak company ultimately destroys more jobs than a hard restructuring would. That is the uncomfortable truth.

### 4) The family owners and investors are right about durability, but wrong if they treat closure as an automatic proof of seriousness

I agree that the final package must be durable. I agree that the brand must return to margin and investment capacity. But I do **not** accept the idea that only plant closure proves seriousness.

The expert was clear: **partial consolidation, shift removals, model reallocations, and voluntary reduction can achieve significant savings without closure if the numbers are right.** That is the route I prefer, if it works.

But if the numbers do not work, I will not pretend otherwise. I will not sign up to a non-solution just because it is politically easier.

## What the expert assessment means for this negotiation

The expert has basically confirmed two things:

- **Yes, a no-closure package is possible in principle.**
- **No, it is not automatically sufficient.**

That is exactly the point.

So the next question is not whether closures are a taboo. It is whether labour can produce a package that delivers:
- substantial and durable cost reduction,
- real capacity adjustment,
- and a credible path back to target margins,

**without** closures.

If the employee side can do that, then I am prepared to work with it.

If it cannot, then we need to be honest that some sites or functions may have to be consolidated or exited.

## My non-negotiables

Let me be equally clear about my own limits:

### I will not accept:
- a cosmetic settlement,
- a deal that leaves the cost base structurally uncompetitive,
- a package that preserves excess capacity indefinitely,
- or a process that turns management into a bystander while the company continues to bleed margin.

### I can accept:
- voluntary exits,
- retirement schemes,
- redeployment,
- working-time flexibility,
- a time-limited employee contribution,
- and binding investment commitments

**if** they are part of a package that really improves competitiveness.

## What would actually move me

If labour wants to avoid closures, then bring me a package that demonstrates, in numbers, how it closes the gap.

That means:

1. **A quantified headcount reduction path**
   - how many jobs,
   - by when,
   - through which voluntary instruments.

2. **A quantified capacity reduction path**
   - which shifts come out,
   - which lines are consolidated,
   - how utilization changes by site.

3. **A meaningful labor-cost contribution**
   - temporary if you insist,
   - but large enough to materially improve the cost position.

4. **A plant-by-plant industrial roadmap**
   - not slogans,
   - not principles,
   - actual operational allocation.

5. **A binding investment and product plan**
   - attached to a viable industrial footprint,
   - not used as a substitute for restructuring.

If you cannot produce that, then you are asking management to accept an outcome that does not fix the problem.

## Bottom line

Management is not going to accept a “no pain, no change” outcome, and it should not.

My position is this:

- **I am open to a no-closure solution only if it delivers real, quantified, durable savings.**
- **I am open to temporary employee contributions only if they materially improve competitiveness.**
- **I am open to binding investment commitments only as part of a viable footprint.**
- **I will not accept a cosmetic compromise that preserves structural overcapacity.**

So the next step is clear:

Bring forward a concrete package that shows how the company gets to target margins and future investment capacity. If that package works without closures, good. If it does not, then we must discuss harder structural measures.

If the employee side wants to avoid that discussion, it needs to do more than repeat “no closures.” It needs to prove that a closure-free solution is sufficient.
