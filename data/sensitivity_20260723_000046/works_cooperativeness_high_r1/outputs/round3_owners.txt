I will be direct.

The discussion is now clearer, and that helps. But I need to say plainly: **I do not accept a settlement that preserves an uncompetitive structure and calls it stewardship**. At the same time, I also do not accept a package that triggers prolonged industrial conflict and destroys value in the process. My standard remains the same: **restore competitiveness and margins, and do it in a way the company can actually execute**.

## Where I agree

### To management
I agree that:
- the current cost base is too high,
- the status quo is not sustainable,
- temporary fixes are not enough,
- and the company needs real structural improvement.

I also agree that voluntary tools matter, and that investment in future products and German production must remain part of the picture.

### To the expert
The expert assessment is useful because it corrects two simplifications:
- a **no-closure** package is not impossible in principle;
- but it is **not automatically sufficient**.

That is exactly the right framing. The burden now is to show whether the numbers work.

### To the state shareholder
I agree that we should avoid hollowing out the industrial base. A devastated German footprint would be bad for the company, the region, and the owners.

### To labour
I agree that a cosmetic deal would be worthless, and that prolonged conflict would damage the company. I also agree that voluntary exits, retirement pathways, redeployment, and working-time flexibility are legitimate tools.

## Where I disagree

### 1) On “no closures” as an absolute rule
I do **not** accept an absolute no-closure rule.

The works council and union have presented this as if it were the only legitimate basis for negotiation. It is not. If a site is structurally uncompetitive, governance cannot pre-commit to keeping it open regardless of economics. That would be a refusal to face the problem.

I am **not** saying closures are the preferred answer. I am saying they must remain possible if the economics require them.

### 2) On compulsory redundancies
I understand the workforce’s opposition. But I will not support a restructuring that assumes voluntary tools will solve a large structural gap if the numbers do not justify that belief.

If voluntary exits and retirement pathways can deliver enough adjustment, excellent. If they cannot, then the package must be honest about that. A commitment to “no compulsory redundancies” cannot become a veto on meaningful restructuring.

### 3) On temporary employee contributions
I am open to a temporary employee contribution, but only if it is **meaningful enough to matter**.

A symbolic or very short-lived gesture is not a restructuring instrument. If labour contributes, that contribution must materially improve the cost position, not merely buy time.

### 4) On guarantees that freeze the current structure
I do **not** support guarantees that lock in the present footprint before the economics are fixed.

Site and job guarantees can be part of a viable package, but only if they sit inside a plan that actually restores competitiveness. Guarantees first, economics later, is the wrong sequence.

## My response to the others

### To the works council
You are right to insist that the workforce should not be the sole shock absorber. I agree with that principle.

But your current position still does not answer the central economic question: **how do you close the gap if closures are ruled out in advance and redundancies are excluded entirely?** The expert has said that a no-closure route is only plausible if the required adjustment is moderate and the package is strong enough. That is a conditional answer, not a blank cheque.

So I need more than a defensive stance. I need a **quantified industrial plan**:
- how much headcount reduction,
- through which voluntary tools,
- by when,
- at what cost,
- and how capacity is reduced without leaving the network structurally weak.

If you cannot show that, then “no closures” is not a plan. It is a slogan.

### To the union
I agree that permanent wage erosion is unacceptable. I am not asking for that as a headline demand.

But I do reject the idea that any deep labor-cost adjustment is illegitimate. If the company remains structurally too expensive, the burden has to be shared in a way that materially improves competitiveness.

A temporary contribution is acceptable only if it is:
- limited in time,
- meaningful in effect,
- part of a broader package,
- and matched by contributions from management, owners, and capital.

### To management
You are right to insist on structural change. But be careful not to confuse seriousness with maximalism.

If the restructuring is designed in a way that cannot be executed because it triggers institutional blockage, then it fails. The package has to be hard enough to matter, but credible enough to stick.

### To the state shareholder
You are right to care about industrial legitimacy and regional employment. But I cannot support a position that effectively forbids difficult site decisions in advance.

If the state wants to help preserve the footprint, it must help produce a plan that makes that footprint viable. Otherwise the problem just comes back later, worse.

### To investors
I agree that the final outcome must be durable and value-accretive. But durability does not come from theatrically closing a site. A bad closure package can destroy more value than it saves.

## What would move me

I would support a package if it shows, in numbers:

- substantial and durable cost reduction,
- real capacity reduction,
- improved productivity,
- meaningful voluntary exits and retirement pathways,
- a temporary and fair employee contribution,
- and binding investment commitments tied to a viable industrial roadmap.

That means:
1. a headcount reduction path,
2. a capacity reduction path,
3. a plant-by-plant operating model,
4. and a credible funding logic for the next product cycle.

## My limit

I will **not** support:
- a cosmetic compromise,
- a deal that leaves the cost base structurally too high,
- a package that preserves excess capacity indefinitely,
- or a compromise that avoids hard decisions at the expense of enterprise value.

## Bottom line

The family holding is prepared to back a **hard but credible restructuring** that restores competitiveness and preserves the company’s long-term industrial strength.

We are **not** prepared to back:
- an absolute no-closure taboo,
- a plan that freezes legacy structure regardless of economics,
- or a settlement that shifts the entire burden onto labour while asking owners and governance to accept lower value.

So the next step is not another round of principles. It is numbers.

If the employee side wants to avoid closures, it must show a closure-free package that truly closes the gap. If it cannot, then we have to keep all structural options on the table.
