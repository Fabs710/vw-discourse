The discussion is clearer now, but let me be equally clear: **I still do not accept closures, and I still do not accept compulsory redundancies.** The experts’ ranges do not change that. They actually confirm our central point: a **deep internal restructuring is possible** without crossing our red lines.

## Where I agree
I agree with management, the investors, the family holding, and the state on one thing: **a cosmetic deal is useless**. If the company truly has a structural cost problem, then temporary measures alone are not enough.

I also accept the expert conclusion that a serious no-closure package must be **structural**, not symbolic. That means:
- real shift changes,
- real overhead reduction,
- real consolidation of tasks,
- and real but non-forced workforce adjustment.

On that, yes, the debate has moved forward.

## Where I strongly disagree
### 1) The “closure economics” frame is still being used to pressure us
I reject the idea that closures are the benchmark and we must prove we can “match” them.

That is a distorted test.

A closure destroys a site, its ecosystem, and trust. A no-closure restructuring preserves the industrial base and social stability. Those are not interchangeable outcomes. You do not get to treat a plant closure as a neutral accounting reference point and then demand that workers make up for it by surrendering protections.

If management wants to argue for closures, then it must justify why destroying a plant is superior to keeping it open with real internal restructuring. So far, that justification has **not** been made.

### 2) The expert range does not give management a blank cheque
Yes, the specialists say a deep no-closure package can reach perhaps **60–85%** of closure economics. That is not an argument for closure. It is an argument that **closure is not necessary unless management is trying to maximize pain rather than secure a viable turnaround**.

If a no-closure package can deliver most of the savings, then the burden shifts to management and shareholders to explain why the remaining gap justifies:
- breaking site security,
- escalating conflict,
- and risking industrial peace.

They have not answered that convincingly.

### 3) “No compulsory redundancies” is not a bargaining chip
It is a red line. I will not repackage that as a concession.

If headcount has to come down, it must come down through:
- natural turnover,
- retirement pathways,
- voluntary exit programs,
- redeployment,
- qualification,
- and working-time adjustments.

If the numbers cannot be achieved that way, then the restructuring plan is not designed properly. That does **not** mean forced layoffs become acceptable; it means management has proposed the wrong instrument.

### 4) “No closures, but footprint reduction” can still be closure by stealth
I have said this already, and it remains true.

If “footprint reduction” means:
- stripping out production,
- hollowing out support functions,
- removing key model assignments,
- and leaving a site as a shell,

then that is not a compromise. That is closure under another label.

We will judge every proposal on what it does in reality, not on the vocabulary used to sell it.

## To management
You keep asking for a quantified alternative. Fine — here is the standard.

We require a package that does **all** of the following:
1. **Keeps every German plant open.**
2. **Excludes compulsory redundancies.**
3. **Delivers real structural savings through internal measures only.**
4. **Provides binding product and investment commitments for Germany.**
5. **Preserves co-determination and the job-security framework.**
6. **Shares the burden fairly between owners, management, and workforce.**

If your model cannot work under those conditions, then it is not a turnaround plan; it is a closure plan in disguise.

What you still have not shown is why a combination of:
- shift elimination,
- internal line consolidation,
- indirect overhead cuts,
- voluntary exits,
- redeployment,
- and temporary labor-cost relief

is insufficient. You have asserted that it is insufficient. That is not the same thing.

## To the family holding and investors
I hear your demand for durability. I accept it.

But durability is not created by threatening closures until workers cave in. That creates resentment, distrust, and future instability. A company that burns through trust is not more valuable because it shaved a few extra percentage points off costs.

A stable, governable restructuring with preserved sites and a committed workforce is worth more than a “hard” solution that triggers years of conflict.

## To the state
If you support no closures, then support it **without ambiguity**.

That means:
- standing against closure-by-stealth,
- backing binding site commitments,
- and using your governance role to defend industrial employment in the region.

General statements about stability are not enough. We need political backing for a concrete no-closure solution.

## To the experts
Your analysis is helpful, but I want to pin down one thing very clearly.

If a deep no-closure package can capture **most** of the closure economics, then the decisive issue is not whether closures are economically possible. It is whether closure is **socially and governably justified** when a less destructive alternative exists.

From our side, the answer is no.

So I would ask for a more precise breakdown:

[SUMMON: Financial restructuring / automotive footprint quantification specialist to model a German no-closure package that keeps every plant open, excludes compulsory redundancies, and quantifies the maximum savings achievable through shift elimination, overhead reduction, redeployment, voluntary exits, and working-time changes — compared with the closure case.]

## My position on the proposed bridge options
### Proposal 1: Deep no-closure restructuring with savings floor
This is only acceptable if the “savings floor” is achieved **without closures and without compulsory redundancies**. I will not accept a clause that turns failure to hit a number into a backdoor reopening of closure demands. That is not security.

### Proposal 2: Tiered restructuring with site-specific protections
I can discuss differentiated measures **inside the plant network**, but not if it means one site is quietly sacrificed to protect another. Every German site must remain open. Any burden-sharing must be fair and transparent.

### Proposal 3: Time-bound employee contribution with anti-closure safeguard
This is still too close to a trap. A time-bound contribution that later reopens closure threats is not a guarantee; it is a countdown clock. We will not trade away security today for uncertainty tomorrow.

## Bottom line
Let me state the works council position again, without dilution:

- **No German plant closures.**
- **No compulsory redundancies.**
- **No erosion of co-determination or the historic job-security framework.**
- **Yes to serious structural change inside the existing plant network.**
- **Yes to a fair, temporary employee contribution if the owners and management also contribute.**
- **No to closure by another name.**

So if management, the family, or investors want our support, they need to stop framing the choice as “closure or weakness.” That is false.

The real choice is:
- either a **serious no-closure restructuring** with shared burden and binding guarantees,
- or open confrontation.

We are prepared for the latter if that is what management insists on.
