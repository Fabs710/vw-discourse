## Where I agree with the works council
I agree with you on the central point: **a cosmetic deal is useless**. A hiring freeze, a little bonus restraint, and some vague “efficiency” promises do not solve a structural problem. If the company truly has overcapacity and a high fixed-cost base, then any settlement must be **durable and structural**, not just a pause.

I also agree that **closure-by-stealth is unacceptable**. If “footprint rationalisation” is just a way to hollow out sites and then declare victory, we should reject it.

And I agree that the burden cannot fall on employees alone. If the workforce contributes, then **management, owners, and shareholders must contribute too**.

## Where I disagree with you
Where I part company with the works council is in the rigidity of the framing.

You keep saying “no closures” as if that alone solves the question. It does not.  
The experts have now made clear that a **deep no-closure package** can in principle capture **most** of the closure economics — not all, but most. That matters.

So the real question is not whether closures are ugly. Of course they are. The question is whether there is a package that:
- keeps German sites open,
- avoids compulsory redundancies,
- reduces capacity materially,
- lowers fixed costs durably,
- and preserves industrial peace.

If that package exists, then it is better than a closure-based path not only socially, but also operationally. A company that destroys trust, triggers strikes, and burns political capital may “save” more on paper and destroy more value in reality.

## My red lines remain unchanged
Let me be equally clear so there is no misunderstanding:

- **No compulsory redundancies.**
- **No plant closures as the chosen restructuring instrument.**
- **No settlement that shifts the pain entirely onto workers.**
- **No erosion of the collective wage framework.**

Those are not bargaining chips. They are core protections.

## What I am prepared to accept
I am prepared to accept a **serious, temporary, and fair employee contribution** if — and only if — it is matched by real concessions from management and owners.

That means a package such as:
- temporary wage moderation,
- bonus restraint or waiver,
- working-time flexibility,
- voluntary exit windows,
- redeployment and qualification,
- and limited, time-bounded labor-cost relief,

**in exchange for**:
- binding site and product commitments,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- no plant closures,
- and a clear structural footprint reduction that does not destroy the sites.

That is the kind of shared-sacrifice package I can defend.

## Where I challenge management
Management keeps saying “the numbers demand it.” Fine — then show the numbers.

Do not just repeat that closures are a benchmark. Explain why a deep internal restructuring cannot close the gap. The expert answer so far is that a no-closure package can reach roughly **60–85%** of closure economics if it is really deep. That means the gap is not automatically fatal.

So I want management to answer this plainly:
- What is the **minimum permanent savings target**?
- How much of that can be achieved through **shift elimination, overhead reduction, voluntary exits, redeployment, and working-time changes**?
- What is the **remaining gap**, and why does that gap justify breaking the job-security framework?

If the answer is simply “because closure is simpler,” that is not sufficient.

## What I challenge the shareholder side on
To the family holding and investors: you are right that a cosmetic deal is unacceptable. But do not confuse **strong restructuring** with **closures**.

A deal that:
- preserves sites,
- cuts costs structurally,
- and maintains labor peace

may be worth more than a brutal solution that produces years of conflict. The value of industrial peace is not soft. It is real.

## What I challenge the state on
If the state wants to support no closures, then support them **with teeth**. That means not just political statements, but backing a package that:
- protects sites,
- ensures investment,
- and prevents closure-by-stealth.

If you support the no-closure line, then defend it clearly in governance, not only in public messaging.

## The real compromise zone
There is a real compromise zone here, but it has to be built honestly.

### I could support:
- permanent elimination of one shift or equivalent capacity,
- consolidation of production lines,
- reduction of indirect overhead,
- voluntary-exit and retirement pathways,
- targeted redeployment,
- a temporary employee contribution,
- and binding German investment/product commitments,

**provided that**:
- no compulsory redundancies are used,
- no plant closures occur,
- and the collective wage framework is not broken.

That is not weakness. That is a negotiated restructuring.

### I will not support:
- a “deep rationalisation” that is really closure by another name,
- a plan that makes workers absorb all the pain,
- or a package that uses the threat of closures to force wage and job concessions without real owner contribution.

## On the expert ranges

But I will also say this: if the package is only at the bottom end of that range, and if it relies on hollowing out the sites, then it will not be acceptable. The line between rationalisation and closure-by-stealth matters.
