Yes — I’ll stay within the restructuring / footprint / labour-cost / turnaround domain and test the economics, not the politics.

## Bottom line first

A **credible no-closure package is possible in principle**, but only if it delivers **permanent fixed-cost reduction on a scale that is much closer to a closure case than a cosmetic compromise**.

In large German automotive restructuring, the practical test is usually:

- **Temporary measures**: useful for liquidity and labour peace, but low structural value.
- **Internal rationalisation**: can deliver meaningful savings if it removes shifts, consolidates lines, cuts indirect overhead, and reduces headcount materially.
- **Closure**: delivers the largest and cleanest fixed-cost reset, but also the highest political / governance / execution risk.

If management is asking for a **durable return-on-sales reset**, then a no-closure package must typically close **most** of the gap, not just half of it. As a rule of thumb:

- **Labour-only compromise**: often only **20–35%** of closure economics.
- **Deep no-closure footprint rationalisation**: potentially **60–85%** of closure economics.
- **Full closure**: **100%** of the site-level fixed-cost relief, but with large one-off costs and much higher implementation risk.

That range is the key decision point.

---

# 1) What “closure economics” usually means

When people talk about “closure savings,” they often mean three buckets:

1. **Direct labour cost removal**
   - wages/salaries
   - employer social charges
   - shift premiums
   - temporary labour
   - overtime

2. **Indirect fixed-cost relief**
   - plant management
   - maintenance
   - utilities baseline
   - support functions
   - local overhead
   - logistics / internal transport
   - facilities

3. **Capacity / depreciation / capital efficiency**
   - lower idle capacity
   - better absorption
   - reduced complexity
   - possible working capital improvement

A full closure is attractive because it removes **a whole fixed-cost stack**.  
But the catch is that it comes with:

- severance / social plan costs
- transfer / redeployment costs
- ramp-down costs
- tooling write-downs
- contractual termination costs
- potential political and industrial conflict costs
- possible supply-chain disruption
- possible lost volume if capacity has to be rebuilt elsewhere

So closure savings are real, but they are not free.

---

# 2) What a no-closure package can realistically do

A serious no-closure package needs to remove cost through **four structural levers**:

## A. Shift elimination / line consolidation
This is the biggest non-closure lever.

Examples:
- 3-shift to 2-shift
- 2-shift to 1-shift on selected lines
- model assignment consolidation
- removal of underutilized lines
- shared production cells across plants

**Savings effect:** often very material because one shift carries labor, supervision, maintenance, energy, and support cost.

**Rule of thumb:**  
If a plant is genuinely overstaffed or underloaded, eliminating one shift can recover roughly **15–30% of site operating cost**, depending on utilization and fixed overhead mix.

## B. Indirect overhead reduction
This includes:
- site administration
- engineering support
- quality / logistics duplication
- local management layers
- purchasing / planning duplication

**Savings effect:** usually meaningful, but not enough alone.

**Rule of thumb:**  
A strong overhead reset can contribute another **5–15% of site cost** if functions are consolidated aggressively.

## C. Headcount reduction without forced layoffs
Mechanisms:
- natural attrition
- retirement corridors
- voluntary exit programs
- redeployment to growth areas
- internal conversion / qualification programs
- hiring freeze

**Savings effect:** good, but slow.

**Rule of thumb:**  
Can deliver sizeable medium-term savings, but usually too slow for a pure turnaround unless paired with shift/footprint changes.

## D. Labour-cost contribution
Measures like:
- wage moderation
- bonus restraint
- working-time flexibility
- temporary reduction in allowances
- deferred increases

**Savings effect:** useful but limited.

**Rule of thumb:**  
This often produces only **low single-digit percentage savings on total site cost**, unless the concessions are unusually deep and broad.

---

# 3) Quantified comparison: closure vs no-closure vs labour-only

Below is a simplified decision framework. Numbers are illustrative, not company-specific facts.

## Scenario 1: Closure-based restructuring
**Potential savings**
- annual fixed-cost reduction: **100% of the target site cost base**
- annual savings can be large and immediate once run-off is complete

**Costs**
- one-off restructuring cost often high:
  - severance
  - site run-down
  - tooling write-offs
  - contract termination
  - transfer costs
- depending on workforce size, this can easily be **1.0x to 2.5x annual plant payroll** or more, plus non-labour closure costs

**Pros**
- strongest and cleanest cost reset
- permanent
- simple operating model after execution

**Cons**
- high governance and industrial resistance
- high strike / legal / political risk
- risk of execution delay
- risk of value destruction if repeated elsewhere

## Scenario 2: Deep no-closure footprint rationalisation
**Potential savings**
- annual savings often **60–85% of closure economics**
- can be enough if the original closure case was partly about excess shift capacity rather than total site elimination

**Costs**
- lower one-off cost than closure, but still substantial:
  - voluntary exit packages
  - retraining / redeployment
  - line reconfiguration
  - inventory and ramp-down costs
  - possible productivity dip during transition

**Pros**
- much more feasible under co-determination
- preserves industrial footprint and brand/social stability
- lower political and board veto risk
- more likely to be negotiated successfully

**Cons**
- must be genuinely deep
- savings may take longer to realize
- can be undermined if “rationalisation” is too shallow

## Scenario 3: Labour-only compromise
**Potential savings**
- usually only **20–35% of closure economics**
- often insufficient for a major ROS reset

**Costs**
- low one-off cost
- relatively easy to implement if peace is bought

**Pros**
- fast
- politically easier
- preserves sites and jobs in the short term

**Cons**
- generally not durable enough
- high risk of backsliding
- does not remove structural overcapacity
- likely unacceptable to capital providers if the cost gap is truly structural

---

# 4) Can a no-closure package “close the gap”?

## Yes, if these conditions are met:
1. **One or more shifts are removed permanently**
2. **Model allocation or volume is consolidated**
3. **Indirect overhead is structurally cut**
4. **Headcount falls materially over time**
5. **Labour-cost concessions are real, not symbolic**
6. **There is a binding volume / investment commitment to keep the plants loaded enough**

If all six happen, a no-closure package can often get **most of the way** to the economics of closure.

## No, if the package is mainly:
- hiring freeze
- wage moderation
- bonus cap
- voluntary exits only
- vague efficiency promises

That package is usually too weak.

---

# 5) What level of employee contribution is “acceptable” economically?

From a restructuring finance perspective, “acceptable” means **enough to materially reduce the gap without requiring closure**.

A practical benchmark would be:

- **temporary labour cost relief**: 1–3 years
- **permanent labour-cost reset**: moderate but real
- **headcount reduction**: large enough to affect fixed cost, not just natural drift

## Roughly, what that means:
A credible employee contribution might include:
- 3–8% labour-cost relief on the affected population for a limited period
- no or reduced bonus payout for 1–2 cycles
- working-time flexibility / reduced premium cost
- voluntary exits sized to remove a meaningful percentage of indirect and direct labour

If the company needs a far larger reset than that, then employee concessions alone won’t do it.

---

# 6) One important point: savings timing matters

This is often overlooked.

A closure can create **large eventual savings**, but it may take **12–36 months** to fully realize them because of:
- run-off
- labor law / collective process
- redeployment
- technical transfer
- customer approval cycles

A no-closure package can sometimes start faster on:
- overtime reduction
- shift changes
- overhead cuts
- freeze on backfilling

So even if closure gives larger ultimate savings, a deep no-closure package can sometimes deliver **faster near-term EBITDA relief** if implemented cleanly.

That matters for liquidity and confidence.

---

# 7) Board feasibility under co-determination

This is critical.

Even if the closure case is financially stronger, it may be **less board-feasible** because:
- employee representatives can block
- state/anchor shareholders can block
- family owners may prefer value-preserving compromise
- prolonged conflict can make the closure case unattractive

So from a practical standpoint, the “best” restructuring is not the one with the largest theoretical savings. It is the one with the best combination of:

- savings durability
- execution certainty
- governance passability
- industrial peace
- investment credibility

On that basis, a **deep no-closure package** can actually be the superior board option even if it is not the absolute cheapest in a spreadsheet.

# 9) Practical conclusion

## If the question is:
**Can a no-closure package with shift elimination, overhead reduction, and voluntary exits close the required savings gap?**

## My answer:
**Yes, potentially — but only if it is deep enough to remove most of the fixed-cost benefit that closure would have delivered.**

In practical terms, it must likely include:
- permanent shift elimination on selected lines
- footprint consolidation across plants
- significant overhead removal
- sizeable voluntary headcount reduction
- temporary labour-cost contribution
- binding product and investment allocation to avoid underutilization

If it is built that way, it can plausibly achieve **roughly 60–85% of closure economics**, which may be enough if:
- closure would trigger unacceptable governance/industrial risk, and
- the company’s immediate objective is a durable competitiveness reset rather than maximum theoretical savings.

If it falls short of that, then it is likely just a delay tactic.
