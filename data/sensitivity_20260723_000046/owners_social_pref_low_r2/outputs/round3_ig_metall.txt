I will speak plainly.

The works council is right to reject a closure-by-stealth strategy, and I support that. I also agree with the state and the experts on one important point: **temporary measures alone are not enough** if the company truly has a structural cost problem. That is the point where the debate has become more serious.

But I do **not** accept the attempt to turn “equivalent to closure” into the standard of truth. A plant closure is not a neutral benchmark. It is the most destructive option, and you do not get to define the burden of proof in a way that assumes our people must first accept that destruction before any alternative is considered.

## Where I agree
### To management
Yes, the company needs durable change.  
Yes, a symbolic deal would fail.  
Yes, investment and future products in Germany have to be part of the package.

### To the experts
Yes, a no-closure package can only work if it is **structural**, not cosmetic.  
Yes, wage moderation alone is not enough.  
Yes, real capacity reduction and overhead reduction matter.

### To the state
Yes, industrial stability and regional employment matter.  
Yes, a value-destroying strike or deadlock would hurt everyone.

## Where I disagree
### 1) I reject closures as the default logic
Management still talks as if closures are the natural reference point and everything else must be measured against them. That is backwards.

The proper question is not:  
“Which package is closest to a closure?”

The proper question is:  
“Which package actually reduces cost durably while preserving jobs, sites, and industrial trust?”

That distinction matters. A company can achieve substantial structural savings without wiping out a site. If management insists otherwise, it must prove it with numbers, not slogans.

### 2) I reject any strategy that hollows out sites and then calls it compromise
The works council is right to warn about closure by another name. I share that concern.

So let me be equally clear:  
if “footprint rationalisation” means removing a shift, transferring volume, reducing indirect overhead, and keeping the site operational, that can be part of a deal.  
If it means draining a plant until closure becomes inevitable, then it is unacceptable.

### 3) I reject compulsory redundancies
This remains a hard red line.

If the company needs headcount reduction, it must come through:
- natural attrition,
- retirement pathways,
- voluntary exits,
- redeployment,
- qualification and conversion,
- and negotiated working-time and flexibility measures.

We will not agree to forced layoffs as the price of restructuring. That is not a bargaining point for us; it is a line.

## What would move us
The expert discussion has clarified something useful: a **no-closure deep rationalisation** can, in principle, capture a large share of closure economics if it is real. That matters.

But then management has to stop using closures as a pressure tactic and put a serious package on the table. That package would need to include:

1. **No compulsory redundancies.**
2. **No German plant closures.**
3. **Real structural capacity reduction** through shift elimination, model reassignment, and overhead reduction.
4. **A temporary, fair employee contribution** that is limited and reversible.
5. **Binding commitments on investment, future products, and German site roles.**
6. **A time horizon that allows adjustment without breaking the workforce.**

That is the minimum basis for a fair settlement.

## To management
You say you need durable savings. I accept that.  
But durable savings do not automatically require closures. What I still have not heard is a convincing explanation of why the alternatives are insufficient.

If the company can get 60–85% of closure economics through a no-closure package, then the remaining question is not just financial. It is also strategic and social: is the marginal gain worth the industrial conflict, the governance risk, and the destruction of trust?

That burden is still on management.

## To investors and the family holding
You are right to reject a cosmetic compromise. But do not confuse **serious restructuring** with **plant closure**. They are not the same thing.

A stable company with protected labor peace, reduced footprint, and a fair burden-sharing arrangement is more valuable than a “quick” solution that triggers conflict and weakens the entire industrial model.

## To the state
I welcome your clearer support for site preservation. But I want to be equally blunt: if you support no closures, then support it with real political backing for a structural compromise, not just with general statements about stability.

## To the works council
We are aligned on the red lines:
- no closures,
- no forced redundancies,
- no erosion of the collective framework.

Where I want to sharpen the discussion is this: if real structural savings can be achieved **inside the existing plant network**, then we should not reject them just because they are uncomfortable. The question is not whether the workforce contributes. We already said we are prepared to contribute. The question is whether everyone else contributes too, and whether the package truly protects jobs and sites.

## My bottom line
I am not retreating from our core position.

- **No compulsory redundancies.**
- **No German plant closures.**
- **Yes to a fair, temporary employee contribution.**
- **Yes to real structural savings, but through negotiated internal measures, not destruction of sites.**
- **Yes to binding investment and product commitments.**
- **No one-sided restructuring at the expense of the workforce.**

If management wants a settlement, it should stop treating closure as the benchmark and start quantifying a genuine no-closure package. If it can show a structurally deep, durable alternative, then we can talk. If not, then the conflict will escalate, and it will be management — not the workforce — that will have chosen that path.
