From the capital markets’ perspective, the discussion is now useful because it has finally narrowed to the real question: **does the package permanently restore competitiveness, or does it merely postpone the same margin problem?**

I will be blunt. We are **not** interested in a cosmetic no-closure settlement, and we are **not** interested in a settlement that protects every legacy arrangement while leaving the cost base structurally uncompetitive. If that is the outcome, it will not be supportable.

## Where I agree

### To management
You are right that the company needs **durable structural savings**. Temporary measures alone do not fix an overcapacity problem. A hiring freeze, bonus restraint, and working-time flexibility may help in the short term, but they do not by themselves restore return on sales.

### To the experts
Your key point is sound: **a no-closure package can work, but only if it is genuinely structural**. If “no closure” simply means temporary concessions and vague efficiency promises, then it is too weak. We agree that the savings must come from:
- shift elimination or equivalent capacity reduction,
- lower indirect overhead,
- material headcount reduction through attrition and voluntary exits,
- and a meaningful employee contribution.

### To the state and workforce side
We also agree that a prolonged strike or governance deadlock would destroy value. Share price, investor confidence, supplier stability, and management focus all suffer if this drags on.

## Where I disagree

### To the works council and union
Your position remains too absolute.

A blanket **“no closures, no compulsory redundancies, no meaningful guarantee changes”** line does not answer the economic problem. It may be understandable politically, but it does not yet show how the company reaches competitive margins. If the final result is simply to preserve all sites and all protections, then the restructuring is too weak.

We do **not** say closures are desirable. We say the burden is on you to demonstrate that an alternative delivers **equivalent durable savings**. That is the only financially relevant test.

### To the regional state
I understand the political role, but economic reality still matters. Preserving regional employment is valuable only if the enterprise remains competitive enough to support it. A solution that protects the footprint but leaves the business structurally impaired is not a long-term win for us, or for the region.

### To the family holding
You are right to reject a value-destructive confrontation. But I would caution against treating “no closures” as the default acceptable answer. If the package cannot materially reduce the cost base, then a no-closure position becomes an expensive postponement, not a solution.

## What we would support

We are open to a settlement that is **not closure-first**, but it must still be **hard enough to matter**.

That means:

1. **Real structural capacity reduction**  
   Not just slogans. There must be measurable footprint rationalization.

2. **Material labour-cost contribution**  
   Limited, negotiated, but real.

3. **Durable headcount reduction**  
   Preferably through voluntary exits, retirement pathways, attrition, and redeployment — but large enough to move the numbers.

4. **Productivity and flexibility gains**  
   Otherwise the restructuring remains incomplete.

5. **Binding investment commitments**  
   If employees give, management and owners must commit to future products and capital allocation in Germany.

## What we will not support

We will not support:
- a cosmetic peace deal,
- an outcome that leaves margins structurally impaired,
- or a package where the company buys short-term labour peace at the cost of long-term competitiveness.

We also will not support open-ended delay. The market is already judging whether this business can restore return on sales and reset its fixed-cost base.

## Our practical position

We are not ideological about whether the savings come from closures or from deep rationalisation. We care about **economic outcome, durability, and execution certainty**.

So our test is simple:
- Does the package credibly close the cost gap?
- Is the savings durable over a 3–5 year horizon?
- Can it pass the supervisory board and survive industrial relations?
- Does it free capital for investment?

If the answer is yes, we can support it. If the answer is no, then the package is not good enough, regardless of how politically attractive it may be.

## On the current debate

The specialists have basically confirmed the main point we care about: a **no-closure package is only credible if it removes enough fixed cost**. That is the bar.

So, to be explicit:

- **We are open to a no-closure restructuring**, if it includes real capacity reduction and not just temporary concessions.
- **We are not open to a settlement that preserves the current cost structure** and calls that a compromise.
- **We are not open to prolonged uncertainty** while the margin problem continues.

## What would move us

A credible package would need to quantify:
- annual permanent savings,
- one-off restructuring costs,
- headcount reduction,
- footprint reduction,
- and the timing to get back to competitive margins.

If management and the employee side can show that a no-closure solution really does that, then we can support it. If they cannot, then the current position is not financially acceptable.

## Bottom line

We are prepared to back a **serious no-closure restructuring** only if it is genuinely structural and big enough to restore competitiveness. If it is only a compromise that avoids hard decisions, we will not support it.

We want:
- durable fixed-cost reduction,
- a credible return to competitive margins,
- and deal certainty.

Without those three, the outcome is insufficient.
