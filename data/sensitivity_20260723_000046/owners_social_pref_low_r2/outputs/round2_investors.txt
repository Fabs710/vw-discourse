From the capital markets’ perspective, the discussion is now moving closer to the real issue: **what package actually removes enough fixed cost to restore margins, while still being executable under this governance structure**.

I will be blunt. We are **not** interested in a cosmetic compromise, and we are **not** interested in a settlement that preserves every legacy arrangement and calls that “transformation.” If the outcome leaves the cost base structurally uncompetitive, we will judge it as insufficient.

## Where we agree

### To management
You are right on the core financial point: the company needs **durable structural savings**, not just a pause. Temporary working-time measures, hiring freezes, and short-term wage moderation may help cash flow, but they do not by themselves fix an overcapacity problem.

### To the specialist
Your framing is useful and, frankly, realistic: **no-closure can work only if it is actually deep**. If “no closure” just means shifting the pain into temporary concessions while the footprint stays broadly intact, then that will not be enough. We agree that the savings must come from:
- shift elimination or equivalent capacity reduction,
- lower indirect overhead,
- headcount reduction through attrition/voluntary exits,
- and a meaningful employee contribution.

### To the state and workforce side
We also agree that a prolonged strike or governance deadlock would destroy value. The share price, supplier confidence, and management focus all suffer if this becomes a drawn-out industrial conflict.

## Where we disagree

### To the works council and union
Your position is still too absolute.

A blanket **“no closures, no compulsory redundancies, no meaningful changes to guarantees”** line does not answer the economic problem. It may be understandable from a labor-protection standpoint, but it does not yet show how the company reaches competitive margins. If the result is simply to protect every site and every guarantee, then the restructuring is too weak.

We do not say closures are desirable. We say **the burden is on you to prove that an alternative delivers equivalent durable savings**. That is the only financially relevant test. If the alternative is smaller, slower, or reversible in practice, then it fails.

### To the regional state
I understand the political and industrial anchor role, but that does not change the economics. Preserving regional employment is valuable only if the enterprise remains competitive enough to support it. A solution that protects the footprint but leaves the business structurally impaired is not a long-term win for us, or for the region.

### To the family holding
You are right to reject a value-destructive confrontation, but I would caution against treating “no closures” as the default acceptable answer. If the package cannot materially reduce the cost base, then a no-closure position becomes an expensive postponement. That is not a solution; it is drift.

## What we would consider acceptable

We are open to a settlement that is **not closure-first**, but it must still be **hard enough to matter**.

That means:
1. **Real structural capacity reduction**  
   Not just slogans. There must be measurable footprint rationalization.

2. **Material labour-cost contribution**  
   Limited, negotiated, but real.

3. **Durable headcount reduction**  
   Preferably through voluntary exits, retirement pathways, attrition, and redeployment — but large enough to move the numbers.

4. **Productivity and flexibility gains**  
   Otherwise the restructuring remains incomplete.

5. **Binding investment commitments**  
   If employees give, management and owners must commit to future products and capital allocation in Germany.

## What we will not support

We will not support:
- a cosmetic “peace deal,”
- an outcome that leaves margins structurally impaired,
- or a package where the company buys short-term labor peace at the cost of long-term competitiveness.

We also will not support indefinite delay. The market is already judging this business on whether it can restore return on sales and reset its fixed cost base.

## Our practical position

We are not ideological about whether the savings come from closures or from deep rationalisation. We care about **economic outcome, durability, and execution certainty**.

So our test is simple:
- Does the package credibly close the cost gap?
- Is the savings durable over a 3–5 year horizon?
- Can it pass the supervisory board and survive industrial relations?
- Does it free capital for investment?

If the answer is yes, we can support it. If the answer is no, then the package is not good enough, regardless of how politically attractive it may be.

## Bottom line

We are prepared to back a **serious no-closure restructuring** only if it is genuinely structural and big enough to restore competitiveness. If it is only a compromise that avoids hard decisions, we will not support it.

If management, the state, and the workforce want market support, then bring a quantified package that shows:
- how much cost comes out permanently,
- over what time frame,
- and how the company regains margin discipline without triggering a destructive conflict.

Until then, our position remains: **no cosmetic settlement, no structurally impaired margins, no prolonged uncertainty.**
