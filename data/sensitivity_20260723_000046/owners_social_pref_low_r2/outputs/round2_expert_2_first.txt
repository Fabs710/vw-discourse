## Expert assessment: can a no-closure deep rationalisation package be made to work?

**Yes, plausibly — but only under strict conditions.**  
A **no-closure, no-compulsory-redundancy** package can sometimes deliver enough savings in an automotive footprint **if it is genuinely structural**, not just a wage freeze or temporary flexibility deal. In other words: **it can work if “no closure” does not mean “no footprint reduction.”**

### My headline view
A credible package would need to combine:

- **permanent capacity reduction** through shift elimination and line consolidation,
- **lower indirect overhead** across plants,
- **material headcount reduction** via attrition, voluntary exits, and redeployment,
- **temporary but meaningful labour-cost contribution**,
- **tight investment/product allocation in Germany**, and
- **clear enforceability** over several years.

If those elements are present, then a no-closure deal can often capture something like **60–85% of closure economics**, depending on the starting overcapacity and how much duplicate fixed cost is removed. If the package is mostly temporary or symbolic, it will usually fall short.

---

## 1) The economic test is not “no closure” — it is “does fixed cost come out?”

For a carmaker, the biggest savings typically come from removing:

- direct labour,
- indirect labour and support functions,
- shift-related costs,
- utilities and maintenance,
- plant overhead,
- logistics complexity,
- and future capex obligations.

A plant closure is powerful because it removes a full stack of fixed and semi-fixed cost.  
But **a deep rationalisation without closure can get surprisingly close** if it removes an entire shift system, consolidates models, and strips out duplicated overhead.

### Important distinction
There is a major difference between:

- **a site staying open with reduced output but unchanged structure**, and
- **a site staying open but losing a shift, a product family, and much of its overhead base**.

Only the second can approach closure-like savings.

---

## 2) What size of savings would be needed?

I cannot know the company’s internal target, but for a large German OEM brand with an uncompetitive cost base, the savings requirement is often in the range of:

- **hundreds of millions of euros annually**, and sometimes
- **low single-digit billions annually** if the margin gap and overcapacity are severe.

A labour-only package usually does **not** solve that scale of problem unless overcapacity is modest.

### Rule of thumb
If management is serious about restoring return on sales, it will need a package that reduces the cost base **permanently**, not just delays pain. That means:

- reducing the number of production hours required per unit,
- reducing indirect employees,
- lowering fixed plant burden,
- and aligning German output more closely with demand.

---

## 3) What measures are reversible vs structural?

### Reversible measures
These help, but are not enough on their own:

- hiring freeze
- overtime reduction
- temporary working-time cuts
- wage moderation for a defined period
- bonus restraint
- short-time work
- voluntary exit programs
- redeployment

These are useful bridge measures. They buy time and cash.

### Structural measures
These are what make the deal durable:

- permanent elimination of one or more shifts
- transfer of production of a model or variant
- consolidation of plants’ roles
- reduction of indirect staffing
- outsourcing of non-core functions
- redesign of compensation architecture for new hires
- long-term footprint reduction within sites

This is what distinguishes a real restructuring from a pause.

---

## 4) Can “no compulsory redundancies” still work?

**Yes, but only if headcount falls materially through non-forced mechanisms.**

The realistic tools are:

- natural attrition,
- retirement,
- voluntary severance,
- internal redeployment,
- qualification/retraining,
- reduced hiring,
- and, where legally and operationally possible, transition to lower headcount through attrition over multiple years.

### Limitation
If the headcount reduction required is large and rapid, then a no-forced-redundancy constraint becomes much harder to meet. The more severe the overcapacity, the more the company needs either:

- time, or
- stronger incentives for voluntary exits, or
- structural output transfer to other sites.

So the feasibility depends heavily on the **time horizon**. Over **3–5 years**, no-forced-redundancy is much more plausible than over 12–18 months.

---

## 5) What employee contribution is economically meaningful?

A meaningful employee contribution usually has to be **material, multi-layered, and time-limited**, for example:

- wage moderation for 1–3 years,
- reduced bonuses or deferred variable pay,
- temporary reduction in special payments,
- more flexible working-time arrangements,
- productivity-related changes to shift patterns,
- limited concessions for new hires or certain groups.

### What is usually not enough
- a one-off symbolic payment,
- a small temporary freeze,
- or a concession that only shifts timing, not total cost.

If management needs durable structural savings, the employee contribution should be framed as **one part of the package**, not the whole package.

---

## 6) Board feasibility under co-determination

Given the governance structure you described, the package has to satisfy several veto holders. That means the solution must be:

- **economically credible** to management and shareholders,
- **site-preserving** enough for employee representatives and the state,
- **non-catastrophic** for industrial relations,
- and **simple enough to monitor and enforce**.

That strongly favours a package of the following type:

### Most feasible compromise
- no full German plant closures at the outset,
- elimination of one shift or equivalent capacity,
- relocation of some product volumes,
- a defined voluntary exit program,
- temporary labour-cost concessions,
- and binding commitments on investment and product allocation.

This is the best chance of getting through co-determination because it preserves sites while still creating genuine structural savings.

---

## 7) What is the biggest risk to feasibility?

The biggest risk is **false equivalence**: calling a package “deep rationalisation” when it does not actually remove enough cost.

For example, these alone are usually insufficient:
- hiring freeze
- wage freeze
- bonus restraint
- temporary working-time cuts
- internal redeployment only

Those can improve cash flow, but if the network is truly overbuilt, they do not solve the structural problem.

### The make-or-break question
Does the package remove enough of the **fixed cost stack**?

If yes, it can work without closures.  
If no, the company will be back in the same place in 12–24 months.

## 9) Bottom line

### My neutral conclusion
A **no-closure package can plausibly achieve the required savings** **if and only if** it includes:

1. **real capacity reduction**  
   — at least shift elimination or equivalent footprint consolidation;

2. **real cost-stack removal**  
   — not just wage restraint, but overhead and indirect reductions;

3. **material headcount reduction without forced layoffs**  
   — via attrition, voluntary exits, redeployment, and qualification;

4. **time-bound employee contribution**  
   — enough to bridge the gap while structural changes take effect;

5. **binding investment/product commitments**  
   — to make the deal acceptable and durable.

If the package does **not** remove a meaningful amount of structural cost, then it will **not** be enough. In that case, “no closure” would be politically attractive but economically incomplete.
