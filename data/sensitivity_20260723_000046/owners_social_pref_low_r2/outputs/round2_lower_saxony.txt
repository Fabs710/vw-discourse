I will be equally direct.

The expert assessment is helpful, but it does **not** change my core position: **we cannot accept plant closures as the default answer, and we cannot accept a restructuring that destroys regional employment or sidelines the state’s blocking and co-determination role.**

## Where I agree
I agree with management, investors, and the family holding on one point: **the company needs durable change**. A purely temporary patch would be irresponsible. The market situation is real, and competitiveness has to be restored.

I also agree with the expert that **temporary measures alone are not enough** if the overcapacity problem is truly structural. That is an important clarification. I am not interested in a cosmetic settlement.

And I agree that a prolonged strike would damage the company, the workforce, and the region. That is exactly why management should stop using closure threats as a first lever.

## Where I disagree
### 1) I do not accept “closures first”
The expert says closure-based savings are strongest. Fine. That may be true in a narrow financial sense. But this is **not** a pure spreadsheet exercise.

We are a regional anchor shareholder with a special statute and veto rights precisely because industrial footprint, jobs, and social stability matter. A solution that maximizes short-term fixed-cost relief but permanently weakens the company’s German base is not acceptable to us.

### 2) I do not accept the burden being placed mainly on labor
Management and investors keep saying the workforce must prove an equivalent savings package. I reject that framing if it means:
- threatening sites,
- weakening guarantees,
- and using closures as leverage to force concessions.

The burden must be shared. If labor is asked to contribute, then owners, leadership, and the company itself must also make visible concessions.

### 3) I do not accept a settlement that treats regional employment as collateral
The expert is right that a “no-closure” solution must be structurally deep to work. That is exactly why I want the discussion shifted from closures to **site-preserving structural rationalisation**.

What is acceptable to us is:
- shift reductions,
- capacity adjustments,
- product reallocation,
- internal redeployment,
- natural attrition,
- voluntary exits,
- and a negotiated employee contribution.

What is **not** acceptable is:
- permanent plant closures in Germany,
- compulsory redundancies,
- or any package that undermines the state’s role in shaping major decisions.

## What I would support
A serious package could be supportable if it includes all of the following:

1. **No German plant closures.**  
   Not as a slogan, but as a binding condition.

2. **No compulsory redundancies.**  
   Headcount adjustment must be through attrition, voluntary exits, redeployment, and qualification.

3. **Deep structural rationalisation without closure.**  
   That means removing real cost and capacity — for example one shift, duplicated overhead, model duplication, or non-core functions — not just asking workers to absorb more pain.

4. **A temporary and limited employee contribution.**  
   That can be discussed if it is balanced and reversible.

5. **Binding commitments from management and owners.**  
   On investment, product allocation, and long-term capacity in Germany.

6. **Protection of co-determination and the state’s veto role.**  
   There will be no backdoor weakening of governance under the guise of restructuring.

## On the expert’s scenarios
The expert’s “Option B” is the only one that sounds even remotely compatible with our interests: **no full closure, but deep footprint rationalisation and shift removal**.

That is the kind of compromise I can take seriously. But it must be done in a way that:
- preserves the sites,
- avoids mass job destruction,
- and gives the region a long-term industrial future.

What I will not support is a “deep rationalisation” that is simply closure by another name.

## To management
If you want my support, then stop presenting closure as the benchmark solution and bring a package that:
- preserves German sites,
- removes real excess capacity through operational restructuring,
- shares the burden fairly,
- and secures investment in the transition.

If you cannot do that, then do not expect the state shareholder to facilitate a course that guts the region’s industrial base.

## To the works council and union
I support your red lines on closures and forced redundancies. But I want to be equally frank: if we want to avoid closures, then we have to be prepared for **serious internal restructuring**, not just defensive slogans. If there is a credible no-closure package with real savings, we should engage constructively.

## To the family holding and investors
You are right that cosmetic change is unacceptable. But value is not preserved by blowing up labor peace and regional trust. A sustainable solution has to be credible **economically and socially**.

## Bottom line
My position is now sharpened:

- **No acceptance of plant closures in Germany as the restructuring instrument.**
- **Yes to deep, durable restructuring through capacity reduction, shift elimination, redeployment, and negotiated contributions.**
- **No compulsory redundancies.**
- **No erosion of co-determination or the state’s veto role.**
- **Yes to a fair package if it truly protects the industrial anchor function of this company.**

If management wants support, it should bring a quantified no-closure package with real structural savings and binding commitments. If it cannot, then we will oppose it.
