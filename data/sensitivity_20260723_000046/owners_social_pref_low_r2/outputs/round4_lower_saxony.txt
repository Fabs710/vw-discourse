I will be direct.

## My position remains unchanged on the essentials
I support a **serious restructuring**, because the company clearly faces a structural competitiveness problem.  
I do **not** support a cosmetic deal or endless drift.

But I also do **not** accept plant closures in Germany as the starting point, and I do **not** accept compulsory redundancies. Those two instruments would do lasting damage to jobs, regional stability, and the company’s role as an economic anchor. They would also make the governance situation much harder, not easier.

## Where I agree
I agree with management, the family holding, investors, and the experts on one important point: **temporary measures alone are insufficient**.

A hiring freeze, bonus restraint, and short-term flexibility are helpful, but they are not enough if the company has real overcapacity and a structurally high fixed-cost base.

I also agree that a **purely symbolic compromise would fail**. If we are going to ask the workforce, the owners, and the state to carry political and economic costs, then the package must be real, durable, and measurable.

And I agree with the expert conclusion that a **deep no-closure package can, in principle, achieve most of the savings of a closure-based package**. That is the key point.

## Where I disagree
### 1) I reject closure as the benchmark
Management and investors keep returning to closure economics as if plant closure were the neutral reference case. It is not.

Closure is not just a cost instrument. It is a social and industrial rupture. In a company with this governance structure and this regional importance, closure cannot be treated as the default benchmark to which everyone else must adapt.

If a deep internal restructuring can capture most of the savings while preserving sites and jobs, then closure is not automatically justified.

### 2) I reject “no compulsory redundancies” being dismissed as unrealistic
From our side, that is not a bargaining slogan. It is a hard line.

If headcount has to come down, then it must be through:
- voluntary exits,
- retirement pathways,
- redeployment,
- qualification and conversion,
- natural turnover,
- and working-time adjustments.

If management says the numbers do not work without forced layoffs, then I want to see the quantified proof, not just a repeated assertion.

### 3) I reject closure-by-stealth
I share the works council’s concern here.

If “footprint rationalisation” means hollowing out plants, stripping out core volume, and leaving a shell behind, then that is closure under another name. I will not support that, and I do not think it would be politically or industrially sustainable.

A real no-closure package must keep the sites alive and viable, not simply postpone the final step.

## What would make a no-closure solution acceptable
I am prepared to support a deep restructuring **without closures** if it includes all of the following:

1. **No German plant closures**
2. **No compulsory redundancies**
3. **Real structural capacity reduction inside the existing plant network**
4. **Permanent overhead reduction**
5. **Material headcount reduction through non-forced means**
6. **Temporary but meaningful employee contribution**
7. **Binding investment and product commitments for German sites**
8. **Preservation of co-determination and the state’s veto role**

That is the framework I can defend in the region, in the supervisory board, and publicly.

## On the expert ranges
The specialists have now given us a very important range:
- **Labour-only compromise:** roughly 20–35% of closure economics
- **Deep no-closure rationalisation:** roughly 60–85% of closure economics

That tells me two things.

First, management is right that the package must be structural and deep.  
Second, management is **not** justified in claiming that closure is the only serious route.

If a no-closure package can achieve most of the economics, then the remaining question is not technical alone. It is strategic, social, and governance-related.

And on that question, I do not see a case for forcing closures at this stage.

## To management
You still have not shown why the alternatives are insufficient. You keep saying they are not enough, but the expert assessment says a deep internal package can get most of the way there.

So I want a quantified answer:
- What exact annual savings are required?
- How much comes from shift reduction?
- How much from overhead cuts?
- How much from voluntary exits and redeployment?
- What is the remaining gap?
- And why does that remaining gap justify closing German plants?

If you cannot answer that convincingly, then you have not made the case for closures.

## To the works council and union
I support your red lines on closures and compulsory redundancies. But I will also say this: a serious no-closure solution requires **real internal restructuring**. That means:
- shift reductions where necessary,
- line consolidation,
- overhead reduction,
- productivity improvements,
- and a fair employee contribution.

Protecting sites does not mean protecting every current structure unchanged. If we want to keep the plants open, we need a package that actually changes the economics.

## To the family holding and investors
I understand your demand for durability and competitiveness. I agree that cosmetic peace is not enough.

But I caution you against treating closures as the cleanest answer just because they are the most obvious one on paper. A destructive solution that provokes long conflict, political escalation, and governance deadlock may destroy more value than it creates.

A governed, negotiated deep no-closure restructuring may be the better long-term choice if it is strong enough.

## To the state
My role is to defend the regional economic base and the state’s co-determination role. I will not back a settlement that undermines either.

That means I am open to a **hard, structural, negotiated compromise** — but only one that preserves:
- German sites,
- jobs to the greatest possible extent,
- and our governance position.

General statements about stability are not enough. If we support a no-closure solution, we need it to be real and enforceable.

## My bottom line
My position is:

- **No German plant closures as the restructuring instrument**
- **No compulsory redundancies**
- **Yes to deep structural restructuring inside the existing network**
- **Yes to temporary employee contribution if burden-sharing is real**
- **Yes to binding German investment and product commitments**
- **Yes to preserving co-determination and the state’s veto role**
- **No to cosmetic compromise**
- **No to closure by stealth**

So I am prepared to engage constructively with a **deep no-closure package**, but only if it is genuinely capable of delivering most of the closure economics without destroying the regional industrial base.
