Thank you. Let me be very clear from the regional state’s perspective.

We do **not** accept a restructuring logic that simply treats German plants and jobs as disposable cost items. This company is not a normal abstract portfolio holding for us; it is a central industrial anchor for the region, with deep employment, supplier, and social spillovers. A solution that permanently damages that base would be economically and politically irresponsible.

### Where I agree
I agree with management on one point: **the company needs a credible path back to competitiveness**. Pretending that nothing has changed in the market would be dishonest. We also recognize that the transition to electric vehicles, international competition, and weak European demand require adaptation.

I also agree that **a negotiated solution is preferable to an open conflict**. Prolonged strikes, mutual escalation, and uncertainty would hurt the company, employees, and the region.

And yes, I accept that **costs must be addressed**. But that does not mean the burden can be dumped on labor alone, or that closures are the first answer.

### Where I disagree strongly
I do **not** accept management’s framing that plant closures in Germany are a necessary starting point.

You say there is overcapacity and a structural cost problem. That may be true. But the conclusion cannot automatically be: close sites, cut jobs, weaken guarantees. That would create irreversible damage, and it would do so in a way that also destroys trust in the company’s long-term social partnership model.

I also do **not** accept the idea that a binding job-security framework through 2030 is somehow incompatible with responsible restructuring. If the only way management sees to “restore competitiveness” is to tear up guarantees and make workers absorb the full adjustment, then the restructuring is too one-sided to support.

And I want to be explicit: **there is no political or governance path here that sidelines the state shareholder or the co-determination rights of the workforce**. Any attempt to force through a solution by ignoring these blocking positions is simply not realistic.

### What would be acceptable
We are open to a package that:
- preserves German sites,
- avoids compulsory redundancies,
- relies first on internal adjustments, working-time solutions, qualification, and redeployment,
- includes a fair and limited employee contribution,
- and is matched by real commitments from management and owners on investment, products, and future capacity in Germany.

If management believes a durable savings target is needed, then it must show that the same effect can be achieved **without plant closures and without mass job destruction**. That is the burden of proof here.

### On the union and works council positions
I support the central line that has been stated by the workforce side: **no closures, no forced redundancies, no one-sided sacrifice**. That is not just a slogan; it reflects the legitimate interest of the region in preserving industrial employment and social stability.

If there is room for an employee contribution, it must be:
- temporary,
- proportionate,
- negotiated,
- and tied to enforceable commitments on jobs, investment, and site security.

### My bottom line
The state shareholder will not back a restructuring that guts regional employment or removes the company’s co-determination and veto structure in practice.

But we are not blocking change for its own sake. We are prepared to support a **serious, negotiated restructuring** if it protects the plants and the core workforce, restores competitiveness through balanced measures, and preserves the company’s role as a regional economic anchor.

So my message to management is simple: bring us a solution that is economically credible **and** socially sustainable. If the proposal is based on closures and structural job destruction, we will oppose it. If it is based on shared sacrifice, site protection, and a long-term industrial strategy, then we can work with that.
