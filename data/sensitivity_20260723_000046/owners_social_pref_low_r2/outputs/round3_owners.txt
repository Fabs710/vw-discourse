I will be direct.

The discussion has now reached the point where the key issue is clear: **everyone agrees the company must become materially more competitive, but the parties still disagree on whether that can be done without German plant closures or compulsory redundancies.**

From the controlling family’s perspective, that is the right question. Everything else is secondary.

## Where I agree

I agree with management, the experts, the state, and even the employee side on several important points:

- **This company needs durable structural change.**
- **Temporary measures alone are not enough** if there is genuine overcapacity.
- **A prolonged strike or governance deadlock would destroy value.**
- **A cosmetic compromise would be worse than no deal.**
- **Investment and future products in Germany must be part of any settlement.**

So I am not here to defend the status quo. I am here to protect the long-term value of the core asset, which means restoring margins and competitiveness.

## Where I disagree

### 1) I do not accept a blanket “no closures, no redundancies, no guarantee changes” position
I understand why the works council and union are drawing hard lines. But from an ownership perspective, that position is not yet a solution. It is a constraint.

If the company truly has structural overcapacity and a cost base above competitors, then **some form of durable footprint reduction and labor-cost reset is necessary**. If the answer is simply “not through closures,” then the burden shifts to proving that an alternative package is genuinely equivalent in economic effect.

### 2) I do not support closure-first as the default either
That said, I also do not accept management’s tendency to make closure the benchmark instrument. In this governance structure, closure-first is too blunt and too risky if there is another way to achieve materially similar savings.

A closure strategy that triggers:
- prolonged conflict,
- political escalation,
- supervisory-board deadlock,
- and lasting damage to labor relations,

may destroy more value than it creates. That would be a poor outcome for the owners.

### 3) I do not accept cosmetic internal rationalisation
If “no closure” simply means:
- wage moderation,
- a hiring freeze,
- some attrition,
- and vague efficiency promises,

then that is not enough. It may reduce friction, but it will not solve the structural problem. I will not support a deal that preserves the appearance of stability while leaving the underlying economics unchanged.

## What I take from the expert assessments

The specialists have clarified the real trade-off very well:

- A **labour-only** compromise is too weak.
- A **no-closure** package can work **only if it is structurally deep**.
- That means real capacity reduction, not slogans.
- It also means reduction of indirect overhead and material headcount decline.
- And it means the employee contribution must be meaningful, not symbolic.

That is the right framework.

## What I would support

I would support a settlement only if it includes all of the following:

1. **No immediate German plant closures as the starting point**  
   I am not prepared to endorse closures as the first move.

2. **Real structural capacity reduction**  
   This must be measurable and permanent.

3. **A meaningful negotiated employee contribution**  
   It must be more than a short pause.

4. **Material headcount reduction primarily through non-forced means if possible**  
   Voluntary exits, retirement, redeployment, and attrition are preferable — but only if the numbers are sufficient.

5. **Binding investment and product commitments in Germany**  
   The company must remain an industrial platform, not a hollowed-out shell.

6. **A package that can actually pass the supervisory board and hold industrial peace**  
   A deal that cannot survive governance reality is not a deal.

## What I will not support

I will not support:
- a cosmetic settlement,
- indefinite delay,
- a return to open-ended drift,
- or a restructuring that permanently weakens the company’s economics.

I will also not support any outcome that threatens ownership control or makes the enterprise ungovernable. That is not negotiable.

What I need from you is a **quantified alternative** that shows:
- how much cost comes out permanently,
- over what time horizon,
- how capacity is reduced,

If you can show that a deep no-closure package truly closes the gap, then I will examine it seriously. If you cannot, then harder measures remain on the table.

## To the works council and union

I respect your insistence on protecting jobs and sites. But I need to be equally clear: **you cannot ask for full protection of every legacy arrangement and also expect a structural turnaround**.

If you want to avoid closures, then the alternative must be more than defensive language. It must be a real package with:
- structural capacity reduction,
- productivity gains,
- flexibility improvements,
- and a genuine employee contribution.

That is the price of keeping the sites open.

## To the regional state

I appreciate your role and your concern for industrial stability. But stability without competitiveness is not durable. If the state wants to keep the company rooted in the region, then it must support a serious restructuring, not just oppose the most visible instrument.

My position is this:

- **No** to a cosmetic compromise.
- **No** to a plan that leaves the company uncompetitive.
- **No** to a value-destructive conflict.
- **No** to any outcome that weakens governance control.
