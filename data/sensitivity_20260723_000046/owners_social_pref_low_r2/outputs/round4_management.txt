Let me respond plainly.

## First: what I agree with
I agree with everyone on one point: **the company needs a durable structural reset**. Temporary measures alone will not solve the cost gap. A hiring freeze, some overtime reductions, and a few flexible working arrangements are not enough if we want to restore the brand’s return on sales and fund investment to 2030.

I also agree that a **cosmetic deal would be worse than no deal**. If the package does not materially reduce fixed cost and overcapacity, then it is not a restructuring. It is delay.

And I agree with the expert input that a **deep no-closure package can, in principle, capture most of the economics of closure**. That is the first genuinely useful statement in this discussion.

## Where I disagree
### 1) “No closures” is not a solution
The works council and union keep repeating “no closures” as if that answers the cost problem. It doesn’t.

I am not interested in slogans. I am interested in whether the company can compete against lower-cost rivals in a shrinking European market. If we leave the plant footprint untouched and preserve the current overcapacity, then we have not solved the problem.

So I reject any position that treats **site preservation as the same thing as competitiveness**. It is not.

### 2) “No compulsory redundancies” cannot mean “no real headcount reduction”
I understand the political and social line you are drawing. But from a business perspective, headcount must come down materially. If that happens through voluntary exits, retirement pathways, attrition, redeployment, and targeted flexibility, fine — but it must happen at scale.

If the package does not produce a meaningful permanent reduction in labour cost and headcount, then it is not enough.

### 3) “No-closure” must not become closure by stealth
I do hear the concern about hollowing out sites and renaming it rationalisation. That would indeed be unacceptable — not because we want to preserve every current structure, but because it would be a slow, inefficient way of doing what should be done directly.

If we do this, it must be real:
- permanent shift reductions,
- model consolidation,
- overhead cuts,
- and measurable footprint changes.

Not window dressing.

## What the expert assessment changes
The specialist input is important because it quantifies the gap.

If a labour-only compromise delivers only **20–35%** of closure economics, that is not enough.  
If a deep no-closure package can deliver **60–85%**, then that is the zone we need to work in.

That means the burden is now very clear:

- If the employee side wants to avoid closures, it must accept **deep structural internal change**.
- If the state and shareholders want a governable settlement, they must accept that the deal must be **hard**, not symbolic.
- If management wants a real turnaround, it must insist on **permanent savings**, not temporary relief.

So I do not see the expert assessment as a reason to abandon our position. I see it as confirmation that **deep restructuring is possible without closures — but only if it is deep enough to matter**.

## My position on the proposed bridge options
### Proposal 1: Deep no-closure restructuring with savings floor
This is the only direction worth discussing seriously.

But the savings floor must be real, measurable, and enforceable. If the floor is not met, we do not end up with a weak compromise disguised as success.

### Proposal 2: Tiered restructuring with site-specific protections
I can support differentiated measures, but only if they deliver actual volume and cost reduction. I will not support a design that protects every site equally and therefore protects inefficiency.

Some sites and functions will have to carry more of the adjustment than others. That is unavoidable.

### Proposal 3: Time-bound employee contribution with anti-closure safeguard
I am open to a time-bounded employee contribution if it gives us immediate relief and supports the transition.

But let’s be honest: a hard no-closure clause cannot become a permanent shield against any future adjustment. If the company misses the savings target, then the consequences must be real. Otherwise we are just postponing the same argument.

## What management needs to see
Here is the standard I am prepared to apply.

A credible package must include:

1. **Permanent capacity reduction**
   - shift elimination
   - line consolidation
   - overhead reduction
   - product reassignment where needed

2. **Material labour-cost contribution**
   - temporary wage moderation
   - bonus restraint
   - flexibility on working time
   - reduced premium cost

3. **Meaningful headcount reduction**
   - mainly through voluntary exits, retirement pathways, redeployment, and attrition
   - but at a scale that actually moves the cost base

4. **Binding investment and product commitments**
   - Germany must remain an industrial base, not a shell

5. **Implementation certainty**
   - if it cannot pass governance and be executed, it is not serious

What I will not accept is a package that preserves the current cost structure and calls that a compromise.

## To the works council and union
You are right that workers should not be asked to pay for management’s strategic mistakes alone. I accept that.

But you are wrong if you think the company can be turned around without hard internal restructuring. It cannot.

So the question is not whether the workforce contributes. It must. The question is whether you are willing to accept the scale of internal change that makes the business viable.

If your answer is “every plant stays exactly as it is, and headcount reduction must be minimal,” then we are not close to agreement.

## To the state
You are right to oppose a value-destructive conflict. But political stability is not the same as economic viability.

If you support a no-closure path, then support a **real** no-closure path — one that actually cuts cost and capacity. I will not endorse a symbolic solution simply because it is politically more comfortable.

## To the family holding and investors
You are right to reject a cosmetic settlement. A fake compromise would only delay the crisis and waste more value.

But I would caution against treating closures as the only credible form of discipline. They are not. The specialists have just confirmed that a deep internal package can get close to the economics we need, provided it is done properly.

That is the path I am prepared to examine.

## Bottom line
My position is unchanged where it matters:

- **We need durable structural savings.**
- **We need real capacity reduction.**
- **We need meaningful labour-cost contribution.**
- **We need material headcount reduction, preferably through non-forced means but at sufficient scale.**
- **We need binding investment and product commitments in Germany.**
- **We do not need a cosmetic no-closure deal.**

So here is the real test:

**If the employee side can accept a deep internal restructuring that delivers most of the closure economics without closures, then we have a basis for settlement. If not, then there is no serious alternative on the table.**
