> **What is the minimum savings, capacity reduction, and headcount change a no-closure package must achieve to be economically credible, compared with a closure case?**

## Executive answer

A **credible no-closure package** in a large German automotive footprint typically needs to achieve roughly:

- **60–85% of the annual run-rate savings** of the closure-based option,  
- while delivering **most of the capacity reduction** and a **material permanent headcount reduction**,
- with one-off restructuring costs and implementation risk low enough that the board can defend it as a durable solution under co-determination.

If the no-closure package lands materially below that range, it becomes hard to justify as a true restructuring rather than a delay mechanism.

---

## 1) What “credible” means in this context

For board feasibility, a no-closure package must do three things:

1. **Restore competitiveness**
   - meaningfully reduce the fixed-cost base,
   - lower labour intensity,
   - and improve plant utilization.

2. **Be durable**
   - not just a temporary wage concession or a one-year freeze.

3. **Be executable**
   - it must survive co-determination, strike risk, and supervisory-board constraints.

A package that keeps all plants open but only trims variable costs will not be enough if the core problem is structural overcapacity.

---

## 2) Practical savings benchmark

### Closure-based case
A closure-based restructuring generally produces the **maximum theoretical cost relief** because it removes:

- direct labour,
- indirect overhead,
- site fixed costs,
- maintenance/security,
- logistics complexity,
- and some capex burden tied to the site.

But it also brings:
- one-off severance / shutdown costs,
- legal and industrial-relations friction,
- execution delay,
- and high governance risk.

### No-closure case
A no-closure package can typically capture:

- **most direct labour savings** through headcount reduction and work redesign,
- **part of indirect overhead savings** through consolidation,
- **some fixed-cost savings** through shift elimination and line rationalization,
- but **not all site-fixed-cost relief** because the plant remains open.

That is why the realistic ceiling is usually **60–85%** of closure economics, not 100%.

---

## 3) Minimum structural ingredients of a credible no-closure deal

To be board-credible, the package usually needs all of the following:

### A. Permanent capacity reduction
This is the key. A credible package usually requires:

- **one shift eliminated at selected plants**, or equivalent capacity reduction,
- **line consolidation / model reassignment**,
- **lower plant-specific complexity**,
- and reduction of support/indirect functions.

This is the “hard” part. Without real capacity reduction, no-closure becomes superficial.

### B. Headcount reduction at scale
If there are no forced redundancies, the headcount reduction has to come from:

- voluntary exits,
- early retirement pathways,
- natural attrition,
- redeployment,
- internal mobility,
- and qualification-based transfer.

For a large German automotive site, a credible no-closure package often needs a **low-to-mid-teens percentage reduction in workforce at affected sites**, sometimes more, depending on current overstaffing and utilization.

If the workforce reduction is only a few percentage points, it will usually not move the fixed-cost base enough.

### C. Overhead reduction
Management will want:
- engineering/support simplification,
- fewer layers,
- shared-service consolidation,
- procurement/admin rationalization.

This is often worth a meaningful amount, but by itself it is rarely enough. It is an amplifier, not the main lever.

### D. Temporary employee contribution
This can help close the gap in the first 2–3 years:
- wage moderation,
- bonus restraint,
- working-time flexibility,
- premium-payment reductions,
- temporary reductions in fringe-cost load.

Important: these are useful **bridges**, but not substitutes for structural measures.

### E. Binding investment / product commitments
This is what makes the deal acceptable and executable:
- future model allocation,
- technology investment,
- capacity commitments,
- and a credible product pipeline.

Without this, sites become underfilled and the package loses durability.

---

## 4) What the minimum package might look like in numbers

I’ll give this as an **illustrative estimate**, not a company-specific fact.

### If closure economics equal 100
Then a credible no-closure package should aim for roughly:

- **Annual permanent savings:** 60–85
- **One-off restructuring cost:** often lower than closure, but still substantial
- **Capacity removed:** roughly 50–80% of the capacity that a closure package would remove, via shifts and line rationalization
- **Headcount reduction:** enough to create real fixed-cost relief, often roughly 10–20% of affected-site employment depending on baseline utilization and indirect load

### Below that range
If the package only delivers, say:
- 20–35% of closure economics, or
- limited shift tweaks with minimal headcount impact,

then it is usually not sufficient for a board that believes the cost base is structurally uncompetitive.

---

## 5) Closure vs no-closure trade-off

### Closure case
**Pros**
- Highest theoretical savings
- Cleanest footprint reset
- Removes persistent fixed costs

**Cons**
- High governance risk
- High strike / conflict risk
- Long implementation timeline
- Potential brand / political damage
- Risk of value destruction through disruption

### Deep no-closure case
**Pros**
- Better governability
- Lower political and industrial-relations shock
- Faster to stabilize if negotiated well
- Preserves future flexibility and product continuity

**Cons**
- Savings ceiling lower than closure
- Requires very deep internal change to be credible
- Harder to prove sufficient unless the capacity reduction is real

So from a board perspective, the no-closure deal is attractive **only if it comes close enough to closure economics** to justify rejecting the more destructive option.

## 7) The key board question

The actual board question is not:

> “Can we match closure exactly?”

It is:

> “Can we achieve enough permanent savings and capacity reduction without closure to justify the lower execution risk and preserve governability?”

In many German co-determined industrial situations, the answer is **yes, but only if the package is deep**.
