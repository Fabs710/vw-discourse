## Expert assessment: can a no-closure package deliver “equivalent, durable savings”?

Short answer: **possibly, but only if the package is large, multi-layered, and genuinely structural**.  
A purely temporary, symbolic, or wage-only concession will **not** usually substitute for a German plant closure if management is trying to remove a material overcapacity problem and reset fixed costs.

### My core view
In a large German automotive plant network, the savings hierarchy is usually:

1. **Plant closure / major footprint reduction**  
   Highest fixed-cost relief, strongest durability, hardest to implement politically and under co-determination.

   Shift elimination, line consolidation, model transfer, footprint consolidation, and asset write-downs.

3. **Labour-cost measures**  
   Wage moderation, bonus restraint, working-time flexibility, reductions in allowances, temporary solidarity contributions.

4. **Temporary measures**  
   Short-time work, hiring freezes, deferrals, internal redeployment.  
   Useful bridge measures, but weak durability.

So the question is not “can we get some savings without closures?” — yes.  
It is “can we get enough **durable** savings without closures?” — that is much harder.

A no-closure package can approximate closure savings if it removes enough of the **cost stack**. That means not just wages, but also:

- indirect labour and overhead,

- logistics and internal transport,

- local management and support functions,
- and, critically, future capex commitments.

There is a big difference between:

- short-time work

### Structural measures

- permanent elimination of one shift system
- product-line transfer to another site
- consolidation of production stages
- permanent reduction in indirect headcount
- closure of specific buildings or sub-operations
- outsourcing of non-core activities

- a one-off symbolic concession,
- a small annual savings package,

- **a permanent productivity/flexibility gain**, plus
- **a headcount pathway through attrition**, plus

**Management and capital providers want durable, structurally material cost reduction; employee representatives and the state insist that this must not be achieved through German plant closures or compulsory redundancies.**

So the impasse is **not** over whether the company needs change. It is over **which instruments are permissible** and **what counts as an equivalent, durable alternative**.

A second layer of impasse is governance:  
**who carries the burden of proof, who makes binding commitments, and what can be enforced under co-determination and the special company statute.**

---

### Possible compromise positions
These are facilitator-style bridging options, not recommendations:

#### 1) “No immediate closure, but binding footprint rationalisation”
- No full German plant closures for a fixed period.
- In exchange, the employee side accepts:
  - permanent elimination of one shift or equivalent capacity,
  - consolidation of production lines or model assignments,
  - reduction of indirect overhead,
  - and a voluntary-exit / attrition program with defined headcount targets.
- Management, owners, and state commit to:
  - investment in German future products,
  - site-specific capacity allocation,
  - and a review clause tied to measurable milestones.

**Purpose:** preserve sites while still delivering structural savings.

---

#### 2) “Time-bounded no-redundancy pact with trigger-based review”
- No compulsory redundancies for a defined period, but not indefinitely.
- During that period:
  - wage moderation, bonus restraint, and working-time flexibility are implemented,
  - voluntary exits and redeployment are accelerated,
  - and one or more plants undergo deep internal restructuring.
- If savings and capacity targets are not met by agreed checkpoints, the parties reopen the question of stronger measures under predefined governance rules.

**Purpose:** buy time and reduce conflict, while keeping a performance-based backstop.

---

#### 3) “Two-tier package: immediate cash relief + structural second phase”
- Phase 1: immediate temporary measures to stabilize cash and avoid confrontation:
  - hiring freeze,
  - overtime reduction,
  - bonus moderation,
  - short-term working-time flexibility.
- Phase 2: jointly designed structural package:
  - shift elimination,
  - footprint consolidation,
  - product reassignment,
  - and durable overhead reduction.
- The package is sealed only if both phases are linked to binding investment and site commitments.

**Purpose:** separate urgent stabilization from the harder structural redesign.

# 1) What “closure economics” usually means

When people talk about “closure savings,” they often mean three buckets:

1. **Direct labour cost removal**
   - wages/salaries
   - employer social charges
   - shift premiums
   - temporary labour
   - overtime

2. **Indirect fixed-cost relief**
   - plant management
   - maintenance
   - utilities baseline
   - support functions
   - local overhead
   - logistics / internal transport
   - facilities

3. **Capacity / depreciation / capital efficiency**
   - lower idle capacity
   - better absorption
   - reduced complexity
   - possible working capital improvement

A full closure is attractive because it removes **a whole fixed-cost stack**.  
But the catch is that it comes with:

- severance / social plan costs
- transfer / redeployment costs
- ramp-down costs
- tooling write-downs
- contractual termination costs
- potential political and industrial conflict costs
- possible supply-chain disruption
- possible lost volume if capacity has to be rebuilt elsewhere

So closure savings are real, but they are not free.

# 2) What a no-closure package can realistically do

A serious no-closure package needs to remove cost through **four structural levers**:

## A. Shift elimination / line consolidation
This is the biggest non-closure lever.

Examples:
- 3-shift to 2-shift
- 2-shift to 1-shift on selected lines
- model assignment consolidation
- removal of underutilized lines
- shared production cells across plants

**Savings effect:** often very material because one shift carries labor, supervision, maintenance, energy, and support cost.

**Rule of thumb:**  
If a plant is genuinely overstaffed or underloaded, eliminating one shift can recover roughly **15–30% of site operating cost**, depending on utilization and fixed overhead mix.

## B. Indirect overhead reduction
This includes:
- site administration
- engineering support
- quality / logistics duplication
- local management layers
- purchasing / planning duplication

**Savings effect:** usually meaningful, but not enough alone.

**Rule of thumb:**  
A strong overhead reset can contribute another **5–15% of site cost** if functions are consolidated aggressively.

## C. Headcount reduction without forced layoffs
Mechanisms:
- natural attrition
- retirement corridors
- voluntary exit programs
- redeployment to growth areas
- internal conversion / qualification programs
- hiring freeze

**Savings effect:** good, but slow.

**Rule of thumb:**  
Can deliver sizeable medium-term savings, but usually too slow for a pure turnaround unless paired with shift/footprint changes.

## D. Labour-cost contribution
Measures like:
- wage moderation
- bonus restraint
- working-time flexibility
- temporary reduction in allowances
- deferred increases

**Savings effect:** useful but limited.

**Rule of thumb:**  
This often produces only **low single-digit percentage savings on total site cost**, unless the concessions are unusually deep and broad.

# 3) Quantified comparison: closure vs no-closure vs labour-only

Below is a simplified decision framework. Numbers are illustrative, not company-specific facts.

## Scenario 1: Closure-based restructuring
**Potential savings**
- annual fixed-cost reduction: **100% of the target site cost base**
- annual savings can be large and immediate once run-off is complete

**Costs**
- one-off restructuring cost often high:
  - severance
  - site run-down
  - tooling write-offs
  - contract termination
  - transfer costs
- depending on workforce size, this can easily be **1.0x to 2.5x annual plant payroll** or more, plus non-labour closure costs

**Pros**
- strongest and cleanest cost reset
- permanent
- simple operating model after execution

**Cons**
- high governance and industrial resistance
- high strike / legal / political risk
- risk of execution delay
- risk of value destruction if repeated elsewhere

## Scenario 2: Deep no-closure footprint rationalisation
**Potential savings**
- annual savings often **60–85% of closure economics**
- can be enough if the original closure case was partly about excess shift capacity rather than total site elimination

**Costs**
- lower one-off cost than closure, but still substantial:
  - voluntary exit packages
  - retraining / redeployment
  - line reconfiguration
  - inventory and ramp-down costs
  - possible productivity dip during transition

**Pros**
- much more feasible under co-determination
- preserves industrial footprint and brand/social stability
- lower political and board veto risk
- more likely to be negotiated successfully

**Cons**
- must be genuinely deep
- savings may take longer to realize
- can be undermined if “rationalisation” is too shallow

## Scenario 3: Labour-only compromise
**Potential savings**
- usually only **20–35% of closure economics**
- often insufficient for a major ROS reset

**Costs**
- low one-off cost
- relatively easy to implement if peace is bought

**Pros**
- fast
- politically easier
- preserves sites and jobs in the short term

**Cons**
- generally not durable enough
- high risk of backsliding
- does not remove structural overcapacity
- likely unacceptable to capital providers if the cost gap is truly structural

- overtime reduction
- shift changes
- overhead cuts
- freeze on backfilling

## 1) What “credible” means in this context

For board feasibility, a no-closure package must do three things:

1. **Restore competitiveness**
   - meaningfully reduce the fixed-cost base,
   - lower labour intensity,
   - and improve plant utilization.

2. **Be durable**
   - not just a temporary wage concession or a one-year freeze.

3. **Be executable**
   - it must survive co-determination, strike risk, and supervisory-board constraints.

A package that keeps all plants open but only trims variable costs will not be enough if the core problem is structural overcapacity.

## 2) Practical savings benchmark

### Closure-based case
A closure-based restructuring generally produces the **maximum theoretical cost relief** because it removes:

- direct labour,
- indirect overhead,
- site fixed costs,
- maintenance/security,
- logistics complexity,
- and some capex burden tied to the site.

But it also brings:
- one-off severance / shutdown costs,
- legal and industrial-relations friction,
- execution delay,
- and high governance risk.

### No-closure case
A no-closure package can typically capture:

- **most direct labour savings** through headcount reduction and work redesign,
- **part of indirect overhead savings** through consolidation,
- **some fixed-cost savings** through shift elimination and line rationalization,
- but **not all site-fixed-cost relief** because the plant remains open.

That is why the realistic ceiling is usually **60–85%** of closure economics, not 100%.

## 3) Minimum structural ingredients of a credible no-closure deal

To be board-credible, the package usually needs all of the following:

### A. Permanent capacity reduction

- **one shift eliminated at selected plants**, or equivalent capacity reduction,
- **line consolidation / model reassignment**,
- **lower plant-specific complexity**,
- and reduction of support/indirect functions.

This is the “hard” part. Without real capacity reduction, no-closure becomes superficial.

### B. Headcount reduction at scale
If there are no forced redundancies, the headcount reduction has to come from:

- voluntary exits,
- early retirement pathways,
- natural attrition,
- redeployment,
