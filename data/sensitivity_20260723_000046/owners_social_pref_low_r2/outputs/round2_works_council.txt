I will respond directly.

First: the works council’s position has not changed, and it will not change under pressure.

### What we agree on
I agree with management, the family holding, the state, and investors on one point: the company needs a credible future path and cannot simply drift. The market is difficult, the transition is expensive, and we all know that investment in new products matters.

I also agree that a drawn-out conflict would hurt everyone. That is precisely why management should stop threatening a confrontation that it cannot realistically impose without consequences.

### What I reject
I reject the core assumption that **German plant closures are necessary**.

I reject the idea that **binding job security through 2030 is somehow incompatible with restructuring**.

And I reject the notion that the workforce must prove, in advance and alone, how to solve management’s strategic failure.

Let me be very clear: **we will not accept compulsory redundancies, and we will not accept any package that opens the door to closing German plants.**

### On the “durable savings” argument
Management and investors keep saying the savings must be durable. Fine. But durability does not automatically mean closures. It does not automatically mean layoffs. It does not automatically mean tearing up guarantees.

There are alternatives that are durable and much less destructive:
- capacity adjustments through shifts and utilization,
- working-time reductions or flexibility agreements,
- internal redeployment across plants,
- natural attrition,
- qualification and conversion programs,
- postponement or resizing of certain investments,
- and a negotiated employee contribution.

If those are dismissed out of hand, then this is not about efficiency; it is about shifting risk and pain onto the workforce.

### On the burden of proof
The burden of proof is not on the workers to justify their own jobs. It is on management to prove that closures are unavoidable. So far, I have heard assertions about overcapacity and cost pressure — not a convincing case that every alternative has been exhausted.

And I will say this plainly to the controlling family and investors: **a deal that destroys trust, triggers strikes, and tears apart the company’s social compact will not be a value-preserving solution**. It will be a self-inflicted wound.

### What would actually move us
There is no mystery here. If management wants a serious conversation, it must put forward:
1. **A binding guarantee: no German plant closures.**
2. **No compulsory redundancies.**
3. **Preservation of co-determination and the job-security framework.**
4. **A fair, temporary employee contribution, if needed, in exchange for real commitments.**
5. **Hard commitments on investment, future products, and capacity allocation in Germany.**

That is the minimum basis for negotiation. Without that, there is nothing to negotiate.

### Where I disagree with the other parties
- **To management:** “No closures is unrealistic” is not an argument; it is a threat.
- **To investors:** your demand for “durable structural savings” cannot justify social destruction as the default mechanism.
- **To the family holding:** you cannot claim to want long-term value while backing a course that would obliterate labour peace and trust.
- **To the state:** I appreciate the support, but the line must be stronger: political caution cannot become a backdoor for cuts that violate job security.

### Bottom line
If the company wants cooperation, it must come with a real offer:
- protect every German site,
- protect every job from forced redundancy,
- and respect the negotiated framework that has underpinned this company for decades.

If management will not accept that, then we will continue to resist with all available means — in the plants, in the public, and in the supervisory board.
