Yes — within the restructuring / automotive footprint mandate, I can sharpen this into a **minimum-credible no-closure package** and compare it to the closure case.

## Bottom line first
A **no-closure, no-compulsory-redundancy** package is **plausible only if it is genuinely deep**. In practice, for a large German OEM, I would expect it to need to deliver roughly:

- **60–85% of the annual run-rate savings** of a closure-based plan
- **most of the capacity reduction** that closure would have achieved, but via shifts/lines/functions rather than site shutdown
- **material permanent headcount reduction**, mainly through voluntary exits, retirement, attrition, and redeployment
- **durable overhead reduction**, not just labour concessions
- **binding product/investment commitments** to prevent the remaining footprint from becoming underutilized

If the package sits materially below that band, it is usually **not board-credible** as a substitute for closure.

---

## 1) What a closure case typically buys you
A closure-based package captures savings from removing:

- direct labour
- indirect labour / support functions
- site fixed costs
- utilities / maintenance / security
- internal logistics complexity
- some depreciation / capex drag tied to the site
- management attention and planning complexity

But it also creates:
- one-off shutdown and severance costs
- industrial-relations and strike risk
- governance risk
- political backlash
- longer execution timelines

So closure is usually the **maximum theoretical saving**, not the maximum value outcome.

---

## 2) What a deep no-closure package can realistically capture
A serious no-closure package can still remove a large part of the cost base through:

### Structural levers
- **Shift elimination** at selected plants or functions
- **Line consolidation / model reassignment**
- **Lower staffing norms**
- **Indirect overhead cuts**
- **Layer reduction in management and support**
- **Redeployment across the network**
- **Voluntary exit / retirement programs**
- **Productivity resets and standardization**

### Temporary levers
- wage moderation
- bonus restraint
- working-time flexibility
- premium-pay reductions
- hiring freeze

Important distinction:
- **Temporary levers help cash and bridge the transition**
- **Structural levers create the durable savings**

The durable part is what management and investors will care about.

---

## 3) Minimum savings threshold
For a package to be credible as a closure alternative, I would normally expect it to reach:

- **at least ~60% of closure-case savings** as a floor
- **~70–80%** as the sweet spot
- above that, it becomes a strong substitute

Why this range?
Because a no-closure plan avoids shutdown costs and conflict risk, but it cannot fully eliminate site-fixed costs. So if it only delivers, say, one-third of closure savings, it is not really solving the structural problem.

---

## 4) Minimum capacity reduction
A no-closure package needs real footprint change. In practical terms that usually means:

- eliminating one or more shifts at selected sites
- reducing installed operating capacity
- consolidating production lines
- concentrating some model or component work into fewer processes
- shrinking indirect support functions

As a rule of thumb, if you do **not** remove meaningful capacity, the deal will not hold.

A credible package usually needs to remove something like:
- **a significant fraction of the “effective” plant capacity** that would otherwise be closed,
- often via **50–80% of the closure-equivalent capacity reduction**, not necessarily 100%.

---

## 5) Minimum headcount change
Without compulsory redundancies, the workforce reduction must come from non-forced means. For a large automotive footprint, a credible no-closure plan often needs:

- **low-to-mid-teens percentage reduction** at affected sites, sometimes higher if overstaffing is severe
- with the mix coming from:
  - voluntary exits
  - retirement pathways
  - natural attrition
  - internal mobility / redeployment
  - qualification-based transfers

If headcount reduction is only a few percent, it will usually be too weak to move the fixed-cost base.

---

## 6) What a board-credible package usually contains
If I were stress-testing a no-closure package for board feasibility, I would look for all of these:

1. **No German plant closures**
2. **No compulsory redundancies**
3. **Shift elimination / line consolidation**
4. **Permanent overhead reduction**
5. **Voluntary-exit and retirement programs at scale**
6. **Temporary employee contribution**
7. **Binding investment and product commitments**
8. **A monitoring and trigger mechanism**

The trigger mechanism matters because it gives management and owners confidence that the deal is not just deferred pain.

---

## 7) What is not credible
These are usually insufficient on their own:

- hiring freeze only
- bonus restraint only
- short-term wage moderation only
- vague “efficiency improvement” promises
- attrition without a target
- “footprint rationalisation” without actual capacity removal
- no-closure with minimal headcount change

That kind of package tends to be politically easier but economically too small.

---

## 8) Simple comparative framing

### Labour-only compromise
Typical outcome:
- **~20–35% of closure economics**
- too weak for a structural turnaround

### Deep no-closure package
Typical outcome:
- **~60–85% of closure economics**
- potentially credible if the internal restructuring is genuinely hard

### Closure case
Typical outcome:
- **100% of closure economics on paper**
- but often less attractive on a risk-adjusted basis due to execution and conflict costs

---

## 9) My judgement on feasibility
A deep no-closure package is **not only possible, but often the only governable route** in a German co-determined setting **if**:

- site closures are blocked
- forced redundancies are excluded
- and all parties accept that the package must still be materially painful

The key point is that **“no closure” does not mean “no sacrifice.”**  
It means the sacrifice must be delivered by footprint rationalization inside the network.

---

## 10) Practical conclusion
If the parties want a settlement that is:
- economically durable,
- legally/governance feasible,
- and socially/politically survivable,

then the no-closure package should aim for:

- **60–85% of closure-case annual savings**
- **real shift and line cuts**
- **material voluntary headcount reduction**
- **permanent overhead reduction**
- **binding investment and product allocation**

Below that, the package is likely too weak.  
Above that, it can be a genuine substitute for closure.
