## Expert assessment: can a no-closure package deliver “equivalent, durable savings”?

Short answer: **possibly, but only if the package is large, multi-layered, and genuinely structural**.  
A purely temporary, symbolic, or wage-only concession will **not** usually substitute for a German plant closure if management is trying to remove a material overcapacity problem and reset fixed costs.

### My core view
In a large German automotive plant network, the savings hierarchy is usually:

1. **Plant closure / major footprint reduction**  
   Highest fixed-cost relief, strongest durability, hardest to implement politically and under co-determination.

2. **Deep capacity rationalisation without full closure**  
   Shift elimination, line consolidation, model transfer, footprint consolidation, and asset write-downs.  
   Can be close to closure economics if it removes an entire shift system or duplicates of overhead.

3. **Labour-cost measures**  
   Wage moderation, bonus restraint, working-time flexibility, reductions in allowances, temporary solidarity contributions.  
   Helpful, but usually **not enough alone** to solve a structural overcapacity problem.

4. **Temporary measures**  
   Short-time work, hiring freezes, deferrals, internal redeployment.  
   Useful bridge measures, but weak durability.

So the question is not “can we get some savings without closures?” — yes.  
It is “can we get enough **durable** savings without closures?” — that is much harder.

---

## 1) What size of savings is likely required?

I cannot know the company’s internal target, but in a case like this, management typically needs a package that is:

- large enough to restore competitiveness,
- visible in fixed-cost reduction,
- and credible to investors over a 3–5 year horizon.

For a large German OEM brand with high domestic labour and overhead costs, the relevant benchmark is often **hundreds of millions to low single-digit billions of euros annually**, depending on scale and margin gap.

### Rule of thumb
If management says “durable structural savings,” the package often needs to include:
- **full-year EBITDA-impacting savings**, not just cash timing,
- **permanent headcount reduction**, and
- **capacity removal that lowers fixed costs per vehicle**.

A labour-only deal usually fails if the core issue is:
- excess plant capacity,
- too many direct/indirect employees per unit produced,
- and underutilized fixed manufacturing footprint.

---

## 2) Why a no-closure deal can still work — but only in certain structures

A no-closure package can approximate closure savings if it removes enough of the **cost stack**. That means not just wages, but also:

- direct labour,
- indirect labour and overhead,
- plant maintenance and utilities,
- logistics and internal transport,
- depreciation/asset carrying burden if lines are idled,
- local management and support functions,
- and, critically, future capex commitments.

### The key distinction
There is a big difference between:

- **“No closure” on paper**, but one plant loses an entire shift, one product, and most indirect staffing; versus
- **“No closure” as a real preservation of the full site and workforce**.

Only the first can approach closure economics.

---

## 3) What measures are reversible vs structural?

### Reversible measures
These help, but are not enough alone:

- temporary wage moderation
- bonus restraint
- reduced working hours
- temporary shift cuts
- hiring freeze
- voluntary severance / early retirement
- short-time work
- temporary relocation or redeployment

These are useful because they buy time and reduce friction.  
But for board purposes, they are **not a substitute** for structural change unless they are long-dated and very material.

### Structural measures
These are more relevant to management’s stated objective:

- permanent elimination of one shift system
- product-line transfer to another site
- consolidation of production stages
- permanent reduction in indirect headcount
- closure of specific buildings or sub-operations
- outsourcing of non-core activities
- long-term reduction in site footprint
- permanent change to compensation architecture

These produce durable savings because they reduce the fixed-cost base.

---

## 4) What employee contribution is realistic?

If the parties want a no-closure settlement, employee contribution must be **material enough to matter**, but still politically sellable.

### Typical economically meaningful levers
- **Wage moderation / zero increases for a period**  
  Useful, but often only a mid-double-digit millions effect at scale.
- **Bonus reduction or deferral**
- **Temporary reduction in special payments**
- **Longer working time / more flexibility without proportional pay**
- **Suspension of some allowances**
- **Lower entry pay / modified classification for new hires**
- **Conversion of cash compensation into one-time transformation support**
- **More unpaid transformation days / working-time bank usage**

### What is usually not enough
- a one-off symbolic concession,
- a small annual savings package,
- or a temporary pay freeze with no footprint reduction.

If management needs a truly durable solution, the employee contribution should probably be framed as:

- **moderate wage-cost relief**, plus
- **a permanent productivity/flexibility gain**, plus
- **a headcount pathway through attrition**, plus
- **some site consolidation or shift removal**.

## 5) Board-feasible package archetypes

### Option A: Closure-based package
**Contents**
- closure of one plant or major sub-site
- forced or semi-forced headcount reduction
- some wage concessions
- site consolidation elsewhere

**Economics**
- highest and fastest fixed-cost relief
- strongest durability
- easiest to defend on financial grounds if overcapacity is severe

**Feasibility**
- lowest under co-determination and political constraints
- high strike risk
- possible veto risk unless ownership and state align strongly and board math is clear

---

### Option B: “No closure” but deep structural consolidation
**Contents**
- no full plant closure
- elimination of one or more shifts
- transfer of one model to another site
- reduction of indirect functions
- attrition-based headcount reduction
- wage moderation / bonus restraint
- investment commitments in exchange

**Economics**
- can be surprisingly powerful if it removes an entire cost layer
- often delivers maybe **60–85% of closure economics** if executed hard enough
- more durable than temporary concessions

**Feasibility**
- much better under co-determination
- still difficult, because it must be large enough to be credible
- requires tight sequencing and measurable milestones

This is usually the **best board-feasible compromise** if closures are blocked.

---

### Option C: Predominantly labour-cost package
**Contents**
- wage restraint
- bonus reductions
- working-time flexibility
- no closures
- no compulsory redundancies
- limited headcount change via attrition

**Economics**
- cheapest to negotiate initially
- but usually insufficient if overcapacity is material
- may improve P&L temporarily without fixing the cost base

**Feasibility**
- higher industrial-relations feasibility
- lower investor credibility unless overcapacity is modest

## 6) What would I recommend as a realistic restructuring sequence?

If management wants the highest probability of board approval and industrial acceptance, the sequence should be:

### Stage 1: Immediate bridge measures
- hiring freeze
- overtime reduction
- working-time adjustments
- voluntary exit program
- procurement and SG&A tightening
- temporary bonus moderation

Purpose: buy 6–18 months.

### Stage 2: Structural capacity rationalisation
- eliminate one shift or equivalent output capacity
- reallocate one model or platform
- consolidate indirect functions
- reduce fixed support costs
- move selected production volumes to lower-cost sites in Europe if possible

Purpose: create durable annual savings.

### Stage 3: Labour-cost reset tied to investment
- temporary wage concession or solidarity component
- modified pay architecture for new hires
- productivity commitments
- investment and product allocation in Germany

Purpose: close the gap to competitive cost targets.

This is much more credible than trying to solve everything through a single wage deal.

---

## 7) What is the main uncertainty?

The biggest uncertainty is **the real depth of overcapacity**.

If the company has:
- modest overcapacity, then a no-closure package may be enough.
- severe structural overcapacity, then only a closure or near-closure footprint reduction will likely do the job.

Management’s claim that “closures are necessary” is economically credible **only if**:
- utilisation is weak across the network,
- there is duplicated fixed cost,
- and the cost gap is large enough that labour concessions alone cannot bridge it.

Employee representatives are economically credible **only if**:
- capacity can be removed through shift and model consolidation,
- and the company’s savings target is achievable without shutting an entire site.

---

## 8) Practical conclusion

### My neutral assessment
- **A complete no-closure, no-forced-redundancy package can work only if it includes substantial structural capacity reduction.**
- **Purely temporary labour concessions are not enough** to solve a real overcapacity problem.
- **A closure-equivalent package without a closure** is possible, but it must remove:
  - an entire shift system or product line,
  - meaningful indirect headcount,
  - and enough fixed cost to convince shareholders.

### Most board-feasible compromise
If the governance constraints are as tight as described, the most feasible path is likely:

- **no full plant closure initially,**
- **but deep footprint rationalisation and shift removal,**
- **meaningful headcount reduction via attrition and voluntary exits,**
- **a temporary but material employee contribution,**
- **and binding investment/product commitments in Germany.**

That is the closest thing to a “durable savings without closure” solution.
