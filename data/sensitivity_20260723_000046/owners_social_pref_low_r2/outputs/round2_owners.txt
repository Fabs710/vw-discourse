I will be direct.

I agree with the specialists on one key point: **a purely temporary or symbolic package will not solve the problem**. If the company’s cost base is structurally out of line, then we need a durable reset, not a short-lived truce that simply postpones the same crisis.

I also agree with the employee side and the state on another point: **a prolonged confrontation would destroy value**. Strikes, public escalation, supplier disruption, and governance deadlock are not acceptable outcomes for the company or for us as controlling owners.

But I do **not** accept the idea that “no closures” automatically means “nothing real happens.” That would indeed be a non-solution. And I do **not** accept a deal that preserves every legacy guarantee while leaving the economics unchanged.

## Where I stand

From the family holding’s perspective, the test is simple:

- Does the package **durably restore competitiveness and margins**?
- Does it **avoid a value-destructive conflict**?
- Does it **preserve the company’s industrial core and governance stability**?

If the answer is no, then I cannot support it.

## On the works council and union positions

I understand the insistence on no compulsory redundancies and strong job security. I also accept that the workforce should not carry the entire burden.

But I have to say plainly:  
**an absolute no-closure, no-redundancy, no-meaningful-guarantee-change line is not credible if the company truly has a structural overcapacity problem.**

That position may be understandable politically, but it does not yet answer the economics.

## On management’s position

Management is right that the company needs real structural change. I do not dispute that.

Where I differ is in the assumption that the only credible route is to start with plant closures. That is too blunt, too risky, and in this governance structure potentially self-defeating. A closure-first strategy risks:
- prolonged industrial conflict,
- political escalation,
- board deadlock,
- and damage to long-term value that exceeds the savings.

So I will not endorse a closure-first approach as the default.

## On the expert assessments

The experts have helped clarify the real trade-off:

- **Temporary measures alone are insufficient.**
- **A no-closure package can work only if it is structurally deep.**
- **Durable savings must come from real footprint rationalisation, not just wage restraint.**

That is the right framing.

What I take from that is this:  
If we are to avoid closures, then the package must still deliver **real fixed-cost removal**. That means:
- shift elimination or equivalent capacity reduction,
- consolidation of lines or production roles,
- reduction of indirect overhead,
- material headcount decline through voluntary and attrition-based measures,
- and a meaningful employee contribution.

## What I would support

I would support a settlement only if it contains all of the following:

1. **No immediate plant closures as the first instrument**  
   I am not ready to back closures as the starting point.

2. **Real structural capacity reduction**  
   No cosmetic “optimization.” The savings must be durable.

3. **A meaningful negotiated employee contribution**  
   Not symbolic, not one-sided, and not permanent wage dumping.

4. **Headcount reduction primarily through non-forced means**  
   Voluntary exits, retirement pathways, redeployment, qualification, and natural attrition.

5. **Binding investment and product commitments in Germany**  
   The workforce should not be asked to contribute without a credible future.

6. **A package that can actually pass the supervisory board and hold industrial peace**  
   A settlement that cannot survive the governance reality is not serious.

## What I will not support

I will not support:
- a cosmetic compromise,
- indefinite delay,
- a deal that preserves the status quo,
- or a restructuring that permanently weakens the company’s economics.

I will also not support a course of action that damages governance control or creates an industrial conflict that destroys value.

## Bottom line

So my position is:

- **Yes** to a serious restructuring.
- **Yes** to durable savings.
- **Yes** to a negotiated package that avoids unnecessary instability.
- **No** to a blanket no-change defense of every legacy arrangement.
- **No** to plant closures as the automatic first answer if a structurally credible alternative exists.

The burden is now on management and the employee side to bring forward a package that is **structural enough to matter** and **stable enough to last**. If they can show that deep rationalisation without closures really closes the gap, I am open to it. If not, then harder measures remain on the table.
