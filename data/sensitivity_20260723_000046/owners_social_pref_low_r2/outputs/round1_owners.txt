Let me respond plainly.

I agree with one central point from management: **the company cannot afford a purely cosmetic solution**. If the cost base stays structurally out of line and the savings are only temporary, that is not a rescue plan. It is delay. From a shareholder perspective, that is unacceptable.

I also agree with the regional state and the workforce representatives on another point: **a prolonged confrontation would destroy value**. Strikes, reputational damage, supplier disruption, and political escalation would all weaken the enterprise. That is precisely why we need a durable settlement, not a theatrical showdown.

But I disagree with the idea that the only credible path is to start from plant closures and large-scale structural job destruction.

### On management’s position
Management is right that competitiveness has to be restored. But I am not convinced that “closure first” is the right framing, especially in a company with this governance structure and this strategic importance.

- A proposal that cannot pass the supervisory board is not a serious strategy.
- A plan that triggers prolonged industrial conflict risks destroying the very value it claims to protect.
- And a restructuring that permanently impairs trust in the company’s social model will make future transformation harder, not easier.

So I need to be clear: **if management insists on closures as the default solution, it will face resistance from us**. Not because change is unnecessary, but because value-destructive conflict and irreversible damage are not acceptable.

### On the works council and union position
I understand the refusal of compulsory redundancies and the desire to protect sites. Those are legitimate concerns. I also accept that the workforce cannot be expected to carry the entire burden alone.

However, I do not accept a position that effectively says:
- no closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- no meaningful change to guarantees,
- and no substantial employee contribution.

That would amount to preserving the present structure while asking the company to absorb the adjustment elsewhere. That is not a credible answer to overcapacity and margin pressure.

So I cannot endorse a blanket defense of every existing site and every existing guarantee if the economics do not support it. The company must regain competitiveness.

### On the regional state’s position
I agree with the state shareholder that the company’s industrial base matters and that social stability is a real asset. But protection of regional employment cannot mean insulating the business from hard decisions forever.

The state is right to insist on balanced measures. It would be wrong to force the entire adjustment onto labor. But it would also be wrong to treat any structural change as politically or socially forbidden.

### Where I stand
From the family holding’s perspective, the priorities are:
1. **Restore competitiveness and margins durably.**
2. **Avoid value-destroying conflict.**
3. **Protect the long-term industrial core of the company.**
4. **Preserve governance control and decision credibility.**

That means I would support a settlement only if it has all of the following:
- a **credible structural savings package**,
- a **meaningful, negotiated employee contribution**,
- **real productivity and flexibility improvements**,
- and **investment commitments** that justify the sacrifices.

What I will not support is a deal that:
- permanently weakens the enterprise’s economics,
- preserves every legacy guarantee regardless of cost,
- or creates the impression that governance can be held hostage by the threat of disruption.

### Practical implication
I am open to alternatives to closures if — and only if — they produce **equivalent, durable savings** and not just temporary deferrals. That could include capacity reductions, working-time measures, redeployment, and other negotiated tools. But the burden is on the parties proposing “no closures” to show how the numbers work.

If they cannot show that, then management’s call for deeper structural measures remains on the table.

### Bottom line
I am **not** endorsing plant closures as a first principle.  
I am **not** accepting a settlement that preserves the status quo.  
And I am **not** willing to see the company’s long-term value eroded by endless delay.

My position is straightforward: **bring forward a settlement that restores competitiveness, secures labor peace, and preserves the company’s long-term industrial strength.** If that can be done without closures, fine. If not, then the alternatives must be serious enough to achieve the same durable effect.
