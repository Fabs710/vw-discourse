Yes — based on the restructuring economics alone, a **no-closure deep rationalisation package is plausibly capable of closing most of the savings gap**, but **only if it is materially deeper than a labour-only compromise**.

## My assessment in plain terms

### 1) What is economically plausible
A package built around:
- permanent **shift elimination**
- **line / model consolidation**
- **overhead cuts**
- **voluntary exits / attrition**
- **working-time and bonus moderation**
- and some **temporary labour-cost relief**

can often capture roughly **60–85% of the annual savings** that a closure-based package would deliver.

That is the key range.

### 2) What is not enough
A package consisting mainly of:
- hiring freeze
- wage moderation
- bonus restraint
- overtime reduction
- natural attrition only

is usually only worth about **20–35% of closure economics**.  
That is generally **too weak** if management is trying to reset a structurally high cost base.

---

# What closure economics usually contain
A plant closure removes three things:
1. direct labour cost
2. indirect fixed site overhead
3. idle capacity / complexity cost

A no-closure package can recover a lot of #1 and #2, but only if it **actually removes capacity**, not just costs.

The main value drivers are:
- eliminating a shift permanently
- consolidating production volume
- reducing local management / support layers
- shrinking indirect functions
- taking headcount down materially through non-forced means

---

# Rough comparison

## Closure-based restructuring
- **Savings:** 100% of site-level fixed-cost relief
- **One-off cost:** high
- **Risk:** very high industrial / governance risk
- **Execution:** often slower than it looks because of run-off, transfer, and conflict

## Deep no-closure footprint rationalisation
- **Savings:** about **60–85%** of closure economics if done properly
- **One-off cost:** moderate to high, but less than closure
- **Risk:** materially lower than closure
- **Execution:** feasible under co-determination if real capacity is removed

## Labour-only compromise
- **Savings:** about **20–35%**
- **One-off cost:** low
- **Risk:** low initially, but high risk of failing the turnaround
- **Execution:** fastest, but usually insufficient

---

# Can it work while preserving German sites and avoiding compulsory redundancies?
**Potentially yes**, but only under strict conditions:

1. **Permanent shift elimination**
   - This is the biggest non-closure lever.

2. **Real footprint consolidation**
   - Not just “optimization”; actual reduction in operating scope.

3. **Material headcount reduction**
   - Mostly via voluntary exits, retirement corridors, redeployment, and attrition.
   - If headcount doesn’t fall meaningfully, savings will likely fall short.

4. **Indirect overhead removal**
   - Site management, support duplication, logistics, engineering, and admin layers must shrink.

5. **Some employee contribution**
   - Wage moderation, bonus restraint, or working-time flexibility can help, but it cannot carry the package alone.

6. **Binding product and investment commitments**
   - Otherwise the remaining footprint risks being underutilized.

---

# My conclusion
**A no-closure package can be economically credible, but only if it is a “deep rationalisation” package, not a peace deal.**

If management needs a major durable cost reset, then the package must likely get **close to closure-like savings**, even if it avoids actual closures. In practice, that means:

- **closure-equivalent on capacity reduction**
- **not closure-equivalent on social destruction**

That is the realistic trade-off.
