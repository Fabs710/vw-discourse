We agree on the diagnosis, but not yet on the remedy.

## Where we agree
To management, the family holding, the state, and the experts: yes, the company needs a **durable structural reset**. Temporary measures alone are not enough. A cosmetic peace deal would be worse than no deal. We also agree that **investment and product commitments in Germany** matter — because without future volume and modernization, the remaining footprint will not be worth much.

We also agree with the specialist conclusion that a **deep no-closure package can, in principle, deliver most of the economics of closure**. That is the only reason a no-closure path remains investable from our side.

## Where we disagree
### To the works council and union
Your “no closures, no compulsory redundancies” line is understandable, but it is not sufficient as a response to a structural cost problem. If that position is paired only with reversible concessions and vague internal efficiencies, then it leaves the cost base uncompetitive. We cannot support that.

At the same time, we do **not** accept closure as the default benchmark either. The relevant question is not whether the package matches the destructiveness of a closure. The relevant question is whether it **permanently restores competitiveness**.

### To management
You are right that a labour-only compromise is too weak. But you still have not shown that closure is necessary rather than merely convenient. The expert range given here matters: if a deep no-closure package can achieve roughly **60–85% of closure economics**, then closure is not automatically justified. It may be the maximum theoretical reset, but it is not automatically the best value outcome once governance risk, strike risk, and execution risk are priced in.

That said, we would **not** support a shallow no-closure package. If “no closure” means hiring freeze, bonus restraint, and attrition alone, then it is too weak and will not restore margins.

## Our position
We are prepared to back a **serious no-closure restructuring**, but only if it includes all of the following:

1. **Permanent capacity reduction**  
   Real shift elimination, line consolidation, and footprint rationalisation.

2. **Material overhead reduction**  
   Not just labour cost trimming; the fixed-cost stack must come down.

3. **Meaningful headcount reduction**  
   Preferably through voluntary exits, retirement pathways, redeployment, and attrition — but at a scale that materially changes the cost base.

4. **A real employee contribution**  
   Temporary wage moderation, bonus restraint, and flexibility can be part of the deal, but not as a substitute for structural change.

5. **Binding investment and product commitments**  
   Otherwise the remaining footprint risks being underutilized and the savings will not be durable.

6. **Execution certainty and board feasibility**  
   A package that cannot pass governance or survive industrial relations is not a real package.

## What we will not support
We will not support:
- a cosmetic no-closure deal,
- a settlement that preserves the current uncompetitive cost base,
- or open-ended conflict that destroys margins and investor confidence.

We will also not support a closure-first approach unless management can quantify why the deep internal package fails to close the gap. That burden is still not met.

## What we need quantified now
Before any support can be given, management should put forward a **side-by-side quantified comparison** of:

- closure-based restructuring,
- deep no-closure rationalisation,
- and labour-only compromise,

including:
- annual permanent savings,
- one-off restructuring costs,
- headcount impact,
- implementation risk,
- and expected timing to restore return on sales.

## Bottom line
We are open to **no closures** if — and only if — the package is structurally deep enough to restore competitive margins and protect long-term value. If not, then it is not a compromise; it is a delay.

That is our line.
