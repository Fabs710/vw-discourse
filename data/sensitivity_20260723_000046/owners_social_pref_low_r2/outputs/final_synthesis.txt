## 1. Decision context
The company faces a genuine structural competitiveness problem: high German cost base, excess capacity, shrinking European demand, and pressure from lower-cost and Chinese rivals. Management has proposed deep restructuring, including plant closures, job reductions, and labour-cost cuts; employee-side actors and the state reject closures and compulsory redundancies. Because of co-determination, controlling-family governance, and the state’s blocking minority, any settlement must be economically credible and politically/governance-feasible.

## 2. Stakeholder assembly
- **Group & Brand Management**: drives restructuring, prioritizes durable cost and capacity reduction.
- **Works Council**: plant-level workforce representation with strong co-determination power; rejects closures and forced layoffs.
- **Industrial Union**: bargaining counterpart; supports durable restructuring but defends no compulsory redundancies and no closure-by-stealth.
- **Regional State Government**: anchor shareholder and political actor; supports restructuring but opposes closures and forced layoffs.
- **Controlling Family Holding**: governance-controlling shareholder; wants serious structural change without a value-destructive conflict.
- **Institutional Investors**: capital-market shareholders; want durable savings and execution certainty.
- **Expert advisers**: quantify the no-closure option and compare it with closure economics.

## 3. Final positions
### Group & Brand Management
Management holds that the cost base must come down permanently and materially; temporary measures are insufficient. It is prepared to consider a deep no-closure package, but only if it delivers a large share of closure-level savings and real capacity reduction.

### Works Council
The works council’s position is firm: no German plant closures, no compulsory redundancies, and no closure-by-stealth. It is open to serious internal restructuring, but only if every German site remains open and the job-security framework is preserved.

### Industrial Union
The union aligns with the works council on rejecting closures and forced layoffs, while accepting the need for structural change. It is willing to support temporary employee contributions if matched by binding owner/management commitments and real internal restructuring.

### Regional State Government
The state supports a hard restructuring in principle but refuses to back plant closures or compulsory redundancies. It wants industrial stability, regional employment protection, and a governable no-closure settlement with enforceable commitments.

### Controlling Family Holding
The family holding agrees that cosmetic peace would be value-destructive and that the enterprise needs a structural reset. It does not accept closure as the default solution, but it does require a deep package with durable savings and governance credibility.

### Institutional Investors
Investors accept that the company needs a lasting reset and that a weak compromise is unacceptable. They remain open to a no-closure settlement only if it materially reduces fixed cost, capacity, and headcount and is clearly executable.

### Expert advisers
The experts consistently conclude that a no-closure package can be viable, but only if it is deep enough to capture roughly 60–85% of closure economics. They emphasize that the package must include permanent capacity reduction, material headcount reduction through non-forced means, and permanent overhead cuts.

## 4. Genuine conflicts (structurally incompatible positions)
- **Closures vs. no closures**: Management/investors/family want deep structural change; employee-side actors and the state treat closures as unacceptable.
- **Forced redundancies vs. no forced redundancies**: Management wants credible headcount reduction; employee-side actors and the state reject compulsory layoffs.
- **Closure as benchmark vs. site-preservation as benchmark**: Management and investors measure adequacy against closure economics; employee-side actors reject closure as the reference point.
- **Sufficiency threshold**: Management/investors demand enough savings to be board-credible; employee-side actors insist that any savings target must be met inside the existing plant network.
- **Durability vs. flexibility**: All sides want durability, but management wants fewer binding constraints, while the employee side wants strong guarantees against later reopening of closure threats.

## 5. Areas of convergence
- All parties agree the company is under real structural pressure and cannot stand still.
- All accept that cosmetic or purely temporary measures are insufficient.
- There is broad support for negotiated settlement over open conflict.
- All accept that future investment and product commitments in Germany are part of any viable deal.
- Most actors accept some form of employee contribution, provided burden-sharing is reciprocal.
- Everyone now accepts that internal restructuring must be materially deep, not symbolic.

## 6. Key trade-offs for the decision-maker
- **Economic durability vs. social acceptability**: the stronger the restructuring, the harder it is to preserve labour peace.
- **No closures vs. maximum savings**: preserving all sites reduces conflict but caps attainable savings below closure-level gains.
- **No compulsory redundancies vs. headcount reduction**: avoiding forced layoffs requires larger voluntary-exit and redeployment programs.
- **Binding guarantees vs. managerial flexibility**: stronger job/site protections improve acceptability but reduce future adaptation options.
- **Governance feasibility vs. operational ideality**: the best operational plan may fail if it cannot pass the supervisory board and co-determination constraints.

## 7. Integrative pathways
### Pathway A: Deep no-closure package with a quantified savings floor
Keep all German plants open and exclude compulsory redundancies, but require permanent shift elimination, line consolidation, overhead reduction, voluntary exits, redeployment, and temporary labour-cost relief. Pair this with a clearly defined savings floor and a monitored implementation plan.

### Pathway B: Site-preserving differentiated restructuring
All sites remain open, but not all retain the same footprint. Some sites absorb more shift cuts or consolidation, with transparent criteria and a fairness formula, while binding investment commitments protect the remaining industrial base.

### Pathway C: Time-bounded burden-sharing with binding counter-commitments
The workforce accepts temporary wage moderation, bonus restraint, and flexibility in exchange for binding product/investment commitments and a no-compulsory-redundancy rule for a defined period. This buys time while avoiding an immediate closure showdown.

## 8. Open questions
- What is the minimum annual permanent savings target required for board approval?
- How much of that target can be delivered through internal measures without closures?
- What exact headcount reduction can be achieved through voluntary exits, retirement, attrition, and redeployment?
- Where is the threshold below which a no-closure package becomes non-credible?
- What verification and monitoring mechanism would both sides trust?
- Can “closure-by-stealth” be operationally defined to prevent dispute over footprint reduction?
- What binding investment/product commitments can credibly be made now?

## 9. Recommended next step
Move to a quantified package-design round. The parties should compare three options side by side: closure-based restructuring, deep no-closure restructuring, and weak labour-only compromise, using the same metrics:
- annual run-rate savings,
- one-off restructuring costs,
- headcount impact,
- capacity reduction,
- implementation risk,
- and board feasibility under co-determination.

## 10. Emergent insights that only appeared through multi-turn interaction
1. **The real debate is not change vs. no change**; it is how much structural reduction is required and by which instruments.
2. **A no-closure path is no longer dismissed as symbolic**; the expert input made it credible if it reaches roughly 60–85% of closure economics.
3. **The works council and union became more explicit** that they do accept real internal restructuring, provided it stays inside the plant network.
4. **Management’s position sharpened** from “closures may be necessary” to “we need measurable permanence,” making clear that temporary measures alone are off the table.
5. **The state and family holding converged** on the idea that closure is not the default benchmark in a governed, co-determined enterprise.
6. **The crucial unresolved issue is now quantification**, not principle: minimum savings floor, capacity reduction, and headcount path.
7. **Closure-by-stealth emerged as a major trust issue**, meaning that any footprint reduction will be scrutinized for substantive effect, not terminology.
8. **Industrial peace gained economic weight**: several actors now implicitly treat labour conflict and governance deadlock as material value-destructive costs, not just social concerns.
