The discussion has clarified one thing: **everyone accepts that the current cost base is unsustainable**. That is useful. ## Where I agree
I agree with the employee side, the state, the family holding, and investors on several points:

- **A drawn-out conflict would damage the company.**
- **Temporary measures alone are not enough.**
- **Investment and future products in Germany must be part of any settlement.**
- **A cosmetic compromise would be unacceptable.**

So yes: the debate has moved beyond slogans.

## Where I disagree
### 1) “No closures” is not, by itself, a solution
The works council and union keep repeating that closures are unnecessary. That is an assertion, not a demonstrated answer.

I am not starting from ideology. I am starting from the numbers: **German factory costs are too high, demand in Europe is shrinking, and the company has structural overcapacity**. If we keep every site intact and refuse real footprint change, then we preserve the problem.

You can call that uncomfortable. I call it economically honest.

### 2) Temporary concessions do not solve a structural gap
Hiring freezes, wage moderation, bonus restraint, working-time flexibility, and attrition are all useful. We should use them. But they are **bridge measures**, not a full restructuring.

If the cost gap is material, then the package must include **permanent** changes:
- lower fixed costs,
- reduced capacity,
- reduced overhead,
- and a real labour-cost reset.

### 3) “No compulsory redundancies” cannot mean “no real headcount reduction”
I understand the concern. But if the workforce says there can be no forced layoffs, no closures, and no meaningful guarantee changes, while still expecting the company to become competitive, then something does not add up.

If we reach a deal without compulsory redundancies, fine — but only if it still produces **material, durable headcount reduction** through:
- voluntary exits,
- retirement pathways,
- attrition,
- redeployment,
- and structural consolidation.

If that does not happen, then the savings are not enough.

## On the arguments from the works council and union
You both keep saying management has not proven closures are unavoidable.

I’ll respond plainly: **the burden is not on management to prove closure is the only possible instrument; the burden is on all of us to produce a package that actually closes the cost gap.**  
If you want to reject closures, then show me an alternative that is:
- durable,
- large enough,
- enforceable,
- and board-feasible.

So far, what I hear is mostly a list of preferred measures, not a quantified equivalent.

## On the regional state
I appreciate your emphasis on industrial stability. But stability without competitiveness is just delay.

If we protect every legacy structure and leave the company unable to earn its cost of capital, then we do not protect the region — we weaken it. The state’s role should be to support a viable industrial base, not to freeze a declining one.

## On the family holding and investors
You are right on one point: a cosmetic compromise is not acceptable.

But I also want to caution against expecting management to deliver a durable turnaround while preserving every existing arrangement. That is not how structural change works. If we are serious about restoring margins and freeing funds for 2030 investment, then something real has to move.

## On the expert assessments
The specialists have been helpful. Their message is clear:

- A **labour-only** package is too weak.
- A **no-closure** package can work **only if it is structurally deep**.
- That means shift elimination, footprint consolidation, indirect overhead reduction, and material headcount decline.

That is exactly my position.

What I will not accept is the idea that “no closure” automatically means “no real restructuring.” If that is the proposal, it is not good enough.

## What I am prepared to accept
I remain open to a **no immediate closure** path — but only if it includes all of the following:

1. **Permanent structural capacity reduction**  
   That means real footprint rationalisation, not just optimization language.

2. **Meaningful labour-cost contribution**  
   We need a real contribution, not a symbolic one.

3. **Material headcount reduction without compulsory layoffs if possible**  
   Voluntary exits and attrition are acceptable only if the numbers are sufficient.

4. **Clear productivity and flexibility improvements**  
   The organization must become more competitive, not just cheaper on paper.

5. **Binding investment and product commitments in Germany**  
   We will not ask employees to contribute without a future.

6. **A settlement that can pass governance and be implemented**  
   If it cannot survive the supervisory board and the economic reality, it is not serious.

## What I will not accept
Let me be equally clear about the limits:

- I will **not** accept a cosmetic no-closure deal.
- I will **not** accept a package that leaves the cost base fundamentally uncompetitive.
- I will **not** accept a solution that protects every legacy guarantee while avoiding all structural adjustment.
- I will **not** accept a return to open-ended drift.
