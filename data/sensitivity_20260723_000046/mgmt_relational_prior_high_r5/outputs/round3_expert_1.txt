As the independent restructuring and capacity auditor, my answer is:

## What I can say with confidence
**Plant closures are usually the most powerful tool for permanent fixed-cost and capacity reduction.**  
They typically remove:
- site overhead,
- indirect labor,
- maintenance and utility baseload,
- local logistics complexity,
- and excess installed capacity.

## What I would classify as genuinely permanent and capacity-reducing
These measures can be durable if they are large enough and not backfilled:

1. **Permanent headcount reduction**
   - voluntary exits
   - early retirement
   - non-replacement of departures
   - redeployment only if the roles truly disappear from the cost base

2. **Model or volume concentration**
   - shifting production away from one site to another
   - consolidating derivatives or variants
   - reducing the number of active production lines

3. **Footprint reduction short of full closure**
   - shutting specific lines, halls, or functions
   - shrinking shifts
   - removing entire product families from a plant
   - partial site consolidation

4. **Permanent overhead removal**
   - eliminating plant-level management layers
   - shared service centralization
   - procurement simplification
   - logistics network redesign

5. **Structural labor-cost changes**
   - only if they are durable and contractual, not temporary
   - for example, permanent changes to allowances, premiums, or work-time regimes

## What I would classify as temporary mitigation
These help cash flow or buy time, but are not the same as structural restructuring:

- temporary wage moderation
- bonus waivers
- short-term working-time reductions
- one-off management pay cuts
- deferred investments
- temporary transfer of labor cost
- short-cycle productivity campaigns without footprint reduction

## The key distinction
A measure is economically comparable to closure only if it removes **permanent fixed cost** and **unused capacity** in a way that is:
- durable through 2030,
- enforceable,
- and not dependent on optimistic assumptions about future utilization.

## My restructuring judgment
Without plant-level data, I cannot say whether the company’s specific alternatives are sufficient. But in general:

- **Non-closure measures can be material**
- **Footprint reduction can sometimes substitute for closure**
- **Temporary labor concessions cannot substitute for closure**
- **A no-closure package is only credible if it includes real capacity removal, not just cost postponement**

## What data is needed to verify equivalence
For each plant and each option:
- current and forecast utilization
- fixed site overhead
- indirect labor by function
- variable conversion cost
- volume transferable to other plants
- incremental cost at receiving sites
- one-time restructuring cost
- payback period
- residual cost after restructuring

## Bottom line
If the parties want to know whether non-closure measures are economically comparable to closures, the answer depends on whether they can remove **enough permanent fixed cost and capacity**.

My preliminary view is:
- **temporary concessions are not comparable**
- **partial footprint reductions may be**
- **full closures usually deliver the deepest savings**
- and the only credible way to decide is a **plant-by-plant scenario comparison through 2030**.
