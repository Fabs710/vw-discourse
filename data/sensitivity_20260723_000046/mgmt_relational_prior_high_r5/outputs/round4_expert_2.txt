From an independent restructuring and plant-economics perspective, the right comparison is:

- **closure**
- **partial footprint reduction / site role change**
- **non-closure restructuring**

The question is whether the non-closure path can remove **enough permanent fixed cost and excess capacity** to be economically equivalent by 2030.

## What is clearly permanent
These measures can produce durable savings if implemented at sufficient scale:

- **Permanent headcount reduction** via voluntary exits plus non-replacement
- **Early retirement / attrition**
- **Overhead and layer removal** in administration, engineering support, and central functions
- **Model concentration / volume reallocation** to fewer plants or fewer variants
- **Shift reduction / line simplification** if it permanently lowers labor and overhead absorption
- **Footprint consolidation short of closure** if buildings, lines, and support functions are truly taken out of service
- **Procurement, logistics, and complexity reduction**
- **Management compensation restraint**, but only as a supporting contribution

## What is mostly temporary
These help cash flow or bridge a period, but are not equivalent to structural restructuring:

- temporary wage moderation
- bonus restraint
- working-time reductions that can be reversed
- short-term partial layoffs
- one-off deferrals
- temporary state or owner support
- accounting savings without physical capacity reduction

## Why closures are the benchmark
A plant closure typically does three things at once:

1. removes recurring fixed cost,
2. removes capacity decisively,
3. gives high execution certainty.

That is why closures usually deliver the cleanest restructuring result.

## When a non-closure package can be comparable
Only if it delivers, by plant and by function:

- enough **permanent cost removal**
- enough **capacity reduction**
- enough **volume concentration**
- and enough **execution certainty**

In practice, that means the company needs a plant-by-plant bridge showing:

- current fixed cost by site
- savings from voluntary exits and non-replacement
- savings from footprint reduction and line simplification
- residual overhead after reorganization
- transferability of volume to other plants
- one-time restructuring costs
- payback period
- margin impact through 2030
- sensitivity if volumes weaken further

## My audit judgment
Based on industrial logic alone:

- **Non-closure measures can be substantial**
- but they often **do not fully match closure** unless they are very large and accompanied by real capacity concentration

So the decisive issue is not the political preference for keeping plants open. It is whether the proposed package can actually remove enough recurring cost to make the post-restructuring business viable.

## Bottom line
- **Permanent measures:** headcount reduction, overhead removal, footprint consolidation, volume concentration
- **Temporary measures:** wage moderation, bonus restraint, working-time cuts, deferrals
- **Comparable to closure only if:** the permanent measures remove enough fixed cost and excess capacity to reach the same economics by 2030
