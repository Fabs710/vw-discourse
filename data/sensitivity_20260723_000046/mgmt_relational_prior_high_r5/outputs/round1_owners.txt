I appreciate the clarity from all sides. I will also be clear, from the perspective of the controlling family holding: **we want a durable turnaround, not a symbolic confrontation, and certainly not a slow-motion erosion of the company’s value.**

### Where I agree
I agree with management on the core diagnosis: the company cannot carry a structurally uncompetitive cost base and still remain strong in a shrinking and highly competitive market. If margins are not restored, the long-term consequences will be worse for everyone — employees, regions, and owners alike.

I also agree with the works council, the union, and the state on one important point: **a settlement that destroys industrial peace or undermines the company’s future capacity is not acceptable either.** This is a core asset, not a short-term transaction.

### Where I disagree
What I cannot support is a posture that says, in effect, “no closures, no compulsory reductions, no meaningful labour-cost change, and no break with legacy structures.” That is not a viable answer to the problem management has described. If the numbers are real, then the adjustment must be real too.

At the same time, I do **not** see value in a blunt, destabilising restructuring that treats plant closures as the only serious instrument. A scorched-earth approach would carry major risks:
- prolonged industrial conflict,
- reputational damage,
- loss of trust in governance,
- and irreversible harm to the enterprise’s long-term value.

### My position
The family holding supports a **credible, durable restructuring package** that restores competitiveness and protects the company’s future. That means:

- **Savings must be material and lasting**
- **Capacity must be reduced where needed**
- **Labour costs must come down in a measurable way**
- **But the solution should prioritise negotiated and socially responsible instruments over open conflict**
- **Any measures should preserve the company’s industrial future and avoid unnecessary permanent damage**

### On closures
I am not making closures an ideological red line, but I am also not prepared to rule them out if they are genuinely necessary to restore competitiveness. What I will not accept is either of these extremes:
1. closures used as a first resort without exhausting alternatives, or
2. a “no-closure at any price” position that leaves the cost problem unresolved.

If the employee side wants to avoid closures, then it needs to engage with an alternative that is not only principled but economically convincing.

### On employee contributions
I support the idea that the workforce should contribute, but it must be designed carefully:
- not as a humiliation,
- not as a one-sided burden,
- and not in a way that breaks the company’s ability to recruit and retain talent.

Temporary or structured contributions, flexibility, and productivity measures are more credible than denying the need for change altogether.

### On management and owners
I also agree that credibility requires visible contribution from leadership and shareholders. A restructuring that asks only the workforce to pay would not be sustainable. Management must show discipline, and the owners must back a plan that is serious about restoring returns, not just shifting pain around.

### Bottom line
So my message is simple:

- **I support a real restructuring.**
- **I do not support a cosmetic compromise.**
- **I do not support a prolonged standoff that destroys value.**
- **I will back a settlement that is economically credible, socially responsible, and durable.**

If there is an alternative to closures that truly delivers comparable savings and restores competitiveness, we will examine it seriously. But it must be concrete, enforceable, and sufficient. If not, then harder measures cannot be excluded.

I encourage the parties to move quickly from red lines to numbers. The company needs an outcome that protects its future, not a debate that protects everybody’s preferred language.
