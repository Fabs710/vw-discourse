From the perspective of dispersed capital-market shareholders, the discussion has now become much clearer.

## Where we agree
I agree with management, the family holding, and the independent auditors on the core diagnosis:

- this is a **structural competitiveness problem**;
- the current German cost base is **too high**;
- **temporary gestures alone are not enough**;
- and any settlement that leaves the company with **structurally impaired margins** is unacceptable.

I also agree with the employee side on one important point: a **cosmetic settlement** or a long industrial conflict would destroy value. Prolonged strikes, uncertainty, and governance paralysis are not acceptable from an investor standpoint.

## Where I disagree

### To the works council and union
I respect the effort to offer an alternative package, but your current line is still too absolute.

A blanket:
- **no closures**,
- **no compulsory redundancies**,
- **no meaningful structural flexibility**,

is not enough unless it is paired with a package that demonstrably restores competitiveness on the same scale and with the same certainty as hard measures.

You have described working-time flexibility, voluntary exits, redeployment, training, and temporary contributions. Those are useful. But they do **not yet convince me** that they will remove enough **permanent fixed cost** and enough **excess capacity** through 2030.

The critical issue is not whether the measures are well-intentioned. It is whether they produce:
- durable margin repair,
- a lower structural cost base,
- and credible return on sales.

Right now, I still do not see proof of equivalence.

### To management
I also do **not** give management a blank cheque.

You are right to insist on a serious restructuring, but you still need to show the **plant-by-plant bridge** in a way that distinguishes:
- temporary savings,
- permanent savings,
- and actual capacity removal.

“Closures remain on the table” is not sufficient as a financial plan. It is only acceptable if you can demonstrate that alternatives fail the audit on scale, permanence, and timing.

If you cannot quantify that, then you are asking investors to accept the most disruptive option without proving necessity.

### To the state government
I understand the political and regional concerns, but the state cannot block every hard adjustment and still expect equity holders to tolerate underperformance.

A veto against closures is not a business case. If the state wants to rule out hard measures, then it must also help deliver a **fully credible substitute** that closes the gap in fixed cost and capacity.

### To the controlling family holding
I appreciate your rejection of scorched-earth tactics. That is sensible stewardship.

But I also need to be blunt: “closures only as last resort” can easily become a way of delaying hard decisions until the company has already lost more value. If the alternative package does not work, the ability to act must remain real, not theoretical.

## Our threshold
Capital markets can support a negotiated solution, but only if it includes all of the following:

1. **Durable structural cost reduction**
   - not temporary optics;
   - not one-off wage pauses.

2. **Real capacity rationalization**
   - whether through closures, line exits, site consolidation, or an equivalent permanent footprint reduction.

3. **Meaningful labor contribution**
   - limited to what is necessary,
   - but sufficient to matter materially.

4. **Execution certainty**
   - low strike risk,
   - enforceable commitments,
   - no endless renegotiation.

## What I would support
I could support a hybrid package if it includes:
- voluntary exits and early retirement,
- redeployment and retraining,
- targeted working-time changes,
- temporary wage moderation,
- management restraint,
- and binding product/investment commitments,

**but only if** the package also delivers a **measurable permanent reduction** in the German fixed-cost base and capacity.

That means I am open on the mechanism. I am **not** open on the economics being weak.

## What I will not support
I will not support:
- a settlement that preserves all capacity and simply spreads pain around;
- a no-closure doctrine that is not backed by quantified savings;
- a deal that leaves margins structurally impaired;
- or a package that substitutes political comfort for financial credibility.

## Bottom line
I am not here to defend closures as ideology. I am here to defend **competitive margins and long-term equity value**.

If the employee side and the state can produce a verified package that permanently closes the cost gap without closures, I am willing to consider it. But the burden of proof is on them, and so far I do not see that burden met.

If they cannot meet it, then management and shareholders must retain the right to pursue hard structural measures, including closures if necessary.
