## My position
The family holding supports a **durable restructuring that restores competitiveness, margins, and investment capacity**. We do **not** support either:
- a **closure-first, scorched-earth approach**, or
- a **no-change / no-hard-measures outcome** that leaves the company structurally uncompetitive.

Both would destroy value, just in different ways.

---

## To management
I agree with you on the diagnosis: this is a **structural** problem, not a temporary one. I also agree that temporary gestures alone will not fix the cost base.

Where I disagree is on the way you keep treating closures and compulsory redundancies as an implied baseline. They may be **available instruments**, but they are not a free pass to stop proving the case.

You have not yet shown, with sufficient specificity, that non-closure measures cannot close the gap. The auditor keeps saying closures often remove the most fixed cost. Fine. “Often” is not “must” and “possible” is not “necessary.”

What I need from management is:
- plant-by-plant fixed and variable cost data,
- utilization by site,
- residual cost after non-closure measures,
- one-time restructuring costs,
- and a comparison through 2030 of:
  1. closure,
  2. partial footprint reduction,
  3. and non-closure restructuring.

Until then, I will not endorse a default closure mandate.

At the same time, I will not endorse a settlement that merely preserves every site and hopes for a turnaround. If the numbers show that alternatives do not remove enough permanent cost and capacity, then the company must retain meaningful options. I will not sign up to permanent governance self-handcuffing.

---

## To the works council
I understand your resistance. Closing plants is serious, and the workforce has every reason to demand proof.

Where I disagree is your absolute line: **no closures, no compulsory redundancies, no fallback**. That is not yet a viable governance position for a company with a real structural cost gap.

You say closures are being used as pressure rather than proof. Sometimes that is true in industrial relations. But it does not follow that closures can simply be ruled out in advance. If a site is structurally underutilized and the alternative package leaves too much residual cost, refusing every hard measure would itself be value-destructive.

Your alternative package is directionally sensible:
- voluntary exits,
- redeployment,
- retraining,
- working-time flexibility,
- temporary labor-cost moderation,
- productivity improvements,
- and better plant utilization.

But you have not yet demonstrated that it can remove enough **permanent** fixed cost and excess capacity through 2030. Temporary relief is not enough. Management is right on that principle, even if I disagree with its instinct to keep closures too readily in play.

So my view is:
- yes to serious non-closure alternatives,
- yes to binding safeguards,
- no to automatic closure threats,
- but also no to a blank no-closure doctrine that ignores the economics.

---

## To the industrial union
I agree with you that a cosmetic compromise would be worthless. I also agree that workers should not be the sole shock absorber.

Where I disagree is your categorical refusal of closures and compulsory redundancies under any circumstance. That position is understandable politically, but it is not enough for an owner responsible for long-term capital preservation.

You are right that the alternative package must be binding and enforceable. But binding is not the same as complete immunity from hard decisions. If the audit shows the package is insufficient, then the owners and management must have the ability to act.

So I can support:
- voluntary departures,
- early retirement,
- working-time changes,
- temporary wage moderation,
- bonus restraint,
- redeployment,
- training,
- management restraint,
- productivity and logistics improvements,
- and model concentration.

I cannot support a settlement that removes all fallback options before the company has shown it can remain competitive.

## To the regional state government
I respect the state’s role and the special statute. I also respect the concern for industrial capacity and regional employment.

But the state cannot use its blocking position to freeze the network in place regardless of financial reality. That would not be stewardship; it would be delay.

You are right to demand a plant-by-plant comparison. I support that. But I do not accept the implication that closures are disqualified unless management can prove a negative beyond all doubt. The burden is to show whether a non-closure package is economically sufficient. If it is, I am open to it. If it is not, then the company must retain the tools to fix the cost base.

I also want to be clear: I do **not** support weakening co-determination or the state’s legal role. Governance stability matters. But stability is not the same as immobility.

## To the independent auditor
Your input is useful and consistent with what I expect in manufacturing: closures are often the strongest instrument for permanent fixed-cost and capacity reduction.

But I want the audit to do more than repeat that general truth. I want it to test whether this company can achieve a credible, durable turnaround **without** closures if it combines:
- footprint reduction short of full closure,
- permanent headcount reduction through voluntary exits and non-replacement,
- model and volume concentration,
- fixed-overhead removal,
- and a negotiated employee contribution.

That is the real question. Not whether closures are the strongest tool in theory, but whether the non-closure package is strong enough in practice.

## Where I stand
My threshold is straightforward:

### I can support
1. A serious independent audit  
2. A quantified non-closure restructuring package if it is durable and sufficient  
3. Voluntary exits, redeployment, retraining, and working-time flexibility  
4. A time-limited and fair employee contribution  
5. Management restraint and visible owner-side support  
6. Binding investment and product commitments for German sites  

### I cannot support
1. A blanket no-closure doctrine  
2. A blanket no-compulsory-redundancy doctrine as an absolute irrespective of economics  
3. A cosmetic settlement that preserves overcapacity  
4. A prolonged conflict that destroys value  
5. Any outcome that permanently impairs enterprise value or governance control  

---

## Bottom line
I am not siding with management’s preference for hard measures, and I am not siding with the employee-side demand to rule them out in advance.

My position is:
- **prove the alternatives properly**,
- **preserve the ability to act if they fail**,
- and **avoid value-destructive instability**.

That is the stewardship responsibility here.
