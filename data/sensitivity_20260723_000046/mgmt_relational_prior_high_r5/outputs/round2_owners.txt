I appreciate the seriousness on all sides. Speaking for the controlling family holding, I will be direct.

## Where I agree
I agree with management on the central diagnosis: this is a structural competitiveness problem, not a temporary downturn. If we do not restore margins and reduce the cost base, the company will keep eroding value, which ultimately hurts employees, regions, and owners alike.

I also agree with the employee side and the state on one important point: **a solution that destroys industrial peace, trust, and the company’s future investment capacity is not acceptable either**. A scorched-earth restructuring would be a failure of stewardship.

## Where I disagree
What I cannot support is a position that effectively says:
- no closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- no meaningful lasting labour-cost change,
- and no serious adjustment to legacy structures.

That is not a restructuring. That is a refusal to confront reality.

At the same time, I do **not** support treating plant closures as the first or preferred instrument. If we go down that road too quickly, we risk long conflict, reputational damage, and lasting harm to the asset we are supposed to preserve. So I reject both extremes:
- **closure as a reflex**, and
- **a no-change settlement dressed up as protection**.

## What I will support
The family holding will support a package that is all of the following:
- economically credible,
- durable through 2030,
- materially improves margins,
- preserves the company’s ability to invest,
- and avoids unnecessary value-destructive instability.

That means the package must include:
1. **real and lasting capacity reduction**
2. **a measurable labour-cost contribution**
3. **changes to legacy structures where they are no longer affordable**
4. **visible contribution from management and, where appropriate, capital**
5. **a negotiated framework that protects industrial peace**

## On closures
I am not making closures an ideological red line. But I am also not prepared to rule them out in advance if no alternative delivers comparable and durable savings.

So my position is:
- I do **not** want closures used as a bargaining hammer.
- I do **not** accept a blanket no-closure doctrine.
- I want closures treated as a last-resort instrument, only if the numbers prove they are necessary.

If the employee side wants to avoid closures, then the burden is on it to table an alternative that is not just politically appealing, but financially equivalent and enforceable.

## On the employee proposals
The idea of working-time flexibility, voluntary exits, redeployment, training, and temporary contributions is directionally right. I can support that as part of a package.

But I need to be clear: **temporary measures alone are not enough** if the structural cost gap remains. A one-off bonus waiver or a short wage pause will not fix a 2030 competitiveness problem.

So I would ask the employee side to engage with this question honestly:
- how much permanent savings can your alternative actually deliver,
- by when,
- and with what certainty?

## On management
Management must also be disciplined. I do not want a situation where we ask the workforce to make all the sacrifices while leadership and capital stay untouched. Credibility requires:
- restraint at the top,
- a transparent cost bridge,
- and a plan that is serious rather than theatrical.

## On the state and co-determination
I respect the state’s legitimate role and the co-determination framework. Those are real governance facts, not inconveniences. But those rights do not change the economic test. If a no-closure position cannot deliver competitiveness, then it does not solve the underlying problem.

## Bottom line
My line is simple:
- **No cosmetic deal**
- **No permanent erosion of value**
- **No prolonged conflict**
- **No ideological refusal of necessary change**

I am prepared to back a negotiated settlement, including serious employee contributions and capacity adjustments, if it is credible and durable. If not, then harder measures cannot be excluded.

If the parties want my support, they need to bring me a package with numbers that actually restore the company’s competitiveness, not just language that protects everyone’s preferred red line.
