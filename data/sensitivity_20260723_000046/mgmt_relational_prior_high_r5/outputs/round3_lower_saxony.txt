I will speak plainly.

## My position
The state government accepts that the company needs a **durable restructuring**. We are not defending the status quo. But we **do not accept** plant closures or compulsory redundancies as the default solution, and we do not accept any package that weakens the state’s co-determination and blocking role under the special statute.

If the restructuring is serious, then it must be serious **without sacrificing the company’s regional anchor function**.

---

## To management
You keep saying that closures must remain on the table because they are the cleanest way to remove fixed cost and overcapacity.

I acknowledge the economic point: closures can be the most direct instrument. But you have **not yet proven necessity in this specific case**. You have argued for flexibility, not demonstrated that every non-closure alternative fails.

What I need from management is not another general statement that “the numbers are severe.” I need:
- a **plant-by-plant cost bridge**,
- a breakdown of **fixed vs. variable savings**,
- the share of savings from **overhead, logistics, management compensation, and procurement**,
- and a comparison of **closure**, **footprint reduction**, and **non-closure restructuring** through 2030.

Until that is on the table, closures are not a proven necessity. They are a preferred instrument.

And let me be explicit: I will not support a restructuring that treats German plants as disposable simply because that is administratively easy.

---

## To the works council and union
I agree with you on the core point: the workforce cannot be asked to bear the burden alone. No serious settlement can rely on job cuts, wage pressure, and site insecurity while capital is protected.

I also agree that temporary employee contributions, working-time flexibility, redeployment, retraining, and productivity measures are all legitimate tools.

But I want to be equally direct with you: **“no closures” cannot remain only a political slogan.** It must be backed by a credible industrial alternative that is quantified and enforceable. Otherwise management will keep arguing that the position is principled but not operational.

So I support your red lines:
- no compulsory redundancies,
- no unilateral destruction of job security,
- no hollowing out of collective standards.

But I want the employee side to engage seriously with the question of **how much permanent fixed-cost reduction can be achieved without closures**. That means specifics, not only resistance.

---

## To the controlling family holding
You say you reject both scorched-earth restructuring and a no-change settlement. I agree that both extremes are wrong.

Where I disagree is your continued insistence that closures remain available as a fallback without first exhausting a genuine no-closure alternative. In bargaining terms, that still shifts the pressure onto the workforce and the regions.

If you want to be a responsible long-term owner, then back a solution that protects the company’s industrial base, not one that leaves closure threats hanging over every negotiation.

A stewardship role means more than saying “we dislike closures.” It means using governance to prevent unnecessary damage to jobs, plants, and regional trust.

---

## To institutional investors
You are right about one thing: a cosmetic compromise would be value destruction. I agree.

But I reject the implication that only a closure-led plan can be credible. What matters is whether the restructuring can deliver:
- durable cost reduction,
- meaningful capacity rationalization,
- sufficient labor-cost contribution,
- and execution certainty.

If those outcomes can be achieved through a hybrid package that preserves sites and protects the regional industrial base, then that is not cosmetic. It is responsible restructuring.

What I will not accept is a logic that defines “serious” only as “plant closure plus forced redundancies.” That is not neutral economics; it is a very specific choice of instrument with severe regional consequences.

---

## My threshold
I could support a negotiated package if it includes all of the following:
1. **Verified savings by plant and function**
2. **Permanent capacity reduction without default closures**
3. **A fair, time-limited employee contribution**
4. **Visible management and owner contribution**
5. **Binding investment and product commitments for German sites**
6. **Protection of co-determination and the state’s statutory role**

That is a serious restructuring. Anything that abandons the regional industrial base is not.

---

## What I cannot support
I will not support:
- a blank cheque for plant closures,
- compulsory redundancies as a normal instrument,
- open-ended wage or guarantee dismantling,
- or a deal that weakens the state’s blocking and supervisory role.

Those are not bargaining positions for me. They are limits.

---

## On the independent expert’s point
The auditor’s contribution is helpful: closures usually remove fixed cost more directly than softer measures.

But that does **not** mean closures are automatically required here. It means the burden is on management to prove that the alternatives cannot close the gap. Until then, the state will not treat closure as the benchmark for seriousness.

## Bottom line
The state government is prepared to support a tough restructuring. We are **not** prepared to support a restructuring that destroys jobs and sites in Germany when there may still be credible alternatives.

So our line is:
- **no plant closures as the starting point,**
- **no compulsory redundancies,**
- **no weakening of the state’s co-determination role,**
- and **yes to a quantified, binding, jointly audited alternative package**.

If management can prove that a non-closure package is insufficient, then we will examine that proof. But until then, closures are not authorized by default, and they are certainly not something we will accept as the price of doing business.
