I’ll respond directly, because the pattern is now clear: management, investors, and even the advisory voices keep treating **closures** as the benchmark and then asking us to prove a no-closure solution is “equivalent.” That framing is backwards.

## To management
You are right about one thing: this is a structural competitiveness problem and we need a durable package. I do **not** dispute that.

What I do dispute is your repeated attempt to make **plant closures and compulsory redundancies pre-authorized bargaining tools** before the alternatives are fully tested and quantified. The auditor has confirmed what we already know in principle: closures are often the most direct way to remove fixed cost. That does **not** mean they are automatically necessary here, and it certainly does not justify writing them into the settlement upfront.

Your position is still:
- “we need durable savings,”
- “temporary measures are insufficient,”
- therefore “closures must remain available.”

That is not a proof. It is a preference.

If you want us to engage seriously, then put the full plant-by-plant cost bridge on the table:
- fixed overhead,
- utilization,
- logistics,
- indirect labor,
- volume transfer options,
- one-time restructuring costs,
- and the savings from each alternative.

Until then, your closure demand remains a threat, not a demonstrated necessity.

## To the Works Council
We are aligned on the essentials:
- **no plant closures**
- **no compulsory redundancies**
- **no dismantling of job-security protections**
- **no restructuring that makes workers the sole shock absorber**

I also agree with your point that “no closures” cannot just be a slogan. It has to be backed by a concrete alternative package.

Where I want to sharpen this is: the alternative package must be **binding and enforceable**, not a soft promise that management can later reinterpret as insufficient. We need a deal with real numbers, not just industrial relations language.

## To the Regional State Government
Your support matters, and your insistence on protecting industrial capacity is justified. But I need to be equally clear: a “conditional no-closure framework” is only acceptable if the condition is **strictly substantive** and not a trapdoor that lets management come back later and reopen the same blackmail.

I can work with:
- verified savings targets,
- a joint audit,
- and a defined review mechanism.

I cannot work with:
- an arrangement where the workforce gives concessions now,
- management banks them,
- and then closures reappear later because the company keeps moving the goalposts.

If the state wants a serious no-closure settlement, it should use its influence to lock in:
- investment,
- future model allocation,
- training,
- and a real redistribution of work across sites.

## To the Controlling Family Holding
You say you reject both extremes. I agree that neither a scorched-earth restructuring nor a no-change settlement is acceptable.

But “closures as last resort” still means closures are on the table, and that matters because in bargaining terms it shifts all pressure onto the workforce. We know how that works: employees concede first, and then the “last resort” gets defined later by management.

So my answer is:
- if you truly reject scorched-earth tactics, then back a **no-closure settlement** now;
- do not leave the closure hammer hanging over the table while asking workers to trust the process.

A real stewardship role means using governance to prevent avoidable job destruction, not merely promising you dislike it.

## To Institutional Investors
You are right that a cosmetic compromise is not acceptable. I agree with that.

Where I disagree is your implication that a no-closure package is only acceptable if it is economically equivalent to closures in every respect. That is too narrow. What matters is whether the company achieves:
- durable cost relief,
- sufficient capacity rationalization,
- and a stable path to investment and margins.

The auditor is right that closures are often the deepest fixed-cost instrument. But if non-closure measures can remove enough permanent cost through:
- voluntary exits,
- shift and working-time changes,
- real overhead reduction,
- model concentration,
- and management restraint,

then they are not “cosmetic” simply because they do not shut a gate.

What I will not accept is an attempt to define “serious” only as “job destruction plus site closure.” That is not neutrality; that is a very specific ideological preference for the balance sheet over the workforce.

## The key issue management still has not answered
The auditor says closures usually deliver the deepest permanent savings. Fine. But the question here is not whether closures are useful in general.

The question is:

**Can management prove that alternatives are insufficient in this specific case, plant by plant, without simply preferring the cleanest instrument?**

Until that proof exists, we reject closures and forced redundancies outright.

## Bottom line
