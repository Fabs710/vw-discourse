I will respond directly.

## To the Works Council
You keep repeating “no closures, no forced redundancies, no breaking of guarantees” as if stating red lines makes the cost problem disappear. It doesn’t.

I accept one part of your argument: **we need a durable settlement, not a cosmetic fix**. And yes, redeployment, training, working-time flexibility, voluntary exits, and productivity measures must all be part of the package.

But I do **not** accept your central premise that every German plant must stay open and that the existing job-security framework is effectively untouchable. If German factory costs remain far above target, then preserving all structures unchanged is not protection — it is drift.

You ask for numbers. Fine: the number we need is not symbolic. We need a package that delivers **real, lasting, structural cost relief and capacity reduction**. If you want to avoid closures, then bring an alternative that is **quantified, enforceable, and comparable in effect**. So far, I have heard principles, not an equivalent solution.

Your proposal of “capacity reduction through better utilization” is not enough unless it actually removes excess fixed cost and overcapacity. If it doesn’t, then it is a delay tactic.

## To the Industrial Union
I appreciate that you are at least willing to discuss temporary wage moderation, bonuses, flexibility, and capacity changes. That is a more serious starting point than a blanket no.

But let me be equally clear: **temporary contributions alone are insufficient**. We are not dealing with a short-term squeeze. This is a structural competitiveness gap. A one-off bonus waiver, a temporary solidarity contribution, or a limited wage pause does not fix a cost base that is out of line through 2030.

You say management has not shown the full cost bridge. I’m willing to discuss the bridge in detail, but the direction is not in doubt:
- overcapacity has to come down,
- labour costs have to come down materially,
- and the result must be durable enough to restore margins.

You also say closures are “a threat.” No — they are an option that remains necessary if the alternative cannot deliver the same savings with the same certainty. That is not theatre. That is reality.

## To the Regional State Government
I understand the political constraints, but I will not pretend that political discomfort is a substitute for economic viability.

You say closures should not be the starting point. Agreed: they are not my ideological starting point. But they are part of the toolbox because the company needs hard, durable savings. If you use your blocking position simply to prevent any structural adjustment, then you are not protecting industrial capacity — you are protecting uncompetitive capacity.

You ask that alternatives be exhausted first. That is reasonable in principle. But “exhausted” means genuinely capable of delivering the required savings, not merely politically more comfortable. So far, no one has shown a package that matches the scale and permanence of what we need without touching the cost base in a serious way.

## To the Controlling Family Holding
You support a real turnaround, which I welcome. But support for “durability” cannot mean soft-pedalling the hard choices.

If the family holding wants long-term value, then it must back a package that actually restores it. That means:
- capacity reduction,
- durable labour-cost relief,
- and, if necessary, site adjustments.

I agree that a scorched-earth approach would be bad. I am not arguing for chaos. I am arguing for a restructuring strong enough to matter.

## To the Institutional Investors
You are right on one point: **a deal that leaves the cost base uncompetitive is unacceptable**. I agree.

Where I part company with you is this: you speak as if any measure short of closures is automatically “cosmetic.” That is not true. There are non-closure instruments that can deliver real savings — but only if they are deep, binding, and permanent. I am open to them.

What I am not open to is a settlement built on:
- vague future promises,
- temporary gestures,
- or political assurances that do not change the economics.

## Management’s minimum acceptable package
Let me put our position on the table plainly.

A settlement must include:
1. **A material, durable reduction in overcapacity**
2. **A substantial labour-cost contribution**
3. **Binding changes to legacy structures that are no longer affordable**
4. **A package that restores medium-term return on sales**
5. **Clear room for investment in future products and technology through 2030**

If the employee side can deliver this without closures, I am prepared to examine it seriously. But if the package leaves the cost base structurally too high, then I will not pretend it is sufficient just because it avoids confrontation.

## What I will not accept
I will not accept:
- a “no closures” rule that is absolute and unconditional,
- compulsory guarantees that freeze the company in its current cost structure,
- a temporary contribution dressed up as a structural solution,
- or an arrangement that leaves us unable to invest in the future.

## Bottom line
I am not here to negotiate slogans. I am here to restore competitiveness.

That means:
- I will support a negotiated solution if it is **deep, durable, and economically credible**.
- I will not support a compromise that protects every legacy claim while leaving the company uncompetitive.
- And I will keep closures and compulsory measures on the table until I see a package that truly matches the scale of the crisis.

If the other sides want to avoid that outcome, then they need to come back with something more than red lines. They need to come back with numbers that work.
