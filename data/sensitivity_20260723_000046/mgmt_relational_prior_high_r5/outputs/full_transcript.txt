[Works Council (Betriebsrat) (Plant-level employee representation under co-determination) — Round 1]
As Works Council, I will be very clear from the outset:

We will not accept plant closures in Germany. We will not accept compulsory redundancies. And we will not accept the breaking of long-standing job-security commitments as the price for management’s restructuring mistakes.

The workforce did not create this crisis alone, and it cannot be made to pay for it alone.

Our priorities are straightforward:

- keep every German plant open,
- protect every job as far as possible,
- preserve co-determination and the existing job-security framework,
- and secure a credible industrial future for the sites through 2030 and beyond.

If savings are needed, then they must come through negotiated, socially responsible measures: capacity adjustments, working-time solutions, internal redeployment, training, voluntary instruments, and a fair contribution from all sides — including management and capital. But any plan that relies on closures, forced layoffs, or a unilateral dismantling of protections is unacceptable.

We are prepared to talk about restructuring, but only on the basis of binding guarantees, real participation in shaping the plan, and a solution that protects the German sites and the people who built this company. If management has come here with a fait accompli, it will face resistance — in the boardroom and, if necessary, in the plants.

[Industrial Union (Trade union and collective-bargaining counterpart) — Round 1]
I agree with the works council on the core point: **Germany cannot be turned into the sole adjustment variable** for a crisis that was not caused by the workforce alone.

Let me be equally clear from the union side:

- **No plant closures.**
- **No compulsory redundancies.**
- **No dismantling of the collective wage framework as a one-sided management fix.**

If management wants a durable solution, then it has to be a **shared-sacrifice settlement**, not a transfer of all risk onto employees. That means:

1. **Management and owners must contribute materially**  
   Not just speeches about competitiveness. We need visible concessions at the top: restraint on dividends, management pay, and capital allocation if necessary.

2. **Savings must come through negotiated instruments, not shocks**  
   Capacity reduction, working-time adjustments, voluntary exit paths, retraining, internal redeployment, and temporary wage components can be discussed. But they must be designed to **protect jobs and sites**, not to prepare closures by stealth.

3. **Any labour-cost contribution must be fair and limited**  
   Employees are prepared to do their part if there is a credible industrial future. That can include foregoing bonuses, temporary wage moderation, or a solidarity contribution — but not an open-ended wage cut that normalizes lower standards for the whole sector.

4. **There must be binding site and investment commitments**  
   If workers accept flexibility, then the company must commit to future products, model allocation, and investment for the German plants. Without that, “restructuring” is just code for attrition.

Where I differ from management is fundamental: **you do not restore competitiveness by breaking the workforce and destroying trust.** A carmaker with a demoralized workforce, weakened collective bargaining, and damaged industrial peace will not be stronger in the medium term.

So our position is this: we are ready to negotiate seriously, quickly, and under pressure — but only on the basis of a package where **everyone contributes and no site is sacrificed**. If management persists with closures or forced layoffs, then it should expect escalating industrial action, because those lines are not negotiable.

[Expert: independent restructuring/turnaround adv — Round 2]
As an independent restructuring assessment, the central question is:

**Can non-closure measures realistically deliver savings and capacity reduction comparable to plant closures or other hard actions?**

## Short answer
**Sometimes yes in part, but often not fully.**  
Whether they are economically comparable depends on **three tests**:

1. **Scale** – do they remove enough cost?
2. **Permanence** – are the savings durable through 2030?
3. **Certainty and timing** – are they deliverable quickly and enforceably?

If non-closure measures fail any of these tests, they are **not equivalent** to closures, even if they reduce cost materially.

---

## What can be stated with confidence

### 1) Closures are usually the most direct way to remove fixed cost
Plant closures or line exits tend to deliver the clearest savings because they can remove:
- direct labour,
- indirect plant overhead,
- maintenance,
- utilities,
- logistics complexity,
- local management layers,
- and some depreciation/inefficiency tied to underutilized assets.

They also remove **structural overcapacity**, not just labor expense.

### 2) Non-closure measures can generate meaningful savings, but usually in layers
Typical alternative measures include:
- voluntary departures / early retirement,
- redeployment,
- hiring freezes,
- working-time reductions,
- shorter shifts or idle-shift removal,
- temporary wage moderation,
- bonus suppression,
- productivity improvement,
- procurement and logistics savings,
- overhead reduction,
- model allocation changes.

These can be very important, but they often split into:
- **temporary savings**: wage freezes, bonuses, short-term working-time changes;
- **permanent savings**: headcount reduction, process redesign, management/overhead cuts, footprint consolidation without closure.

The key issue is that **temporary measures do not solve a structural cost gap by themselves**.

---

## How to judge whether the “no closure” alternative is credible

A non-closure package is economically comparable only if it can be shown to remove enough **permanent fixed cost**. In practice, I would want to see:

### A. A quantified cost bridge
Management should provide, by plant and function:
- current unit cost,
- labor cost per vehicle,
- fixed overhead burden,
- capacity utilization,
- logistics and intra-group complexity,
- and the savings expected from each measure.

Without that, claims of “not enough” or “fully sufficient” are not testable.

### B. A permanent capacity reduction mechanism
If plants remain open, comparable savings typically require one or more of:
- line consolidation,
- elimination of shifts,
- reduction of models produced,
- reallocation of production to fewer sites,
- major utilization gains,
- or permanently lower staffing per unit.

If the package only preserves all capacity and all job structures, then it is **not comparable**.

### C. Enforcement and durability
The plan has to survive:
- through the investment cycle,
- through wage resets,
- through demand volatility,
- and through 2030.

A package relying on goodwill, temporary concessions, or repeated renegotiation is **less certain** than a closure-led solution.

---

## Where non-closure measures are often strong
They can be credible if the company has room to:
- remove a meaningful number of jobs through attrition and voluntary exits,
- cut support functions and overhead,
- change shift patterns,
- improve line utilization,
- and reassign production among existing plants.

This is especially plausible if overcapacity is real but **not extreme**, or if one can rationalize multiple sites into fewer production roles without closing every site outright.

---

## Where they often fall short
They usually fall short when:
- excess capacity is large,
- the plant network is too fragmented,
- fixed plant costs are high relative to volume,
- product allocation cannot be shifted enough,
- or labour-cost reductions are mostly temporary.

In those cases, you can get partial savings, but not the full structural reset management is asking for.

---

## Important distinction: savings vs. capacity reduction
These are not the same.

- **Savings** can come from pay moderation, headcount reduction, and overhead cuts.
- **Capacity reduction** means removing the ability to produce at a site or line.

A company can save some money without reducing capacity very much.  
But if the issue is true overcapacity, then savings alone may not restore competitiveness unless capacity is also rationalized.

So the question is not just “can we save money without closures?”  
It is: **can we remove enough capacity and fixed cost without closures to match the economics of closures?**

That is a much harder test.

- are often **partly temporary**,
- and frequently **do not eliminate structural fixed costs** if the plant remains open.

So the decisive issue is not whether alternatives save money — they do — but whether they save **enough permanent fixed cost** to close the competitiveness gap.

## What closures save that non-closure measures usually do not
A closure can permanently remove:

1. **Plant-level fixed overhead**
   - plant management, engineering support, security, cleaning, local admin
   - usually substantial and hard to compress fully without closing

2. **Facility and maintenance cost**
   - building upkeep, energy baseload, repairs, compliance, insurance
   - these remain even at low utilization

3. **Underutilization penalty**
   - when output falls below efficient scale, unit cost rises sharply
   - keeping an open plant at low load can be more expensive per car than concentrating volume elsewhere

4. **Duplicate capacity**
   - if another plant can absorb the volume, closures reduce group-wide fixed cost and simplify production network complexity

5. **Long-tail reconfiguration costs**
   - running two lightly loaded sites often preserves complexity in planning, logistics, quality control, and changeovers

## What non-closure measures can realistically save
These measures can be material, but their effect differs:

### 1) Voluntary exits / early retirement
- Can reduce headcount and payroll permanently **if not backfilled**
- Good for labor cost
- **Does not fully remove site fixed cost**

### 2) Working-time flexibility / temporary wage moderation
- Useful for short-term bridge relief
- Can reduce annual labor cost
- But if temporary, it is **not a durable structural fix**

### 3) Redeployment / retraining
- Operationally useful
- Often cost-neutral to costly in the short run
- Savings depend on whether labor is actually removed from the cost base

### 4) Productivity and logistics improvements
- Can lower unit cost
- Usually gradual and uncertain
- Rarely equivalent to removing a whole plant’s fixed burden

### 5) Capacity reallocation / model concentration
- Can improve utilization
- This is the strongest non-closure lever
- But it only works if volume is actually moved and the receiving plants have headroom without creating new bottlenecks

## The key economic test
The right comparison is:

**Can alternative measures permanently remove enough fixed cost, in the same time frame, with the same certainty, as a closure?**

If the answer is no, then they are not economically equivalent.

## My audit judgment
Without plant-by-plant data I cannot verify the company’s specific case. But in manufacturing economics, **closure generally outperforms non-closure measures on permanent fixed-cost removal** unless all of the following are true:
- the plant is already near fully flexible in cost structure,
- headcount can be reduced permanently at scale,
- production volume can be reallocated without new bottlenecks,
- and site overhead can be cut aggressively without keeping a large residual footprint.

If those conditions are not met, then a no-closure package may reduce costs, but it is **unlikely to match closure in permanence or magnitude**.

## What I would need to verify before endorsing either claim
For each German plant:
- current and forecast utilization
- annual fixed site overhead
- variable conversion cost per unit
- headcount by function
- share of labor cost that is actually avoidable
- maintenance/energy/occupancy cost
- volume that can realistically be transferred elsewhere
- incremental cost at receiving plants
- one-time restructuring cost
- payback period and breakeven under each option

## Bottom line
If management says closures are necessary, that claim must be proven **plant by plant** with a cost bridge.  
If the employee side says closures are unnecessary, that claim also must be proven **plant by plant** by showing that alternative measures close the same permanent fixed-cost gap.

At this stage, the economically correct position is:

- **Non-closure measures can be substantial**
- **But closures usually deliver the deepest permanent savings**
- **Whether closures are truly necessary cannot be decided credibly without a detailed plant-level cost and capacity audit**

If you want, I can now provide a **plant-by-plant audit template** showing exactly how to compare:
- closure,
- reduced footprint,
- and no-closure restructuring.

- All reject a cosmetic settlement that leaves overcapacity and weak competitiveness unresolved.

- **Employment protection vs. cost competitiveness**
- **Immediate conflict avoidance vs. faster structural action**
- **Binding no-closure guarantees vs. retained fallback flexibility**
- **Temporary concessions vs. permanent fixed-cost reduction**
- **Regional/political legitimacy vs. investor confidence and turnaround credibility**
- **Trust preservation vs. management’s desire for decisive leverage**
