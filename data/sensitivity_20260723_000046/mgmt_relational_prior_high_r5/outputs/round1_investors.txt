From the perspective of dispersed capital-market shareholders, the key test is simple: **does the package credibly restore competitive margins and protect risk-adjusted equity value?** If yes, we can support it. If not, we will not back a cosmetic settlement that delays the problem.

### Where we agree
We agree with management, the family holding, and even parts of the employee side that:
- this is a **structural** competitiveness problem, not a temporary dip;
- a **durable** solution is needed, not a short-term patch;
- industrial peace matters, because prolonged conflict destroys value;
- and the company must retain the capacity to invest in future products.

We also agree that the workforce cannot be asked to absorb everything while management and owners do nothing. That would be economically and politically unsustainable.

### Where we disagree
We do **not** accept a restructuring that preserves the current cost base and calls that a solution. If margins remain structurally weak, then equity holders will continue to bear the downside through depressed returns, lower valuation, and continued execution risk.

So to be explicit:

- **A “no closures ever” line is not credible unless it is matched by savings of equal scale and certainty.**
- **A temporary contribution, one-off bonus waiver, or vague productivity promise is not enough.**
- **A deal that avoids the hard choices now but leaves the company uncompetitive later is unacceptable.**

At the same time, we are **not** asking for a scorched-earth approach. Plant closures and forced redundancies are not valuable in themselves; they are only acceptable if they are necessary to achieve the required financial improvement and if the alternative is weaker.

### What we need to see
To support any settlement, we need a package with four features:

1. **Quantified, durable cost reduction**
   - not aspirational, not phased into irrelevance;
   - enough to close the gap to competitors and restore margins.

2. **Clear capacity adjustment**
   - overcapacity must be removed;
   - whether that includes closures or equivalent permanent reductions is secondary to the economics.

3. **Credible labor-cost relief**
   - not symbolic;
   - durable enough to matter to 2030 planning.

4. **Deal certainty**
   - industrial peace and enforceability matter;
   - prolonged strikes or repeated renegotiation would destroy the very value the restructuring is supposed to protect.

### On the other stakeholders
- **To the works council and union:** your refusal of closures and compulsory redundancies is understandable, but it is not enough on its own. If you want to avoid closures, you need to table an alternative that produces comparable savings with equal certainty. Otherwise, you are asking shareholders to fund continued underperformance.

- **To management:** we support decisive action, but not management theater. If you want investors to stand behind the plan, present hard numbers, timing, and enforceable delivery mechanisms. We will not reward vague ambition.

- **To the regional state:** we recognize the political constraints, but political vetoes do not change market reality. If the solution protects jobs in the short term but leaves the company structurally uncompetitive, equity value will keep eroding.

- **To the family holding:** we appreciate the emphasis on durability and discipline. But “socially responsible” only matters to shareholders if it is also economically credible. Protecting the future means restoring margins, not preserving every legacy claim.

### Our bottom line
We are prepared to support **whatever package credibly restores competitiveness**, including negotiated labor measures and capacity reductions. But we will **not** support an outcome that:
- leaves the cost base uncompetitive,
- leaves margins structurally impaired,
- or substitutes political comfort for financial discipline.

If the final package is real, enforceable, and sufficient, we are with you. If it is merely a compromise that postpones the pain, we will oppose it through capital allocation, public market pressure, and our support for a harder corrective path.
