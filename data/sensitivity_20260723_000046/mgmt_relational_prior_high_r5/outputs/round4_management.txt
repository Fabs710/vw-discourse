## To the works council
You keep repeating “prove necessity first” as if that alone settles the issue. It does not.

I **agree** that we need a plant-by-plant cost bridge and that a cosmetic deal would be useless. That is exactly why I support the independent audit. But I **disagree** with your attempt to make “no closures” the default before the audit has even tested whether the non-closure package can remove enough **permanent fixed cost**.

The auditor has already said the key point: closures usually remove the deepest structural cost and excess capacity. That is not a slogan. It is industrial reality. If your alternative package cannot match that on permanence, scale, and timing, then it is not a real substitute.

So no: I will not accept a blanket rule that every German plant must stay open regardless of economics. That would lock in overcapacity and leave the company underpowered through 2030.

## To the union
We are aligned on one thing: temporary gestures are not enough. I also agree that a negotiated deal needs binding terms and not vague promises.

Where I **disagree** is your categorical refusal of closures and compulsory redundancies. That position ignores the actual cost problem we are trying to solve. If the company remains structurally too expensive and underutilized, then saying “no closures ever” is not responsible bargaining. It is demanding that the company absorb the entire burden while preserving every site.

You say closures are pressure, not proof. Fine. That is why we are asking for the audit. But pressure cuts both ways: strikes and hard red lines also create pressure. The question is not who is more forceful. The question is whether the package achieves durable competitiveness.

## To the regional state government
I respect the political responsibility you carry, and I agree that a scorched-earth approach would destroy value. But I **do not** accept the idea that the state can simply block hard structural measures and still expect the economics to fix themselves.

You say closures are not proven necessary. Correct — not yet. That is exactly why we are demanding the evidence. But do not confuse “not yet proven” with “must be ruled out.” If the audit shows that non-closure measures leave too much residual fixed cost, then the company must have the ability to act.

A political veto is not a business model.

## To the controlling family holding
You say you reject both extremes. I agree with that. But “closures only as last resort” is not a neutral position if “last resort” is defined after concessions have already been handed over.

I need a clear answer from you: will you support a package that is economically sound even if it includes closures or compulsory redundancies where the numbers require them? Or will you insist on preserving every site regardless of the evidence?

If it is the latter, then you are not arbitrating the turnaround — you are constraining it.

## To the institutional investors
You are right to insist on durability, fixed-cost reduction, and execution certainty. I agree completely.

Where I disagree is on the implication that only closure-led restructuring can be credible. I am open on the mechanism. I am **not** open on weak economics.

If the alternative package truly removes enough permanent cost and capacity, then fine — it will stand up to scrutiny. But if it doesn’t, then investors should not ask management to pretend that it does.

## Management’s position
Here is the point we are not moving on:

1. We need **structural, permanent cost reduction**.  
2. We need **real capacity reduction**, not just postponement.  
3. We need a package that restores competitiveness and funds investment to 2030.  
4. We are open to alternatives — but only if they are **economically equivalent in substance**, not in rhetoric.

That means:
- voluntary exits,
- early retirement,
- redeployment,
- working-time flexibility,
- temporary and limited wage moderation,
- management restraint,
- productivity gains,
- and model/volume concentration

are all possible components.

But if, after audit, they do **not** close the structural gap, then closures and compulsory redundancies remain necessary instruments. I will not take them off the table in advance.

## Where I stand after hearing everyone
I hear the social and political objections. I do not dismiss them. But none of them answers the core business question:

**How do we get to a competitive cost base, with enough permanent fixed-cost reduction, on a durable path through 2030?**

Until the alternative package can answer that in numbers, closures remain part of the toolbox.
