From the capital markets’ perspective, the discussion is still too abstract in one key respect: **we need a package that can credibly close the margin gap, not just a statement of intent.** On that basis, I agree with some of what has been said, but I am not persuaded by a “no closures, no compulsory redundancies” doctrine unless it is paired with savings that are clearly equivalent in scale, timing, and certainty.

### Where I agree
- **Management is right** that this is a structural competitiveness problem, not a temporary dip.
- **The workforce representatives are right** that a purely punitive, confrontation-first approach would destroy value.
- **The state is right** that a prolonged fight or a cosmetic deal would be damaging.
- **The family holding is right** that the solution must be durable and not theatrical.

### Where I disagree
#### To the works council and union
I respect the social red lines, but from an equity-holder point of view I cannot support a package that simply preserves all German capacity, all legacy guarantees, and all existing cost structures while asking shareholders to tolerate continued underperformance.

Your alternative is directionally acceptable only if it is **measurable and sufficient**. Right now I still do not see that. “Working-time flexibility,” “voluntary exits,” “redeployment,” and “temporary contributions” may help, but unless you can show:
- how much permanent fixed-cost reduction they deliver,
- which plants or lines are actually made redundant in economic terms,
- and why the result is durable through 2030,

then this remains a partial mitigation, not a real restructuring.

#### To management
I do not support management using closures as a negotiating threat if the real objective is simply to extract concessions. That destroys trust and raises execution risk. But I also will not support management if it comes back with vague references to “material and lasting” change without a transparent bridge.

If you want capital markets behind the plan, you need to show:
- the full cost gap,
- the exact savings by measure,
- the timing of delivery,
- and the enforceability of the package.

“Closures remain on the table” is not a financial plan. It is only acceptable if it is genuinely the last resort after a credible alternative has been tested and found insufficient.

#### To the state
We understand the political constraints, but a blocking position cannot be used to lock in an uncompetitive structure. If the state vetoes every hard option without underwriting an equivalent economic solution, then it is not protecting value; it is eroding it.

#### To the family holding
I appreciate the rejection of scorched earth. But “supporting a turnaround” has to mean supporting whatever actually restores margins. If the family wants to avoid closures, it should help force a real, quantified alternative through governance — not just call for seriousness in general terms.

### Our threshold
We can support a negotiated solution if it meets all four of these conditions:

1. **Durable cost reduction**
   - not one-off savings;
   - not temporary wage optics;
   - real structural improvement in the cost base.

2. **Capacity adjustment that removes overcapacity**
   - whether via closures, line exits, or equivalent permanent reductions;
   - the mechanism matters less than the economics.

3. **Credible labor-cost contribution**
   - meaningful enough to matter to margins;
   - limited to what is necessary, but not symbolic.

4. **Deal certainty**
   - enforceable commitments;
   - low strike risk;
   - no endless renegotiation.

### My bottom line
- I **do not** support a cosmetic compromise.
- I **do not** support preserving the current cost base and calling it restructuring.
- I **do** support a deal that restores competitive margins, even if it requires hard capacity decisions.
- I **can** accept a no-closure outcome only if it produces savings and certainty comparable to closures.

If the employee side and state can produce a quantified package that truly matches the scale of the problem, we are open. If not, then closures and deeper structural measures remain economically justified, and we will not support a settlement that leaves equity value impaired.
