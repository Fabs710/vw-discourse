From a restructuring and turnaround perspective, the key issue is not whether closures are “good” or “bad,” but whether the non-closure package can deliver **equivalent permanent fixed-cost and capacity reduction**, with acceptable execution certainty, by 2030.

## My classification

### Measures that can be genuinely permanent
These can reduce recurring cost if they are real, sizable, and enforceable:
- **Permanent headcount reduction** through voluntary exits plus non-replacement
- **Early retirement / attrition programs** where the cost base is structurally reduced
- **Layer and overhead elimination** in administration, engineering support, and central functions
- **Model concentration / volume reallocation** to fewer plants or fewer variants
- **Shift reduction or line simplification** if it permanently lowers labor and overhead absorption
- **Footprint consolidation short of full closure**, if lines, buildings, and support functions are actually taken out of service
- **Management compensation restraint**, but only as a contribution, not a core savings lever
- **Procurement, logistics, and complexity reduction** if they structurally lower recurring conversion cost

### Measures that are usually temporary or only partially durable
These may help liquidity or bridge a period, but are not a full substitute for closure:
- **Temporary wage moderation**
- **Bonus restraint**
- **Working-time reductions that can be reversed later**
- **Short-term partial layoffs**
- **One-off cost deferrals**
- **Temporary state or owner support**
- **Inventory or production pull-forward**
- **Accounting-based savings without physical capacity reduction**

## What closures do that alternatives often do not
Closures usually provide three things at once:
1. **Permanent removal of fixed cost**
2. **Clear capacity reduction**
3. **High execution certainty**

That is why management often sees them as the benchmark. But that does **not** mean they are automatically necessary. A non-closure package can be economically comparable **only if** it removes enough recurring cost and capacity, and does so in a way that is credible under the operating plan.

## What would make a non-closure package credible
To compare fairly with closures, the company would need a plant-by-plant bridge showing:

- **Current fixed cost by site**
- **Savings from voluntary exits and non-replacement**
- **Savings from footprint reduction and line simplification**
- **Residual overhead after reorganization**
- **Transferability of volume to other plants**
- **One-time restructuring costs**
- **Payback period**
- **Margin impact through 2030**
- **Sensitivity if volumes fall further**

Without that, nobody can say whether the non-closure package is truly equivalent or only politically preferable.

## My practical assessment
In most automotive restructurings:
- **Non-closure measures can be meaningful**
- but they often **do not fully match the cost certainty and depth** of a plant closure unless they are very large and accompanied by real volume concentration

So the critical question is whether the proposed alternatives are:
- large enough in scale,
- durable enough in effect,
- and enforceable enough in practice.

## What I would need to conclude more firmly
I would need:
1. Plant-by-plant P&L or cost allocation
2. Utilization and capacity data by line/site
3. A quantified alternative package with timing
4. The one-time cost of each option
5. Forecast demand through 2030
6. Contractual details on employee contributions and reversibility

## Bottom line
- **Permanent**: headcount reduction, overhead removal, volume concentration, footprint consolidation, and some compensation restraint.
- **Temporary**: wage moderation, bonus restraint, working-time cuts, and deferrals.
- **Comparable to closure** only if the permanent measures remove enough fixed cost and excess capacity to reach the same post-restructuring economics.
