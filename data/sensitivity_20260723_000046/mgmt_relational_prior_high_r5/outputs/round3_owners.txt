I will be direct.

## Where I agree
I agree with management, the investors, and the independent auditor on one central point: **this is a structural competitiveness problem, not a short-term hiccup**. The company does need a durable restructuring. We cannot preserve the current cost base and expect long-term value to recover.

I also agree with the works council, the union, and the state that **a scorched-earth conflict would destroy value**. A prolonged industrial confrontation, reputational damage, and loss of trust would be bad stewardship and bad governance.

So the common ground is real:  
- the status quo is unacceptable,  
- industrial peace matters,  
- and the solution must be durable through 2030.

## Where I disagree
I do **not** support a blanket “no closures, no compulsory redundancies” doctrine as a settled outcome.

That position may be understandable politically, but it is not enough on its own to restore competitiveness. If the company remains structurally overcapacity and materially uncompetitive, then ruling out all hard measures in advance is simply another way of preserving the problem.

At the same time, I also do **not** support closures as the first resort or as a bargaining hammer. If management uses closure threats too early, it risks exactly the instability and trust erosion that would destroy value. So I reject both extremes:
- closure as reflex,
- and a no-change settlement disguised as protection.

## On management
Management is right to insist on a package that is **material, permanent, and enforceable**. I support that principle.

But I also want to be clear: management has not yet fully proven that closures are necessary in this specific case. The auditor confirmed that closures are often the strongest instrument for removing fixed cost and overcapacity, but “often” is not “always,” and “possible” is not “necessary.”

So management should stop speaking in generalities and bring the actual bridge:
- plant-by-plant cost base,
- fixed vs. variable savings,
- utilization,
- logistics,
- indirect labor,
- one-time restructuring costs,
- and the comparative effect of each option through 2030.

That is the standard I will use.

## On the works council and union
I understand and respect the insistence on:
- no compulsory redundancies,
- no breaking of collective standards,
- strong job-security protections,
- and a genuine industrial future for German sites.

Those are legitimate concerns.

But I do **not** accept that the workforce can simply veto every hard adjustment while expecting the owners to absorb a structurally weaker business forever. That is not stewardship; it is drift.

Your alternative package is directionally better than a closure-first approach:
- working-time flexibility,
- voluntary exits,
- redeployment,
- retraining,
- temporary labor-cost contributions,
- productivity improvements,
- and management restraint.

However, it still needs to answer the hard question: **how much permanent fixed cost does it remove, and with what certainty?** Temporary relief is not enough if the structural cost gap remains. If you want closures off the table, then show a package that really closes the gap.

## On the regional state
I respect the state’s role and the special statute. I also respect the state’s legitimate concern for regional employment and industrial capacity.

But I do not accept the idea that political veto power should be used to freeze the network in place regardless of economics. If the state blocks every difficult adjustment without helping to underwrite a credible alternative, it is not protecting value; it is delaying the problem.

That said, I am open to a serious hybrid settlement if it is verified, enforceable, and preserves the company’s ability to invest and compete.

## On the investors
You are right that a cosmetic compromise is unacceptable. I agree with that.

But I will not endorse a settlement that equates “serious” only with closures and compulsory redundancies. The mechanism matters less than the economics, but the economics must be real:
- permanent cost reduction,
- actual capacity rationalization,
- and certainty of delivery.

If non-closure measures can do that, then they deserve serious consideration. If they cannot, then harder measures remain available. That is the correct governance test.

## On the independent auditor
The auditor’s point is useful: closures usually remove fixed cost more directly than softer measures, and non-closure packages often fail on permanence or scale.

That does not settle this case. It tells us what must be proven:
- either the non-closure package removes enough permanent fixed cost,
- or it does not.

So I support the call for a plant-by-plant audit and a scenario comparison through 2030.

## My position
Here is the family holding’s position, clearly:

### I will support
1. A **serious audited review** of plant-level costs and capacity  
2. A **negotiated restructuring package** if it is durable and credible  
3. **Voluntary exits, redeployment, retraining, and working-time flexibility**  
4. **Meaningful employee contributions**, if time-limited and fair  
5. **Visible management restraint and capital-side contribution**  
6. **Binding commitments on investment and future product allocation**

### I will not support
1. A **blanket no-closure rule**  
2. A **blanket no-compulsory-redundancy rule** as an absolute without fallback  
3. A **cosmetic or temporary package** that leaves the cost base structurally wrong  
4. A deal that **permanently impairs enterprise value** or our governance control  
5. A prolonged conflict that destroys trust and value

## Bottom line
I am not choosing between “jobs” and “value.” That is the wrong framing. I am choosing between:
- a **real restructuring that restores competitiveness**, and
- a **politically comfortable arrangement that delays the erosion**.

I will back the former, not the latter.

If the employee side wants closures off the table, it must deliver a quantified alternative that is genuinely comparable in permanence, scale, and certainty. If it cannot, then management and shareholders must retain the ability to act.

That is where I stand.
