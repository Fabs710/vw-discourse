We have now reached the point where the positions are clear enough to judge them against reality.

## Where I agree

I agree with management, investors, and the specialists on the central diagnosis: **the company needs permanent structural change**. Temporary relief alone will not restore margins, and a cosmetic settlement would be value-destructive.

I also agree with the works council, the union, and the state on one crucial point: **a one-sided confrontation would damage the company’s value, legitimacy, and operational stability**. That would be bad governance and bad ownership.

So yes: change is necessary, and yes: conflict for its own sake would be unacceptable.

## Where I disagree

### 1) With the employee side: no blanket immunity
I do **not** accept a blanket rule excluding plant closures and compulsory redundancies in advance.

That is not a credible restructuring position if the network truly has structural overcapacity. If every site is protected absolutely, the company may end up preserving the problem instead of solving it.

I understand the concern about trust and social stability. But trust cannot be bought by pretending that hard trade-offs do not exist. If the alternatives are insufficient, then closures and headcount measures cannot be removed from consideration by declaration.

### 2) With management: no closure-first reflex
At the same time, I do **not** support treating closures and forced redundancies as the starting point.

That would be too blunt, too risky, and likely to provoke the very instability that destroys value. Management must not assume that the fastest accounting answer is the best ownership answer. It may be the fastest way to a fight.

### 3) With the state: preservation is not enough by itself
I respect the state’s role and regional responsibility, but preserving every current structure is not a strategy. A hollowed-out company is no service to the region either. If we protect sites without restoring competitiveness, we simply delay the erosion.

## My position

I will support a **serious, durable restructuring** that restores competitiveness and protects long-term value.

I will **not** support:
- a cosmetic no-closure compromise that leaves the cost base intact,
- a closure-driven shock strategy that destroys trust and stability,
- or a settlement that permanently impairs enterprise value.

## What I need to see

The debate now has to move from principle to proof.

If the employee side wants to avoid closures and forced redundancies, then the package must show:
1. **how much permanent cost comes out**
2. **how much permanent capacity comes out**
3. **how the result is enforced**
4. **why it is enough to restore margins through 2030**

If that cannot be shown, then no amount of insistence that closures are undesirable will make the problem disappear.

## What I could live with

I am open to a **hybrid, site-preserving restructuring** if it is real, not symbolic.

That means:
- permanent site specialization,
- elimination of a full shift or equivalent durable capacity reduction where justified,
- voluntary exits and natural attrition,
- redeployment and retraining,
- working-time flexibility,
- wage and bonus moderation,
- management overhead cuts,
- and a measurable owner contribution.

If that package truly closes the economic gap, then I have no ideological need to insist on closures.

But if it does **not** close the gap, then closures remain available. I will not pre-emptively rule them out and call that stewardship.

## On the guarantees

I do **not** support a blanket, unconditional guarantee through 2030 for every site and every job.

What I can support is a **conditional framework**:
- no forced redundancies for a defined period,
- binding milestones on savings and capacity reduction,
- site specialization and investment commitments,
- and a governance trigger if the assumptions fail.

That gives the workforce security without freezing the company in place.

## To the works council and union

You are right that workers should not be the sole adjustment variable. I agree.

But your red line cannot be: “nothing permanent, no closures, no forced layoffs, and full security regardless of economics.” That is not a restructuring; that is a refusal to confront the scale of the problem.

If you want me to support a no-closure path, then it must be **permanent**, **quantified**, and **enforceable**. Temporary gestures are not enough.

## To management

You have the right to insist on economic reality. But I expect discipline:
- no escalation for its own sake,
- no bargaining strategy built on fear,
- no assumption that labour will accept pain without governance-level burden sharing.

If you want ownership support, bring a package that is tough enough to matter and stable enough to hold.

## To the specialists

Your work has confirmed the important point: non-closure solutions can work, but only if they are substantial and durable. I accept that.

What I do **not** accept is any attempt to turn that into a promise that closures are never necessary under any circumstances. That is not finance; that is wishful thinking.

## My governance line

As controlling shareholder, my test is simple:

- protect long-term value,
- restore competitiveness,
- avoid value-destructive instability,
- preserve governance control,
- and ensure the burden is shared fairly.

I will support a real restructuring.  
I will not support paralysis.  
I will not support theatre.

- closure-heavy,
- hybrid site-specialization,
- and no-closure scenarios,
