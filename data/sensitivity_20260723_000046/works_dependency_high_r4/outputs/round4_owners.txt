We are now at the point where the discussion must become concrete, not rhetorical.

## My position

I agree with management, investors, and the specialists on the diagnosis: **the company needs permanent structural change**.  
I also agree with the works council, the union, and the state that **a one-sided shock would be value-destructive and unstable**.

What I do **not** accept is a settlement that preserves the current cost base by calling it compromise.  
And I do **not** accept a closure-first reflex either.

The test is simple:

> Can we restore competitiveness with a package that delivers **permanent savings, real capacity reduction, and enforceability** without destroying trust and governance stability?

If yes, I am open to it.  
If no, then closures and deeper structural measures remain available.

## Where I agree

### With management
You are right that temporary concessions are not enough.  
You are right that the company needs durable fixed-cost reduction and a credible path back to margins.

### With the works council and union
You are right that the workforce cannot be treated as the sole adjustment variable.  
You are also right that a destructive conflict would damage value and production stability.

### With the state
You are right that regional industrial strength matters and that closures are not the preferred answer.

### With the specialists
You are right that the key issue is not slogans, but whether the package can match the economics of the closure case through permanent measures.

## Where I disagree

### 1) With the employee side: a blanket no-closure commitment is too rigid
I will not support a blanket rule that excludes closures and forced redundancies in advance regardless of economics.

That would amount to freezing the problem rather than solving it.  
If the non-closure package cannot remove enough permanent cost and capacity, then the company would be left weaker for longer.

### 2) With management: closure is not the first answer
At the same time, I do not support using closures as the default bargaining threat.

If the company can get the same economic result through site specialization, shift loss, voluntary exits, overhead cuts, and labour-cost moderation, that is preferable. It protects value better than a confrontation that destroys trust.

### 3) With the idea that a “hybrid” can be purely cosmetic
I agree with the works council on one point: **closure by attrition is unacceptable**.

A deal that strips a plant of product, shifts, and future role while pretending the site is preserved would not be a serious solution.  
That would be a delayed closure in substance.

## What I need to see

Before I would support a no-closure path as sufficient, I need a **quantified site-by-site comparison** showing:

1. which German sites can lose a full shift or equivalent durable capacity without losing strategic viability  
2. what permanent annual savings that produces  
3. what one-time costs are involved  
4. what product and investment commitments are required to keep those sites viable through 2030  
5. how the package is enforced if targets are missed

Without that, we are still arguing in abstractions.

## What I could live with

I can support a **hybrid, site-preserving restructuring** if it is real and enforceable.

That means:

- permanent site specialization
- elimination of at least one full shift or equivalent durable capacity reduction where justified
- meaningful voluntary exits and natural attrition
- redeployment and retraining
- working-time flexibility
- wage and bonus moderation
- management overhead cuts
- measurable owner contribution
- binding investment and product commitments
- milestone-based review and enforcement

If that package truly closes the economic gap, then I have no need to insist on closures.

## What I reject

I reject:
- a blanket no-closure rule
- a blanket no-forced-redundancy rule if no equivalent structural alternative exists
- a 2030 guarantee that freezes flexibility while leaving the cost base uncompetitive
- closure by attrition disguised as site preservation
- a settlement that is politically comfortable but financially insufficient

## To management

You have not yet proved that closures are necessary.  
But you also have not shown that the non-closure alternative is sufficient.

So I expect the expert work to be put on the table immediately:
- plant-by-plant capacity options,
- permanent savings,
- utilization effects,
- investment needs,
- and implementation risk.

If the alternative package does not produce closure-level economics, I will not pretend otherwise.

## To the works council and union

Your red lines are understandable, but you cannot turn them into an economic veto over all permanent adjustment.

If you want a site-preserving settlement, then it must be more than temporary sacrifice. It must show:
- real capacity reduction,
- real labor-cost contribution,
- real burden sharing,
- and real enforceability.

## To the state

I support your insistence on regional stability.  
But stability without competitiveness is not a solution. The region needs a company that can invest and earn its way forward.

## To the investors

You are right that a cosmetic compromise is not enough.  
But you are wrong if you think credibility comes only from closure language. Credibility comes from execution and permanence.

## My bottom line

I will support a **serious, quantified, site-preserving restructuring** if it:
1. permanently reduces cost,
2. permanently reduces capacity,
3. preserves industrial viability,
4. avoids unnecessary confrontation,
5. and is enforceable through governance.

If it does not, then I will not rule out deeper structural measures, including closures. I will not sign off on value erosion dressed as peace.
