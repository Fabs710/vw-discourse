From the perspective of dispersed capital-market shareholders, the key test is simple: **does the package credibly restore competitive margins and protect enterprise value, or does it merely defer the pain while preserving an uncompetitive cost base?**

On that basis, I agree with several points made so far:

- Management is right that the current cost structure is not sustainable.
- The workforce is right that a purely one-sided solution would be unstable and likely value-destructive.
- The state is right that prolonged conflict would damage the region, operations, and share value.
- The controlling family is right that there is no value in paralysis.

But I need to be equally direct about where we disagree.

## What we will not support
We will not support an outcome that:
- leaves German costs structurally above peers,
- preserves excess capacity simply through rhetorical compromises,
- substitutes temporary optics for permanent savings,
- or results in years of dispute with no credible margin repair.

A cosmetic settlement is not acceptable. Nor is a “socially balanced” deal that looks politically neat but still leaves the company unable to earn its cost of capital.

## On closures and redundancies
I understand why plant closures and compulsory redundancies are so strongly resisted. But from an investor standpoint, **they cannot be ruled out as a matter of principle** if they are necessary to restore competitiveness. The business problem is economic, not ideological.

That said, we also do not automatically prefer closures if there is a **credible alternative that achieves equivalent savings and capacity reduction** with less disruption. We are not attached to a particular method; we are attached to the result.

So the real question is whether the alternative package is:
- durable,
- measurable,
- enforceable,
- and large enough to matter.

If it is not, then avoiding closures becomes a way of avoiding the necessary adjustment.

## What would move us
We would support a package that contains all of the following:

1. **A quantified, durable reduction in fixed cost**
   - not just one-off savings,
   - not just deferred pain,
   - but a structurally lower cost base.

2. **Real capacity reduction**
   - whether through closures, deep consolidation, or other mechanisms that genuinely remove excess capacity.

3. **A meaningful employee contribution**
   - but only if it is part of a balanced package and not just wage restraint with no structural change.

4. **Management and owner discipline**
   - overhead cuts,
   - investment prioritization,
   - and no protection of corporate comfort zones at the expense of returns.

5. **Implementation certainty**
   - because prolonged industrial conflict destroys value quickly.

## On guarantees
I am skeptical of blanket guarantees through 2030 if they effectively freeze the restructuring. Long guarantees are only acceptable if they are tied to a credible plan that still delivers the necessary savings.

In other words: we can support stability, but not if stability is just another word for inertia.

## My bottom line
Capital markets will back **disciplined restructuring**. We will not back:
- a symbolic compromise,
- a process that preserves every site and every legacy entitlement,
- or a negotiation that protects short-term peace while sacrificing long-term competitiveness.

If the other stakeholders can produce a package that credibly restores margins, I would support it. If not, then the company should not pretend that it has solved the problem merely by avoiding confrontation.
