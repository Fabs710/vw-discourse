## Where I disagree

### 1) With management: you still have not proven closures are necessary
You keep saying the non-closure path must match closure-level savings. Fine. Then prove that closures are necessary **after** all site-preserving alternatives are fully modeled, not before.

What I have heard so far is:
- a preference for the bluntest instrument,
- a refusal to give binding security,
- and a constant attempt to shift the burden of proof onto the workforce.

That is not sufficient.

If you want to claim that plant closures are unavoidable, then show:
- exact savings from each site-preserving option,
- exact cost of closures,
- exact effect on utilization,
- and exact effect on competitiveness through 2030.

Until then, “closure remains on the table” is not a conclusion. It is a threat.

### 2) With investors: markets do not reward industrial vandalism
You keep using “credibility” as a code word for job destruction. I reject that.

A company earns credibility when it can:
- execute,
- stabilize production,
- preserve skills,
- and secure labour peace around a real plan.

A company that triggers permanent conflict and destroys trust in co-determination is not more credible because it closes a plant. It is simply more confrontational.

### 3) With the family holding: flexibility cannot mean permanent insecurity
You want a conditional framework, not a blanket guarantee. But your version of “conditional” keeps shifting all risk onto workers while preserving management’s right to reopen the knife later.

That is not acceptable.

If employees give wage restraint, flexibility, retraining, and voluntary contributions, then in return they need **binding site and job security**, not a promise that management may revisit closures if it later decides the deal was insufficient.

### 4) With the state: preservation cannot be diluted into managed decline
I appreciate your rejection of closures as the default answer. But “site-preserving restructuring” is only meaningful if it preserves **sites**, not just postal addresses.

If a plant loses:
- its product,
- its shift,
- its future assignment,
- its workforce,
- and its industrial role,

then it has been closed in substance even if the gate remains open.

I will not accept that semantic trick.

### 5) With the specialists: your models are useful, but your threshold is being weaponized
You are right that savings must be durable and implementation must be certain. But that logic must not be used to pre-emptively exclude all non-closure solutions.

The correct test is not:
> “Can we imagine closures being more elegant on paper?”

The correct test is:
> “Can the site-preserving package, if properly designed, preserve all German plants while delivering enough permanent cost reduction?”

That question has **not** been answered decisively. Therefore closures cannot be treated as inevitable.
