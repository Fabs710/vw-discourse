I appreciate the clarity from all sides. Let me be equally clear from the perspective of the controlling family owners acting through governance.

We are not indifferent to the workforce, and we are not looking for a headline-grabbing confrontation. But we are also not prepared to preside over a slow erosion of the company’s competitiveness, margins, and investment capacity. If the business cannot restore its economics, then in the medium term there will be less to protect for anyone — employees, regions, or owners.

So I see three points very plainly:

1. **Management is right that the current cost base and capacity structure are not sustainable.**  
   There is real pressure here, not rhetoric. If the brand misses its return targets and the company cannot fund the next investment cycle, the enterprise value of the core asset is damaged.

2. **The employee side is right that a one-sided solution will not be stable.**  
   A process that simply announces closures and then expects acceptance is likely to trigger prolonged conflict, reputational damage, and operational instability. That is value-destructive and should be avoided if there is a credible alternative.

3. **The state’s emphasis on the regional and political consequences is also valid — but it cannot become a veto on all structural change.**  
   Preserving the industrial base matters, but preserving every current structure does not. If all flexibility is ruled out, then the eventual adjustment will be harsher, not softer.

Where I disagree with the employee representatives is on the implicit assumption that **plant closures are automatically disqualified**. They are not. I am not saying they must happen, but I am saying they remain on the table if no comparable structural solution is offered. The ownership side will not support a package that freezes overcapacity indefinitely.

Where I disagree with management is on the idea that **closures and forced redundancies should be treated as the default starting point**. That may be the cleanest internal model, but it is not necessarily the best outcome for a company with this governance structure and this stakeholder landscape. A purely confrontational approach risks destroying value through conflict before any savings are realized.

What would move us is not slogans, but a **credible, durable restructuring** with four features:

- **Real capacity reduction** that is economically meaningful, not cosmetic.
- **A substantial employee contribution**, but one that is tied to clear protections and a predictable framework.
- **Management and owner-side discipline**, including overhead reduction, investment prioritization, and governance-backed commitment.
- **Labour peace and implementation certainty** sufficient to avoid years of dispute.

On the employee red lines: I understand the desire for job and site guarantees, but **permanent guarantees through 2030 regardless of economic conditions are too rigid** if they prevent necessary adaptation. I would not support a blanket commitment that removes all restructuring capacity. What I could support is a negotiated package with:
- strong redeployment and retraining,
- no compulsory redundancies for a defined period if sufficient alternative measures are agreed,
- targeted capacity adjustments,
- and a phased approach that gives the company room to execute.

On management’s side, I would say this: **if you want owner support for difficult measures, you need to show that you are exhausting less destructive options first**. The family holding will not back a strategy that unnecessarily damages the company’s reputation, labour relations, and long-term asset value.

So my position is:

- I will support a serious restructuring.
- I will not support paralysis.
- I will not support a settlement that leaves the cost base structurally unchanged.
- But I also will not support a value-destructive confrontation or a closure-first strategy if a credible negotiated alternative can achieve the same economic result.

If the next round produces a concrete package with measurable savings, real capacity reduction, and a stable labour framework, then we can move. If it does not, then it should be understood that the ownership side will not shield the status quo from necessary change.
