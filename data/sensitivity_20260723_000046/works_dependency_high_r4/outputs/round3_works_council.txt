Let me answer the room plainly.

We have now heard enough to identify the real divide. It is **not** about whether the company needs change. It does.  
It is about whether management and capital expect the workforce to pay for a restructuring that still has not been justified as unavoidable in the form they want.

My position remains unchanged:

- **No plant closures in Germany**
- **No compulsory redundancies**
- **No dismantling of the historic job-security framework**
- **Binding co-determination in shaping the restructuring**
- **A fair burden-sharing deal through 2030**

That is not a slogan. It is the minimum for any serious settlement.

## Where I agree with the others

I agree with management, investors, the family holding, and the advisor on one point: the status quo cannot simply continue unchanged. We are not pretending otherwise. The company must become more competitive, and there must be real, permanent adjustments.

I also agree with the state and the union that a one-sided solution would be unstable, destructive, and likely to trigger prolonged conflict. That would hurt value and jobs alike.

And yes, I accept the specialist point that **temporary measures alone are insufficient**. If the company needs a durable reset, then it must be durable.

## Where I disagree — fundamentally

### 1) Management’s claim that closures must remain on the table
No. Not as your default lever, and not as your bargaining threat.

You have still not shown why German plants must be sacrificed before you have fully used the alternatives that preserve sites, skills, and social stability. You say non-closure measures may be insufficient. That may be true in the abstract. It is **not yet a justification** for closing plants in practice.

The burden is on management to prove that:
- all non-destructive options are exhausted,
- the required savings cannot be achieved otherwise,
- and closure is truly the only remaining path.

So far, what I have heard is a preference for the bluntest instrument, not proof of necessity.

### 2) Investors’ insistence on “credibility” through closure risk
You keep saying the market will only believe a package with closures. That is not a law of nature. Markets believe in **execution**, not just in cruelty.

A package with:
- site specialization,
- shift reduction,
- voluntary exits,
- retraining,
- management cuts,
- owner contribution,
- and binding milestones

can be credible if it is enforceable and serious.

What markets do not believe is a company that provokes a workforce conflict, loses production stability, and destroys trust in co-determination while calling that discipline.

### 3) The family holding’s dismissal of a blanket guarantee
You argue that a 2030 guarantee is too rigid. I understand that you want flexibility. But flexibility cannot mean permanent insecurity for the workforce while asking them to deliver concessions.

A guarantee is not a luxury. It is the condition under which workers can accept any contribution at all. If you want sacrifices, then you need to offer security in return.

### 4) The idea that a hybrid package can be “no closures” in name only
This is where I will be very direct: I will not accept a so-called hybrid solution that quietly destroys sites by stripping them of future production, one shift at a time, until closure is simply delayed closure.

If a plant loses its product, its capacity, its workforce, and its future role, then calling it “preserved” is dishonest. We know what closure means, and we know what closure by attrition looks like. I will not sign up to that.

## My answer to the moderator’s core question

### What I most want
A **binding no-closure, no-forced-layoff agreement through 2030**, with real co-determination over the restructuring and future product allocation.

### What I could live with
A **negotiated capacity and working-time package** that uses:
- voluntary exits only,
- redeployment,
- retraining,
- internal mobility,
- temporary pay restraint,
- bonus moderation,
- management overhead cuts,
- and owner contributions,

provided that:
- all German plants remain open,
- there are no forced layoffs,
- and the agreement is legally and industrially enforceable.

### What I reject outright
- plant closures dressed up as optimization
- compulsory redundancies
- “voluntary” departures under threat
- a restructuring that removes job security and calls it modernization
- any arrangement that turns co-determination into a rubber stamp

## To management

You asked for numbers. Fine. Then show us the numbers for the alternatives properly and transparently.

Not:
- broad assertions,
- not vague “structural gap” language,
- not the suggestion that closures are the only serious option.

Show us:
- how much permanent cost you remove with site specialization,
- how much capacity you remove with shift rationalization,
- how much overhead you cut,
- how much owner money comes in,
- what product commitments secure the German footprint,
- and how you intend to keep every German plant viable through 2030.

If you cannot demonstrate that, then your case for closures remains unproven.

## To the union

We are aligned on the core red lines. I support labour action if management tries to force a one-sided package through. But I also want discipline in the negotiation: no drifting toward “less bad” closure logic. The workforce did not strike to trade plant closures for polite language.

## To the state

You are right to resist closures and forced redundancies. But that resistance must become concrete influence. Use your blocking position to insist on a site-preserving settlement, not a managed decline of the German footprint.

## To the family holding and investors

You both speak of value. Then understand this: destroying trust in co-determination is also destroying value. A workforce that feels betrayed will not cooperate, will not stabilize production, and will not give you durable savings.

If you want a credible, value-preserving outcome, then stop treating site closures as the benchmark for seriousness.

## My line on the specialists

The specialists have confirmed something important:  
Non-closure solutions can work **if they are real**.  
That is the key phrase.

But the same experts also confirm that temporary measures are not enough and that capacity reduction must be permanent.

Fine. Then let’s discuss permanent capacity reduction **without closures**:
- remove a shift,
- specialize sites,
- cut duplication,
- reduce management layers,
- use voluntary exits,
- retrain for future products,
- and share the burden fairly.

That is the path that respects both economics and co-determination.

## Final statement

I will not bargain away the workforce’s security under pressure.

If management wants a durable settlement, it must come back with a plan that:
1. keeps **every German plant open**,
2. excludes **compulsory redundancies**,
3. preserves **co-determination**,
4. and distributes the burden fairly between labour, management, and owners.

If that cannot be done, then the conflict is not caused by the works council. It is caused by those who insist that the first solution must be to close plants and threaten jobs.

That is our position. It has not changed.
