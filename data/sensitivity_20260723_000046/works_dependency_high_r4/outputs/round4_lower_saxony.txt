## My position
I **do not support plant closures or compulsory redundancies in Germany as the default path**.  
I **do support a serious restructuring** that restores competitiveness **without hollowing out the regional industrial base** and without sidelining the state’s co-determination role.

That is the line from the regional state government and anchor shareholder.

## Where I agree

### With management, investors, the family holding, and the specialists
You are right on the core diagnosis: **the status quo is not sustainable**.  
The company needs **permanent** cost and capacity adjustment. Temporary concessions alone will not solve this.

I also agree that a purely symbolic compromise would be value-destructive. The company must remain investable and capable of funding future products.

### With the works council and union
You are right that a one-sided shock would be unstable, socially damaging, and likely to provoke prolonged conflict.  
You are also right that the workforce cannot be treated as the sole adjustment variable.

### With the specialists
The experts have made the crucial point clearly: a non-closure solution is only credible if it produces **durable** fixed-cost reduction, **real** capacity reduction, and is **enforceable**.

That is the correct test.

## Where I disagree

### 1) On plant closures
I do **not** accept closures as the starting point or preferred answer.

Yes, closures are the bluntest way to cut fixed costs. But they are also the most destructive for:
- jobs,
- suppliers,
- apprenticeships,
- regional stability,
- and the company’s social legitimacy.

If management wants closures on the table, then it must prove that all site-preserving options have been fully tested and found insufficient. That proof has not been provided yet.

### 2) On compulsory redundancies
I do **not** support forced layoffs as a bargaining premise.

If structural adjustment is needed, then the burden must be shared through:
- management overhead cuts,
- owner contribution,
- voluntary exits,
- redeployment,
- retraining,
- working-time flexibility,
- and only then further measures if absolutely unavoidable.

### 3) On a blanket 2030 freeze
I understand why labour wants security. But I also understand management’s concern that a rigid blanket guarantee can freeze the company.

So I do **not** support an unconditional guarantee that leaves the firm unable to adapt.  
What I do support is a **conditional, enforceable security framework**:
- no forced redundancies for a defined period,
- no closures without proper governance and board approval,
- binding milestones on savings and capacity reduction,
- and binding commitments on investment and future product roles.

That is different from giving management a free hand.

### 4) On “closure remains available” as a bargaining weapon
I think management and investors are using “closures remain on the table” too easily as a negotiating lever.

That is not a plan; it is pressure.  
And pressure alone does not create a durable settlement. It creates resistance.

## What I think is actually workable

A **hybrid, site-preserving restructuring** is still the most sensible route if it is real and not cosmetic.

That means:
- permanent site specialization,
- elimination of at least one full shift or equivalent durable capacity reduction where genuinely justified,
- voluntary exits and natural attrition,
- retraining and redeployment,
- working-time flexibility,
- temporary wage and bonus moderation,
- management overhead cuts,
- and a measurable owner contribution.

That is a restructuring. It is not a status quo disguise.

## What I want most
A **negotiated industrial pact** that:
1. preserves German sites as regional anchors,
2. protects jobs as far as possible,
3. delivers permanent cost and capacity reduction,
4. and keeps co-determination meaningful.

## What I could live with
A package with **permanent site specialization and shift rationalization**, if it is backed by:
- voluntary workforce reduction,
- internal mobility,
- retraining,
- management cost cuts,
- owner-side financial support,
- and binding investment/product commitments for German sites.

## What I reject outright
- plant closures as the first answer
- compulsory redundancies
- a “no-closure” package that is merely closure-by-attrition
- any erosion of the state’s governance and veto role
- any deal that leaves the region with the costs and management with the credit

## To management
You keep saying the non-closure route must match closure-level savings. Fine. Then show the numbers properly.

I want a **site-by-site comparison** that shows:
- which plants can lose a shift without losing their strategic role,
- what product and investment commitments are required to keep them viable,
- how much permanent fixed cost is removed,
- how much capacity is permanently removed,
- and what the one-time versus recurring costs are.

If that analysis proves the non-closure route cannot deliver enough, then we can discuss additional structural measures. But until that is shown, closures are not justified by assertion.

## To the works council and union
I support your stance against forced layoffs and closure-by-stealth. I also support your insistence that workers cannot carry the burden alone.

But I need the workforce side to be equally concrete:
- what permanent capacity reduction are you prepared to accept,
- what voluntary mechanisms can be scaled,
- and how will you make a no-closure package economically serious rather than merely politically reassuring?

If you want me to defend the regional industrial base, I need a package that is strong enough to defend.

## To the family holding and investors
You are right that the company must remain competitive and investable. But do not confuse “credible” with “closure-first.”

A conflict-heavy closure path would destroy value too.  
The best value-preserving route is a negotiated one that combines:
- real burden sharing,
- real capacity reduction,
- real site specialization,
- and real security for the workforce.

## My bottom line
I will support a **binding, quantified, site-preserving restructuring** if it:
1. delivers permanent cost reduction,
2. delivers real capacity reduction,
3. preserves German plants as industrial anchors,
4. avoids compulsory redundancies,
5. and is enforceable through governance and milestones.

I will **not** support:
- a closure-first strategy,
- forced layoffs,
- or a hollowing-out of sites dressed up as compromise.
