I will be direct.

## My position
I do **not** support a restructuring path built around **German plant closures** or **compulsory redundancies** as the default answer.  
I do support a **serious, permanent restructuring** that protects the company’s future **without hollowing out the regional industrial base**.

That is the line.

## Where I agree
I agree with management, investors, the family holding, and the specialists on one point:  
**the status quo is not sustainable**. The company needs permanent cost and capacity adjustment.

I also agree with the workforce side and the union that:
- a one-sided solution would be unstable,
- trust matters,
- and management, owners, and the group must contribute materially.

I agree with the specialists that temporary measures alone are not enough. A durable solution has to be structural.

## Where I disagree
### 1) On closures
I do **not** accept the argument that closures must remain the default or preferred instrument.

Yes, closures are the bluntest way to take out fixed cost. But they are also the most destructive way to damage:
- regional employment,
- supplier networks,
- skills,
- apprenticeships,
- and the company’s legitimacy in the state.

If management wants me to accept closures, it must prove that **no non-destructive package can deliver comparable permanent savings and capacity reduction**. That proof has not been shown.

### 2) On compulsory redundancies
I do **not** accept forced layoffs as a bargaining premise.

If there is genuine overcapacity, then the burden of adjustment must still be shared fairly:
- management overhead cuts,
- owner contribution,
- voluntary departures,
- retraining and redeployment,
- working-time adjustments,
- and only then, if absolutely unavoidable, further structural measures.

I am not going to endorse a model where the workforce carries the main pain while capital insists it is merely “economic necessity.”

### 3) On a blanket 2030 guarantee
I understand the employee side’s demand for security. It is politically and industrially legitimate.

But I also see the concern that a **blanket, unconditional guarantee** can freeze the company.

So I do **not** support a guarantee that is purely symbolic or unconditional.  
What I do support is a **conditional security framework**:
- no forced redundancies for a defined period,
- no closures unless agreed through the proper governance channels,
- and binding commitments on investment, product allocation, and site roles.

That is different from handing management a free hand.

## What I think is actually workable
The only credible path is a **hybrid, site-preserving restructuring** that is permanent, enforceable, and fair.

That means:

- **site specialization**
- **elimination of a shift or equivalent durable capacity reduction where genuinely justified**
- **voluntary exits and natural attrition**
- **redeployment and retraining**
- **working-time flexibility**
- **temporary pay or bonus moderation**
- **management overhead cuts**
- **owner-side contribution**
- **binding investment and product commitments for German sites**
- **clear milestone-based review clauses**

That is a restructuring. It is not a cosmetic compromise.

## What I want most
A **negotiated industrial and employment pact** that:
- preserves German sites,
- protects jobs as far as possible,
- restores competitiveness,
- and keeps the company anchored in the region.

## What I could live with
A package with **permanent site specialization and shift rationalization**, if it is backed by:
- voluntary workforce reduction,
- retraining,
- internal mobility,
- management and owner contributions,
- and enforceable commitments on future products and investment.

## What I reject outright
- plant closures as the first answer
- compulsory redundancies
- a “hybrid” that is really closure by attrition
- any package that strips the state’s co-determination role in practice

## To management
You keep saying the non-closure path may not be enough. Fine. Then show us the numbers on a **real** alternative, not an abstract threat.

I want a quantified comparison of:
- permanent savings from site specialization,
- savings from removing a full shift,
- savings from overhead cuts,
- workforce reductions via voluntary means,
- and the one-time costs versus closure.

If the non-closure package cannot close the gap, say so with evidence. But do not use that uncertainty as a reason to force the most destructive option upfront.

## To the works council and union
You are right to insist that workers cannot be the adjustment variable of first resort. I support that.

But I also need clarity: if we are going to defend site preservation, then the workforce side must be prepared for **real structural adjustment**, not just temporary relief. A permanent no-closure position is strongest if it comes with credible, measurable cost reduction.

## To the family holding and investors
You are right that the company must remain investable. But investability also depends on social legitimacy and industrial peace. A conflict-heavy closure path would destroy value too.

So my message is simple:  
I will support tough restructuring, but not a strategy that sacrifices the regional anchor model when a negotiated alternative may still be available.

## My bottom line
I am prepared to back a **binding, quantified, non-closure restructuring package** if it:
1. delivers permanent cost reduction,
2. delivers real capacity reduction,
3. preserves German sites,
4. avoids compulsory redundancies,
5. and is enforceable through governance and milestones.

I am **not** prepared to endorse closures or forced layoffs as the starting point.

If management and shareholders want my support, bring forward a package that proves the savings **without** gutting the regional industrial base.
