Here is my assessment, focused on **non-closure restructuring options** and their **feasibility in a German co-determination setting**.

## Executive judgment

A **credible no-closure restructuring is possible in principle**, but only if it does **more than protect all current structures**. To substitute for plant closures, it must combine:

- **permanent capacity reduction**
- **site specialization**
- **measurable headcount reduction through voluntary and natural means**
- **working-time and cost-flexibility**
- **management and owner-side burden sharing**
- **enforceable governance and follow-through**

If the package relies mainly on **temporary labor-cost relief** or **general promises of productivity improvement**, it will not be sufficient for a large German OEM facing structural overcapacity.

In practice, there are three distinct questions:

1. **Operational feasibility:** Can capacity be reduced without closures?
2. **Labor-relations feasibility:** Can this be negotiated under German co-determination?
3. **Economic sufficiency:** Does it remove enough permanent cost and capacity to restore competitiveness?

My answer is:

- **Operationally:** yes, partially.
- **Labor-relations-wise:** yes, if structured correctly.
- **Economically:** only if the package is substantial and permanent; otherwise, no.

---

## 1) What non-closure restructuring can realistically do

### A. Site specialization
This is one of the strongest alternatives to closure.

A plant can be reassigned to:
- one platform family
- one powertrain architecture
- one vehicle derivative segment
- battery-related assembly or module work
- CKD/low-complexity final assembly
- component production with stable demand

### Why it works
- reduces duplication
- lowers complexity and logistics cost
- improves line loading
- can remove indirect labor and overhead
- allows fewer model changeovers and better scheduling discipline

### Limitation
Specialization only works if there is a **credible product allocation plan** for the remaining sites. Without that, it becomes an accounting maneuver, not a restructuring.

---

### B. Capacity reduction without closure
This is feasible through:
- shift elimination
- line de-bottlenecking in reverse
- output throttling
- permanent removal of overtime capacity
- mothballing a line or hall
- insourcing only the volumes that are economically efficient
- moving selected volume to higher-productivity plants in the network

### What it can achieve
It can reduce:
- direct labor hours
- utilities
- maintenance
- some logistics and support costs
- some variable manufacturing overhead

### What it cannot fully achieve
It usually does **not** eliminate:
- site overhead
- depreciation burden
- fixed property and infrastructure cost
- all indirect labor
- all maintenance and support complexity

So if the real issue is **network overcapacity**, you may reduce output without removing enough fixed cost.

---

### C. Redeployment and retraining
This is feasible and often necessary, especially in a transition period.

It works best for:
- EV-related new processes
- battery/thermal/electronics assembly
- quality, logistics, and industrial maintenance roles
- software-enabled production support
- internal mobility to growth areas within the group

### Constraint
Retraining only helps if there are **real absorbing functions**. If all sites are shrinking at once, retraining becomes a holding pattern rather than a solution.

---

### D. Voluntary exits and natural attrition
This is the most co-determination-compatible way to reduce workforce size without compulsory redundancies.

Tools include:
- early retirement windows
- severance-based voluntary programs
- partial retirement
- internally funded transition models
- hiring freeze
- replacement discipline
- transfer to internal labor pools

### Operational risk
These programs often remove:
- older, more expensive staff first
- not necessarily the exact profiles management wants to reduce
- and may erode skills if badly sequenced

Still, for German industrial settings, they are a standard first layer in negotiated adjustment.

---

### E. Working-time and compensation flexibility
This includes:
- temporary or permanent reduction in weekly hours
- shift pattern changes
- elimination of certain allowances
- bonus moderation or deferral
- deferred wage increases
- flexible working-time accounts
- short-time work if conditions justify it

### Important distinction
These measures are good for **cost relief** but weaker for **structural restructuring**.

They help if the problem is cyclical. They are not enough if the problem is enduring overcapacity and uncompetitive fixed cost.

---

## 2) What a serious non-closure package would need to contain

If management wants to avoid closures, the package must meet a hard test:

### It must deliver all three:
1. **Permanent annual cost reduction**
2. **Permanent capacity reduction**
3. **Operational simplification**

If any one of those is missing, the package is usually too soft.

---

### A plausible structure
A credible no-closure package would usually have five layers:

#### Layer 1: Immediate cost relief
- wage restraint
- bonus reduction
- overtime limitation
- hiring freeze
- overhead cuts
- management compensation restraint

#### Layer 2: Workforce reshaping
- voluntary departures
- early retirement
- internal mobility
- retraining
- redeployment to EV / software / component functions

#### Layer 3: Site specialization
- fewer products per plant
- clearer role definitions
- elimination of duplicate functions
- transfer of low-value-added volume to the most efficient sites

#### Layer 4: Capacity reduction
- remove a shift
- reduce line speed or volume
- discontinue low-margin variants
- consolidate certain production steps
- freeze selective expansion

#### Layer 5: Governance and enforcement
- binding implementation milestones
- board-level oversight
- works agreement plus collective-bargaining linkage
- clawback / review mechanisms if commitments are not met

---

## 3) Which non-closure measures are strongest, and which are weak

### Strongest measures
These are structurally credible:

- **permanent shift removal**
- **site specialization**
- **voluntary workforce reduction**
- **overhead reduction**
- **product and portfolio simplification**
- **platform consolidation**
- **clear internal transfer rules**

These can create real, durable savings.

### Weaker measures
These help cash flow but are not a substitute for restructuring:

- one-off employee contributions
- temporary wage freezes
- short-time work
- investment deferrals
- bonus suspensions
- temporary hiring freezes

Useful, yes. Sufficient, no.

---

## 4) How much can non-closure measures save?

I will not invent plant-specific numbers without audited cost data. But in industry terms:

- **temporary measures** can deliver modest to material short-term relief
- **voluntary headcount reduction plus overhead simplification** can produce meaningful savings
- **site specialization and shift removal** can produce substantial savings
- **but full substitution for closure is difficult** if overcapacity is large and persistent

The crucial point is not the nominal savings headline. It is whether savings are:

- **permanent**
- **legally enforceable**
- **operationally implementable**
- **large enough to close the structural gap**

In many automotive networks, a no-closure package can plausibly cover a **significant share** of the needed correction, but not always all of it.

---

## 5) Labor-relations feasibility under German co-determination

This is where a non-closure package has a real chance.

### What is feasible
A negotiated solution is feasible if it is built through:

- collective bargaining with the union
- works agreements at site and group level
- supervisory board co-determination
- transfer and qualification arrangements
- social plan / reconciliation of interests where needed
- trust-based implementation with clear milestones

### What is not feasible
A unilateral management program demanding:
- closures
- compulsory redundancies
- wage cuts
- and removal of guarantees
without compensation or commitment

That is likely to trigger:
- labor conflict
- supervisory board blockage
- delayed implementation
- reputational damage
- operational instability

### Practical reality
In German automotive restructuring, employees will usually trade flexibility for security if the deal is:
- bounded in time
- balanced in burden
- and credible on investment and future products

---

## 6) Enforceable job and security frameworks that are compatible with co-determination

If the goal is to avoid closures, the security framework must be **conditional, not absolute**.

### What works better than a blanket guarantee
A workable framework would typically include:

- **no compulsory redundancies for a defined period**
- **site employment corridors rather than absolute headcount guarantees**
- **investment commitments tied to site roles**
- **product allocation commitments**
- **retraining rights**
- **internal placement obligations**
- **a defined review mechanism if market assumptions deteriorate**
- **triggered negotiation if volumes fall below thresholds**

### Why this is better
It gives employees security, but preserves management flexibility if the market changes materially.

### What usually fails
A universal, unconditional guarantee for every site and every job to 2030, regardless of market development, is usually too rigid for management and owners to accept in a true restructuring.

---

## 7) Social safeguard mechanisms that are standard and workable

A durable package should normally include some combination of:

- voluntary exit windows
- age-based transitions
- partial retirement
- transfer company / employment bridge
- qualification budget
- mobility centers
- redeployment guarantee
- severance corridors
- hardship protections
- no forced layoff commitment for a period
- priority return rights if conditions improve

These are standard tools in German industrial restructuring and are much more realistic than simple job-loss absorption.

---

## 8) Key implementation risks

### Risk 1: temporary relief masquerading as restructuring
If the package mostly postpones adjustment, it will fail later, usually under worse conditions.

### Risk 2: savings leakage
If labor cost is reduced but complexity stays high, the savings get eaten by inefficiency.

### Risk 3: underdelivery on capacity reduction
A deal may reduce headcount without removing enough production structure. That does not fix overcapacity.

### Risk 4: skills erosion
Voluntary exits can remove critical knowledge if not carefully sequenced.

### Risk 5: industrial relations drift
If milestones are vague, every future volume decline reopens the conflict.

### Risk 6: governance mismatch
If the supervisory board agreement and the works agreement are not aligned, implementation can stall.

---

## 9) My conclusion on the specific decision

### Operational feasibility
A non-closure restructuring is feasible if it is built around:
- specialization
- shift/volume rationalization
- voluntary workforce reduction
- retraining and redeployment
- overhead cuts
- limited but real labour-cost contributions

### Labor-relations feasibility
It is feasible in the German system if:
- negotiated with the union and works councils
- protected by conditional job/security arrangements
- balanced by management and owner contributions
- tied to investment and product commitments

### Economic sufficiency
This is the real test. A non-closure package is sufficient **only if** it removes enough **permanent** cost and capacity to restore competitiveness. If it does not, then it is not a substitute for closures; it is only a delay mechanism.

---

## Practical recommendation

If the parties want to avoid closures, I would recommend a **hybrid non-closure reset** with these elements:

1. **Permanent site specialization**
2. **One or more shift eliminations at selected plants**
3. **Voluntary headcount reduction and attrition**
4. **Retraining / redeployment into future product areas**
5. **Temporary wage and bonus moderation**
6. **Management overhead cuts**
7. **Owner-side contribution**
8. **Conditional no-compulsory-redundancy agreement**
9. **Site-level investment and product commitments**
10. **Milestone-based review clauses**

That is the kind of package that can be defended operationally and industrial-relations-wise.

What I would not recommend is pretending that:
- temporary measures alone,
- or a blanket 2030 guarantee,
- or symbolic contribution formulas

can substitute for real structural adjustment.
