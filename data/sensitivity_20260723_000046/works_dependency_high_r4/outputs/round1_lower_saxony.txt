I appreciate that management is being explicit about the seriousness of the situation. I also recognize the works council and the union are right to insist that this cannot be a one-sided adjustment imposed on the workforce. On that point, I agree: if the company expects sacrifice, then management and owners must contribute materially as well.

But I need to be equally clear about the state’s position as a shareholder and political actor:

- **We will not support a solution that closes German plants as the first or preferred answer.**
- **We will not support compulsory redundancies as the central instrument of restructuring.**
- **We will not accept a process that sidelines co-determination or treats the workforce and the regional stakeholders as obstacles to be bypassed.**

Why? Because this company is not just another balance-sheet exercise. It is a regional economic anchor, and its German sites are part of an industrial ecosystem that supports many thousands of families and suppliers. A restructuring that destroys that base may create short-term accounting relief, but it would carry long-term economic and political damage that is hard to reverse.

That said, I am not defending stagnation. I do think a credible restructuring can and must include:
- capacity adjustments,
- working-time flexibility,
- redeployment and retraining,
- targeted efficiency measures,
- management overhead reductions,
- and a real owner-side contribution.

What I cannot accept is the logic that only plant closures and job cuts are “real” savings. That is too narrow and too risky. It may be true that the company has excess capacity, but the burden of correcting that cannot fall primarily through irreversible destruction of jobs and sites in Germany.

So my message to management is this: if you want support from this state, bring forward a restructuring that **preserves the industrial core, protects as many jobs as possible, and is negotiated with the workforce in good faith**. A package that combines moderate, reversible adjustments with a long-term product and investment commitment is much more plausible than a closure-driven reset.

To the employee side: I hear your red lines, and they are understandable. But I would also ask that you remain open to a meaningful contribution if it genuinely secures competitiveness and jobs in the long run. A total refusal of all adjustment could end up strengthening those in management who argue for harsher measures.

To be direct:  
**A negotiated social-partnership solution is still the right path.**  
But it must be a real solution to competitiveness, not a symbolic preservation of the status quo. And it must not sacrifice the company’s role as a regional anchor in the process.

If management comes back with a package that still relies mainly on closures and forced cuts, I will not regard that as a serious basis for agreement.
