The finance specialist’s answer is directionally correct. From an industrial restructuring perspective, I would sharpen it as follows:

## Short answer
A **credible no-closure restructuring** becomes financially and operationally plausible only if it achieves:

1. **Permanent fixed-cost reduction**
2. **Meaningful installed-capacity reduction**
3. **High implementation certainty**
4. **A lower one-time cost than closures without losing most of the recurring savings**

If it does **not** remove real fixed cost and real capacity, it is not a restructuring alternative — it is a delay mechanism.

---

## What would make closures non-necessary in practice

I would not say “closures are unnecessary” unless the package includes most of the following:

### 1) One full shift removed at one or more sites
This is often the cleanest non-closure way to take out durable capacity.

- It reduces labour hours structurally
- It lowers indirect support load
- It can improve utilization at remaining plants
- It is more credible than vague “flexibility” promises

If no shift is actually removed, the economic case is much weaker.

### 2) Site specialization with hard product allocation
A site must be given a clear future role.

That means:
- fewer overlapping products
- less duplication across plants
- a defined model mix
- a stable long-term volume base

Without specialization, “preserving all sites” often just preserves inefficiency.

### 3) Voluntary headcount reduction at meaningful scale
This needs to go beyond natural attrition.

Useful instruments:
- age-related voluntary exits
- transfer offers
- internal mobility
- severance-based exit windows
- retraining-linked displacement

But if the reduction is too small versus overcapacity, the package won’t close the gap.

### 4) Management and overhead reduction
A credible deal must cut:
- support functions
- layers of management
- duplicated engineering or administrative work
- non-core overhead

If labour is asked to contribute but central overhead stays intact, the package is unbalanced and often insufficient.

### 5) Compensation moderation with a time limit
This can help, but it is not a substitute for structural change.

Acceptable forms in German industrial practice often include:
- bonus moderation
- temporary wage restraint
- working-time flexibility
- reduced supplements
- paid-time-off conversion

But these are usually **support measures**, not the core of the savings case.

---

## What would still be too weak

I would consider a non-closure package inadequate if it relies mainly on:

- temporary wage concessions
- a hiring freeze
- generic efficiency promises
- optimistic future volume assumptions
- “voluntary” exits that are too small
- no real shift loss
- no hard site specialization
- no enforceable milestone structure

That kind of package may buy peace, but not necessarily competitiveness.

---

## Enforceability is the key industrial-relations issue

In German co-determination settings, the question is not only whether savings are theoretically possible, but whether they are **governable**.

A no-closure deal is credible only if it is tied to:
- binding milestones
- transparent reporting
- joint monitoring
- clear fallback measures if targets are missed

Without that, management will later argue that the deal underdelivered, and the workforce will argue that concessions were extracted without security. That is exactly how trust collapses.

---

## My practical test for “closure-level savings”

I would ask:

> Does the non-closure package permanently remove enough cost and capacity that the company would still choose it if closures were politically neutral?

If the answer is yes, then closures may be avoidable.

If the answer is no, then closures remain financially relevant, even if politically difficult.

---

## Bottom line
A no-closure package can be enough **only if** it combines:
- permanent shift reduction,
- site specialization,
- meaningful voluntary workforce reduction,
- management/overhead cuts,
- and enforceable delivery through 2030.

If it lacks those elements, it is not an equivalent alternative to closures.
