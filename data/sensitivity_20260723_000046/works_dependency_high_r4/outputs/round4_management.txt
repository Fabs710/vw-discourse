We are now fully into the substance, so I will be equally direct.

## To the works council and union

I hear your red lines. I also understand why you are drawing them. But let’s be clear: **repeating “no closures, no forced redundancies, no erosion of job security” does not solve the structural cost problem**.

You say closures are being used as a bargaining gun. I say the opposite: **economic reality is being treated as optional**.

We are not talking about a marginal efficiency exercise. We are talking about a brand that is missing its return-on-sales target, facing a shrinking European market, and carrying a German cost base that is too high to fund the 2030 product cycle. If we leave the structure broadly intact, we simply postpone the crisis and consume the money we need for future vehicles and technology.

You keep asking for proof that closures are necessary. Fine — but you have not yet shown that your preferred alternative is sufficient either. A package built on:
- site specialization,
- shift reduction,
- voluntary exits,
- redeployment,
- temporary wage restraint,
- bonus moderation,
- and overhead cuts

may be helpful, but **helpful is not the same as enough**. If it does not produce permanent fixed-cost reduction at closure-level scale, then it is not an equivalent solution.

And I need to be frank: a “no closures” rule through 2030, combined with “no compulsory redundancies,” is only acceptable if you can demonstrate that the same economic outcome is reached. So far, that demonstration is missing.

## To the state

I respect your regional responsibility and your rejection of a closure-first reflex. But you are also drifting toward a position that risks preserving employment structures that the market can no longer support.

A strong regional base is not preserved by freezing the current footprint. It is preserved by making the company investable again. If the cost base remains too high, the region loses anyway — only later, after more value has been destroyed.

So I agree with you on one thing: **a destructive shock would be bad policy**.  
I disagree with your implicit assumption that site preservation can be achieved without hard structural cuts. That has not been proven.

## To the controlling family holding

You are right to reject theatre and cosmetic compromise. I agree.  
But you are too quick to elevate “conditional flexibility” into a meaningful answer while still keeping the workforce in permanent uncertainty.

If we are serious about restoring competitiveness, then we need a **structural package that actually lowers the fixed cost base**, not a promise that management will revisit the issue later if the numbers disappoint. That is exactly the kind of half-solution that breeds distrust and fails to reset the business.

## To the investors

You are right to insist on durability and implementation certainty. That is precisely why I am not prepared to sign up to a deal that looks good politically but leaves the economics unchanged.

What I disagree with is the assumption that the only credible signal to capital is closure. It is not. A hard, enforceable package with:
- real capacity reduction,
- permanent labor cost contribution,
- lower overhead,
- management sacrifice,
- and a measurable owner contribution

can be credible if it is actually substantial.

But let me be equally clear: **credibility cannot rest on promises alone**. It has to show up in hard numbers. If the no-closure path does not deliver those numbers, then it is not credible.

## To the specialists

I accept your logic, and in fact it supports my position:  
non-closure solutions are only acceptable if they are **permanent, meaningful, and enforceable**.

That is the test.

What I have not yet heard from the employee side is a quantified package that shows:
1. enough permanent annual savings,
2. enough permanent capacity reduction,
3. enough implementation certainty,
4. and enough impact on the return-on-sales target.

Until I see that, I cannot remove closures from consideration.

## My position, clearly restated

### What I most want
A **durable structural restructuring** that:
- materially reduces overcapacity,
- materially lowers fixed labour and overhead costs,
- restores the brand to its return-on-sales target,
- and frees funds for 2030 investment.

### What I could live with
A **hybrid package without immediate closures** only if it includes:
- permanent site specialization,
- removal of at least one full shift or equivalent durable capacity reduction at the relevant plants,
- meaningful voluntary workforce reduction,
- wage and bonus moderation,
- management overhead cuts,
- and a measurable owner contribution.

### What I still reject
- a blanket no-closure rule,
- a blanket no-forced-redundancy rule if no equivalent structural alternative exists,
- and a 2030 guarantee that freezes flexibility while the cost base stays uncompetitive.

## On your claim of “closure by attrition”

If a site is stripped of product, shifts, workforce, and future role, that is indeed closure in substance. I agree with that definition. That is exactly why I am saying we need a package that does **not** drift into cosmetic preservation.

If you want to avoid closures in substance, then the package must still produce:
- real capacity reduction,
- real specialization,
- and real cost savings.

A plant cannot be preserved by naming it so. It must remain economically viable.

## What I need now

The conversation cannot remain at the level of principle. I need a **site-by-site, quantified comparison** that tells us:

- which German plants can lose a shift without losing strategic viability,
- what product and investment commitments are required to keep them viable,
- how much permanent cost comes out under each scenario,
- and which scenario actually hits the 2030 margin target.
