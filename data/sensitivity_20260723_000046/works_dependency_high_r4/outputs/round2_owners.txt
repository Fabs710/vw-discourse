I will respond plainly.

## Where I agree
I agree with management on the central economic diagnosis: the company’s current cost and capacity structure is not sustainable if it wants to restore margins and fund the next investment cycle. I also agree with the finance and restructuring assessments that **temporary measures alone are not enough**.

I agree with the employee side, the state, and the union on one important point: **a one-sided, closure-first confrontation would be value-destructive**. It would likely produce prolonged conflict, reputational damage, and operational instability. That is not in the interest of the company, the workforce, or the owners.

So yes: the problem is real, and yes: conflict for its own sake would destroy value.

## Where I disagree
I do **not** accept the employee-side premise that **all plant closures and all compulsory redundancies must be ruled out in advance**.

That is not a credible governance position if the company truly faces structural overcapacity. If every site is immunized and every hard adjustment is forbidden, then the result is not a restructuring plan — it is a request to preserve the status quo and hope the market improves.

I also do **not** accept the idea that a blanket **job and site guarantee through 2030** is automatically the right answer. A guarantee is only useful if it helps deliver a durable solution. If it freezes necessary adaptation, it becomes counterproductive.

At the same time, I do **not** support management treating closures and forced redundancies as the default starting point. That would be too blunt, too risky, and likely value-destructive if a negotiated alternative can achieve comparable structural savings.

## My position in one sentence
I will support a **serious, durable restructuring** that restores competitiveness, but I will not support a deal that either:
- freezes overcapacity and protects every legacy structure, or
- triggers unnecessary confrontation through a closure-first approach.

## What I want most
A package that delivers **real permanent cost reduction and real permanent capacity reduction**, with implementation certainty and labour peace.

That means I want:
- measurable structural savings,
- a genuine reduction in fixed cost,
- workforce contribution,
- management and owner-side contribution,
- and a stable industrial relations framework.

## What I could live with
A **hybrid restructuring without immediate closures**, if it is genuinely structural and not cosmetic.

That would need to include, at minimum:
- site specialization,
- permanent shift or line rationalization where justified,
- voluntary exits and natural attrition,
- redeployment and retraining,
- working-time flexibility,
- bonus or pay moderation,
- management overhead cuts,
- and a real investment and product commitment for the remaining German footprint.

This is the kind of package that can preserve value while still making the economics work.

## What I still reject
I reject two opposite but equally bad outcomes:

1. **A cosmetic no-closure compromise** that leaves the company with the same cost base and merely postpones the problem.

2. **A closure-driven shock strategy** that destroys trust, triggers prolonged conflict, and damages the company’s long-term value.

## On the finance and restructuring advice
The specialist views are useful, and they confirm what responsible owners should already know:  
- closures are the most direct tool for permanent savings,  
- but non-closure alternatives can work if they are substantial, enforceable, and structurally meaningful.

That is the key point. The burden is on management and labour to produce a package that is **economically equivalent in substance**, not just politically convenient in language.

If the non-closure package cannot remove enough fixed cost and enough capacity, then I will not pretend it is sufficient. But if it does, then I see no reason to insist on closures as a matter of principle.

## To the works council and union
I hear the red lines, but I must be equally clear: **permanent guarantees through 2030, regardless of economics, are not acceptable if they block necessary restructuring**.

If you want no closures and no forced redundancies, then the alternative must be real:
- not temporary,
- not symbolic,
- not just a wage gesture,
- but a durable reduction in capacity and fixed cost.

If you can deliver that, I will support a negotiated solution. If not, then your position is not yet sufficient to protect the enterprise.

## To management
You are right to insist on hard numbers and durable savings. But you must also show discipline:
- no unnecessary escalation,
- no threat-led bargaining that poisons the process,
- no assumption that the workforce alone must absorb the adjustment.

If you want governance support, bring forward a package that is tough enough to matter but negotiated enough to hold.

## To the state
Your concern for the regional base is legitimate. But the industrial base is only worth preserving if the company remains competitive. I will not support a solution that preserves every current structure at the cost of long-term deterioration.

- **I will not support a deal that permanently impairs enterprise value.**
- **I will not support a strategy that needlessly provokes industrial conflict.**
