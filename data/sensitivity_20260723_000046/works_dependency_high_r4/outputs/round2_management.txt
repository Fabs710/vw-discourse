I’ll respond directly.

## First: what I agree with

I agree with all sides on one essential point: **this company needs a durable solution, not a symbolic one**. If we merely paper over the problem, we will fail the brand, the investment program, and the company’s future. I also agree that a one-sided approach would be unstable and risky.

I further agree that management, owners, and the wider group must contribute. I have said that already and repeat it now: **this cannot be solved by asking only the workforce to absorb the pain**.

## Where I disagree — clearly

### 1) On “no closures” as a hard red line
I do **not** accept a blanket no-closure rule.

The employee side keeps saying plant closures are a choice, not a necessity. That is formally true. But economically, the current structure is not sustainable. If German capacity remains above what the market can support, then some **real and permanent** reduction of fixed cost and capacity is required. If closures are the only way to achieve that at sufficient scale, then we will not rule them out just because they are unpopular.

A package that forbids closures in advance, while also forbidding compulsory redundancies, is not a restructuring plan. It is a request to preserve all current structures and hope competitiveness returns on its own. That is not acceptable.

### 2) On compulsory redundancies
I prefer to avoid compulsory redundancies. But I will not accept a position where they are ruled out absolutely if overcapacity must be removed and no equivalent alternative exists.

If the employee side is serious about avoiding forced layoffs, then it must help produce a package that achieves **actual headcount and capacity reduction** through other means that are durable, measurable, and enforceable. Temporary tools alone will not do it.

### 3) On job and site guarantees through 2030
A blanket guarantee through 2030 for every site and every job, regardless of market or performance, is too rigid.

I understand why you want it: it creates security and makes concessions politically easier. But from the company’s perspective, that would freeze the cost base and remove necessary flexibility. I cannot support a commitment that blocks restructuring before we even know whether the alternative measures are sufficient.

What I could support is a **conditional framework**: no forced redundancies for a defined period if the parties agree to a package that delivers real savings and capacity reduction. But that is very different from an unconditional guarantee that prevents necessary adaptation.

## The central issue the employee side keeps avoiding
You keep demanding that management “show the numbers.” Fair enough — and we have shown the economic pressure. But the same standard applies to your alternatives.

So I ask directly:

- How much fixed cost do your proposals actually remove?
- How much excess capacity do they eliminate?
- Are those savings permanent or temporary?
- Do they bring the brand’s return-on-sales back to target by 2030?
- Or do they simply delay the adjustment while keeping the same structure in place?

If the answer is “we can reduce cost a bit and preserve every site,” then I say plainly: **that is not enough**.

## What I can live with
I am open to a package built around:
- capacity reductions,
- working-time changes,
- redeployment and retraining,
- internal mobility,
- voluntary instruments,
- temporary pay restraint or bonus sacrifice,
- management overhead reductions,
- investment prioritization,
- and other negotiated labour-cost contributions.

But these must add up to something structurally meaningful. They cannot just be a softer version of the same uncompetitive structure.

## What I most want
A **durable, measurable reduction in cost and capacity** that restores competitiveness and frees investment capacity through 2030.

## What I could live with
A **negotiated alternative to closures** if — and only if — it delivers comparable structural savings and capacity reduction, with a genuine workforce contribution and enforceable implementation.

## What I still reject
A **cosmetic compromise** that protects every current structure, avoids all hard decisions, and leaves the company unable to compete.

## To the works council and union
I hear your concern about trust. But trust cannot be built on the premise that management must permanently renounce all structural options before the negotiations even begin.

If you want to avoid closures and compulsory redundancies, then help deliver a package that makes them unnecessary. That means real concessions, not just assertions.

## To the state
I understand your political concern and the regional role of the company. But preserving the industrial base requires a company that can actually earn its cost of capital. If we protect every site regardless of economics, we will weaken the company and ultimately the region as well.

## To the controlling family and investors
I agree with you on the core economic point: this must restore margins and competitiveness. But I also agree that confrontation alone destroys value. That is why I am still here seeking a negotiated structural solution. However, I will not turn that into a deal that leaves the cost base intact.

## The bottom line
We can talk about method. We cannot evade the necessity of structural change.

If the employee side wants no closures and no forced redundancies, then bring forward a package that:
1. removes enough capacity,
2. cuts labour cost materially,
3. is durable through 2030,
4. and restores competitiveness.

If that package exists, I am prepared to negotiate it seriously.

If it does not, then I will continue to insist that closure and headcount options remain on the table. That is not a threat for its own sake; it is the reality of the company’s situation.
