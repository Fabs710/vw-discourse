I hear the red lines you are drawing, but I need to be equally clear about the company’s reality.

We are facing a structural competitiveness problem, not a temporary dip. German factory costs are too high, the European market is shrinking, and we are under intense pressure from lower-cost competitors. The brand is not meeting its return-on-sales target, and if we do not act decisively now, we will not have the funds to invest in the next generation of products, software, and battery technology through 2030.

So let me respond directly:

- **No plant closures** as a starting demand is not credible in this situation.
- **No compulsory redundancies** is not something I can accept if it means the company has no real mechanism to reduce overcapacity.
- **Binding guarantees through 2030** cannot be granted in the abstract if they freeze the current cost base and block necessary restructuring.

That said, I am not saying the only path is immediate closure. I am saying we need a **durable reduction in capacity and labour cost**. If you can bring me an alternative that achieves that structurally and credibly, I am open to it. But it has to be real, not cosmetic.

What would move us:

1. **A measurable, sustained labour-cost contribution** from the workforce.
2. **A genuine reduction in overcapacity** — whether through closure, deep consolidation, or equivalent structural measures that actually remove fixed cost.
3. **Flexibility on working time, deployment, and compensation** that produces lasting savings.
4. **A credible agreement that restores margins** and frees investment capacity for the future.

What will not move me:

- A deal that simply postpones the problem.
- A package of temporary measures that leaves us with the same cost structure.
- A solution that protects every site and every status quo while asking management to absorb the adjustment alone.

On your point about fairness: I do not deny that management and shareholders must also contribute. We will look at overhead reductions, prioritisation of investment, and all levers available. But those measures alone will not close the gap. The workforce contribution must be material.

So my position is this:  
**We are prepared to negotiate on the method, but not on the necessity of a deep structural reset.** If the employee side wants to avoid closures, then it must bring forward an alternative that delivers comparable savings and capacity reduction. Otherwise, management will continue to press for the strongest available restructuring options.

I would prefer an agreement over confrontation, but I will not sign off on a cosmetic compromise that leaves the company uncompetitive by 2030.
