## What I would need to see before saying closures are not financially necessary

To conclude that **immediate German plant closures are not financially necessary**, I would need evidence that a non-closure package can credibly deliver all three of the following:

1. **Permanent annual cost savings** roughly comparable to the closure case, after one-time costs  
2. **Permanent capacity reduction** sufficient to align output with demand and protect utilization  
3. **Implementation certainty** high enough that the savings are not mostly temporary or contingent on perfect cooperation

In practice, that means the package must show:
- a material reduction in installed capacity or shifts,
- a durable reduction in fixed labour and overhead cost,
- and a clean path through 2030 without relying mainly on temporary concessions.

## Standard financial logic

For a restructuring like this, the relevant comparison is not “does the non-closure package save some money?” but:

> **Does it produce enough net present value of savings, after execution risk and one-time costs, to match the closure-heavy option?**

So I would compare:

### A) Closure-heavy scenario
Likely features:
- plant closure(s),
- compulsory and voluntary headcount reduction,
- fixed-cost removal from payroll, site overhead, utilities, maintenance, and depreciation-linked operating inefficiency,
- higher one-time costs: severance, decommissioning, environmental and logistics costs, transfer costs, and often higher labor conflict risk.

### B) Hybrid scenario
Likely features:
- no immediate closures,
- one or more plants lose a shift,
- site specialization,
- product consolidation,
- voluntary exits and attrition,
- management overhead cuts,
- wage/bonus moderation,
- possible working-time changes.

This can work if the shift removal and specialization really remove **permanent fixed cost**, not just variable cost.

### C) No-closure scenario
Likely features:
- no closures,
- no shift removal or only limited network changes,
- mostly wage moderation, overhead cuts, attrition, redeployment, and temporary measures.

This is usually the weakest economically unless the company is already structurally very close to balance.

## Minimum permanent savings test

A non-closure solution becomes plausible only if it can deliver, in broad terms:

- **permanent annual savings** in the same order as the closure alternative, net of ongoing support costs
- and **capacity reduction** that is large enough to keep utilization from remaining structurally too low

The exact euro amount depends on:
- headcount base,
- average fully loaded labour cost,
- plant fixed overhead,
- product mix,
- and the amount of volume that can be reassigned or eliminated.

## Practical rule of thumb

If the company’s problem is genuinely structural overcapacity, then a viable non-closure package usually needs some combination of:

- **one full shift eliminated at one or more plants**, or equivalent capacity removal
- **meaningful voluntary workforce reduction**, not just attrition
- **durable labour cost moderation**
- **management overhead reduction**
- **site specialization / product concentration**

If those measures do not produce a step-change in fixed cost, then they are unlikely to be equivalent to closures.

## One-time costs vs. recurring savings

A closure-heavy option often has:
- **higher one-time costs**
- **stronger recurring savings**

A hybrid non-closure option often has:
- **lower one-time costs**
- **lower recurring savings**
- but also **lower disruption risk**, which has value

The decision hinges on whether the recurring savings gap is small enough to justify preserving sites.

## What I would flag as warning signs

I would not treat a non-closure package as financially sufficient if:

- savings are mostly temporary wage givebacks
- capacity is reduced only on paper
- specialization does not actually remove fixed labor and overhead
- voluntary exits are too small relative to the overcapacity
- implementation depends on perfect labour peace without enforceable milestones
- the company still ends up with materially underutilized plants

## Minimum credible structure for a no-closure deal

To be financially credible, I would want the package to include:

1. **Permanent shift loss or equivalent capacity removal**
2. **Headcount reduction through voluntary exits / attrition at meaningful scale**
3. **Site specialization with clear product allocation**
4. **Management and support-function cuts**
5. **A limited, time-bound employee contribution**
6. **Milestone-based enforcement and a fallback if targets are missed**

Without at least items 1–4, the package is likely too weak structurally.

## Bottom line

My answer to the question “when can closures be avoided?” is:

> Only when the non-closure package can show **closure-level permanent fixed-cost reduction, meaningful capacity removal, and enforceable delivery**, after accounting for one-time costs and implementation risk.
