I will respond plainly.

## Where I agree
I agree with the works council, the state, and even management on one fundamental point: **this company needs a durable restructuring, not a temporary patch**.

I also agree with the expert that the decisive issue is not whether change is needed, but whether the company can restore competitiveness **without making workers absorb all the pain**.

And I agree with management, the family holding, and investors on one more point: **labour cannot be the only contributor**. If there is to be a settlement, it must be a shared-sacrifice package.

## Where I disagree
I do **not** accept the repeated attempt to make **plant closures and compulsory redundancies** the baseline answer.

That is not a neutral economic fact. It is a choice.  
And it is a choice I reject unless management can prove, transparently and plant by plant, that there is truly no other way to close the gap.

So let me be clear:

- **No compulsory redundancies.**
- **No plant closures as part of this settlement.**
- **No tearing up of the collective bargaining framework.**
- **No one-sided burden on the workforce.**

Those are not bargaining slogans. They are the conditions under which I can negotiate seriously.

## To management
You keep saying the economics require deep change. I do not dispute that. But you have not yet proven that the only route is to shut plants or force people out.

If you want the union to move, then bring forward a **fully quantified alternative scenario** that includes:
- plant-by-plant utilization,
- fixed-cost gaps,
- overhead savings,
- procurement effects,
- product allocation,
- executive and central-function cuts,
- and the exact remaining gap after all non-closure measures.

Until then, closures are not justified. They are asserted.

If you really want a solution, then start by putting on the table:
- management restraint,
- executive restraint,
- overhead cuts,
- investment commitments,
- and a credible plan to absorb flexibility through working time, voluntary exits, redeployment, and qualification.

## To the works council
I support your refusal to accept forced layoffs and closures. That is the correct line.

But I also want to be constructive: the union is prepared to discuss **real contributions** from labour if the package is fair and balanced. That can include:
- working-time flexibility,
- temporary or structural adjustments to work schedules,
- voluntary exit instruments,
- partial retirement,
- redeployment,
- qualification,
- and an employee financial contribution if it is embedded in a broader deal.

What I will not accept is a package in which workers contribute while management and owners ask to keep their hands off the real levers.

## To the controlling family
If you mean stewardship, then prove it with money and patience, not only with words about discipline.

Stewardship means:
- supporting investment,
- accepting management and owner-side contribution,
- and not treating the workforce as the easiest adjustment variable.

If you want labour peace, you need to back a package that protects jobs and sites while restoring competitiveness. Not a package that quietly offloads the structural cost onto employees.

## To investors
You are right that a fake compromise would destroy value. I agree.

But a deal that tears up trust, triggers prolonged conflict, and destroys know-how is also value-destructive.  
So I reject the idea that the only “serious” solution is a closure-first approach.

If you want a value-preserving outcome, then support a package that is:
- durable,
- measurable,
- and socially executable.

That means labour flexibility, yes — but also management and owner-side sacrifice.

## To the regional state government
You are right to insist that the company matters for the region and for industrial continuity. I support that.

But your influence should be used to prevent the workforce from being forced to pay alone.  
That means insisting on:
- no closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- investment in German sites,
- and a settlement that preserves co-determination and the collective wage framework.

## On the moderator’s compromise paths
### 1) “No immediate closures” with audited trigger
This is only acceptable if it is **not** a hidden closure corridor.

I am not interested in a staged process designed to soften resistance now and reopen closures later. If there is a review, it must be based on independently audited numbers and agreed metrics, and it must first exhaust all non-closure measures. Even then, my position remains: **no closures**.

### 2) Shared-sacrifice package with quantified contribution layers
This is the right direction.  
But the layers must be truly shared:
- management cuts,
- owner contribution,
- company cost reduction,
- and labour flexibility.

Not labour first, labour most, labour alone.

### 3) Capacity adjustment without closure, with rationalization corridor
I reject the corridor.  
That is just a polite word for future closures. We should not pretend otherwise.

## What I am prepared to put on the table
I remain open to:
- working-time adjustments,
- redeployment,
- qualification,
- partial retirement,
- voluntary exits,
- capacity changes **without closures**,
- and a fair, limited employee contribution if it is matched by management and ownership.

I am also prepared to discuss a temporary contribution from labour if it is part of a package that protects:
- jobs,
- sites,
- collective bargaining,
- and a durable industrial future.

## What I am not prepared to accept
Let me repeat it clearly:
- **No forced redundancies.**
- **No plant closures.**
- **No dismantling of the collective wage agreement.**
- **No backdoor closure mechanism.**
- **No settlement where the workforce carries the pain while others remain protected.**

If that means management must work harder on alternatives, then yes, it does.

## Bottom line
The choice is not:
- accept closures and layoffs, or
- accept decline.

That is a false choice.

A serious company should be able to restore competitiveness through shared sacrifice, better planning, management and owner contributions, and labour flexibility **without** closing plants or forcing people out.

If management and investors refuse that, then they are not asking for a fair restructuring. They are asking the workforce to underwrite their preferred solution. I will not do that.
