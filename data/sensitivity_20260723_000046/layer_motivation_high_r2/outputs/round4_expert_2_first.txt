## Short answer

Based on the positions stated so far, the **economically decisive question** is not whether restructuring is needed — it is — but whether the company can close a **durable structural cost gap** with **non-closure measures alone**.

My assessment is:

- A **no-closure, no-forced-redundancy package** can sometimes close a moderate gap in an automotive plant network.
- But if the company’s German footprint is carrying **multiple layers of fixed cost** — excess capacity, underutilized lines, duplicated overhead, and structurally high labor/unit costs — then **soft measures alone are often insufficient**.
- In that case, some combination of:
  - capacity reduction,
  - line consolidation,
  - product reallocation,
  - and possibly footprint change
  is usually required to restore a sustainable cost base.

## What I can say with confidence

In automotive manufacturing, the cost problem is usually driven by **fixed-cost absorption**, not just hourly wages.

That means the key variable is:

- **utilization** of each plant,
- **volume per site**,
- **model mix and complexity**,
- **logistics distance and sequencing costs**,
- **overhead per vehicle**,
- and **how much capacity remains after demand weakens**.

If utilization falls below a certain point, the plant’s fixed costs get spread over too few vehicles, and unit costs rise sharply. At that stage, wage concessions alone rarely solve the problem.

To determine the **smallest package of capacity reduction and labor-cost relief** that could plausibly close the structural gap, I would need, plant by plant:

   - voluntary exits

   - permanent wage adjustments

9. **Closure cost / restructuring cost**
   - severance
   - transfer support
   - write-downs
   - remediation
   - changeover cost

Without that dataset, any view is necessarily directional rather than definitive.

## Directional assessment of the scenarios

### 1) No closures, no forced redundancies, but flexibility and voluntary exits
This can work **if the savings gap is modest** and the problem is mainly short-term overstaffing or temporary underutilization.

Economically, this is most plausible when:
- utilization can be lifted materially by shifting volume,
- a site has room to take additional models,
- and the company can reduce labor cost without changing the footprint.

But if the structural gap is large, this approach often produces only **partial savings** and may not restore competitiveness on a durable basis.

### 2) Capacity reduction without formal closures
This is often the most economically realistic compromise.

Examples:
- eliminating one shift,
- consolidating production into fewer lines,
- moving selected models between plants,
- reducing final assembly volume at a marginal site,
- converting a site to component, battery, or niche production.

This can achieve meaningful savings while avoiding a headline closure. It is frequently more effective than wage cuts alone because it attacks the **fixed-cost base**.

### 3) Full preservation of all sites plus only labor concessions
If the company’s issue is genuine overcapacity, this is usually the weakest option economically.

Why:
- wage concessions reduce variable cost,
- but overcapacity creates fixed costs that remain,
- so the company may still carry too much plant overhead per vehicle.

This package can be attractive socially, but from a plant economics perspective it often leaves the structural cost problem unresolved.

## My judgment on the minimum credible package

If the company truly has a structural competitiveness gap, the **smallest plausible package** is usually something like:

- **some capacity reduction** at one or more low-utilization sites,
- **no forced redundancies initially**, if voluntary exits and redeployment are sufficient,
- **targeted labor-cost relief**,
- **shift or working-time changes**,
- **overhead reductions at corporate and site level**,
- and **product/investment commitments** to improve remaining plant utilization.

In other words:  
**labor measures alone are rarely enough; capacity and footprint measures are usually needed if the gap is structural.**

## What would be inadequate on its own

From a site economics perspective, these are usually insufficient by themselves if the gap is meaningful:

- temporary wage restraint only,
- one-off voluntary exits only,
- management symbolism without footprint changes,
- promises of future productivity improvement without volume allocation,
- blanket site guarantees with no matching utilization plan.

These may buy time, but they do not necessarily fix the cost base.

## The key threshold

The decisive threshold is not a political one; it is an economic one:

> Can the company reach a competitive unit-cost structure at expected post-restructuring volume **without** closing or materially reconfiguring any site?

If yes, a no-closure solution may be viable.

If no, then the company is likely facing a **capacity/footprint problem**, not just a labor-cost problem.

## Bottom line

My expert assessment is:

- A pure no-closure package is only credible if the gap is **small to moderate** and there is enough room to recover utilization.
- If the gap is **structural and large**, then some degree of **capacity reduction and/or footprint rationalization** is economically hard to avoid.
- The most likely workable compromise, if negotiations succeed, is not “no change,” but **selected capacity reduction combined with labor flexibility and voluntary instruments**.
