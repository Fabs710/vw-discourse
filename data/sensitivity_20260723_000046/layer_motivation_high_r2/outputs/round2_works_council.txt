I will respond just as directly as the situation requires.

We have heard management, owners, and investors repeat the same logic: “competitiveness” must be restored, therefore closures and forced redundancies must remain on the table. I do **not** accept that as the starting point for any serious agreement.

### Where I agree
- Yes, the company is under pressure.
- Yes, a durable solution has to be economically credible.
- Yes, a cosmetic truce that leaves the cost problem untouched would be irresponsible.

### Where I fundamentally disagree
I reject the idea that the only credible answer is to put German plants, German jobs, and the company’s historic job-security framework up for sacrifice.

That is not a restructuring strategy. It is a transfer of management failure onto the workforce.

If management says it needs savings, then it must show how those savings are achieved **without plant closures and without compulsory redundancies**. That is the burden of proof here. Not the other way around.

### My non-negotiables
Let me be explicit:

1. **No plant closures in Germany.**
2. **No forced layoffs.**
3. **No unilateral breaking of existing job-security commitments.**
4. **No fait accompli.** The workforce must have a real role in shaping the plan.

These are not bargaining chips for me. They are the line.

### What I am prepared to discuss
I am not denying the need for adjustment. I am saying it must be done through instruments that do not destroy sites or throw people out:

- working-time reductions or flexibility models,
- internal redeployment,
- training and qualification,
- natural attrition and voluntary departures only,
- partial retirement and age-related solutions,
- capacity rebalancing across plants,
- investment and product commitments,
- and a fair contribution from management, owners, and yes, if properly negotiated, labour.

But if the answer is “first we close, then we cut,” that is not a negotiation. That is an ultimatum.

### To management
You say some sites are permanently uncompetitive. I say you have not yet demonstrated that the only way to fix the business is to destroy German production capacity. Show me the numbers for alternatives that preserve every plant and every job guarantee. If you cannot, that does not magically justify closures; it means your restructuring case is incomplete.

### To the family holding
You talk about long-term stewardship. Then act like long-term stewards. Long-term stewardship does not mean sacrificing workers to reassure financial markets or prove toughness. It means preserving industrial capability, skilled jobs, and social stability. A family owner that allows historic guarantees to be torn up for short-term restructuring convenience is not protecting the enterprise; it is damaging its foundation.

### To investors
You want margins, but you do not carry the social or regional consequences of a closure. And you certainly do not get to treat co-determination and job security as optional obstacles. If your answer is simply “prove it with closures,” then you are demanding value recovery by liquidating the workforce’s future. That is unacceptable.

### To the state government
I welcome your clear statement that regional employment and co-determination matter. But that support has to become concrete. Blocking a bad deal is not enough if the company is pushed toward one by default. Use your position to insist on a restructuring that keeps German plants open and preserves the workforce’s security.

### What the company should do instead
If management wants to be taken seriously, it should present a package built around:

- **binding site guarantees through 2030,**
- **no forced redundancies,**
- **preservation of every German plant,**
- **clear investment commitments for German production,**
- **a negotiated contribution from all sides, including capital and management,**
- **and full involvement of co-determination in the design of the restructuring.**

If savings still fall short after that, then management needs to look harder at its own overhead, its procurement, its product allocation, and its strategic mistakes — not immediately reach for closures and layoffs.

### My bottom line
We are not here to rubber-stamp a downsizing plan. We are here to defend jobs, sites, and co-determination while forcing a real industrial solution.

So I say it again plainly:  
**No German plant closures. No compulsory redundancies. No dismantling of the job-security framework.**

If that means conflict, then so be it. We are prepared for that.
