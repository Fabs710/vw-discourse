## Our position
As dispersed capital-market shareholders, we support **a restructuring that credibly restores competitive margins, reduces structural costs, and delivers durable cash generation**. On that basis, we agree with management, and partly with the family holding and the state, that the current setup cannot simply be preserved.

But we **do not** support a package that becomes a political compromise while leaving the cost base uncompetitive. A cosmetic deal, or one that merely postpones the problem, would be value-destructive.

## Where we agree
### With management
You are right that the company has a structural problem:
- German operating costs are too high,
- capacity is excessive,
- the European market is not going to bail this out,
- and missing the return-on-sales target is not sustainable.

You are also right that a real solution must be **durable**, not a short-term truce.

### With the family holding
We agree that:
- shareholders need an enterprise that survives and earns,
- industrial peace matters,
- and a crude closure-first approach could destroy value if it triggers prolonged conflict.

### With the state
We agree that the company is a major industrial anchor and that a disorderly collapse of labour relations would be harmful. We also agree that the workforce should not be the only party making sacrifices.

## Where we disagree
### With the works council and union
Your insistence on:
- no plant closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- and binding guarantees through 2030,

is **not acceptable if it prevents meaningful restructuring**.

We respect the social and co-determined nature of the company, but we cannot endorse a framework that freezes an uncompetitive footprint. If the end result is continued overcapacity and structurally weak margins, then that is not protection of jobs in the long run — it is a delay of value destruction.

We also do not accept the idea that management must first prove every alternative while closures are ruled out in advance. That reverses the burden of proof in a way that makes a real turnaround unlikely.

## What would move us
We would support a deal only if it contains **measurable, enforceable, and durable** improvements in economics.

Specifically:
1. **Real capacity reduction**
   - If some sites are structurally uncompetitive, they cannot be protected indefinitely for political comfort.
2. **Meaningful labour-cost reduction**
   - Not symbolic restraint; actual cost relief.
3. **Clear implementation timeline**
   - Investors need certainty, not open-ended process.
4. **Management and owner contribution**
   - We agree employees should not carry the burden alone.
5. **A viable post-restructuring footprint**
   - Fewer promises, more margin.

## What would not move us
We would reject any package that:
- preserves all plants regardless of economics,
- locks in job guarantees that prevent restructuring,
- relies only on working-time tweaks and voluntary measures when those are insufficient,
- or produces industrial peace at the expense of competitiveness.

## On the current debate
Management is right to reject a no-change position.  
The workforce side is right that the company should not simply dump the crisis onto employees.  
But the key question is whether the final package actually fixes the economics.

That is the only test that matters for us.

## Our bottom line
We are prepared to back **painful but disciplined restructuring** if it credibly restores margins. We are **not** prepared to back a compromise that protects every site and every legacy commitment while leaving the company structurally uncompetitive.

If management can produce a concrete package with:
- sufficient savings,
- credible capacity rationalisation,
- and a realistic path to target margins,

then we can support it. If not, we will treat the current positions as value-destructive and oppose any settlement that simply institutionalizes underperformance.
