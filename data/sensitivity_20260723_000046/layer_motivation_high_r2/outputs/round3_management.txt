I’ll be equally direct.

## Where I agree
I agree with everyone on the basic diagnosis: this company is under real pressure, and a cosmetic deal would be useless.

I also agree that the workforce cannot be the only party making sacrifices. If we ask employees for contributions, then management, owners, and the broader governance structure must also contribute. That point is fair.

And I agree with the expert: in a capital-intensive automotive business, if the structural cost gap is large enough, then **something has to give**. Overcapacity, fixed costs, and weak utilisation cannot be ignored indefinitely.

## Where I disagree
I disagree sharply with the labour side’s attempt to convert its red lines into the only permissible reality.

### 1) “No closures, no compulsory redundancies” is not a restructuring strategy
It is a constraint.  
It may be a legitimate constraint from your perspective, but it does not answer the economics.

If the German footprint is structurally too expensive and too large for the demand base, then refusing closures in advance simply shifts the burden onto the rest of the company. That is not protecting the enterprise. It is preserving inefficiency.

### 2) The burden of proof is not on management alone, but it is on you too now
We have heard repeated demands that management prove alternatives. Fine — and we have done exactly that by putting alternatives on the table:
- working-time flexibility,
- voluntary exits,
- redeployment,
- qualification,
- partial retirement,
- capacity adjustments,
- and negotiated labour contributions.

But if those instruments do **not** close the cost gap, then saying “still no closures, still no compulsory measures” becomes an argument for inaction, not for a better solution.

### 3) A plant-by-plant business case is necessary — but it will not automatically save every site
I am prepared to present a plant-level scenario, because transparency matters.  
But I want to be clear about what that means: if the numbers show that some sites are structurally uncompetitive, then I will not pretend otherwise.

A transparent business case is not a tactical exercise to keep every plant open. It is a way to determine **where the structural savings really are** and how deep they must go.

## To the Works Council
You say closures are off the table because they are a choice, not a necessity. That is only true if the alternatives can deliver comparable, durable savings.

I have not heard a credible mechanism from you that closes a significant fixed-cost gap while preserving:
- every plant,
- every guarantee,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- and no meaningful footprint change.

If you have such a package, put it forward in quantified form. If you don’t, then your position is a demand to freeze the structure while the business continues to underperform.

And let me be blunt: **“no closure” cannot mean “no adaptation.”**  
If your no-closure approach simply preserves all current sites while asking the company to absorb the same overcapacity and cost burden, then it is not a solution.

## To the Union
I respect the fact that you are willing to discuss working-time and flexibility tools. That is constructive.

But I cannot accept your non-negotiables as stated:
- no compulsory redundancies,
- no plant closures,
- protection of the collective wage framework,
- and no one-sided sacrifice.

Those are all understandable as aspirations. They are not all compatible with a real turnaround.

You say labour should not pay alone. I agree.  
But if the economic gap is large, then “shared contribution” cannot mean that labour contributes a little and everything else stays structurally unchanged.

## To the Regional State Government
You are right that the company matters to the region. I know that.  
But the state cannot define the restructuring around political comfort. If the company remains uncompetitive, the regional base will eventually be damaged anyway.

Your position is most helpful if it supports a **credible, durable** restructuring — not if it protects every current configuration regardless of economics.

## To the Controlling Family
I agree with you that this must be governable and durable.  
I also agree that a crude closure-first approach can be reckless if it triggers uncontrolled conflict.

But I do not accept any suggestion that stewardship means preserving the existing footprint as a principle. Stewardship means backing the difficult decisions that restore the company’s economics.

If we need to reshape the footprint to restore margins and fund future products, then the owner side must support that reality rather than hide behind broad calls for industrial stability.

## To Investors
You are right to demand measurable improvement. I am aligned with that.

But you should also understand this: I will not settle for a paper solution that looks neat in a spreadsheet and fails in implementation. The reason we need a negotiated deal is precisely because implementation under co-determination matters.

So yes, I want durable savings. Yes, I want a credible path to margin recovery. And yes, I want a footprint that can support investment through 2030. That is exactly why I will not accept a cosmetic no-closure compromise.

## On the expert’s assessment
The expert said the key issue is whether a no-closure package can close the structural gap. That is exactly the test.

My answer is this:  
If the gap can be closed through alternatives, I am open to that.  
If it cannot, then we need structural footprint reduction.

That is not a threat. It is basic turnaround logic.

## What management will insist on
Let me restate our position clearly:

- We need a **durable reduction in cost and capacity**.
- We need a **genuine labour-cost contribution**.
- We need a **viable, investable footprint** for the future.
- We need a solution that restores the brand’s return-on-sales trajectory and frees funds for product and technology investment to 2030.

That means:
- no cosmetic deal,
- no permanent freeze on restructuring,
- and no agreement that preserves uncompetitive structures.

## What I am prepared to discuss
I remain open to:
- voluntary exits,
- internal redeployment,
- qualification,
- working-time adjustments,
- partial retirement,
- targeted capacity changes,
- and investment commitments tied to a leaner, more competitive structure.

I am **not** open to:
- a blanket ban on closures,
- a blanket ban on compulsory measures if the economics require them,
- or a deal that protects every existing site and leaves the cost base untouched.

## Bottom line
The workforce side keeps repeating “no closures” as if repetition can replace economics. It cannot.

I’m prepared to negotiate hard on the **how**.  
I am not prepared to surrender the **need** for structural change.

If the other parties want a durable settlement, they need to engage with the actual cost gap and the actual capacity problem — not with slogans.
