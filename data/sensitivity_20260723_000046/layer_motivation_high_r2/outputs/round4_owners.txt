I’ll be direct.

## Where I agree
I agree with management, investors, and the expert on the core point: **this company has a structural competitiveness problem, not just a temporary earnings issue**. If we do nothing material, we will erode value over time. That would be a failure of stewardship.

I also agree with the labour side and the state on one important point: **a value-preserving restructuring must be executable under co-determination and must avoid a prolonged industrial war**. A “hard” plan that is socially ungovernable is not a solution; it is a trigger for further damage.

And I agree that the workforce cannot be the only side contributing. If we ask employees for sacrifices, then management, ownership, and the broader cost structure must also contribute materially.

## Where I disagree
I do **not** accept the labour side’s attempt to convert:
- no German plant closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- and full preservation of job-security guarantees

into absolute preconditions.

That is not a restructuring strategy. It is a veto on the very instruments that may be needed to restore competitiveness.

I also do **not** accept the state’s position if it is meant to freeze the industrial footprint regardless of economics. Co-determination and regional responsibility matter, but they do not repeal the cost base problem.

## On the works council
You are right that management must not hide behind slogans like “competitiveness” without data. I support your demand for a plant-by-plant business case.

But I do **not** accept your conclusion that because management has not yet proven closures are unavoidable, closures are therefore off the table. That is too absolute.

If the plant-level numbers show a persistent fixed-cost gap after non-closure measures, then refusing any footprint change is not defending jobs in the long run; it is postponing the damage.

## On the union
You are correct that labour should not be the sole shock absorber. That is fair.

But your non-negotiables are too rigid if they are meant to hold regardless of the economics. “No compulsory redundancies” and “no closures” may be understandable preferences, but they cannot be treated as unconditional business rules.

If a credible turnaround requires more than voluntary exits and working-time flexibility, then we have to face that honestly.

## On management
I agree with management that the restructuring must be real, not cosmetic. I also agree that the business case has to be serious, quantified, and durable.

Where I would push back is on any rush toward closure-first tactics. That is too blunt and too risky. If the company goes down that road too aggressively, it risks execution failure, reputational damage, and prolonged conflict that destroys value.

So: yes to hard numbers, yes to structural change if necessary, no to recklessness.

## On the regional state
I respect the state’s concern for industrial continuity and regional employment. That concern is legitimate.

But the state cannot demand a solution that preserves every current configuration if the economics do not support it. The state’s most constructive role is to support a hard but governable restructuring, not to block adjustment altogether.

## On the expert
The expert has clarified the central issue correctly:  
**Can non-closure measures close the structural gap, or does the company need some degree of capacity reduction and footprint rationalisation?**

That is the right test.

What matters to me is not whether a solution is emotionally comfortable. It is whether it is economically sufficient and executable without destroying the company’s governance stability.

## My position
I remain open to:
- working-time flexibility,
- voluntary exits,
- redeployment,
- partial retirement,
- overhead and management cuts,
- targeted capacity reduction,
- product allocation changes,
- and investment commitments tied to a leaner structure.

I am **not** open to:
- a blanket promise that all German plants remain open regardless of economics,
- a permanent freeze on restructuring,
- or a deal that preserves all legacy protections while leaving the cost base untouched.

## What would move me
Three things:

1. **A plant-by-plant business case**  
   Show me the fixed-cost gap, utilization, model allocation, and residual savings need after softer measures.

2. **A quantified shared-sacrifice package**  
   Management, owners, and labour all need material contributions.

3. **A credible implementation path under co-determination**  
   If the plan cannot survive the governance environment, it is not a plan.

## My bottom line
I support a **serious restructuring that restores competitiveness and margins**.

I do **not** support a deal that simply protects every site by principle and leaves the structural problem unresolved.

If non-closure measures can truly close the gap, fine — prove it and I will support that route.  
If they cannot, then capacity reduction and footprint change must remain on the table.

That is the line I will hold.
