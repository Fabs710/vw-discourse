I’ll be equally direct.

## Where I agree
I agree with everyone on the basic diagnosis: the company is in a serious crisis, and we need a durable solution, not a headline deal that leaves the cost base untouched.

I also agree that labour cannot be the only side contributing. If we are asking employees for sacrifices, then management, owners, and investors must also contribute materially. That is fair, and I have said so repeatedly.

And I agree with the expert’s central point: **the real test is whether non-closure measures can close the structural gap**. That is the right question.

## Where I disagree
I do **not** accept the works council and union’s attempt to turn:
- no plant closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- and the full preservation of job-security guarantees

into absolute preconditions.

That is not a restructuring strategy. It is a constraint set that may sound principled, but it does not by itself solve the economics.

### 1) “No closures” is not a substitute for a business case
You keep saying management has not proven closures are necessary. Fair.  
But that does not mean closures are off the table by default.

We have a structural problem:
- German factory costs are too high,
- the European market is shrinking,
- competition is intensifying,
- and the brand is missing its return-on-sales target.

If you want to rule out footprint reduction, then you need to show, with numbers, how the gap closes another way. Not with slogans, not with moral assertions, but with a verified savings bridge.

### 2) A no-closure position only works if it is economically equivalent
I am open to alternatives **if** they deliver comparable, durable savings. That is the condition.

What I am **not** open to is a package that:
- preserves every site,
- preserves all legacy guarantees,
- avoids meaningful capacity reduction,
- and still claims to restore competitiveness.

That would be a cosmetic compromise. It would not free the funds we need for future products and technology to 2030.

### 3) The burden of proof cuts both ways
The labour side says management must prove closures are unavoidable.  
I agree that management must present the data.

But if management presents plant-by-plant economics showing a structural gap remains after non-closure measures, then the burden shifts. At that point, the argument cannot just be “still no closures.” At that point, you are asking the company to carry permanent overcapacity.

That is not protecting jobs in the long run. It is postponing the damage.

## To the Works Council
You say closure is a choice, not a necessity. Sometimes that is true. Sometimes it is not.

If you want to keep every German plant open, then bring forward a **credible alternative package** that closes the cost gap through:
- working-time flexibility,
- shift simplification,
- redeployment,
- voluntary exits,
- partial retirement,
- product allocation,
- overhead cuts,
- and a real labour-cost contribution.

If you cannot close the gap with those tools, then you are not defending a viable structure. You are defending the status quo.

And let me be blunt: **a no-closure position that leaves the cost base uncompetitive is not a solution.**

## To the Union
I respect that you are willing to discuss flexibility, voluntary exits, and partial retirement. That is constructive.

But your non-negotiables as stated are too rigid:
- no compulsory redundancies,
- no plant closures,
- full protection of the collective wage framework,
- and no one-sided sacrifice.

Those are understandable as labour goals. They are not all compatible with a deep turnaround.

I need more than general willingness. I need quantified, binding contributions that actually move the economics. If the savings gap is large, then labour’s contribution must be real — not symbolic.

## To the Works Council and Union together
You keep warning about trust, know-how, and industrial peace. I do not dismiss that. A prolonged conflict would be bad for everyone.

But let’s be honest: **the biggest threat to trust is a company that cannot restore competitiveness and then drifts into decline anyway**. That is what destroys jobs over time.

A durable settlement must do three things at once:
1. Reduce structural cost,
2. Improve capacity alignment,
3. Restore margin and investment power.

If your red lines prevent that, then they are not protecting the workforce. They are protecting an uneconomic structure.

## To the Regional State Government
I respect your role and your concern for regional legitimacy. But the state cannot freeze industrial reality.

If the company’s footprint is too large for the demand base, then the answer cannot be to block adjustment indefinitely. That would eventually weaken the industrial base the state is trying to protect.

Your most constructive role is to support a solution that is:
- economically credible,
- socially manageable,
- and implementable under co-determination.

Not to insist that no structural change is allowed at all.

## To the Controlling Family Holding
I agree that stewardship matters. If the family wants long-term industrial strength, then it has to support hard decisions.

But stewardship does **not** mean preserving every site at any cost. It means backing the choices that restore competitiveness and future investment capacity.

So yes, I expect owner-side contribution and governance support. But I also expect the owner side to back a restructuring that is actually tough enough to work. Not one that merely preserves appearances.

## To Institutional Investors
I agree with you on the need for measurable improvement. A soft compromise that leaves the cost base uncompetitive would destroy value.

But I also want to be clear: I will not support a paper solution that looks neat financially and fails in implementation because co-determination and labour resistance were ignored.

So I need a package that is both:
- economically sufficient,
- and executable in the real governance environment.

That is why I want the numbers on the table.

## On the expert’s intervention
The expert has been useful in one critical respect: they confirmed that small, incremental tools are unlikely to be enough if the structural gap is large.

That matters.

If the data show that non-closure measures cannot close the gap, then we cannot pretend otherwise. If the data show they can, then fine — we can work with that. But the decision must be made on evidence, not on absolutes.

So yes: I support the call for a plant-by-plant business case. Not because I want closures for their own sake, but because we need to know what is actually viable.

## On the moderator’s compromise paths
### 1) “No immediate closures” with audited trigger
This can only work if the review mechanism is real and not just a delay tactic.

I am not interested in a process that defers hard decisions forever. If we adopt a staged approach, it must be tied to:
- clear savings milestones,
- audited performance,
- and pre-agreed consequences if the gap remains open.

That is not a trap. That is discipline.

### 2) Shared-sacrifice package with quantified contribution layers
This is the right direction.

But “shared” has to mean everyone contributes:
- labour through flexibility and cost contribution,
- management through overhead and executive restraint,
- owners through capital and investment support,
- and the company through portfolio and capacity discipline.

No one side can be exempt if we are serious.

### 3) Capacity adjustment without closure, with an optional rationalization corridor
I will be honest: if this becomes a backdoor ban on any future footprint change, then it will not be credible.

A corridor is only meaningful if it is reserved for cases where the data prove that the non-closure route fails. I am not seeking a loophole to force closures regardless. I am seeking a credible route to structural savings if the softer measures do not add up.

## What I am prepared to do
I remain open to:
- working-time flexibility,
- voluntary exit instruments,
- redeployment,
- partial retirement,
- qualification,
- targeted capacity changes,
- overhead and management cost cuts,
- and investment commitments for the German sites.

I am also prepared to discuss a no-immediate-closure phase, **if** it is paired with a serious, audited restructuring plan and a real test of whether equivalent savings can be achieved.

## What I am not prepared to do
I will not accept:
- a blanket ban on closures,
- a blanket ban on compulsory measures if the economics require them,
- or a deal that preserves every legacy guarantee while leaving the cost base structurally uncompetitive.

That would not restore competitiveness. It would defer the crisis.

## Bottom line
The choice is not between social peace and competitiveness. The choice is between:
- a restructuring that actually fixes the cost base, or
- a compromise that preserves too much and solves too little.

I am prepared to negotiate hard on the **how**. I am not prepared to surrender the **need** for deep, durable structural change.

If the labour side wants to keep every plant open, then it must prove that this is economically sustainable. If it cannot, then we have to face the structural reality.
