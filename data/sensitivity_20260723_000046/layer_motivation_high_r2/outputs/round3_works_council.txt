I will be very clear again, because the pressure is clearly now being used to try to move the burden onto the workforce.

## My position has not changed
I do **not** accept:
- German plant closures,
- compulsory redundancies,
- or tearing up the job-security framework.

Those are not “starting points” for negotiation from my side. They are the line. If someone thinks repeating “competitiveness” enough times will make us accept that line being crossed, they are mistaken.

## Where I agree
I agree with the expert, and partly with management, on one thing:  
**the company does need durable restructuring, not a cosmetic pause.**

I also agree that a weak or fake compromise would be irresponsible. If the business cannot be made viable, then simply postponing the problem would hurt workers as well.

But that is precisely why I reject the lazy conclusion that the only serious instruments are closures and layoffs.

## Where I disagree with management, investors, and the family
### 1) You have not proven that closures are unavoidable
The expert says the key question is whether non-closure measures can close the structural gap. Exactly. That question has not been answered conclusively.

So I reject the attempt to present closures as the default “reality.”  
Reality is not a slogan. It is a business case.

If management wants to argue for closures, then it must show:
- plant-level utilization,
- fixed-cost gaps,
- alternative savings,
- and why internal measures, redeployment, working-time changes, procurement, overhead cuts, and product allocation are insufficient.

Until that is done transparently, closures are not justified — they are a choice.

### 2) “No closure” is not the same as “no restructuring”
This is where I need to correct the repeated distortion in the room.

We are **not** saying “do nothing.”  
We are saying:
- no closure,
- no forced redundancies,
- but yes to serious restructuring through other means.

That can include:
- capacity reductions without shutdowns,
- shift adjustments,
- working-time flexibility,
- voluntary exit instruments,
- partial retirement,
- redeployment,
- qualification,
- overhead reduction,
- procurement savings,
- and fair contributions from management and ownership.

If those instruments are not enough, then management must explain why its own past planning failed so badly that the first response is to dismantle plants and jobs.

### 3) The burden of proof is still on management
Management says the package must be economically credible. Fine. Then prove it with data.

I will not accept the inversion where labour is told:
“Accept closures unless you can prove an alternative at your own expense.”

No.  
Management is the one proposing structural destruction. Management has to prove necessity.

## On the expert advisor’s intervention
The expert has at least been honest: a no-closure deal is only viable if the savings gap is not too large, and if the numbers support it.

That is exactly why I insist on a plant-by-plant business case. Not because I am open to closure — I am not — but because management has to stop hiding behind general statements.

If the numbers show that the company needs more savings, then management should start first with:
- its own overhead,
- executive restraint,
- procurement,
- product allocation,
- and capital discipline.

Only after every one of those has been exhausted would it even be legitimate to talk about moving production out of German plants — and I do not accept that as the outcome anyway.

## On the moderator’s compromise paths
### Path 1: phased restructuring with review trigger
This is the kind of thing management likes because it creates a trap:  
you accept a “temporary” protection period, then later the same pressure returns and the workforce is told the review demands closures anyway.

I do **not** accept a staged process that is designed to soften resistance today and reopen the closure agenda tomorrow.

### Path 2: hybrid footprint reduction
That is simply a euphemism for closures by another name.  
If a site is reduced to the point where its future is undermined, then you are not preserving the plant — you are paving the way for closure.

I reject that logic.

### Path 3: no closures but equivalent savings
This is the only direction worth discussing, and only if it is real.

But “equivalent savings” must not become a hidden route to layoffs.  
It must mean:
- real management and owner contributions,
- real flexibility,
- real cost discipline,
- and real industrial planning,
while keeping every German plant open and every worker protected from compulsory dismissal.

## To management
If you want us to take your case seriously, then stop using the plant workforce as the adjustment variable.

Bring forward:
- a full alternative scenario without closures,
- a quantified cost bridge,
- executive and overhead cuts,
- investment commitments,
- and a guarantee that there will be no forced redundancies.

If you cannot do that, then your restructuring plan is incomplete and politically contaminated.

## To the family holding
If you really mean stewardship, then back it with capital and patience.  
Long-term stewardship does **not** mean threatening workers first and calling it responsibility.

You have the ability to support a solution that keeps the industrial base intact. Use it. Do not hide behind “discipline” while asking workers to bear the consequences of strategic errors.

## To investors
You are focused on margins. I understand that. But your preferred solution can destroy the very basis of value if it tears apart the workforce, trust, and co-determination architecture.

A company cannot be run like a spreadsheet detached from industrial reality.  
And a “clean” restructuring that triggers conflict, demoralization, and loss of know-how is not clean at all.

## To the regional state government
You are right that the state should not rubber-stamp decline. But the state’s role is not to help management break the social contract.  
Use your power to enforce a solution that:
- keeps plants open,
- protects jobs,
- preserves co-determination,
- and secures investment in the German sites.

If the state allows closures through the back door, it will have abandoned its responsibility.

## What I will accept
I will discuss:
- working-time flexibility,
- voluntary departures only,
- internal redeployment,
- training and qualification,
- partial retirement,
- capacity rebalancing **without plant closures**,
- and a fair, negotiated contribution from all sides.

I will also discuss binding commitments on:
- investment,
- product allocation,
- and a genuine role for co-determination in the restructuring design.

## What I will not accept
Let me repeat the non-negotiables once more:
- **No German plant closures.**
- **No compulsory redundancies.**
- **No dismantling of the job-security framework.**
- **No solution imposed as a fait accompli.**

If the price of “restructuring” is that workers lose their sites and their protection, then that is not a balanced solution — it is surrendering the company’s future for a short-term accounting fix.

## Bottom line
The discussion is now trying to force a false choice: either accept closures and layoffs, or accept decline.

I reject that false choice.

If the company wants a durable solution, it must prove that the business can be repaired **without crossing those red lines**. Until then, my answer remains the same: **no closures, no forced layoffs, no breaking of the social contract.**
