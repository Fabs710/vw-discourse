Based on the exchange so far, my assessment within plant economics is:

## Bottom line
A **pure no-closure / no-forced-redundancy package** is only credible if the company’s structural gap is **moderate** and there is enough unused capacity to recover utilization materially.  
If the gap is **large and durable**, then **labor measures alone are very unlikely to close it**. In that case, some combination of:
- **capacity reduction**,  
- **line consolidation**,  
- **product reallocation**, and possibly  
- **site rationalization / closure**  

becomes economically hard to avoid.

## What likely matters most economically
In automotive manufacturing, the main issue is usually **fixed-cost absorption**, not just hourly labor cost.

The key variables are:
- plant utilization,
- vehicles per site,
- model complexity,
- duplicated overhead,
- logistics and sequencing costs,
- and the cost of keeping underused capacity open.

If utilization is too low, unit costs rise sharply. Wage restraint can help, but it often does **not** fix an overcapacity problem on its own.

## What I would call the smallest plausible package
If management is right that the German footprint is structurally too expensive, the **smallest economically plausible package** is usually:

1. **Targeted capacity reduction** at the weakest / most underutilized sites  
2. **Voluntary exits, redeployment, partial retirement** instead of forced layoffs where possible  
3. **Working-time flexibility / shift simplification**  
4. **Management and central overhead cuts**  
5. **Product and volume reallocation** to improve remaining plant utilization  
6. **Investment commitments** for the retained sites

That combination can sometimes preserve most employment while still improving the cost base.

## What would be inadequate on its own
These are usually not enough if the structural gap is meaningful:
- temporary wage concessions only,
- voluntary exits only,
- promises of future efficiency gains,
- management cost cuts without footprint change,
- or blanket guarantees that preserve all sites unchanged.

They may buy time, but they often do not restore competitiveness sustainably.

## My judgment on the compromise options
- **No immediate closures + audited trigger:** economically reasonable as a sequencing device, but only if it is tied to hard metrics and not used to postpone action indefinitely.
- **Shared-sacrifice package:** likely necessary. Labour alone cannot carry the adjustment.
- **Capacity adjustment without closure:** often the most realistic middle path if the company wants to avoid outright closures.

To say whether a no-closure solution is sufficient, I would need plant-by-plant:

- and savings from each proposed measure.

Without that, the best I can say is directional:  
**if the gap is structural and substantial, footprint reduction is likely required; if it is smaller, a negotiated non-closure package may work.**
