I’ll respond directly.

### Where I agree
I agree with everyone on one basic point: **the company is in a real crisis and a cosmetic solution is worthless**.  
I also agree that a negotiated outcome is preferable to a prolonged strike. A strike damages output, customers, reputation, and ultimately the very jobs everyone is trying to defend.

I also agree that labour should not be the only side contributing. If the workforce is asked to accept pain, then management, owners, and the broader governance structure must also contribute.

### Where I disagree
I disagree very clearly with the repeated demand for **absolute exclusions**:
- no plant closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- no meaningful structural adjustment,
- and yet a claim that the company must still somehow become competitive.

That is not a plan. That is a refusal to face the economics.

The German cost base is too high. Capacity is too high. The European market is shrinking. If we try to preserve every site, every guarantee, and every existing structure, then we will not save the company — we will lock in decline.

### To the Works Council
I respect the mandate to defend employees. But your position is too absolute.

Saying “preserve every German plant and every job guarantee” may sound protective, but if a site is structurally uncompetitive, then insisting on its survival by force just spreads the crisis across the rest of the group. That is not solidarity; it is deferral.

You ask management to prove alternatives first. Fair enough — and we are doing that. But if those alternatives cannot deliver the necessary cost and capacity reduction, then refusing closures in advance makes the entire process impossible.

So let me be explicit:  
**I cannot accept a framework that rules out plant closures from the outset.**

### To the Union
I agree with you on one thing: a one-sided sacrifice by employees is unacceptable. But you are going too far if you insist on **no compulsory redundancies and no closures as non-negotiable** while also expecting management to deliver a serious turnaround.

If we are to protect as many jobs as possible, then we need a package that has real economic effect. That may include:
- capacity reduction,
- site rationalisation,
- labour-cost contributions,
- flexible working-time arrangements,
- voluntary exits where sufficient,
- and, where unavoidable, compulsory measures as part of a negotiated framework.

I am not saying compulsory redundancies are the first choice. I am saying I will not rule out the instruments required to restore competitiveness.

### To the Regional State Government
I hear your concern about regional stability, and I understand the political responsibility. But I need to be blunt: a state-backed posture that blocks difficult decisions can become part of the problem.

Industrial continuity is not preserved by freezing the footprint. It is preserved by making sure the company remains viable enough to invest, employ, and compete. That means:
- capacity adjustments,
- potential plant rationalisation,
- and a durable reduction in structural cost.

If the state’s role is used to veto every hard decision, then the result will be weaker competitiveness, not stronger regional security.

### To the Controlling Family
You say you want long-term stewardship. Then long-term stewardship means supporting decisions that restore the company’s economics, even when they are uncomfortable.

I agree that closures are a blunt instrument. But I disagree with any implication that structural adjustment can be achieved while preserving the current industrial footprint everywhere. That is not stewardship — that is avoidance.

If you want this company to have a strong future, then you need to back a package that creates room for investment, margin repair, and future products. That requires real savings, not symbolic ones.

### To Institutional Investors
You are right to insist on measurable, durable savings. I agree with that. But I also want to be clear: we are not interested in shareholder optics or a headline-driven restructuring. We need implementation.

Where I differ from you is that I do not believe the only credible route is simply to demand closures and cuts and then stop there. The package must also be **negotiable and executable** under co-determination. Otherwise it is just a spreadsheet exercise.

### What management is prepared to discuss
We are prepared to discuss a package built around:
- significant capacity reduction,
- labour-cost reduction,
- working-time flexibility,
- voluntary exits,
- redeployment and qualification,
- and investment commitments tied to a viable footprint.

But let me state the limit clearly:

**A package that says “no closures, no compulsory redundancies, no meaningful structural change” does not meet the company’s needs.**

That is not a negotiable middle ground. That is an outcome I cannot accept, because it does not restore competitiveness and does not free the funds needed for future technology and products through 2030.

### On the trade-offs
If the other stakeholders want job and site guarantees, then they must accept one simple economic truth: **guarantees must be financed**.

That means:
- labour-cost contributions,
- flexibility in working arrangements,
- acceptance of capacity reduction,
- and willingness to reshape the footprint.

If those trade-offs are rejected, then there is no serious basis for a durable agreement.

### Bottom line
Management is not asking for pain for its own sake. We are asking for a **structural reset** that makes the business competitive again.

So my position remains:
- I am open to negotiation.
- I am open to alternatives.
- I am open to voluntary tools and shared contributions.
- I am **not** open to a deal that protects every plant and every guarantee while leaving the cost base uncompetitive.

If the workforce side wants to avoid closures, it must bring forward a package that delivers comparable, durable savings. Otherwise, the reality will force the issue.
