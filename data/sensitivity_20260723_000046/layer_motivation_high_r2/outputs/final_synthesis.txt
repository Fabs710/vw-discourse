## 1. Decision context
The company faces a structural competitiveness crisis: overcapacity, high German operating costs, weak returns, and pressure from lower-cost rivals. Because the governance structure gives the workforce, the state, and the controlling family effective blocking power, the decision is not simply about selecting a restructuring instrument but about finding a package that can survive co-determination, political legitimacy, and capital-market scrutiny.

## 2. Stakeholder assembly
- **Group & Brand Management**: drives restructuring and argues that durable savings require structural change.
- **Works Council**: represents plant-level workforce interests and holds half the supervisory-board seats.
- **Industrial Union**: collective-bargaining actor with strike capacity.
- **Regional State Government**: anchor shareholder and political guarantor of regional employment and co-determination.
- **Controlling Family Holding**: majority voting-rights owner with governance influence.
- **Institutional Investors**: dispersed capital-market shareholders seeking value preservation and credible execution.
- **Independent Expert**: provided plant-economics assessment of what measures can realistically close the gap.

## 3. Final positions
### Group & Brand Management
Management’s final position is that the company needs durable fixed-cost reduction and capacity alignment; it does not accept a blanket no-closure/no-forced-redundancy constraint. It is, however, open to a staged process if non-closure measures are demonstrably sufficient, and insists on plant-by-plant economics, quantified savings, and a credible implementation path.

### Works Council
The works council holds firm red lines: no German plant closures, no compulsory redundancies, and no dismantling of job-security protections. It is willing to discuss working-time flexibility, redeployment, voluntary exits, partial retirement, and capacity reduction within existing sites, but rejects any mechanism that functions as a hidden closure corridor.

### Industrial Union
The union aligns closely with the works council on refusing closures and forced redundancies, while insisting labour cannot be the sole adjustment variable. It is open to temporary or structured labour contributions if management, owners, and the company itself make material sacrifices as part of a balanced deal.

### Regional State Government
The state rejects closures and easy recourse to compulsory redundancies, and wants to preserve industrial continuity, regional jobs, and co-determination. It demands transparent plant-level proof before any irreversible footprint change and is willing to support a hard but socially managed restructuring if it is economically credible.

### Controlling Family Holding
The family shares the view that the company needs serious restructuring and cannot live with a cosmetic solution. It does not accept absolute labour red lines as a veto on necessary change, but also recognizes that any solution must remain governable under co-determination and avoid a destructive labour conflict.

### Institutional Investors
Investors support restructuring that delivers durable cost reduction and margin recovery, and they reject a settlement that protects every site while preserving too much fixed cost. They are open to non-closure solutions only if they are quantitatively sufficient and implementation-safe; otherwise they favor deeper structural change.

### Independent Expert
The expert’s position is that a pure no-closure/no-forced-redundancy package can work only if the structural gap is moderate. If the cost gap is large and durable, then labor measures alone are unlikely to suffice; some mix of capacity reduction, line consolidation, product reallocation, and possibly footprint rationalization becomes economically hard to avoid.

## 4. Genuine conflicts
1. **Whether footprint change may be used at all**  
   Labour and the state treat closures as outside the acceptable settlement; management, the family, and investors insist they must remain available if economics require them.

2. **Whether forced redundancies can ever be a fallback**  
   Labour rejects compulsory layoffs categorically; management and investors will not rule them out if alternatives fail.

3. **Whether “review triggers” are safeguards or traps**  
   Labour sees phased review mechanisms as potential delayed closure paths; management and investors see blanket no-closure commitments as a disguised veto on restructuring.

4. **How burden-sharing is defined**  
   Labour demands quantified contributions from management, owners, and the company before asking workforce sacrifices; management and investors want labor contributions to be economically material and not symbolic.

## 5. Areas of convergence
- All parties accept that the company is in serious crisis and needs a durable solution.
- All parties prefer negotiated settlement over escalation or industrial conflict.
- Everyone acknowledges that labour cannot be the only contributor.
- There is broad openness to working-time flexibility, redeployment, training, voluntary exits, and some form of capacity adjustment.
- All sides accept the need for a plant-by-plant business case and more transparent numbers.
- There is shared recognition that any solution must be executable under co-determination and not merely optimal on paper.

## 6. Key trade-offs for the decision-maker
1. **Industrial continuity vs. structural competitiveness**  
   Preserving every German site supports social stability and political legitimacy, but may leave the company with an uncompetitive cost base.

2. **Governability vs. decisiveness**  
   A package that is too hard on labour may fail in co-determination; a package that is too soft may fail financially.

3. **Short-term labor peace vs. long-term restructuring credibility**  
   A no-closure settlement may secure peace now, but if it does not close the cost gap it may only postpone a more damaging crisis.

4. **Burden-sharing vs. clarity of responsibility**  
   Distributed sacrifices improve fairness and legitimacy, but make it harder to isolate who must yield if the numbers remain insufficient.

## 7. Integrative pathways
### Pathway A: Sequenced restructuring with hard data gates
- Initial no-closure, no-forced-redundancy phase.
- Immediate non-closure measures: working-time changes, voluntary exits, redeployment, overhead cuts, procurement savings.
- Independent plant-by-plant audit and pre-agreed metrics.
- If targets are missed, a second-stage negotiation opens on deeper measures.

**Usefulness:** strongest procedural bridge.  
**Risk:** labour will still suspect a backdoor closure path.

### Pathway B: Shared-sacrifice package with quantified contributions
- Management cuts, owner capital support, investor acceptance of staged recovery, labour flexibility and limited concessions.
- Each contribution numerically specified.
- Strong investment and product commitments for remaining sites.

**Usefulness:** addresses fairness and credibility simultaneously.  
**Risk:** may still fail if structural overcapacity is too large.

### Pathway C: Capacity reduction without formal closures
- Keep sites open in name but reduce shifts, consolidate lines, reallocate products, and repurpose weaker sites.
- Combine with voluntary exits, partial retirement, and redeployment.

**Usefulness:** may satisfy labour’s closure taboo while delivering real savings.  
**Risk:** may be insufficient if fixed-cost absorption remains too weak.

## 8. Open questions
- What is the actual plant-by-plant utilization and fixed-cost gap?
- How much savings can non-closure measures deliver, and by when?
- Which sites are structurally weak versus merely temporarily underused?
- What owner-side capital or investment commitment is available?
- What specific labour-cost concessions are feasible without breaking the bargaining framework?
- What minimum financial metrics would satisfy investors if no closures are used?
- What governance safeguards would make a review mechanism acceptable to labour?

## 9. Recommended next step
Require each stakeholder to submit, in writing, three items only:
1. **Non-negotiables**
2. **Concessions**
3. **Numerical threshold for changing position**

At the same time, require management to publish a **plant-by-plant savings bridge** distinguishing:
- savings from non-closure measures,
- savings from capacity reduction,
- and the residual gap.

This is the fastest path from positional conflict to decision-grade bargaining.

## 10. Emergent insights that only appeared through multi-turn interaction
- The central dispute is not simply “how much savings” but whether **irreversible footprint change is permissible at all**.
- The labour side is not rejecting restructuring per se; it is rejecting **being the sole adjustment mechanism** and rejecting a **closure-first default**.
- Management is not demanding closures as ideology; it is using closures as leverage because it believes **fixed-cost absorption** cannot be solved by wage/flexibility measures alone.
- The state’s role is not passive political support; it functions as a **real veto-bearing legitimacy actor**, making economically rational but socially ungovernable solutions non-viable.
- The controlling family and investors are aligned on competitiveness, but differ in tone: both want credible restructuring, yet the family is more sensitive to stewardship and social governability.
- The expert clarified that the decisive variable is **utilization and fixed-cost absorption**, meaning the settlement hinges on whether the company’s problem is temporary underuse or structural overcapacity.
- A “no closure” formula can be economically meaningful only if it is paired with measurable capacity reduction inside the existing footprint; otherwise it risks becoming a symbolic standstill.
- Trust is itself a material variable: all sides recognized that a deal that appears to win on paper but breaks co-determination or industrial legitimacy may destroy value in execution.
