Based on the scenario as presented, the key issue is not whether restructuring is necessary — it clearly is — but whether a **no-closure / no-forced-redundancy package** can realistically deliver enough **durable fixed-cost reduction** to restore competitiveness.

## Short answer
A **small to moderate package of labor flexibility, voluntary exits, and overhead cuts is unlikely to be sufficient on its own** if the German footprint has a genuinely large structural overcapacity problem.  
A **credible turnaround** usually needs some combination of:
- capacity reduction,
- labor-cost relief,
- utilization improvement,
- overhead reduction,
- and, if the gap is large enough, some footprint rationalization.

## What the economics usually require
For a large automotive plant network, the decisive variables are:

1. **Fixed-cost burden per vehicle**
   - Plant labor, depreciation, utilities, maintenance, logistics, local overhead.
   - If volumes are too low, unit cost rises sharply.

2. **Capacity utilization**
   - A plant running far below efficient scale often cannot be fixed by small wage or shift changes alone.

3. **Workforce flexibility**
   - Working-time adjustments, shift simplification, overtime reduction, redeployment.
   - These can help, but they usually produce only partial savings.

4. **Voluntary exits / partial retirement**
   - Useful for smoothing downsizing.
   - Not enough if the required headcount reduction is large.

5. **Central overhead and management cost cuts**
   - Important, but these are typically not sufficient to close a major industrial footprint gap.

6. **Product allocation and investment**
   - More future models on the right sites can improve utilization.
   - This is often the most important non-closure lever, but it only works if the product pipeline is strong enough and timing aligns.

## Practical estimate: what is “smallest plausible”?
Without plant-level data, I can only give a range.

A **smallest plausible package** that might close a structural gap would usually need:
- **meaningful capacity reduction**, not just minor shift changes,
- **double-digit percentage labor-cost relief** at affected sites,
- **substantial voluntary workforce reduction**, and
- **binding product/investment allocation** to improve utilization at remaining sites.

If the required savings gap is only modest, then alternatives may work.  
If the gap is large — as management is implying — then **non-closure measures alone often do not close it**.

## What would be inadequate on its own
These measures are usually **insufficient by themselves** if overcapacity is structural:
- a temporary wage freeze,
- limited working-time flexibility,
- one-off bonuses forgone,
- modest overtime reductions,
- small headcount cuts through attrition only,
- broad management overhead trimming alone,
- vague future investment promises without product allocation.
