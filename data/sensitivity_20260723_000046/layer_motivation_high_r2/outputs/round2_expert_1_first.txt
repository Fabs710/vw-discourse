From a plant-economics and turnaround perspective, the core issue is simple:

**If the company’s German cost base is structurally above market and its domestic capacity is materially underutilised, then some combination of capacity reduction, labour-cost reduction, and footprint rationalisation is usually required.**  
The question is not whether there must be adjustment, but **which package can achieve the needed savings with the least destruction of future operating value and the highest chance of implementation**.

## 1) Economic diagnosis

Based on the scenario, management’s diagnosis is plausible:
- European demand is weak or shrinking.
- Competition is intense, including lower-cost and Chinese producers.
- German manufacturing costs are above target and likely above peer benchmarks.
- The brand is missing return-on-sales targets.

That combination typically means the business has:
- **too much fixed cost** relative to volume,
- **too much installed capacity** for the demand base,
- and possibly **too high a labour and overhead burden per unit**.

In that situation, **cost-saving measures that do not reduce fixed cost enough are usually insufficient**. Working-time changes, voluntary exits, and temporary concessions can help, but they often do not close a large structural gap on their own.

## 2) What usually works in turnaround economics

A durable restructuring in a capital-intensive auto plant network normally needs some mix of:

1. **Capacity rationalisation**  
   Closing lines, consolidating production, reducing shifts, or ending production at structurally weak sites.

2. **Labour-cost reduction**  
   Wage restraint, reduced premiums, lower guarantees, changed working-time patterns, or other negotiated cost relief.

3. **Workforce resizing without immediate mass layoffs**  
   Natural attrition, voluntary exits, internal redeployment, early retirement, and hiring freezes.

4. **Product and investment reallocation**  
   More volume to the best sites, with the best cost and process economics.

5. **Overhead and procurement reduction**  
   Important, but usually not enough alone if plants are fundamentally overcapacity.

## 3) Assessment of the competing positions

### Works council / union position
Their stance is economically understandable as a defensive bargaining position, but from a restructuring standpoint it is **not sufficient if it excludes closures and compulsory redundancies categorically**.

Why:
- If the savings need is large, then **“no closures, no layoffs” can remove the main instruments that actually reduce fixed cost**.
- Voluntary measures and flexibility can bridge only part of the gap.
- A deal that preserves every site regardless of economics often just delays the crisis.

So: **their position may be politically coherent, but it is not automatically financially workable**.

### Management position
Management’s claim that structural change is needed is credible in principle. However, management still must show:
- the quantified savings requirement,
- which plants or lines are truly underutilised,
- what alternatives were modelled,
- and why non-closure measures are insufficient.

If management has not done that rigorously, then its closure case is incomplete. But if the numbers show a large fixed-cost gap, then **management is likely right that some footprint reduction is necessary**.

### Owners, state, and investors
They are correct that:
- the company cannot preserve uncompetitive economics indefinitely,
- a cosmetic compromise would destroy value,
- and labour should not be the only contributor.

But they should also recognise a key turnaround principle:  
**the cheapest cut on paper is not always the best cut in practice**.  
A shutdown that triggers prolonged industrial conflict, low morale, supply disruption, and execution risk can be more expensive than a negotiated alternative that achieves slightly smaller direct savings but is implementable.

## 4) The real trade-off

The real choice is likely between:

### Option A: Hard restructuring
- plant closure(s),
- deeper headcount reduction,
- stronger fixed-cost relief,
- higher short-term conflict risk,
- but better chance of fully restoring margin.

### Option B: Negotiated avoidance package
- no closures,
- fewer involuntary measures,
- more labour flexibility and concessions,
- weaker immediate savings,
- but higher labour peace and less operational disruption.

### Option C: Hybrid package
- targeted footprint reduction at one or more sites,
- no broad compulsory redundancy if possible,
- voluntary exits, redeployment, shorter hours, wage/cost concessions,
- plus investment commitments and sequencing.

From a plant-economics standpoint, **Option C is usually the best outcome if it can truly deliver the required savings**.  
The key is whether the hybrid package closes enough of the structural gap.

## 5) The central feasibility test

Everything turns on one question:

**Can a no-closure or minimal-closure package generate savings roughly equivalent to the structural cost gap?**

If the answer is no, then:
- the company may survive temporarily,
- but the restructuring is not durable,
- and the business may revisit the same problem within 12–24 months.

If the answer is yes, then:
- labour-side flexibility plus selective concessions can be a viable turnaround route,
- especially if paired with product allocation and management overhead cuts.

My expert view is:

- **Some structural reduction in German capacity is likely necessary** if management’s diagnosis is accurate.
- **A deal relying only on labour flexibility, voluntary exits, and temporary concessions is unlikely to be sufficient** if the cost gap is large.

So the decisive issue is not the rhetoric of either side. It is whether the final package delivers enough **durable fixed-cost reduction** to make the business competitively viable.
