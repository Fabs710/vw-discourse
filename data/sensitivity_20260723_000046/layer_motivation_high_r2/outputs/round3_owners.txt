I will be direct.

## Where I agree
I agree with management, investors, and the expert on the core diagnosis: **the business has a structural competitiveness problem, and a cosmetic solution would destroy value**.

If the German cost base remains too high, if capacity stays misaligned with demand, and if margins do not recover, then we are not protecting the enterprise — we are gradually weakening it. That would be irresponsible stewardship and a poor outcome for the family holding as long-term owner.

I also agree with the state that **industrial peace matters**. A prolonged strike spiral or public breakdown would damage the brand, the supplier network, and the company’s ability to invest.

And I agree with labour on one important point: **the workforce cannot be the only party making sacrifices**. A fair package must include material contributions from management, ownership, and the company’s own cost structure.

## Where I disagree
I do **not** accept the attempt by the works council and union to make:

- no German plant closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- no meaningful footprint change,

into absolute preconditions.

That position may be understandable from a labour perspective, but it is not automatically compatible with restoring competitiveness. If some sites are structurally uncompetitive, then refusing to address that reality is not protecting jobs in the long run — it is postponing the damage.

I also do **not** accept the state’s suggestion that the special governance framework can be used to freeze the industrial footprint indefinitely. Co-determination and regional interests matter, but they do not repeal economics.

## On management’s case
Management is right to insist that real cost and capacity adjustment is needed. I support the principle.

But management still has to be disciplined and transparent. I do **not** support a blunt closure-first strategy presented as inevitability. That would risk unnecessary conflict, reputational damage, and execution failure.

So my support is conditional on a package that is:
- economically credible,
- implementable under co-determination,
- and durable enough to restore margins rather than simply create a one-off cut.

## What I need to see
For the controlling shareholder to support a settlement, it must meet three tests:

1. **Durable competitiveness gains**  
   Not symbolic savings. Real improvement in return on sales and fixed-cost absorption.

2. **Governability and labour peace**  
   The solution must be implementable without a permanent legitimacy crisis.

3. **Protection of the enterprise’s long-term industrial core**  
   The future German footprint must remain viable, not hollowed out.

## What I am open to
I am open to:
- significant capacity reduction,
- voluntary exit schemes,
- working-time and flexibility measures,
- negotiated labour-cost contributions,
- redeployment and qualification,
- targeted investment commitments,
- and a leaner footprint if the numbers require it.

I am **not** open to:
- a blanket promise that every plant remains open regardless of economics,
- a permanent freeze on restructuring,
- or a settlement that preserves all legacy guarantees while leaving the cost base untouched.

## To the works council
Your refusal to accept forced redundancies and closures is politically coherent, but it is not sufficient as a business answer.

You say management has not proven that closures are unavoidable. Fair enough — then let management show the numbers. But if the numbers do show a serious structural gap, you cannot simply answer with “no” and expect the company to remain competitive.

I also want to be clear: a no-closure position is only serious if it can deliver **equivalent durable savings**. If it cannot, it is not a solution; it is deferral.

## To the union
I respect your willingness to discuss working-time flexibility, redeployment, and voluntary instruments. That is constructive.

But your non-negotiables are too rigid if the economics require more than that. “No compulsory redundancies” may be a legitimate preference, but it is not automatically compatible with a deep restructuring. If you want a durable deal, then labour’s contribution must be real, not only symbolic.

## To the state government
I respect your role and your concern for regional legitimacy. But if the state uses its influence to block all meaningful adjustment, it will eventually weaken the very industrial base it wants to protect.

Your strongest role is to support a balanced restructuring that preserves as much of the German core as possible while restoring competitiveness.

## To institutional investors
You are right to demand measurable improvement. I agree with that.

But I will not endorse a solution that is “clean” on a spreadsheet and unstable in reality. A settlement that triggers prolonged conflict, destroys trust, and undermines implementation is not value-accretive.

## On the expert’s intervention
The expert made the key point correctly: the real test is whether a negotiated hybrid package can close the structural gap.

That is the right question. Not slogans, not principle statements.

So here is the controlling shareholder’s position:

- If alternatives can deliver the needed durable savings, I am open to them.
- If they cannot, then structural footprint reduction has to remain on the table.
- I will not support a solution that freezes the current structure while expecting the company to somehow become competitive anyway.

## My bottom line
I support a **serious restructuring** that restores competitiveness and margins, even if it includes painful measures.

I do **not** support a deal that:
- permanently protects every plant,
- rules out all meaningful structural adjustment,
- and thereby weakens the enterprise over time.

If the parties want the family holding’s support, they need to bring forward a package that is:
- economically real,
- socially manageable,
- and governance-stable.

Otherwise, we will not sign up to a compromise that merely delays decline.
