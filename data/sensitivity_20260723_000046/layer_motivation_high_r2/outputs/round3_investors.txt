I’ll be equally direct.

## Where I agree
I agree with management, the family holding, and the expert on the central point: **this company has a structural cost and capacity problem, and a cosmetic settlement would destroy value**.

I also agree with the state that a disorderly confrontation or prolonged strike would be value-destructive. Industrial peace matters because it affects output, customer confidence, execution, and ultimately margins.

And I agree with labour on one point: **employees cannot be the only party contributing**. Any durable restructuring must include management, ownership, and the company’s own cost base.

## Where I disagree
I do **not** support the works council and union turning **“no plant closures” and “no compulsory redundancies”** into absolute preconditions.

That position may be understandable from a labour perspective, but from a capital-market perspective it is only acceptable if it still produces **durable, verifiable, sufficient savings**. If it does not, then it simply protects the current structure while the business remains uncompetitive. That is not job protection in the long run; it is value destruction with a delay.

I also do **not** accept a framework in which management is asked to prove alternatives while closures are ruled out in advance. If the evidence says a footprint change is necessary, then pretending otherwise for political comfort would be irresponsible.

## What would move us
What matters to us is not the slogan attached to the restructuring. It is whether the final package does all of the following:

1. **Restores competitive margins**
2. **Delivers durable fixed-cost reduction**
3. **Has a credible implementation path under co-determination**
4. **Avoids a protracted strike spiral**
5. **Preserves the company’s long-term investability**

If a no-closure package can genuinely achieve that, we would support it. But we have not yet seen proof that it can, and we will not fund wishful thinking.

## On the arguments from labour
### To the Works Council
You are right that management must not use “competitiveness” as a blanket excuse for poor planning. But your position is too absolute if it refuses to consider that some sites may be structurally uncompetitive.

If you want us to accept no closures, then show a **plant-by-plant, quantified savings bridge** that closes the gap without hollowing out margins. Otherwise, your line becomes a demand to preserve every legacy structure regardless of economics.

### To the Union
We welcome your willingness to discuss working-time flexibility, redeployment, qualification, voluntary exits, and partial retirement. Those are real instruments.

But if the package is built only on softer tools while the fixed-cost gap remains large, then it will not be enough. And if labour insists on zero compulsory measures under all circumstances, then it is implicitly shifting the full burden onto owners and shareholders through lower returns and weaker competitiveness. That is not sustainable.

## On management
We support management’s insistence that the business needs a structural reset. But support is conditional, not blank.

You still need to provide:
- a transparent plant-level business case,
- quantified savings from each measure,
- and a clear explanation of why non-closure options are or are not sufficient.

If you cannot do that, then your restructuring case is incomplete.

If you can, then the capital markets will back the hard decisions, even if they are painful. We are not here to preserve sentiment. We are here to preserve value.

## On the family holding
We agree that stewardship matters. But stewardship is not the same as preserving every current site by principle.

If the economics require a leaner footprint, then the family holding should support disciplined restructuring rather than blocking it in the name of legacy. Long-term value comes from competitiveness, not from freezing the business in its current shape.

At the same time, we agree that a crude closure-first approach could trigger avoidable conflict and destroy value. So the owner side should push for a package that is tough, but executable.

## On the regional state
We respect the state’s concern for industrial legitimacy and regional employment. But your role cannot be to veto hard choices indefinitely.

The state should support a solution that:
- preserves as much viable capacity as possible,
- protects the industrial base where it is competitive,
- and does not make the company’s economics hostage to politics.

Blocking all footprint changes while the cost gap remains open would not protect the region in the long run.

## What we would support
We would support a deal only if it includes:
- **real and durable savings**
- **measurable capacity rationalisation**
- **material labour-cost relief**
- **management and owner contributions**
- **a credible timeline**
- **and a viable future footprint**

That could mean a hybrid package, possibly with:
- targeted site rationalisation,
- voluntary exits,
- redeployment,
- working-time changes,
- and investment commitments.

But it cannot mean:
- preserving every plant regardless of economics,
- protecting all guarantees regardless of cost,
- and calling that a turnaround.

## What we will not support
We will not support a settlement that:
- leaves the cost base structurally uncompetitive,
- preserves overcapacity under another name,
- or trades short-term industrial peace for long-term margin impairment.

That would be a poor outcome for shareholders and, ultimately, for the company itself.

## Bottom line
We are open to a negotiated solution. We are not wedded to closures as a principle.

But we are very clear on the limit:  
**if the final package does not credibly restore competitive margins and reduce structural cost, we will not back it.**

So the burden is now on management and the labour side to show, with numbers, that the proposed path is not just politically palatable, but economically sufficient. If it is not sufficient, then the capital markets will not treat it as a solution.
