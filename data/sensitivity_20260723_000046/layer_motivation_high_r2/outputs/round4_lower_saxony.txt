I’ll be clear.

## My position
As the regional state government and anchor shareholder, I **do not accept German plant closures as the default answer** and I **do not accept compulsory redundancies as an easy escape hatch**. I also **will not support any restructuring that weakens our co-determination and veto role**.

That is not posturing. It is the core of our legitimacy and responsibility here: regional employment, industrial continuity, and a viable long-term company.

## Where I agree
I agree with management, the family holding, investors, the works council, and the union on one central point: **this company needs a durable restructuring, not a cosmetic pause**.

I also agree with the expert that labour alone cannot carry the adjustment. If there is a real cost gap, then management, owners, and shareholders must contribute materially too.

And I agree with the workforce side that **trust, know-how, and industrial peace are value-relevant**. A plan that destroys those things is not “hard-nosed”; it is self-defeating.

## Where I disagree
I disagree with management, the family holding, and investors when they speak as if closures and forced redundancies are simply the inevitable logic of the situation.

That is **not yet proven**.

You have all argued that the German footprint may be too expensive and too large. Fine. But that does **not** mean the first answer is to close plants in the state or to tear up employment guarantees. Before any irreversible footprint change, I want to see a transparent, plant-by-plant business case that proves:

- what the exact savings gap is,
- what non-closure measures have been tested,
- what management overhead and executive cuts are being made,
- what owner-side support is being put in,
- what product and investment allocation can improve utilization,
- and why those measures are insufficient.

Until that evidence exists, closure is a **choice**, not a necessity.

## To the works council
I support your insistence that:
- no closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- and no dismantling of the job-security framework

must not be treated as trivial bargaining points.

That said, I also want a solution that is credible in the real economy. So I need you to engage seriously with:
- working-time flexibility,
- redeployment,
- qualification,
- partial retirement,
- voluntary exits,
- capacity rebalancing within existing sites,
- and a fair employee contribution if it is part of a balanced package.

What I will not support is a standstill that simply preserves the status quo and hopes the numbers improve by themselves. But I also will not support a settlement that uses “restructuring” as a euphemism for closures.

## To the union
I respect your willingness to discuss flexibility tools, and I agree that labour must not be the only side paying.

But your non-negotiables are too absolute if they are treated as the only serious basis for discussion. If the company can be stabilized without closures, that is the best outcome. If a genuine restructuring package requires deeper cost relief, then we need to know exactly what management, ownership, and the company itself are prepared to contribute before asking the workforce for more.

I support:
- working-time adjustments,
- voluntary exit schemes,
- redeployment,
- qualification,
- partial retirement,
- and a fair negotiated contribution.

I do **not** support forced redundancies, and I do **not** accept plant closures as the starting point.

## To management
You are right to insist on economic credibility. I’m not disputing the crisis.

But you have not yet earned the right to say that closures are unavoidable. If you want support for difficult measures, bring the data. Not general warnings. I want:

1. a plant-by-plant restructuring scenario,
2. quantified savings from non-closure measures,
3. an estimate of the remaining gap,
4. management and overhead cuts,
5. a product and investment plan for German sites,
6. and a clear explanation of what the company itself will sacrifice.

If you cannot show that non-closure measures fail, then you have no justification for demanding closures in our state.

## To the controlling family
If long-term stewardship is real, then it must mean more than demanding discipline from labour.

It means:
- supporting investment,
- accepting owner-side responsibility,
- backing a negotiated solution that preserves the company’s industrial base,
- and not treating workers as the easiest adjustment variable.

If the family wants to be seen as a long-term steward, then help finance a solution that keeps sites viable and jobs protected. That is what stewardship looks like.

## To institutional investors
I understand your concern for returns and implementability. A fake compromise would indeed destroy value.

But a deal that triggers a labor war, damages the brand, and undermines industrial legitimacy is also value-destructive. So your test cannot be only “how fast do we cut costs?” It must also be: “can this be implemented without breaking the company’s social and regional foundations?”

That means I am open to hard restructuring, but only if it is:
- economically sound,
- socially executable,
- and compatible with our governance structure.

## On the expert’s point
The expert is right that the key question is whether a no-closure / no-forced-redundancy package can close the structural gap.

That is precisely why I want the plant-by-plant data.

If the data show that equivalent savings can be achieved through:
- capacity adjustment without closures,
- working-time flexibility,
- voluntary exits,
- product allocation,
- management cuts,
- overhead cuts,
- and investment commitments,

then I will support that path.

If the data show a gap remains, then we need to discuss the **least damaging** way to close it — not jump immediately to closures and layoffs as if those were cost-free. In this company and in this state, they are not.

## My bottom line
I am prepared to support a **hard but socially managed restructuring**.

I am **not** prepared to support:
- plant closures in the state as the default answer,
- compulsory redundancies as a first resort,
- or any settlement that sidelines the state’s co-determination and veto role.

So the next step is clear:
**bring me a transparent, quantified, plant-level case showing exactly why alternatives do or do not work.**

Until then, my answer remains:  
**No default closures, no forced redundancies, and no weakening of the regional anchor role.**
