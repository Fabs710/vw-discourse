I’ll be direct.

## Where I agree
I agree with management, the family holding, the state, and the expert on the central diagnosis: **this company has a structural cost problem, and a cosmetic settlement would destroy value**.

I also agree with the labour side on one important point: **the workforce cannot be the only contributor**. If a restructuring is to be credible and durable, then management, owners, and the broader cost base must also make material sacrifices.

And I agree with the expert that the real test is economic, not rhetorical: **can the company restore competitive margins with non-closure measures alone, or does it need footprint change?** That is the right question.

## Where I disagree
I do **not** support turning “no plant closures” and “no compulsory redundancies” into unconditional red lines.

From a shareholder perspective, that is only acceptable if the resulting package still delivers:
- durable fixed-cost reduction,
- credible margin recovery,
- and a viable future footprint.

If it does not, then it is not a solution — it is a delay tactic that preserves an uncompetitive cost base and destroys value over time.

I also do **not** support management, the family, or the state hiding behind inevitability without publishing the numbers. If closures or deeper footprint change are being argued as necessary, then that case must be proven plant by plant, not asserted.

## What would move us
We would back a deal if it credibly shows:
1. **A measurable return to competitive margins**
2. **Durable structural cost reduction**
3. **A quantified shared-sacrifice package**
4. **A realistic implementation path under co-determination**
5. **No prolonged strike spiral**

If a no-closure package can genuinely hit those targets, we are open to it. We are not ideologically attached to closures. But we will not support a package that protects every site, preserves too much fixed cost, and calls that a turnaround.

## On the works council
You are right to demand a plant-by-plant business case. That is necessary.

But your position becomes unacceptable if “no closures” means the company keeps all current structures regardless of economics. That would amount to asking shareholders to fund permanent underperformance. We will not support that.

So: yes to transparency; no to a blanket preservation principle that leaves the cost base structurally uncompetitive.

## On the union
We welcome your willingness to discuss working-time flexibility, redeployment, voluntary exits, partial retirement, and employee contributions. Those are real levers.

But “no compulsory redundancies under any circumstances” is too rigid if the savings gap is large. If labour wants the market to believe this restructuring is serious, then labour’s contribution has to be materially meaningful, not merely symbolic.

## On management
We support your insistence that the turnaround must be real. But support is conditional.

You still owe the market:
- a transparent plant-level scenario,
- quantified savings from each measure,
- and a clear explanation of the residual gap after non-closure tools.

If that analysis shows some sites are structurally uncompetitive, then you should not hide from it. But you also cannot use that conclusion to justify a reckless closure-first strategy that destroys execution. Discipline matters.

## On the family holding
Stewardship is not preserving every legacy arrangement. Stewardship is restoring competitiveness while protecting long-term value.

If the family wants confidence from capital markets, it should support a package that is:
- economically hard,
- socially executable,
- and clearly sufficient.

## On the state
We respect the state’s concern for employment and industrial continuity. But the state cannot freeze the economics. Its best role is to support a durable deal that preserves as much viable capacity as possible without condemning the business to structural weakness.

## Bottom line
We are prepared to support a negotiated restructuring — **but only if it is economically sufficient**.

That means:
- no cosmetic no-closure deal that leaves the cost base uncompetitive,
- no one-sided burden on labour,
- and no vague promises instead of quantified savings.

If the numbers show that capacity reduction is necessary, then we will support disciplined restructuring. If the numbers show that non-closure measures are enough, then prove it — and implement it quickly.

Anything less is not a turnaround.
