I will respond clearly.

### Where I agree
I agree with the Works Council on the essential point: **employees cannot be asked to carry the whole burden** of this restructuring.  
I also agree that a purely cosmetic deal would be irresponsible. If the economics are real, then the solution has to be durable, not a short pause before the next crisis.

I also agree with management, the owners, and investors on one thing: **the company does need a credible path back to competitiveness**. I am not pretending the cost problem is imaginary.

### Where I disagree
I do **not** accept that the only credible answer is to keep plant closures and compulsory redundancies on the table as if they were normal bargaining tools.

That is not a “hard reality”; it is an ultimatum.

If management wants labour to contribute, then it has to show that it is willing to pursue the restructuring **without breaking the collective wage framework and without forcing people out of their jobs**. That is the minimum condition for any serious discussion.

### My non-negotiables
Let me be explicit, because the pressure is rising and there is no point in ambiguity:

1. **No compulsory redundancies.**
2. **No plant closures as part of the settlement.**
3. **Protection of the collective bargaining framework.**
4. **No one-sided sacrifice by the workforce.**

If those are off the table, then we can talk about how to make the package work. If not, we are not in a negotiation; we are in a confrontation.

### What I am prepared to discuss
I have already signaled this before, and I mean it:
- temporary or structural **working-time adjustments**,
- **capacity reductions** achieved through alternatives,
- **redeployment and qualification**,
- **voluntary instruments** if needed,
- **early retirement / partial retirement / attrition**,
- and a **negotiated employee contribution**.

But that contribution is only acceptable if it is part of a fair package where **management, owners, and the company itself also make material contributions**.

That means:
- executive restraint,
- management overhead reduction,
- owner-side support,
- investment commitments for future products and German sites,
- and binding security for the workforce.

### To management
You keep saying some sites are structurally uncompetitive. Then show us a plan that closes the gap **without closures and without forced layoffs**. If you cannot produce that, then your restructuring case is not yet good enough to justify tearing up employment security.

You want “real cost relief.” Fine. But real cost relief does not automatically mean firing people or shutting plants. It can also mean:
- smarter capacity utilization,
- lower temporary labor costs,
- reduced overtime premiums,
- internal redeployment,
- training for new product lines,
- and a fair contribution from everyone in the company, including leadership.

### To the Works Council
I support your refusal to accept forced redundancies and plant closures. But I also want to be careful not to turn the discussion into a dead end where every adjustment is treated as surrender.  
If we can secure jobs, sites, and the collective framework, then we should be open to flexibility on the instruments. What matters is the outcome: **security, fairness, and durability**.

### To the family holding
Long-term stewardship means more than insisting that the company “must be competitive.” It means accepting that owners also have responsibility when a historic company is under strain. If you want labour to help preserve the enterprise, then the ownership side must not hide behind the call for structural change while workers absorb all the pain.

### To investors
You are right that the company needs a credible margin path. But you are wrong if you think value creation comes only from closures. A settlement that destroys industrial peace, morale, and long-term capability may look efficient on a spreadsheet and still be destructive in reality.

### To the state government
Your role matters. If the state wants stability, then it must help ensure that the restructuring does not become a transfer of risk entirely onto workers. A deal without social legitimacy will not hold.

### What this means in practice
If management wants a serious agreement, it should bring forward a package with:
- **job and site security** for the agreement period,
- **no compulsory redundancies**,
- **capacity adjustments without plant closures**,
- **a fair employee contribution**,
- **management and owner contributions**,
- and **binding investment/product commitments**.

If that package is presented, we can bargain hard on the details. If the package remains built around closures and layoffs, then the union will not soften its position because others repeat the word “competitiveness” often enough.

### Bottom line
We are willing to help solve the crisis.  
We are **not** willing to let the workforce be made the sole shock absorber.

If the company wants peace, it needs a fair settlement. If it wants confrontation, it will get resistance.
