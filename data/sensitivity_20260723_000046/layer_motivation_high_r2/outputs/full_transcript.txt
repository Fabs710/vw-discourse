[Works Council (Betriebsrat) (Plant-level employee representation under co-determination) — Round 1]

2. **No operational layoffs.**

If the proposal remains a package of closures, compulsory layoffs, and unilateral cuts, then I will say plainly: **we will resist it with all the means available to us**.

[Expert: independent restructuring/turnaround adv — Round 2]
From a plant-economics and turnaround perspective, the core issue is simple:

**If the company’s German cost base is structurally above market and its domestic capacity is materially underutilised, then some combination of capacity reduction, labour-cost reduction, and footprint rationalisation is usually required.**  
The question is not whether there must be adjustment, but **which package can achieve the needed savings with the least destruction of future operating value and the highest chance of implementation**.

## 1) Economic diagnosis

Based on the scenario, management’s diagnosis is plausible:
- European demand is weak or shrinking.
- Competition is intense, including lower-cost and Chinese producers.
- German manufacturing costs are above target and likely above peer benchmarks.
- The brand is missing return-on-sales targets.

That combination typically means the business has:
- **too much fixed cost** relative to volume,
- **too much installed capacity** for the demand base,
- and possibly **too high a labour and overhead burden per unit**.

In that situation, **cost-saving measures that do not reduce fixed cost enough are usually insufficient**. Working-time changes, voluntary exits, and temporary concessions can help, but they often do not close a large structural gap on their own.

## 2) What usually works in turnaround economics

A durable restructuring in a capital-intensive auto plant network normally needs some mix of:

1. **Capacity rationalisation**  
   Closing lines, consolidating production, reducing shifts, or ending production at structurally weak sites.

2. **Labour-cost reduction**  
   Wage restraint, reduced premiums, lower guarantees, changed working-time patterns, or other negotiated cost relief.

3. **Workforce resizing without immediate mass layoffs**  
   Natural attrition, voluntary exits, internal redeployment, early retirement, and hiring freezes.

4. **Product and investment reallocation**  
   More volume to the best sites, with the best cost and process economics.

5. **Overhead and procurement reduction**  
   Important, but usually not enough alone if plants are fundamentally overcapacity.

## 3) Assessment of the competing positions

### Works council / union position
Their stance is economically understandable as a defensive bargaining position, but from a restructuring standpoint it is **not sufficient if it excludes closures and compulsory redundancies categorically**.

Why:
- If the savings need is large, then **“no closures, no layoffs” can remove the main instruments that actually reduce fixed cost**.
- Voluntary measures and flexibility can bridge only part of the gap.
- A deal that preserves every site regardless of economics often just delays the crisis.

So: **their position may be politically coherent, but it is not automatically financially workable**.

### Management position
Management’s claim that structural change is needed is credible in principle. However, management still must show:
- the quantified savings requirement,
- which plants or lines are truly underutilised,
- what alternatives were modelled,
- and why non-closure measures are insufficient.

If management has not done that rigorously, then its closure case is incomplete. But if the numbers show a large fixed-cost gap, then **management is likely right that some footprint reduction is necessary**.

### Owners, state, and investors
They are correct that:
- the company cannot preserve uncompetitive economics indefinitely,
- a cosmetic compromise would destroy value,
- and labour should not be the only contributor.

But they should also recognise a key turnaround principle:  
**the cheapest cut on paper is not always the best cut in practice**.  
A shutdown that triggers prolonged industrial conflict, low morale, supply disruption, and execution risk can be more expensive than a negotiated alternative that achieves slightly smaller direct savings but is implementable.

## 4) The real trade-off

The real choice is likely between:

### Option A: Hard restructuring
- plant closure(s),
- deeper headcount reduction,
- stronger fixed-cost relief,
- higher short-term conflict risk,
- but better chance of fully restoring margin.

### Option B: Negotiated avoidance package
- no closures,
- fewer involuntary measures,
- more labour flexibility and concessions,
- weaker immediate savings,
- but higher labour peace and less operational disruption.

### Option C: Hybrid package
- targeted footprint reduction at one or more sites,
- no broad compulsory redundancy if possible,
- voluntary exits, redeployment, shorter hours, wage/cost concessions,
- plus investment commitments and sequencing.

From a plant-economics standpoint, **Option C is usually the best outcome if it can truly deliver the required savings**.  
The key is whether the hybrid package closes enough of the structural gap.

## 5) The central feasibility test

Everything turns on one question:

**Can a no-closure or minimal-closure package generate savings roughly equivalent to the structural cost gap?**

If the answer is no, then:
- the company may survive temporarily,
- but the restructuring is not durable,
- and the business may revisit the same problem within 12–24 months.

If the answer is yes, then:
- labour-side flexibility plus selective concessions can be a viable turnaround route,
- especially if paired with product allocation and management overhead cuts.

Without that, any conclusion is an estimate.

[Expert: independent restructuring/turnaround adv — Round 3]
Based on the scenario as presented, the key issue is not whether restructuring is necessary — it clearly is — but whether a **no-closure / no-forced-redundancy package** can realistically deliver enough **durable fixed-cost reduction** to restore competitiveness.

## Short answer
A **small to moderate package of labor flexibility, voluntary exits, and overhead cuts is unlikely to be sufficient on its own** if the German footprint has a genuinely large structural overcapacity problem.  
A **credible turnaround** usually needs some combination of:
- capacity reduction,
- labor-cost relief,
- utilization improvement,
- overhead reduction,
- and, if the gap is large enough, some footprint rationalization.

## What the economics usually require
For a large automotive plant network, the decisive variables are:

1. **Fixed-cost burden per vehicle**
   - Plant labor, depreciation, utilities, maintenance, logistics, local overhead.
   - If volumes are too low, unit cost rises sharply.

2. **Capacity utilization**
   - A plant running far below efficient scale often cannot be fixed by small wage or shift changes alone.

3. **Workforce flexibility**
   - Working-time adjustments, shift simplification, overtime reduction, redeployment.
   - These can help, but they usually produce only partial savings.

4. **Voluntary exits / partial retirement**
   - Useful for smoothing downsizing.
   - Not enough if the required headcount reduction is large.

5. **Central overhead and management cost cuts**
   - Important, but these are typically not sufficient to close a major industrial footprint gap.

6. **Product allocation and investment**
   - More future models on the right sites can improve utilization.
   - This is often the most important non-closure lever, but it only works if the product pipeline is strong enough and timing aligns.

## Practical estimate: what is “smallest plausible”?
Without plant-level data, I can only give a range.

A **smallest plausible package** that might close a structural gap would usually need:
- **meaningful capacity reduction**, not just minor shift changes,
- **double-digit percentage labor-cost relief** at affected sites,
- **substantial voluntary workforce reduction**, and
- **binding product/investment allocation** to improve utilization at remaining sites.

If the required savings gap is only modest, then alternatives may work.  
If the gap is large — as management is implying — then **non-closure measures alone often do not close it**.

## What would be inadequate on its own
These measures are usually **insufficient by themselves** if overcapacity is structural:
- a temporary wage freeze,
- limited working-time flexibility,
- one-off bonuses forgone,
- modest overtime reductions,
- small headcount cuts through attrition only,
- broad management overhead trimming alone,
- vague future investment promises without product allocation.

## What would materially change the analysis
The answer hinges on data that are not yet provided:
- plant-by-plant utilization,
- fixed cost per site,
- variable contribution per vehicle,
- volume outlook by platform and model,
- required savings target,
- transferability of production between plants,
- severance / closure costs,
- and the value of any future model allocation.

If you have those inputs, I can build a more precise scenario comparison:
1. **No closure / no forced redundancy**
2. **Selective footprint reduction**
3. **Hybrid package with staged trigger**

and estimate which one is economically viable.

[Expert: independent plant-level restructuring an — Round 4]
Thank you. I’ll stay within plant-level restructuring economics.

## Short answer

Based on the positions stated so far, the **economically decisive question** is not whether restructuring is needed — it is — but whether the company can close a **durable structural cost gap** with **non-closure measures alone**.

My assessment is:

- A **no-closure, no-forced-redundancy package** can sometimes close a moderate gap in an automotive plant network.
- But if the company’s German footprint is carrying **multiple layers of fixed cost** — excess capacity, underutilized lines, duplicated overhead, and structurally high labor/unit costs — then **soft measures alone are often insufficient**.
- In that case, some combination of:
  - capacity reduction,
  - line consolidation,
  - product reallocation,
  - and possibly footprint change
  is usually required to restore a sustainable cost base.

## What I can say with confidence

In automotive manufacturing, the cost problem is usually driven by **fixed-cost absorption**, not just hourly wages.

That means the key variable is:

- **utilization** of each plant,
- **volume per site**,
- **model mix and complexity**,
- **logistics distance and sequencing costs**,
- **overhead per vehicle**,
- and **how much capacity remains after demand weakens**.

If utilization falls below a certain point, the plant’s fixed costs get spread over too few vehicles, and unit costs rise sharply. At that stage, wage concessions alone rarely solve the problem.

## What would be needed to answer the question precisely

To determine the **smallest package of capacity reduction and labor-cost relief** that could plausibly close the structural gap, I would need, plant by plant:

1. **Annual capacity and current utilization**
2. **Vehicle output by site and by model**
3. **Direct labor cost per vehicle**
4. **Fixed overhead allocated to each site**
5. **Energy, logistics, and internal transport costs**
6. **Maintenance and ramp-up costs**
7. **Investment required to keep each site competitive**
8. **Savings from each proposed measure**
   - working-time reduction
   - voluntary exits
   - redeployment
   - temporary wage measures
   - permanent wage adjustments
   - management overhead cuts
   - procurement savings
   - product reallocation
9. **Closure cost / restructuring cost**
   - severance
   - transfer support
   - write-downs
   - remediation
   - changeover cost

Without that dataset, any view is necessarily directional rather than definitive.

## Directional assessment of the scenarios

### 1) No closures, no forced redundancies, but flexibility and voluntary exits
This can work **if the savings gap is modest** and the problem is mainly short-term overstaffing or temporary underutilization.

Economically, this is most plausible when:
- utilization can be lifted materially by shifting volume,
- a site has room to take additional models,
- and the company can reduce labor cost without changing the footprint.

But if the structural gap is large, this approach often produces only **partial savings** and may not restore competitiveness on a durable basis.

### 2) Capacity reduction without formal closures
This is often the most economically realistic compromise.

Examples:
- eliminating one shift,
- consolidating production into fewer lines,
- moving selected models between plants,
- reducing final assembly volume at a marginal site,
- converting a site to component, battery, or niche production.

This can achieve meaningful savings while avoiding a headline closure. It is frequently more effective than wage cuts alone because it attacks the **fixed-cost base**.

### 3) Full preservation of all sites plus only labor concessions
If the company’s issue is genuine overcapacity, this is usually the weakest option economically.

Why:
- wage concessions reduce variable cost,
- but overcapacity creates fixed costs that remain,
- so the company may still carry too much plant overhead per vehicle.
