## What a plant-by-plant analysis must include

To determine whether closures are necessary, each German plant must be modeled on the same basis:

### 1) Capacity and utilization
For each plant:
- installed annual capacity by model family and technology,
- current output,
- expected output by year,
- utilization rate,
- number of shifts,
- bottlenecks in body shop, paint, final assembly, logistics.

A plant running at, say, **well below economic scale** often has poor absorption of fixed costs even if variable labor is not excessive.

### 2) Fixed cost structure
For each plant:
- depreciation and maintenance burden,
- utilities and site overhead,
- logistics and internal transport,
- quality and rework costs,
- salaried overhead and support functions.

A high fixed-cost plant with declining volume is often more expensive to keep open than it looks from headcount alone.

### 3) Variable cost structure
For each model/site:
- direct labor hours per vehicle,
- energy intensity,
- material scrap/rework,
- outbound logistics,
- supplier inbound logistics.

In German auto manufacturing, direct wages are only part of the issue; **labor productivity, line balance, and logistics distance** often matter more than nominal wage rates.

### 4) Product and platform allocation
- Which plants are assigned which future vehicles?
- Are those vehicles volume models, premium models, or low-volume variants?
- Are EV lines already committed, or would new tooling be required?

A plant without allocated future products is not a viable “no-closure” candidate unless management is willing to fund a major product transfer.

### 5) Redeployment feasibility
For displaced workers:
- can they be redeployed to other German sites within practical commuting range?
- what is the age profile and skill profile?
- how much retraining is required?
- what is the attrition rate from retirements and natural turnover?

If redeployment capacity is limited, then “no layoffs” becomes much harder to sustain without large headcount overhang.

## Scenario assessment

## Scenario A: Closure / capacity consolidation
This is typically the most credible way to achieve **durable structural savings** when:
- demand is shrinking,
- there is persistent excess installed capacity,
- and certain plants are materially less efficient or less strategically relevant.

### Advantages
- fastest way to remove **fixed cost** and **overcapacity**,
- clearer path to higher plant utilization at remaining sites,
- easier to align product allocation with future EV strategy,
- strongest effect on group margin if the closed plant is high-cost or low-volume.

### Constraints
- high one-time restructuring charges,
- capex required at receiving plants to absorb volume,
- logistics disruption,
- risk of underutilizing remaining plants if volume assumptions are too optimistic.

### When closure is economically justified
If a plant:
- is structurally underutilized,
- lacks a future product assignment,
- needs disproportionate capex to remain competitive,
- and cannot be profitably redeployed,
then closure is often the lowest-cost long-run option.

## Scenario B: No closure, alternative restructuring
This is only credible if the company can produce **equivalent fixed-cost relief** through other means.

### Possible levers
- shift reductions or partial shutdown days,
- wage concession or temporary labor cost moderation,
- productivity improvements,
- internal redeployment,
- product transfer between plants,
- attrition-based workforce reduction,
- outsourcing of non-core activities,
- postponement or cancellation of low-return capex,
- management overhead reduction.

### Main limitation
These measures often reduce **cash cost** faster than they reduce **structural cost**. If capacity remains excessive, the company can still carry:
- too many plants,
- too much equipment,
- too much salaried overhead,
- and too much indirect labor.

That is why no-closure plans often work only if they are paired with:
- a significant product ramp at retained sites,
- very large headcount reductions via attrition,
- or extraordinary labor-cost concessions.

### When no-closure can work
A no-closure package can be viable if:
- one or more plants can be kept open at much lower utilization but with future product volume secured elsewhere in the group,
- the workforce is reduced mainly through retirement/voluntary exit,
- wage and working-time measures produce material savings,
- and capex is concentrated on plants with the strongest future economics.

If none of these are true, the no-closure route is usually unstable.
