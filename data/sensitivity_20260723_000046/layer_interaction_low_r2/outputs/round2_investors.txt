We agree with the basic direction of the discussion: **doing nothing is not an option**. If the company cannot restore competitive margins, then long-term equity value will keep eroding. From our perspective, that is the central issue.

Where we agree:
- We agree that the burden cannot fall only on employees.
- We agree that restructuring must be **durable**, not cosmetic.
- We agree that management has to provide **numbers**, not rhetoric.
- We agree that future product allocation and capex matter.
- We agree that a package that merely delays the problem is value-destructive.

Where we disagree:
- We do **not** accept a starting position of “no closures, no compulsory redundancies, guaranteed site preservation through 2030” if that cannot be shown to close the structural gap.
- We do **not** treat plant closures as politically unfortunate but economically irrelevant. If a site is structurally underutilized, high-cost, and lacks future product allocation, then preserving it for sentiment’s sake is not a shareholder-interest solution.
- We do **not** regard labor peace as a substitute for competitiveness.

To the works council and union:
Your insistence on preserving every German plant may be understandable, but it is not acceptable **unless** you can show a credible no-closure package that achieves the same economic result. So far, I have not seen that. “Fairness” is not enough; we need durable fixed-cost reduction. If the alternative depends on open-ended guarantees and temporary concessions, it is not enough.

To the regional state:
We respect the political responsibility, but we cannot price in regional discomfort if it means locking in structurally weak margins. If the numbers show that some footprint reduction is necessary, then blocking it indefinitely would be value-destructive.

To the controlling family:
We are aligned on the need for structural savings. But that support only matters if it translates into real implementation. We will not reward rhetoric.

To management:
You still need to be more specific. Right now, “closures remain on the table” is not enough, but neither is “we’ll find alternatives” without proof. We need a quantified comparison that shows:
1. the baseline gap to target margins,
2. the savings from a no-closure package,
3. the savings from a closure/consolidation package,
4. the one-off costs and implementation risk of each,
5. the timing of margin recovery.

Our position is straightforward:

- **Acceptable:** a restructuring that materially lowers fixed cost and capacity, even if that means closures or workforce reduction.
- **Potentially acceptable:** a no-closure alternative, but only if it achieves the same durable economic outcome with credible enforcement.
- **Unacceptable:** a settlement that preserves overcapacity, protects all sites, and leaves the company structurally uncompetitive.

So at this point, we support moving to the quantified comparison the moderator requested. If there is no credible evidence that a no-closure plan can restore margins, then we will favor the harder restructuring path.
