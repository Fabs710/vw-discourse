I will be direct.

## Where I agree
I agree with everyone on one fundamental point: **doing nothing is not an option**. The company needs a durable restructuring, and it needs one that secures long-term competitiveness.

I also agree that this discussion must be **plant-by-plant and evidence-based**. If management claims overcapacity, then show it. If alternatives are said to be insufficient, then prove that with numbers. And I agree that **the burden cannot fall on employees alone**. Management, owners, and labour all have to contribute.

## Where I disagree

### To management
I do **not** accept that closures are the default answer before a serious no-closure package has been tested.

You keep saying closures remain on the table as if that were self-justifying. It is not. If there is a credible alternative that preserves sites and jobs while still reducing fixed costs and capacity in a durable way, then that path has to be taken seriously first.

What I do not accept is a restructuring logic that starts with irreversible damage and then asks the state to endorse it because the spreadsheets prefer it.

### To the controlling family and investors
I understand the pressure for structural savings. But I do **not** accept a solution that treats regional employment and site continuity as secondary side effects.

A company like this is not only a capital asset; it is also an industrial anchor. If you weaken that anchor too far, you may buy short-term margin improvement at the cost of long-term legitimacy, labour peace, and political stability. That is not a minor issue in a company of this importance.

### To the works council and union
I share your core concern that the workforce should not bear the full burden. I also support your demand that management prove, plant by plant, why alternatives do or do not work.

Where I am more cautious is the absolute framing. A no-closure solution is not automatically guaranteed by principle; it must be shown to deliver **durable** savings and a real capacity adjustment. If it cannot, then we cannot simply wish the problem away.

That said, management has **not yet earned** the right to claim that closures are unavoidable. The burden of proof is still on those proposing irreversible plant damage.

### To the expert
Your point is exactly right: we still lack the data to decide responsibly. I support the call for a plant-level comparison, but it has to be more than a model in the abstract. It must show:
- baseline gap,
- no-closure savings,
- closure/consolidation savings,
- one-off costs,
- future product allocation,
- redeployment feasibility,
- and timing to margin recovery.

If the comparison is not concrete, it will not resolve anything.

## My position
For the regional state, the line remains unchanged:

- **No German plant closures as a starting point.**
- **No compulsory redundancies as the first resort.**
- **No weakening of co-determination or the state’s blocking role.**
- **A real, quantified no-closure restructuring must be tested first.**

This is not about refusing adjustment. It is about insisting that adjustment be done in a way that preserves the company’s role as a regional economic anchor and avoids unnecessary irreversible harm.

## What would move me
I could support a difficult restructuring if it clearly meets three conditions:

1. **It preserves sites and jobs as far as possible.**
2. **It delivers durable fixed-cost reduction without forced redundancies as the first instrument.**
3. **It includes fair burden-sharing by management, owners, and labour, with future product allocation and investment commitments.**

That means I am open to:
- working-time adjustments,
- productivity measures,
- management restraint,
- owner contribution,
- voluntary exits,
- redeployment,
- and negotiated labour-cost measures.

I am **not** open to a plan that simply cuts plants and calls that responsibility.

## What I want next
Before anyone talks about closures as necessary, I want the promised comparison on the table, plant by plant. If management is serious, then it should support the following being produced immediately:

[SUMMON: independent plant-level financial modeller to compare, for each German plant, current utilization, future product allocation, fixed costs, capex needs, redeployment options, one-off closure/consolidation costs, and recurring savings under a no-closure package versus a closure/consolidation package.]

That comparison should answer:
1. what the baseline gap is,
2. what a no-closure package delivers,
3. what a closure package delivers,
4. how many jobs are affected under each,
5. what future products are allocated where,
6. and how quickly each path restores competitiveness.

## Bottom line
I will support a restructuring that protects the company’s long-term health **and** preserves its role as a regional industrial anchor.

I will **not** support:
- plant closures as the first answer,
- compulsory redundancies as the default,
- or any arrangement that sidelines the state’s co-determination and veto role.

So my message to management and owners is simple:  
**show a credible alternative first, or prove beyond doubt that no such alternative exists.** Until then, closures are not yet justified.
