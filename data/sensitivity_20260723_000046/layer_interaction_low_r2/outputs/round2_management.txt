We are now at the point where we need to be honest about what is and is not acceptable.

I agree with one part of the discussion so far: **doing nothing is not an option**. The company needs a durable restructuring, not a pause. I also agree that any solution must be **quantified, enforceable, and durable**. On that, the expert’s intervention was helpful.

But I do **not** agree with the works council and union that the only acceptable framework is:
- no German plant closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- and binding guarantees through 2030.

That would be a political wish, not an industrial solution, unless it can be shown to close the structural cost gap. So far, that has **not** been shown.

### Where I agree
- **The burden cannot fall on employees alone.** Management and ownership must contribute.
- **Reversible measures alone are not enough** if they do not reduce the structural cost base.
- **Plant-by-plant transparency is necessary.** We need hard data, not slogans.
- **Future product allocation matters.** A site without future volume is not viable.

### Where I disagree
The works council keeps saying closures are only an assumption. That is not correct. In a shrinking market with excess capacity and costs above target, closures are a **valid restructuring instrument**. They may not be the only instrument, but they are absolutely on the table if they are needed to restore competitiveness.

The union says closures and forced redundancies are not acceptable under any circumstances. I cannot accept that as a premise. If the numbers show that capacity must come out of the system, then we need a way to remove it. If not through closure, then through other measures that are equally durable and economically real. But it has to be **real**. “No closures” is not a cost-reduction plan.

The regional state is right that the company has a social responsibility. But social responsibility does not mean preserving every site regardless of economics. If the company stays structurally uncompetitive, then there will be less investment, less future product, and ultimately a weaker industrial base.

The controlling family and institutional investors are also right to insist on structural savings. A cosmetic compromise would destroy confidence and waste time we do not have.

### What I need to see next
The expert was also clear: we need plant-level data. So here is what management will table next:

1. **Baseline gap by plant and group level**  
   What exactly must be closed in fixed cost, labor cost, and capacity.

2. **A no-closure scenario**  
   Quantified savings from working-time adjustments, productivity, attrition, redeployment, overhead cuts, and labor-cost relief.

3. **A restructuring scenario with closures or major footprint changes**  
   Showing the savings, the one-off costs, and the time to impact.

4. **A comparison of both scenarios**  
   Savings, headcount impact, investment needs, implementation risk, and return on sales effect.

That comparison will determine whether there is a credible alternative to closures. But let me be blunt: if the no-closure scenario only works by assuming permanent concessions without real capacity reduction, then it is not sufficient.

### On the red lines being stated
The works council says every German plant must stay open. I cannot commit to that.

The union says no compulsory redundancies. I am not prepared to promise that today.

The state says closures should not be the first resort. I understand that. But “not the first resort” is not the same as “off limits.” If the alternative does not close the gap, then closure remains necessary.

### On burden-sharing
I am willing to discuss:
- management restraint,
- contribution from owners where appropriate,
- workforce flexibility,
- labour-cost measures,
- working-time solutions,
- and redeployment where it is realistic.

But those measures must produce a **durable** reduction in the cost base. I will not sign up to a package that merely delays the problem to 2026 or 2027 while the company continues to miss its targets.

### Bottom line
My position remains unchanged on the core point:

- We need **real structural savings**.
- We need **lower overcapacity**.
- We need **lower labor and fixed costs**.
- We need **a credible path back to target margins**.

If that can be achieved without closures, then show it with numbers and enforceable commitments. If it cannot, then we must be prepared to take the harder decisions.
