We agree on the basic diagnosis: **doing nothing is not an option**. The company needs a restructuring that is durable, not cosmetic.

Where I part company with the employee side and, to a lesser extent, the state is on the attempt to make **“no closures, no compulsory redundancies, guarantees through 2030”** the starting frame. That is not a restructuring plan; it is a constraint set that may or may not be compatible with the economics. If it is not compatible, then insisting on it simply delays the necessary adjustment and erodes value further.

### Where I agree
- **The burden cannot fall on employees alone.** Management, owners, and labour all need to contribute.
- **Hard numbers are necessary.** We should stop debating abstractions.
- **Future product allocation and capex matter.** A plant without future volume is not viable in the long run.
- **A no-closure path should be tested seriously.** I do not object to alternatives being examined if they are real.

### Where I disagree
#### To the works council
I do **not** accept that every German plant must stay open regardless of economics. That is not stewardship; it is a refusal to confront structural overcapacity. If the footprint is too large for the market, then preserving every site simply locks in fixed costs and weak utilization.

I also do **not** accept that compulsory redundancies can be ruled out in advance. If the company needs durable capacity and cost reduction, then all tools have to be on the table until the economics prove otherwise. I understand the social concern, but the owners cannot commit to a false guarantee that may destroy more jobs later through continued decline.

#### To the union
I respect that you are prepared to discuss labour contributions, but I do **not** share the view that closures are just a negotiating threat. They are a legitimate restructuring instrument if the numbers require them. If a no-closure package cannot remove enough fixed cost and overcapacity, then it is not sufficient.

#### To the regional state
I respect your role and your concern for the industrial base. But regional stability cannot become a veto on necessary adaptation. A company that remains structurally uncompetitive will invest less, weaken more, and eventually threaten the very base everyone wants to protect.

#### To management
You are right to push for structural savings, but you still need to show the comparison cleanly. “Closures remain on the table” is not enough. Nor is “we’ll find alternatives” enough. We need a quantified case.

### What I want next
I support the request for a plant-by-plant comparison, but it must be specific and enforceable. I want to see:

1. **Baseline gap to target margins**
2. **No-closure scenario**
   - fixed-cost savings
   - capacity reduction
   - workforce impact
   - redeployment assumptions
   - time to effect
3. **Closure / consolidation scenario**
   - savings
   - one-off costs
   - plant utilization after consolidation
   - margin impact
4. **Future product allocation**
5. **Capex requirements**
6. **Implementation risk and timing**

[SUMMON: Independent plant-level financial modeller to compare, on a plant-by-plant basis, no-closure versus closure/consolidation scenarios, including fixed costs, utilization, product allocation, one-off restructuring costs, redeployment feasibility, and timing of margin recovery.]

### My position
- I am **open to alternatives to closures** if they are economically equivalent, durable, and enforceable.
- I am **not open** to a deal that preserves overcapacity, protects every site by fiat, and leaves the company uncompetitive.
- I am **not open** to permanent governance constraints that would prevent necessary restructuring or weaken the owners’ ability to protect the asset.

If the no-closure path can truly close the gap, it deserves serious consideration. If it cannot, then we should not pretend that sentiment is a substitute for competitiveness.
