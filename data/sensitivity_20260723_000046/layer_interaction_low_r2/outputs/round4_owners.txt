I will be direct.

We agree on the diagnosis that **doing nothing is not an option**. We also agree that this has to be **quantified, plant by plant**, not argued in abstractions. And I agree that the burden cannot fall on employees alone.

Where I disagree is on the repeated attempt to turn **“no closures, no compulsory redundancies, guarantees through 2030”** into the default framework. That is not a restructuring solution; it is a constraint set. If that constraint set cannot deliver durable competitiveness, then insisting on it simply prolongs the problem and destroys value.

## To the works council
I understand your red lines, but I do **not** accept that every German plant must stay open regardless of economics.

If a plant is structurally underutilized, high-cost, and has no credible future product allocation, then preserving it by principle is not stewardship. It is a recipe for ongoing margin erosion. And ongoing erosion ultimately threatens more jobs, more investment, and the company’s long-term position.

You are right to demand proof. Fine — then let us see the numbers. But until a no-closure package is shown to deliver **durable fixed-cost reduction and real capacity correction**, I will not treat your no-closure demand as economically sufficient.

## To the union
I respect that you are willing to discuss employee contributions. That is constructive.

What I do **not** accept is ruling out compulsory redundancies in advance. If the structural gap cannot otherwise be closed, then management needs all legitimate tools on the table. I am not interested in cosmetic solutions that preserve headlines and fail in execution.

If you want us to consider a no-closure package seriously, it must be:
- durable,
- enforceable,
- and materially equivalent in economic effect to footprint reduction.

Anything less is not enough.

## To the regional state
I appreciate your role as an anchor shareholder and your concern for the industrial base. But regional stability cannot become a veto on necessary adaptation.

A company that remains structurally uncompetitive will invest less, weaken more, and eventually endanger the regional base it is meant to support. So I cannot support a framework that treats closures as unacceptable before the economics have been tested honestly.

## To management
You are right to push for structural savings. But you still have not presented the comparison in a form that allows a decision.

I support the independent plant-level comparison, and I want it completed promptly. It should show, for each German plant:
1. current utilization,
2. future product allocation,
3. fixed-cost burden,
4. capex required to stay competitive,
5. redeployment options,
6. one-off closure / consolidation costs,
7. recurring savings under a no-closure package,
8. recurring savings under a closure / consolidation package,
9. and timing to margin recovery.

[SUMMON: Independent plant-level financial modeller to compare, on a plant-by-plant basis, no-closure versus closure/consolidation scenarios, including fixed costs, utilization, product allocation, capex requirements, one-off restructuring costs, redeployment feasibility, and time to margin recovery.]

Without that, none of us is acting on evidence. With it, we can judge whether the no-closure route truly closes the gap or whether footprint reduction is required.

## To institutional investors
Your insistence on structural savings is correct. So is your warning against cosmetic deals.

Where I would caution you is this: a settlement that maximizes near-term simplicity but triggers prolonged industrial conflict, political backlash, and implementation failure can destroy value too. So the objective is not just “more cuts”; it is **credible, enforceable, durable restructuring**.

## My position
I am open to alternatives to closures only if they meet a very high bar:
- durable fixed-cost reduction,
- meaningful capacity reduction,
- credible redeployment,
- future product allocation,
- and a real path back to target margins.

If the no-closure path can do that, it deserves consideration. If it cannot, then closures remain a legitimate and necessary instrument.

So I am **not** accepting blanket guarantees or unconditional site preservation as the starting point. I am accepting a disciplined comparison of options, and I will back the option that best protects the long-term value of the asset.
