Based on the facts in the record, the economically responsible conclusion is **not yet “close” or “do not close”**. The correct conclusion is:

> **We do not have enough plant-level data to prove that a no-closure package is sufficient, but we also do not have enough data to prove that closures are unavoidable.**

## What can be said with confidence

### Facts established
- The German footprint has a **structural cost and capacity problem**.
- The company needs a **durable** restructuring, not a temporary patch.
- All sides accept that **doing nothing is not an option**.
- A comparison must be made between:
  1. a **no-closure package**, and
  2. a **closure / consolidation package**.
- The missing basis for decision is **plant-by-plant economics**.

### What can be inferred
- If overcapacity is real and persistent, then some combination of:
  - capacity reduction,
  - fixed-cost reduction,
  - labor-cost reduction,
  - product reallocation,
  - and capex concentration
  will be necessary.
- A no-closure plan only works if it produces **durable fixed-cost reduction**, not just deferred pain.
- A closure plan only works if the **recurring savings exceed one-off closure and transition costs** over a reasonable payback period.

## Comparative economics: how the two paths usually differ

### No-closure scenario
Economic strengths:
- lower one-off cash cost,
- less launch and supply-chain disruption,
- preserves site optionality.

Economic weakness:
- may leave too much fixed cost in the network,
- savings can be temporary or partial,
- often relies on continuing concessions.

A no-closure route is credible only if it can do all of the following:
- reduce headcount substantially through attrition/voluntary exits/redeployment,
- cut overhead and shift costs enough to lower fixed cost per vehicle,
- secure future product allocation,
- and avoid repeated annual concessions.

### Closure / consolidation scenario
Economic strengths:
- stronger and more durable fixed-cost reduction,
- clearer capacity correction,
- better utilization at the remaining plants if volume is transferred successfully.

Economic weaknesses:
- high one-off closure, severance, write-down, and transfer costs,
- transition risk,
- possible logistics and launch risk at the receiving sites,
- may require significant capex at the receiving plants.

A closure route is credible if:
- the receiving plants can absorb volume,
- the capex is justified by future throughput,
- and the net present value of recurring savings exceeds one-off costs within an acceptable payback window.

## What is still missing
To decide responsibly, we still need for **each German plant**:
1. current utilization,
2. installed capacity,
3. future product allocation,
4. fixed-cost base,
5. capex required to stay competitive,
6. one-off closure / consolidation cost,
7. redeployment feasibility,
8. recurring savings under each scenario.

Without that, no one can responsibly claim:
- “closures are definitely necessary,” or
- “a no-closure plan definitely works.”

## My analytical judgment
If I had to state the **economic default** from industrial restructuring practice, it is this:

- **No-closure is harder to make durable** when the network has genuine overcapacity and duplicate fixed costs.
- **Closure / consolidation is more likely** to deliver the larger structural savings if one or more plants are low-utilization and lack future product.

But that is a **general restructuring inference**, not a company-specific conclusion.

## Bottom line
So the rigorous answer is:

- **A no-closure solution remains possible in principle.**
- **A closure / consolidation solution may produce larger recurring savings.**
- **Neither has been proven with the data currently available.**
- The decision must wait for the plant-by-plant model.
