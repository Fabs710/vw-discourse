We have heard enough now to be very clear.

## My position remains unchanged

I do **not** accept:
- German plant closures,
- compulsory redundancies,
- or the dismantling of the job-security framework.

And I will not pretend that those are “necessary” simply because management repeats it.

## Where I agree

I agree with all sides on one point: **doing nothing is not an option**.  
The company needs a durable restructuring.

I also agree that we need:
- hard numbers,
- plant-by-plant transparency,
- future product allocation,
- and a real assessment of redeployment.

So far, that part of the discussion has been right.

## Where I disagree

### To management
You still have not shown that closures are unavoidable.

You say the German footprint is too large and the cost base too high. That may be your diagnosis, but it is **not yet proof** that every non-closure route fails.

If you want to claim that closures are required, then show us:
- the baseline gap,
- the savings from a no-closure package,
- the savings from closures,
- the one-off closure costs,
- the redeployment options,
- and the product allocation per plant.

Until then, closure is still a **choice**, not a necessity.

And I reject the idea that the workforce should be asked first to give up jobs and guarantees and only later be told whether management’s threat was real.

### To the controlling family and investors
You are speaking as if social damage is just collateral to value creation.

It is not.

A settlement that destroys trust, breaks the job-security framework, and leaves workers to absorb the shock alone will not be stable. It may look efficient in a spreadsheet, but it creates conflict, resistance, and long-term damage to the company’s legitimacy.

If you want sustainable value, then you need a solution that does not simply cut sites and call that strategy.

### To the regional state
I welcome your insistence that this company remain an industrial anchor. But that has to mean more than statements. It means using your blocking position to stop an irreversible settlement that tears up sites and jobs.

If the company’s legitimacy matters, then it must not be sacrificed for a short-term cost-cutting plan.

### To the union
We are aligned on the core red lines:
- no compulsory redundancies,
- no one-sided burdening of the workforce,
- and no dismantling of the collective protection framework.

I support continued pressure, including warning strikes, because management has still not made a convincing case.

## On the expert assessments

The expert has said something important, and I want to be precise about it:

- A **no-closure scenario is possible in principle**.
- A **closure scenario may produce larger recurring savings**.
- But **neither has been proven with actual plant data**.

That means the proper conclusion is not “therefore close plants.”  
The proper conclusion is: **management must prove its case, plant by plant**.

I will not accept a leap from “overcapacity exists” to “therefore closures.”

## What I want to hear next
If management is serious, then table the following for **each German plant**:
1. current and projected utilization,
2. future product allocation,
3. fixed-cost burden,
4. capex required,
5. redeployment options,
6. and the effect of alternative measures.

And for the group as a whole:
1. the baseline gap,
2. the savings from a no-closure plan,
3. the savings from a closure plan,
4. the job impact of each,
5. and the guarantees attached.

## My minimum remains the same
- **Every German plant stays open.**
- **No compulsory redundancies.**
- **Binding site and employment guarantees through 2030.**
- **A genuine co-determination role in shaping the restructuring.**

That is not a bargaining trick. It is the line that protects the workforce and the company’s social stability.

## Final point
I am not rejecting restructuring. I am rejecting a restructuring that uses the workforce as the easiest source of pain.

If management wants a real discussion, then bring a credible no-closure plan with numbers.  
If it cannot do that, then it should stop calling closures “necessary” and admit it is choosing the fastest route at workers’ expense.

We are prepared to defend these interests.
