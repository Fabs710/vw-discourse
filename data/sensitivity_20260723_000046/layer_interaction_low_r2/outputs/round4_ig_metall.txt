I will be equally clear in this round.

## Where I agree

I agree with the works council, the state, and even management on one basic point: **doing nothing is not an option**. The company needs a durable restructuring, not a delay tactic.

I also agree that the discussion must be **plant-by-plant and evidence-based**. If there is overcapacity, show it. If there is a savings gap, quantify it. If future product allocation is the issue, put it on the table. We have been saying this from the start.

And I agree with the expert assessment on one critical point: **a no-closure solution is not automatically impossible**. It remains viable in principle if it delivers durable fixed-cost reduction, real capacity adjustment, and a credible route for redeployment and future workload.

## Where I disagree

### To management
You are still treating closures as the default answer before the no-closure route has been properly proven insufficient.

That is not acceptable.

You have not yet shown, plant by plant, that:
- working-time adjustments,
- productivity gains,
- management restraint,
- wage moderation,
- voluntary exits,
- redeployment,
- and product reallocation

cannot close the gap.

Until you do, closures remain a **choice**, not a necessity.

And I am not prepared to let management reverse the burden of proof by demanding workforce concessions first and evidence later.

### To the controlling family and investors
You keep speaking as if footprint reduction is automatically the responsible solution.

It is not automatically responsible if it simply shifts pain onto employees while protecting capital and governance comfort. A solution that destroys trust, weakens the collective wage framework, and triggers open conflict may look clean on a balance sheet and still be strategically damaging.

If you want durable value, you need a settlement that is not built on one-sided sacrifice.

### To the regional state
I support your insistence that the company remain an industrial anchor. But that has to mean more than calling for “balance.” It means using your blocking position to prevent a settlement that makes employees carry the full burden while irreversible closures are rushed through as the easy route.

If you are serious about industrial stability, then do not let management turn that into a euphemism for layoffs and closures.

### To the works council
We are aligned on the core red lines:
- no compulsory redundancies,
- no dismantling of the collective bargaining framework,
- no one-sided burdening of the workforce,
- and no plant closures as the starting point.

I support your push for transparency and plant-level numbers. That is necessary.

But I want to add one thing: the union will not support a “no-closure” label if it is just a cover for hidden job cuts, degraded standards, or permanent wage damage. If there is an employee contribution, it must be part of a real shared-sacrifice package, not a disguised wage rollback.

## What would actually move us

We have already said this, but I will restate it because it is the real test:

A settlement would have to include:
1. **No compulsory redundancies as the first instrument**
2. **Preservation of the collective wage framework**
3. **Fair burden-sharing across management, owners, and employees**
4. **Binding job and site security where the economics permit**
5. **A serious no-closure package with measurable, durable savings**

That means:
- temporary or targeted working-time changes,
- productivity improvements,
- management contribution,
- owner contribution,
- voluntary and redeployment-based reductions,
- and negotiated labour-cost measures.

It does **not** mean:
- worker-only pain,
- open-ended insecurity,
- or closures used as leverage before alternatives have been tested.

## On the expert contributions
The expert has been careful, and that matters.

What the expert has actually said is:
- a no-closure path is possible in principle,
- closure/consolidation may produce larger recurring savings,
- but **neither side has proved its case without plant-level data**.

That is exactly why I reject any attempt to declare closures “necessary” today.

The correct next step is not escalation for its own sake. It is a **proper plant-by-plant comparison**.

## My position going into the next stage
I am not moving from the essentials:

- **No compulsory redundancies**
- **No one-sided wage sacrifice**
- **No plant closures as the starting point**
- **Yes to a fair employee contribution if management and owners also contribute**
- **Yes to a durable settlement that protects jobs and the collective agreement**

If management cannot produce a credible no-closure path, then it has not yet earned the right to demand irreversible steps from the workforce.

So let’s stop pretending the burden of proof has already been met. It has not.

If the numbers genuinely show that alternatives cannot work, we will deal with that. But until then, the union will not sign off on a restructuring that treats workers as the easiest source of cost reduction.
