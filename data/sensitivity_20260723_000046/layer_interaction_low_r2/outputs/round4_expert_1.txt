# 3) Scenario logic

I’d model two core options:

## Scenario A: No-closure package
Typical tools:
- working-time reduction or flexibility
- wage moderation / temporary labour cost concessions
- voluntary exits / early retirement
- productivity measures
- overhead reduction
- product reallocation without site shutdowns
- partial capacity mothballing rather than closure

### Economic strengths
- lower one-off cash cost
- less disruption to launches / supply chain
- lower transition risk
- preserves optionality

### Economic weaknesses
- savings may be **partial or temporary**
- if capacity is structurally too high, fixed costs remain
- may require continuous concessions to hold the model together
- can fail if product volume does not return

### What must be true for this to work
- meaningful headcount reduction by attrition / voluntary means
- real shift in product mix to higher-volume or higher-margin work
- enough overhead removal to lower fixed-cost per vehicle materially
- a credible commitment to new workload and capex

---

## Scenario B: Closure / consolidation package
Typical tools:
- closure of one or more sites
- consolidation of production into fewer plants
- relocation of equipment and product lines
- workforce reduction linked to footprint reduction
- elimination of duplicate fixed overhead

### Economic strengths
- highest probability of **durable fixed-cost reduction**
- strongest capacity correction
- can improve utilization at remaining sites
- usually clearer margin effect

### Economic weaknesses
- high one-off cost
- transition risk
- launch / ramp risk at receiving plants
- logistics may worsen
- if volumes underperform, remaining plants can still be underutilized

### What must be true for this to work
- remaining plants have enough capex and product allocation to absorb volume
- transfer cost does not erase savings
- shutdown timing does not create customer supply issues
- savings exceed one-off cost within an acceptable payback period

# 4) What I would look for in the financial comparison

For each plant, the core question is:

> Is the plant’s future load, after realistic volume assumptions, sufficient to cover its fixed cost base and earn an acceptable return after capex?

I would calculate:

- **Contribution margin per vehicle**
- **Allocated fixed cost per vehicle**
- **Incremental capex per vehicle**
- **Net cash cost of one-off restructuring**
- **Payback period**
- **Margin recovery timing**

A plant can remain open economically if:
- it has enough future volume,
- its fixed cost base can be reduced enough,
- and it does not require disproportionate capex.

A plant is a closure candidate if:
- volume is structurally too low,
- future product allocation is absent or weak,
- and the site cannot be made competitive without persistent subsidy from the network.

# 5) Likely qualitative ranking of outcomes, absent plant data

This is an analytical generalization, not a fact about any specific site:

## No-closure path is more plausible if:
- overcapacity is moderate, not severe
- one or two plants can absorb volume with only modest capex
- workforce reductions can come largely from attrition
- product allocation can be strengthened quickly
- fixed costs can be lowered via shift reduction and overhead cuts

## Closure / consolidation becomes more likely if:
- one plant has low utilization and weak future product
- multiple plants duplicate the same activity
- capex needed to keep all sites competitive is too high
- labour cost reductions alone do not close the gap
- network complexity is itself a major cost driver

---

# 6) Redeployment feasibility

Redeployment feasibility depends on:

- skill transferability
- geographic distance
- timing relative to product launches
- available vacancies in receiving plants
- training lead time
- age / seniority structure
- willingness to relocate

## Economically, redeployment works best when:
- there is a receiving plant with rising volume
- the labor profile is compatible
- training can be completed before ramp-up
- the receiving plant’s fixed cost absorption improves with added volume

## It works poorly when:
- receiving plants are already near capacity
- skills are highly specialized
- volume is declining across the network
- the timeline is too short for retraining

---

# 7) Capex implications

This is often decisive and frequently underestimated.

## No-closure scenario
Capex is often:
- lower immediately
- but may need repeated spending to keep multiple sites viable
- potentially inefficient if low-volume plants need upgrades

## Closure / consolidation scenario
Capex is often:
- higher at receiving plants
- but concentrated where future volume exists
- potentially better return if it eliminates duplicated spend elsewhere

Key test:
> Does additional capex create enough incremental contribution margin to justify it?

If not, keeping the plant open can become a hidden drain.

---

# 8) One-off cost vs recurring savings

This is the central restructuring trade-off.

## Closure / consolidation
Usually:
- high one-off cost
- high recurring savings

## No-closure package
Usually:
- low one-off cost
- lower recurring savings

The economically superior option is the one with the better **net present value** and acceptable implementation risk.

A closure can be rational if:
- recurring savings are durable and large enough
- payback is not excessive
- transition risk is manageable

A no-closure package can be rational if:
- recurring savings are close enough to closure savings
- one-off costs are much lower
- and the package is enforceable and durable

# 10) Bottom-line analytical conclusion

Based on the information provided so far:

- A **no-closure solution is possible in principle** if it can deliver durable fixed-cost reduction, capacity adjustment, and credible redeployment.
