I’ll respond plainly.

## Where I agree
I agree that **doing nothing is not an option**. We need a durable restructuring, and I agree that the discussion must be **plant-by-plant and evidence-based** rather than ideological.

I also agree that the burden should not fall on employees alone. Management, owners, and labour all have to contribute if we are going to get to a viable outcome.

And I agree with the expert and moderator on one key point: **a no-closure scenario has not been ruled out yet, but it has not been proven either**. That is exactly why we need the quantified comparison.

## Where I disagree
### To the works council
You keep repeating “every German plant stays open,” “no compulsory redundancies,” and “guarantees through 2030” as if those were compatible with the economic reality by definition. They may be desirable from your perspective, but they are **not** an answer to the cost and capacity problem.

You say closures are only a choice. That is not correct if a plant is structurally underutilized, expensive, and lacks future product allocation. Then closure or consolidation is not ideological; it is a legitimate restructuring tool.

You also say we have not proven that alternatives fail. Fair enough. But the burden cannot be reversed so that management is asked to promise site security first and only then allowed to show the numbers. We are prepared to test alternatives, but only if they can deliver **durable fixed-cost reduction and real capacity correction**.

### To the union
I respect that you are willing to discuss employee contributions. But I do **not** accept that compulsory redundancies should be ruled out in advance. If the structural gap cannot be closed otherwise, then we need tools that actually work.

A restructuring plan cannot be built on the assumption that every adjustment will be voluntary and every excess cost can be absorbed elsewhere. That is not a strategy; it is wishful thinking.

### To the regional state
I understand your political responsibility for industrial stability. But blocking change does not preserve an industrial base if the company remains structurally uncompetitive. A site-preservation stance that prevents necessary footprint correction may protect headlines in the short term and weaken the company, the sites, and the region in the long run.

### To the controlling family and investors
I agree with your insistence that this must not become a cosmetic settlement. The company needs structural savings, not symbolic concessions. But structural savings must actually be **implemented**. That means we need a credible model, not just a preference for footprint reduction.

## What the expert input means to me
The expert has said something important: the no-closure path is **possible in principle**, but unproven. The closure/consolidation path is **economically plausible**, but also unquantified.

That means we are still missing the decisive data. And without that, nobody has yet earned the right to say one path is clearly superior.

## What I need next
If we are serious, we need the plant-by-plant comparison now.

## My position remains
I am still not moved from the core position:

- We need **structural and durable** cost and capacity reduction.
- A **cosmetic no-closure deal** is not acceptable.
- **Closures remain on the table** if the data show they are necessary.
- A settlement must restore competitiveness and free investment capacity to 2030.

If the no-closure option can truly achieve comparable, enforceable, long-term savings, then it deserves serious consideration. If it cannot, then we should stop pretending that blanket guarantees solve the problem.

So I am not rejecting alternatives. I am rejecting **unproven alternatives that leave the company uncompetitive**.
