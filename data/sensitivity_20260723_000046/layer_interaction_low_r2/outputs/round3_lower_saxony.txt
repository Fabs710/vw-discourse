I will be clear.

## Where I agree

I agree with all sides on one point: **doing nothing is not an option**. This company needs a durable restructuring, and it needs one that is economically real, not just politically convenient.

I also agree that:
- the burden cannot fall on employees alone,
- hard numbers are necessary,
- and future product allocation and investment are decisive.

The expert input has been useful precisely because it confirms that this is **not yet proven** to be a closure case.

## Where I disagree

### To management
You are still treating closures as if they are the natural endpoint of the discussion. They are not.

You have not yet shown that every alternative has been exhausted, plant by plant. You keep saying the German footprint is too heavy, but that is still an assertion unless you put the plant-level figures on the table:
- utilization,
- fixed-cost burden,
- product allocation,
- capex needs,
- redeployment potential,
- and the actual savings under each scenario.

Until then, I cannot accept closure as “necessary.”

### To the controlling family and investors
I understand the pressure for structural savings. But a settlement that simply shrinks the footprint and damages regional employment is not automatically value-creating. If it undermines the company’s legitimacy, social peace, and long-term workforce commitment, it may solve one problem while creating another.

A durable industrial base is not a sentiment; it is part of long-term value.

### To the works council and union
I support your insistence that the workforce should not be made to pay alone. I also support the demand for plant-level transparency and a serious no-closure scenario.

But I will say this carefully: a no-closure option must be more than a slogan. If it is to be credible, it must show **durable** fixed-cost reduction, not just postponement. That means management and owners are right about one thing: the alternative must be quantified and enforceable.

What I do **not** accept is the idea that the only way to get seriousness is to threaten irreversible plant closures first. That is not a neutral starting point; it is an escalation.

## Our position

For the regional state, the line remains the same:

- **No German plant closures as a default or starting assumption.**
- **No compulsory redundancies as a first resort.**
- **No weakening of co-determination or the state’s blocking role.**
- **A real, quantified no-closure restructuring must be tested first.**

That does **not** mean we ignore competitiveness. It means we insist that competitiveness be restored in a way that does not gut the regional industrial base.

## What would move us

What could move the regional state is a package that:
- preserves sites as far as possible,
- avoids forced layoffs,
- shares the burden fairly across management, owners, and labour,
- and still produces durable savings.

That means:
- working-time adjustments,
- productivity gains,
- management restraint,
- owner contribution,
- voluntary and redeployment-based workforce reductions,
- and product allocation that gives retained plants a future.

I am open to difficult measures. I am not open to a solution that destroys jobs and then calls that prudence.

## What I expect next

The next step must be the quantified comparison the moderator asked for. I want to see:

1. the baseline gap,
2. a genuine no-closure scenario,
3. a closure/consolidation scenario,
4. the employment effect of each,
5. the investment commitments behind each,
6. and the impact on regional industrial stability.

Until that is on the table, no one should pretend that closures are proven necessary.

## My bottom line

I will support a restructuring that protects the company’s long-term health **and** preserves its role as a regional economic anchor.

I will **not** support:
- plant closures as a first answer,
- compulsory redundancies as a default,
- or any arrangement that sidelines the state’s co-determination and veto role.

If management wants my support, it must show a credible alternative that secures competitiveness **without** tearing up the regional industrial base. If it cannot, then it is not yet making a convincing case for irreversible action.
