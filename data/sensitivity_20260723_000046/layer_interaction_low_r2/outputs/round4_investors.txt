We agree on the basic economic diagnosis: **doing nothing is not an option**. From our side, the key test is simple: can the restructuring credibly restore competitive margins and a disciplined cost base, without merely postponing the problem?

## Where we agree
- **Hard numbers are necessary.** The repeated calls for plant-by-plant analysis are correct.
- **The burden should not fall on employees alone.** Management, owners, and labour all have to contribute.
- **A cosmetic settlement would destroy value.** We would not support a deal that preserves overcapacity while calling it a compromise.
- **Future product allocation and capex matter.** A site without future workload is not a viable asset.
- **A no-closure path is not excluded in principle.** But it must prove itself on economics, not sentiment.

## Where we disagree

### To the works council
We do **not** accept your framing that every German plant must stay open and closures are merely a management choice.

That is not a financial position; it is a red line. If the network is structurally overbuilt and some sites are high-cost, underutilized, and lacking future product, then refusing closures in advance risks locking in weak returns for years. That is fundamentally unacceptable to us.

We also do **not** accept binding guarantees through 2030 if they prevent the company from reaching a competitive cost base. Guarantees are only valuable if the underlying economics are sound. If they freeze an uncompetitive structure, they become value-destructive.

### To the union
We respect that you are open to employee contributions, but we do **not** accept the idea that compulsory redundancies must be ruled out now.

If durable savings cannot be achieved otherwise, then management needs the full toolkit. A plan built only on voluntary measures may be too slow, too small, or too reversible to fix the margin problem. We are not prepared to back a weak solution just because it is less confrontational.

### To the regional state
We understand the importance of regional stability, but we do **not** accept a de facto veto on footprint correction.

An industrial anchor that remains structurally uncompetitive is not protected by preserving all sites; it is weakened by doing so. The state’s role should be to ensure an orderly, durable restructuring — not to block necessary change because it is politically difficult.

### To the controlling family
We agree that cosmetic compromise is dangerous. But we would add: **cosmetic hardline rhetoric is equally dangerous** if it prevents a real path back to value creation. Value is restored by execution, not by insisting that every tough measure must be taken.

### To management
You are right to insist that alternatives must be tested. But you have not yet presented the comparison in a way that proves closure is necessary or not necessary. Until then, “closures remain on the table” is not a conclusion; it is an unresolved option.

What we need is not more general statements, but a quantified bridge from current economics to target returns.

## What we need next
We want a comparison for **each German plant** showing:
1. current utilization,
2. future product allocation,
3. fixed-cost burden,
4. capex required to remain competitive,
5. redeployment feasibility,
6. savings from a no-closure package,
7. savings from a closure/consolidation package,
8. one-off restructuring costs,
9. timing to margin recovery,
10. and the probability of successful implementation.

## Our position
We are prepared to support **whatever is economically superior and credibly executable**.

That means:
- if a no-closure plan truly delivers durable fixed-cost reduction, meaningful capacity adjustment, and margin recovery, we can support it;
- if it does not, then closures or deeper footprint reduction cannot be ruled out.

What we will **not** support is:
- preserving every site by principle,
- ruling out compulsory redundancies in advance,
- or accepting a settlement that leaves the cost base uncompetitive.

If the numbers show that the company can get back to target margins without closures, fine. If they do not, then the restructuring must be strong enough to actually fix the problem.
