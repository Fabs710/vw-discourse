A neutral capacity-and-cost assessment is exactly the right next step, because the current dispute is being driven by **strategy claims without a shared operating model**.

## Expert assessment: what can be said now

### 1) The economic problem is real, but “closure vs. no closure” is the wrong first binary
From an industrial-economics standpoint, the relevant question is not whether closures are intrinsically good or bad. The question is:

- how much **durable fixed-cost reduction** is required,
- how much of that can be achieved through **utilization, mix, productivity, labor-cost, and overhead measures**,
- and whether the remaining gap can be closed **without destroying the company’s strategic optionality**.

In a mature European auto business facing volume pressure, EV transition costs, and Chinese/lower-cost competition, the key drivers are typically:
- excess installed capacity,
- low plant utilization,
- high labor and overhead cost per vehicle,
- product-mix weakness,
- and heavy depreciation / transformation expense.

If those are the underlying issues, then management is correct that the solution must be **structural and durable**. Temporary concessions alone rarely restore return on sales.

### 2) Closures are economically powerful, but not the only route to savings
Plant closures generally deliver the largest and most durable savings because they remove:
- fixed labor,
- site overhead,
- maintenance,
- utilities,
- logistics duplication,
- and capital tied up in underused assets.

But closures are not the only mechanism. A non-closure package can produce meaningful savings through a combination of:
- permanent capacity cuts via line reduction or shift elimination,
- lower hours or lower utilization,
- redeployment and attrition,
- outsourcing of non-core operations,
- overhead simplification,
- procurement and engineering productivity,
- and labor-cost adjustments.

The key issue is scale and durability. In auto manufacturing, **reversible or temporary labor measures rarely substitute fully for removing excess capacity**. They may buy time, but they do not always eliminate the fixed-cost burden.

### 3) The decisive variable is utilization
The economic viability of each plant depends heavily on:
- current and projected volume,
- model allocation,
- fixed-cost absorption,
- and the asset’s role in the future product plan.

If a plant can be lifted to high utilization through product assignment or consolidation, closure is less compelling. If a plant remains chronically underutilized, then keeping it open usually means subsidizing excess fixed cost indefinitely.

So the real model must show, for each site:
- capacity installed,
- actual output,
- breakeven volume,
- marginal vs. fixed cost structure,
- and how much volume can realistically be assigned over the next product cycle.

Without that, “save the plant” and “close the plant” are both incomplete statements.

### 4) A non-closure alternative is only credible if it achieves comparable permanent savings
Management is right about one thing: a non-closure solution must be judged by whether it can deliver **comparable economics**, not by whether it sounds cooperative.

For a package to be economically credible, it must show:
- a quantified savings target,
- a timeline for realizing it,
- permanence of the cost reduction,
- enforceability,
- and protection against cost re-inflation.

Typical weak points in non-closure deals are:
- savings back-loaded too far into the future,
- too much reliance on voluntary exits,
- wage moderation that expires before capacity is actually removed,
- and promises of future productivity that are not contractually specific.

If the alternative package does not materially reduce fixed cost and capacity, then it is not a substitute for closure; it is a delay mechanism.

### 5) The burden of proof is now on both sides, but in different ways
From an economics perspective:

- **Management** must prove the required gap: how much savings are needed to hit the target margin and maintain investment capacity.
- **Labor / works council side** must prove that its preferred alternative can generate savings of similar magnitude and durability.
- **Owners and investors** must clarify how much capital they are willing to tolerate being trapped in low-return capacity.

So the debate should move to a simple comparison table:

2. partial closure / consolidation scenario,
3. no-closure restructuring scenario.

# 2) Scenario comparison

## Scenario A: Full plant closure
This means one or more German plants are shut and production is moved elsewhere or removed.

### Economic effect
**Best for durable savings** if the plant is truly underutilized.

Typical annual savings come from:
- direct labor elimination,
- site overhead elimination,
- maintenance and utilities reduction,
- logistics simplification,
- management/indirect cost removal,
- and depreciation avoidance on future underused assets.

### Typical financial profile
- **Annual recurring savings:** high
- **One-time costs:** very high
- **Savings durability:** high
- **Execution risk:** high
- **Workforce impact:** highest

### Indicative ranges
These are broad industrial ranges, not site-specific facts:

- **Annual recurring savings:** often substantial, roughly equivalent to the removed site’s fixed-cost burden, commonly in the **tens to low hundreds of millions of euros per site per year** for a major auto plant
- **One-time costs:** can also be **very large**, due to severance, decommissioning, contract breakage, remediation, logistics reconfiguration, and transfer costs
- **Timeline to most savings:** usually **12–36 months**, depending on product transfer and labor settlement

### When closure is economically justified
Closure tends to be justified if:
- utilization is chronically low,
- there is no credible future product allocation,
- fixed cost per vehicle is structurally uncompetitive,
- and alternative consolidation would not eliminate enough fixed cost.

### Main weakness
The main weakness is that closure creates:
- the largest social cost,
- the highest execution risk,
- and often the biggest political/governance obstacle.

From a financial standpoint, it is strong only if the plant is genuinely surplus to the network.

## Scenario B: Partial consolidation
This means reducing capacity through measures such as:
- shift elimination,
- line consolidation,
- product reallocation,
- site specialization,
- transfer of sub-assemblies,
- or partial footprint reduction without full site closure.

### Economic effect
This can capture a significant part of closure savings if it truly removes fixed cost and excess capacity.

### Typical financial profile
- **Annual recurring savings:** medium to high
- **One-time costs:** medium
- **Savings durability:** medium to high, depending on permanence
- **Execution risk:** medium
- **Workforce impact:** medium to high

### Indicative ranges
Again broad ranges:

- **Annual recurring savings:** often **material but below full closure**, potentially a large fraction of closure savings if enough lines/shifts/sites are removed from the cost base
- **One-time costs:** lower than closure, but still significant because of retooling, transfers, redeployment, and possible duplication during transition
- **Timeline:** typically **6–24 months** to realize meaningful savings, depending on model timing and plant transfer complexity

### When consolidation is attractive
Consolidation is attractive if:
- one plant is underutilized but not uniquely redundant,
- another site can absorb volume efficiently,
- the company can remove whole shifts or lines,
- and product allocation can be redesigned without major cost inflation.

The main risk is **false consolidation**:
- capacity is reduced on paper,
- but overhead and labor remain,
- so savings are delayed or diluted.

- **Savings durability:** often weaker unless locked in contractually
- **Execution risk:** lower industrial disruption, but higher risk of insufficient savings
- **Workforce impact:** lower immediate job loss, but possibly slower attrition over time

- **Annual recurring savings:** often limited unless the package includes real, enduring labor-cost reduction and genuine capacity rationalization
- **One-time costs:** generally below closure, but there can still be material transformation costs

A site is downsized, specialized, or stripped of duplicate functions, but not fully closed.

### Typical economics
- **Recurring savings:** medium to high
- **One-time cost:** medium
- **Headcount impact:** medium to high
- **Capacity reduction:** substantial but not total
- **Implementation risk:** medium

- removal of a shift
- line consolidation
- product reallocation
- centralization of support functions

- working-time reduction
- wage moderation / labor-cost contribution
- voluntary exits
- redeployment
- hiring freeze
- procurement and overhead cuts
- management compensation restraint
- flexibility and shift redesign

# 2) The three scenarios and their economics

## Scenario 1: Full closure
### Economic logic
A full closure is the strongest instrument when a site is:
- chronically underutilized,
- structurally non-competitive,
- not needed for network resilience,
- and expensive to modernize relative to the savings.

### Typical effects
**Advantages**
- Highest fixed-cost reduction
- Eliminates site-level overhead
- Removes persistent underutilization
- Avoids repeated “zombie” restructuring

**Costs**
- Highest one-time cost
- Largest labor and political disruption
- Potential logistics penalties if capacity has to be rebuilt elsewhere
- Risk of product transition bottlenecks
- Higher execution risk if dependent production is not easily relocated

### Economically justified when
Closure tends to be rational if:
- utilization is persistently low,
- breakeven volume is well above realistic demand,
- required future capex is high,
- and another site can absorb volume at lower total system cost.

---

## Scenario 2: Partial consolidation
### Economic logic
Partial consolidation is often the best compromise when the group has:
- overlapping capacity,
- duplicated support functions,
- fragmented product allocation,
- or multiple plants doing similar work.

It means:
- reducing shifts,
- specializing sites,
- moving selected output,
- combining back-office and support functions,
- shutting one line or building rather than the entire site.

### Typical effects
**Advantages**
- Recovers a meaningful share of closure savings
- Lower one-time cost than full closure
- Lower social and political shock
- Can preserve core industrial capacity and skills
- Often enough if the main problem is duplicate overhead and low utilization rather than full site non-viability

**Costs**
- Savings may be incomplete
- Complexity of managing dual footprints
- Risk of “half-closed” sites with weak long-term economics
- Potentially slower to implement than a clean closure

### Economically justified when
Partial consolidation is rational if:
- demand can be reallocated cleanly,
- one site can specialize,
- there is moderate overcapacity but not terminal underperformance,
- and the remaining footprint can achieve acceptable utilization.

---

## Scenario 3: Non-closure restructuring
### Economic logic
This is the most labor-preserving option. It tries to preserve all sites while reducing cost through:
- working-time changes,
- wage moderation or concessions,
- voluntary exits,
- internal redeployment,
- overhead cuts,
- procurement savings,
- management cost reduction,
- and productivity improvement.

### Typical effects
**Advantages**
- Avoids site destruction
- Minimizes political and social conflict
- Preserves skills and future optionality
- Can work if the cost gap is not too large

**Costs**
- Often insufficient if the underlying problem is true excess capacity
- Savings may be less durable
- Requires high trust and repeated renegotiation
- Risk that the company pays for “peace” without fixing structure
- Can leave the network overbuilt and vulnerable

### Economically justified when
A non-closure solution is rational if:
- utilization is not severely depressed,
- the cost gap is mostly labor/overhead rather than true capacity redundancy,
- product allocation can be improved,
- and the permanent savings are large enough to reach target margins.

# 3) What determines whether closures are actually necessary

From an industrial economics standpoint, the key question is not ideological. It is:

> Can the company close the gap through **durable operating savings** without destroying more value than closure would save?

The answer depends mainly on six variables:

### 1. Size of the cost gap
If the gap to target margin is small to moderate, non-closure measures may be enough.
If the gap is large and persistent, capacity reduction usually becomes necessary.

### 2. Underutilization level
Low utilization is the classic closure signal.
If a plant runs far below efficient scale, fixed cost per unit rises sharply.

### 3. Redeployability of output
If volume can be moved without major transport or quality penalties, consolidation is easier.

### 4. Future product allocation
A plant with no credible future model assignment is much harder to defend economically.

### 5. Required future capex
A site that needs major new investment just to remain average may be a poor candidate for retention.

### 6. Network redundancy
If several sites do overlapping work, the system may be paying for duplicate fixed cost.

---

# 4) What would make a non-closure package economically credible?

A non-closure package is credible only if it delivers all of the following:

### A. Enough recurring savings
Not just one-off reductions. It must generate annual run-rate savings large enough to move margins.

### B. Permanence
Temporary wage moderation alone is weak. The savings must last.

### C. Capacity effect
If there is real excess capacity, the package must reduce it somehow:
- fewer shifts,
- fewer lines,
- re-specialization,
- lower overhead,
- or plant-level throughput changes.

### D. Lower fixed cost per unit
The key ratio is fixed cost spread over volume. If the numerator stays too high and the denominator does not rise, the problem remains.

### E. No hidden capex trap
If avoiding closure requires large future capex at low-return sites, the “solution” may be more expensive than closure.

---

# 5) Why compulsory redundancies matter economically

From a restructuring perspective, compulsory redundancies are not a moral question here; they are a mechanism question.

### Economically:
- They may accelerate fixed-cost reduction.
- They can reduce payroll faster than voluntary exits.
- But they also increase industrial conflict, execution risk, and the probability of operational disruption.

### In practice:
A company often prefers:
1. voluntary exits,
2. early retirement,
3. transfer to other sites,
4. short-time and working-time changes,
5. and only then compulsory measures.

If a package **cannot** deliver the needed headcount reduction without forced layoffs, then the package may simply be too weak for the scale of the cost gap.

### Main risk
- Large social/political disruption
- Potential relocation cost
- Execution risk if output cannot be transferred cleanly

---

## B. Partial consolidation
### What it does
- Reduces capacity without fully shutting the site
- Specializes plants
- Moves overlapping output or support functions
- Often closes a line, shift, or building rather than the entire plant

### Typical economics
- **Recurring savings:** medium to high
- **One-time cost:** medium
- **Headcount impact:** material, but smaller than full closure
- **Timing:** often intermediate; can be staged

### When it is rational
Partial consolidation is often the best option if:
- there is overlap between sites,
- volume can be reallocated,
- and the remaining footprint can be brought to acceptable utilization.

### Main risk
- “Half-closed” sites can remain weak long-term
- Savings may be incomplete if overhead is not really removed

---

## C. Non-closure restructuring
### What it does
Preserves all sites while trying to close the cost gap through:
- working-time changes
- wage moderation or labor-cost contribution
- voluntary exits
- redeployment
- qualification
- overhead cuts
- management and procurement savings

### Typical economics
- **Recurring savings:** low to medium, sometimes medium-high if concessions are deep
- **One-time cost:** low to medium
- **Headcount impact:** lower than closure, usually lower than full consolidation
- **Timing:** often faster at first, but savings may be less durable

### When it is rational
This is viable only if:
- the cost gap is moderate,
- utilization is not severely depressed,
- and the savings are durable enough to restore target margins.

### Main risk
- It can preserve the footprint but leave the structural overcapacity untouched
- Temporary concessions may delay, not solve, the problem

---

# 3) What “durable savings” means in this context

A restructuring only works if it produces **permanent fixed-cost reduction**, not just a temporary truce.

Durable savings usually come from:
- permanent headcount reduction
- long-run shift reduction
- structural overhead cuts
- product-line consolidation
- site specialization
- irreversible capacity removal

Less durable:
- temporary wage freezes
- one-off bonus waivers
- short-term working-time sacrifices
- reversible concessions

A non-closure package is only credible if it contains enough of the durable type.

# 5) How to interpret the matrix

## Closure is favored when:
- utilization is structurally low
- breakeven is above credible demand
- product allocation is weak or absent
- required capex is high
- consolidation alternatives do not close the gap

## Partial consolidation is favored when:
- there is redundant capacity but some strategic need to retain the site
- volume can be shifted without large logistics penalty
- overhead can be truly removed, not just shifted elsewhere

## Non-closure restructuring is favored when:
- the cost gap is not too large
- future products are secured
- the site can reach competitive cost through labor/overhead/productivity measures alone

# 6) Job impact and timeline

## Full closure
- **Job impact:** highest, concentrated at closing site
- **Savings timing:** can begin quickly after shutdown decision, but full run-rate often takes 12–36 months
- **One-time costs:** severance, decommissioning, transfer, contractual exit costs

## Partial consolidation
- **Job impact:** significant but less severe
- **Savings timing:** typically 6–24 months
- **One-time costs:** lower than closure, but can still be material if tooling and process transfers are needed

## Non-closure restructuring
- **Job impact:** usually smaller and more spread out
- **Savings timing:** often faster to announce, but full permanent effect may be limited
- **One-time costs:** lower upfront, but risk of insufficient long-term savings

---

# 7) What would make non-closure restructuring economically comparable to closure?

A non-closure package has to deliver most of the same **fixed-cost relief** closure would generate, through other means. That usually requires:

- meaningful and permanent labor-cost reduction
- real overhead reduction
- shift / line reduction
- redeployment or attrition removing surplus roles
- product simplification
- no hidden capex burden
- no offsetting logistics costs that wipe out the savings

If those conditions are not met, non-closure restructuring is usually inferior economically to closure or consolidation.

---

# 8) Sensitivities and uncertainty

The main sensitivities are:

- **Demand trajectory**: if European volume weakens further, retained capacity becomes more expensive
- **Utilization**: small changes in output can materially change unit fixed cost
- **Labor-cost elasticity**: how much permanent cost reduction is actually achievable without closure
- **Relocation cost**: moving volume may be cheap or expensive depending on tooling and supply chain
- **Future capex**: a site needing major reinvestment may be uneconomic even if current operations are stable

6. labor agreement constraints by site

Those six items determine whether closure, consolidation, or non-closure restructuring is rational.

- **Non-closure restructuring** can work only if the cost gap is moderate and the savings are durable
