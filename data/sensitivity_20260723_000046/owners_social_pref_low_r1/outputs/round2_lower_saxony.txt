I will be direct.

I agree with the expert on one key point: **we need a quantified comparison, not slogans**. Management has not yet shown, in a transparent site-by-site way, that closures are the only route to competitiveness. That burden still matters.

But I also need to be equally clear where I disagree with several others:

## To management
I understand the cost pressure and I do not deny the structural problem. However, **closing German plants and forcing redundancies in the state is not my starting point, and not my preferred answer**. Given the special statute, the veto rights, and the regional consequences, you should not assume that closure proposals will simply pass because they are economically convenient on paper.

If you want my support, you need to show:
- the **minimum savings gap**,
- the **site-by-site utilization and breakeven logic**,
- and a **credible non-closure path** before asking for closures.

At this stage, I do **not** accept “closures are necessary” as proven.

## To the Works Council and union
I support your core red lines:
- no compulsory redundancies,
- no hollowing out of co-determination,
- no one-sided burden shift onto workers.

On that, I am aligned.

But I also want to say plainly: if the alternative is only “everything stays as it is,” then we will not protect jobs in the long run. I am **not** asking you to accept destruction; I am asking you to engage with a restructuring that preserves sites and jobs **and** makes the business viable.

So I can support:
- working-time and flexibility solutions,
- capacity reductions across the system,
- internal redeployment,
- voluntary and socially managed exits,
- qualification and transformation measures,
- and a fair, limited employee contribution,

**if and only if** they are tied to:
1. binding site security,
2. job security for the relevant planning period,
3. real investment commitments,
4. and no backdoor closures later.

I will not support a deal that asks workers to contribute while leaving the state’s industrial base exposed.

## To the controlling family and investors
I hear the insistence on durable savings. That is legitimate. But I reject the idea that the only credible version of “durable” is plant closure.

If the economics truly require a reduction in physical capacity, then we need to examine whether that can be achieved through:
- consolidation,
- shift reductions,
- line reallocation,
- product assignment,
- and productivity measures,

before we jump to closures that would damage the regional economy and provoke lasting conflict.

I also want to be clear: **a purely value-maximizing solution that ignores the company’s role as an industrial anchor is not acceptable stewardship** in this case. This is not a normal short-term portfolio asset.

## Where I stand
My position is:

- **I do not accept plant closures in the state as the default solution.**
- **I do not accept compulsory redundancies.**
- **I do accept real restructuring if it preserves jobs, sites, and long-term competitiveness.**
- **I do accept that labor may need to contribute, but only fairly and reversibly, and only with hard guarantees.**
- **I do not accept any outcome that weakens the state’s co-determination and blocking role.**

## What I want next
I support the expert’s call for a proper model. Specifically, I want management to present:
1. the savings requirement,
2. the time horizon,
3. plant-by-plant utilization and breakeven data,
4. the savings from closure-based options,
5. and the savings from credible non-closure alternatives.

Only then can we judge whether closures are really necessary or just the harshest option.
