I will be direct.

## Where I agree
I agree with management, the expert, and the capital side on one fundamental point: **this company has a structural competitiveness problem**.  
I do not dispute that the current setup is too expensive and that temporary gestures alone will not be enough.

I also agree that we need **site-by-site, decision-grade numbers**. If there is overcapacity, it must be shown clearly, not asserted rhetorically.

And I agree with the workforce side that this cannot become a one-sided labor exercise. Any serious restructuring must involve **management, ownership, and the cost structure at the top** as well.

## Where I disagree
I do **not** accept the idea that plant closures and compulsory redundancies should be treated as the default benchmark before the alternative has been properly tested.

That is not prudent restructuring. It is selecting the harshest instrument first and asking the burdened parties to prove why they should not bear it.

### 1) Closures are not a starting point
A closure is not an ordinary efficiency tool. It is the destruction of industrial capacity, regional value, and trust.  
If management wants to close a site in this state, then it must prove:
- that the site is structurally non-viable,
- that partial consolidation cannot achieve comparable economics,
- that employment-preserving alternatives have been exhausted,
- and that the regional consequences are unavoidable.

That burden has not yet been met.

### 2) Forced redundancies remain unacceptable
I will be clear: **compulsory redundancies are not acceptable as a restructuring principle**.  
If workforce adjustment is necessary, it must be done through:
- voluntary exits,
- redeployment,
- qualification,
- working-time changes,
- and negotiated flexibility.

The state will not support a model in which workers are simply made the adjustment variable.

### 3) A blanket 2030 guarantee is not the issue — trust is
I understand management’s objection to freezing the structure. But the employee side and the state are not inventing the trust problem. It exists because closure has already been put on the table.

So yes, I remain in favor of **binding site and job protections** as part of any serious settlement.  
That is not sentimentality. It is the condition for social peace and for any meaningful employee contribution.

## On the expert’s framing
The expert made the key point correctly: **closure is powerful, but it is not the only route**.

That is exactly why I do not accept closure as the reference case.  
The real question is whether the company can achieve the required permanent savings through:
- partial consolidation,
- shift and capacity adjustments,
- product reallocation,
- overhead reduction,
- management contributions,
- and a fair, durable employee contribution,

without gutting sites in the state.

If yes, then that is the better path.  
If no, then management must demonstrate that explicitly, with numbers, not with pressure.

## To management
You keep saying “we cannot rule out closures in advance.” Fine — but you also cannot use that as a threat to force the workforce into accepting your preferred outcome.

So here is the test I expect:
1. show the **minimum savings gap**,
2. show the **site-by-site utilization**,
3. show the savings from **working-time, redeployment, voluntary exits, overhead cuts, and management contribution**,
4. and then show why that package would still fail without closures.

Until you do that, closure is not proven. It is only preferred.

## To the controlling family and investors
I understand your demand for durability. I share that concern. But durability does not mean a permanent erosion of the company’s legitimacy in its home region.

A hard deal that preserves trust and industrial capacity is more durable than a “clean” financial fix that triggers lasting conflict, industrial unrest, and political backlash.

If you want long-term enterprise value, support a package that:
- keeps the company anchored in its home region,
- avoids compulsory redundancies,
- and preserves the state’s meaningful role in governance.

## To the works council and union
You are right to insist that this cannot become a hidden closure program.  
But I also want to be candid: a credible non-closure package must still show genuine adjustment. It cannot just be a moral claim that alternatives ought to work.

So I expect both sides to engage constructively with the numbers, not just with principles.

## My position
I remain on this line:

- **No plant closures in the state as the default or starting point.**
- **No compulsory redundancies.**
- **No outcome that guts regional employment.**
- **No weakening of co-determination or the state’s veto role.**

What I am open to is a **serious restructuring package** that achieves durable savings through:
- partial consolidation where truly necessary,
- working-time and capacity measures,
- internal redeployment,
- voluntary exits only,
- qualification and transformation,
- management and owner contributions,
- and binding site and job guarantees.

That is the only form of compromise that protects both competitiveness and the region.

## Bottom line
I am not moving to closure logic.  
I am prepared to support a hard restructuring, but only if it is **economically credible, socially responsible, and compatible with the company’s role as a regional anchor**.

If the only proposal is closures or forced layoffs, I will oppose it.
