We agree with the expert and with management on the central point: **this is a structural competitiveness problem, not a temporary inconvenience**. If the company does not take out real, durable cost and capacity, then margins stay weak, the market keeps discounting the stock, and capital is wasted on a business that remains uncompetitive.

That said, we are **not** here to endorse the most politically difficult option by default. We care about **risk-adjusted value**, not theatrical toughness.

## Where we agree

### With management
- The current cost base is too high.
- Overcapacity has to be addressed.
- A cosmetic peace deal would be value-destructive.
- Temporary measures alone will not restore return-on-sales.

### With the controlling family
- The solution must be durable and economically hard.
- Owners should not accept a compromise that simply postpones decline.
- A site-by-site comparison is the right way to judge the options.

### With the expert
- The debate must be grounded in quantified economics.
- A pure no-closure package is unlikely to be enough if the gap is truly large.
- Some form of permanent fixed-cost reduction is likely necessary.

## Where we disagree

### With the Works Council and union
We do **not** accept the idea that:
- no plant closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- and strong long-term guarantees

can be treated as non-negotiable **if** they prevent the company from restoring competitiveness.

That position may be understandable socially, but from our perspective it is not a viable economic answer unless the alternative package truly delivers comparable savings. So far, that has not been shown.

We also do **not** accept a settlement that keeps the company overbuilt while relying on reversible workforce concessions. If labor flexibility is the only adjustment, then the burden is not being shared in a way that fixes the business.

### With the regional state
We understand the political and regional stakes. But “no closures in our state” is not, by itself, a financially credible position. If a site is structurally redundant, preserving it for political reasons destroys value and ultimately endangers more jobs later.

## Our position

We would support **whatever package credibly restores competitive margins**, but only if it meets all of these tests:

1. **Durable fixed-cost reduction**
2. **Meaningful capacity reduction**
3. **Measurable labor-cost contribution**
4. **Credible timing to savings**
5. **No prolonged industrial conflict that erodes value**
6. **A clear path back to target return-on-sales**

That means we are open to:
- full closure,
- partial consolidation,
- or a truly strong non-closure restructuring,

but only if the numbers show it works.

## What we are not willing to back
We will not support:
- a symbolic compromise,
- a settlement that preserves every legacy protection and every site regardless of economics,
- or a deal that asks capital to absorb the cost of structural inaction.

## On the current choice
At this stage, the **burden of proof is no longer on investors to accept painful restructuring**. The burden is on the other side to show that a non-closure package can match the economics of more forceful capacity reduction.

Until that is demonstrated, we remain aligned with a hard restructuring logic.
