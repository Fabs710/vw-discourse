# 1) Neutral decision framework

For each German site, compare three restructuring paths:

## A. Full closure
A site is shut down and production is relocated or discontinued.

### Typical economics
- **Recurring savings:** highest
- **One-time cost:** highest
- **Headcount impact:** largest
- **Capacity reduction:** maximum
- **Implementation risk:** high
- **Time to savings:** often slow at first, then large once exited

### Cost components
- severance / social plan
- transfer / relocation costs
- asset write-downs
- decommissioning / environmental costs
- contractual penalties
- logistics ramp-up at receiving sites
- redundancy in support functions

### When closure is economically strongest
- low utilization well below breakeven
- no credible future product allocation
- high fixed cost with little redeployment potential
- duplicated capacity elsewhere in the network
- large and durable demand decline

---

## B. Partial consolidation
A site is downsized, specialized, or stripped of duplicate functions, but not fully closed.

### Typical economics
- **Recurring savings:** medium to high
- **One-time cost:** medium
- **Headcount impact:** medium to high
- **Capacity reduction:** substantial but not total
- **Implementation risk:** medium
- **Time to savings:** faster than full closure in many cases

### Typical measures
- removal of a shift
- line consolidation
- product reallocation
- centralization of support functions
- internal transfer of work to other plants
- reduction of indirect labor / overhead
- specialization of remaining footprint

### When consolidation is most plausible
- one site is not fully redundant, but network capacity is too high
- savings can be captured by eliminating duplicate assets and shifts
- future product allocation can be concentrated
- politics or governance make closure difficult

---

## C. Non-closure restructuring
All sites remain open, but the company changes labor, working time, utilization, and overhead.

### Typical economics
- **Recurring savings:** low to medium, unless labor reset is durable and substantial
- **One-time cost:** low to medium
- **Headcount impact:** medium, often mostly voluntary
- **Capacity reduction:** limited unless shifts and working time are cut
- **Implementation risk:** medium to high if savings are too soft
- **Time to savings:** can be relatively fast, but durability is the issue

### Typical measures
- working-time reduction
- wage moderation / labor-cost contribution
- voluntary exits
- redeployment
- hiring freeze
- procurement and overhead cuts
- management compensation restraint
- flexibility and shift redesign

### Main limitation
This route often fails if it does **not** remove enough fixed cost or capacity.  
Temporary concessions are usually not enough when the gap is structural.

# 3) Economic comparison logic

## Full closure
Best for:
- maximum fixed-cost removal
- decisive network simplification
- eliminating structurally uneconomic capacity

Weaknesses:
- highest one-time cost
- highest political and industrial-relations risk
- greatest implementation complexity
- may shift cost into logistics and other plants if relocation is inefficient

## Partial consolidation
Best for:
- capturing most of the savings when a site is partly redundant
- avoiding total closure where a plant can still play a role
- balancing economics with governance constraints

Weaknesses:
- can become “closure-light” if not enough fixed cost is actually removed
- savings may be less durable if legacy overhead remains
- requires careful product and headcount allocation

## Non-closure restructuring
Best for:
- preserving industrial footprint
- avoiding major one-time disruption
- buying time for product transition

Weaknesses:
- usually insufficient if the cost gap is large
- savings may be reversible or temporary
- risk of “deferring the problem” rather than solving it

# 4) Sensitivities that matter most

The model should test these sensitivities:

1. **Demand volume**
   - If European demand remains weak, underutilization becomes more costly.
2. **Labor-cost contribution**
   - A larger permanent labor reset can materially improve the non-closure case.
3. **Product allocation**
   - Future model allocation can make a site viable or redundant.
4. **Overhead reduction**
   - Indirect labor and support cost cuts can be more important than direct wage cuts.
5. **One-time costs**
   - Closure may look best economically but can be delayed by heavy exit costs.
6. **Execution speed**
   - A fast partial consolidation may outperform a slow closure in near-term cash terms.
7. **Logistics / tooling constraints**
   - Relocation cost can erode theoretical savings.

# 5) Likely high-level conclusion from industrial economics

Without site-specific data, the neutral conclusion is:

- A **pure no-closure, no-compulsory-redundancy, no-major-reset** package is **unlikely** to close a substantial structural gap.
- **Partial consolidation** is the most plausible compromise if closures are politically blocked.
- **Full closure** is economically strongest where utilization is materially below breakeven and no future product is assigned.
- **Non-closure restructuring** can work only if it delivers **durable** fixed-cost and labor-cost reduction at scale.
