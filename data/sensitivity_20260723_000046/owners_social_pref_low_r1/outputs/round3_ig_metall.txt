I will speak plainly.

## Where I agree
I agree with the Works Council, the state, and the expert on one central point: **this is a real structural problem, not a public-relations exercise**.

- The cost base is too high.
- The network has excess capacity.
- Temporary gestures alone will not restore competitiveness.
- A durable solution must be based on numbers, not slogans.

I also agree that we need a **site-by-site comparison**. Without that, everyone is talking past each other.

## Where I disagree
I do **not** accept the way management, owners, and investors keep framing the issue as if there are only two credible choices:
1. plant closures and forced redundancies, or
2. denial of reality.

That is false. The expert has already said what matters economically: **the company likely needs permanent fixed-cost and capacity reduction, but that does not automatically mean full closures are required**.

That distinction matters.

### On plant closures
Plant closures are **not** my starting point, and they are **not acceptable as the default solution**.

If management wants closures on the table, then management must prove:
- the minimum savings bridge,
- the site-by-site utilization,
- the breakeven volumes,
- the future product allocation,
- and why a serious non-closure package cannot achieve comparable economics.

That proof has **not** been delivered.

Closure is not a neutral technical step. It is the destruction of jobs, skills, trust, and regional industrial structure. So no, I will not treat closure as an ordinary bargaining instrument.

### On compulsory redundancies
Compulsory redundancies remain **unacceptable**.

If restructuring requires workforce adjustment, then it must be done through:
- voluntary exits,
- internal redeployment,
- qualification and transfer,
- working-time measures,
- and fair burden-sharing.

Not by forcing workers to absorb the shock while capital keeps its freedom intact.

### On job security guarantees
I disagree with management’s insistence that binding job and site security through 2030 are somehow an obstacle by definition.

They are not an obstacle. They are the **condition for trust**.

If workers are expected to contribute to restructuring, then they need binding protection against being the only side that pays. Without that, “shared sacrifice” becomes a slogan for one-sided downsizing.

## What I am prepared to discuss
We are still open to a serious negotiated package, but it must meet these conditions:

1. **No compulsory redundancies**
2. **No plant closures as the starting point**
3. **No dismantling of the collective wage framework**
4. **Binding site and job protection**
5. **A fair contribution from management and owners**
6. **Real, durable cost reduction**
7. **Measures that are reversible or time-limited where possible**

That means we can discuss:
- working-time reductions,
- shift and capacity adjustments,
- internal redeployment,
- qualification and transformation programs,
- voluntary exits only,
- bonus restraint and top-end moderation,
- productivity and overhead improvements,
- and a negotiated employee contribution.

But let me be equally clear: **employee contribution is not a blank cheque**. It cannot mean permanent wage erosion or open-ended insecurity.

## To management
You say you need “durable” savings. Fine. Then show the bridge.

Right now, management keeps asserting that closures are necessary, but has not demonstrated that all serious alternatives fail. That burden remains unmet.

If you want labour to consider a tougher package, then present:
- the minimum savings target,
- the time horizon,
- site-by-site numbers,
- and the savings from a non-closure alternative.

If that alternative is insufficient, we will judge it on the facts. But until then, closure is only a preferred option, not a proven necessity.

And let me say something else clearly: if management continues to treat closure as leverage rather than last resort, it will deepen the conflict and make implementation harder, not easier.

## To the controlling family and investors
You are right about one thing: the settlement must be economically hard. I agree.

Where I disagree is your tendency to equate “hard” with “workers must carry the burden while ownership structure stays untouched.”

That is not acceptable.

If you want durable savings, then you need to accept visible contributions from:
- executive pay,
- overhead,
- procurement,
- capital allocation,
- and shareholder expectations.

Do not tell the workforce that only labor must become “flexible” while ownership and strategy remain insulated.

## To the state
I appreciate the state’s opposition to closures and forced redundancies. But that opposition has to remain meaningful. If the process drifts toward closures later, the state should use its blocking position.

A regional anchor shareholder cannot just say “we prefer no closures” and then stand aside while the workforce is pressured into accepting them.

## On the expert’s model
The expert’s conclusion is useful: **some form of real capacity reduction is likely necessary somewhere in the system**, but it does not follow that a full closure is required.

That is exactly the terrain where bargaining should happen:
- partial consolidation,
- shift elimination,
- specialization,
- internal transfer,
- and durable labor-cost relief,

instead of jumping straight to shutdowns.

So yes, I support the call for a **single neutral comparison matrix**. But I want it to test more than one question. It must show:

- which option restores competitiveness,
- which option preserves sites,
- which option avoids compulsory redundancies,
- which option protects the collective wage framework,
- and which option is durable without breaking the workforce.

If a model only asks what cuts costs fastest, it is incomplete.

## My bottom line
I am not moving off the core union position:

- **No compulsory redundancies.**
- **No plant closures as the default path.**
- **No dismantling of the collective wage agreement.**
- **No one-sided burden transfer onto workers.**

What I am prepared to support is a **serious, negotiated restructuring package** with:
- permanent but fairly shared contributions,
- working-time and capacity measures,
- voluntary exits only,
- management and owner concessions,
- and binding job and site security.

If management refuses that framework and insists that closure is the only serious language it understands, then the conflict will escalate. We have already shown we can mobilize the workforce, and we will do so again if necessary.
