I agree with the Works Council on the core point: **closures and compulsory redundancies are not an acceptable starting point**. If management wants the workforce to accept sacrifices, then those sacrifices must be **shared, limited, and tied to a credible future for the sites and jobs**.

Let me be equally clear from the union side:

- **No plant closures.**
- **No compulsory layoffs.**
- **No dismantling of the collective wage framework.**
- **No one-sided “worker pays, owners and management watch” restructuring.**

Management cannot solve structural mistakes by emptying out the workforce and tearing up standards that were negotiated over decades. That would not be a durable settlement; it would trigger conflict and undermine trust for years.

Where we are prepared to discuss movement is here:

- temporary or negotiated **labour-cost contributions**,
- **working-time and capacity adjustments**,
- voluntary and socially responsible exit options,
- internal redeployment,
- qualification and transformation measures,
- and, if necessary, a **limited waiver of bonuses or other elements**, provided this is matched by real commitments from management, owners, and the company to the sites.

But any such contribution must come with hard conditions:

1. **Job security and site security must be guaranteed.**
2. **Sacrifices must be reversible or time-limited.**
3. **Management and owners must contribute visibly and proportionately.**
4. **No hidden backdoor toward closures later.**

So my position is not “no change.” My position is: **change yes, destruction no**. If management wants a solution, it must present one that protects industrial capacity in Germany, preserves collective bargaining, and distributes the burden fairly across all stakeholders.

If the proposal remains based on plant closures, forced redundancies, or unilateral wage-cutting, then it will not find consent on the workforce side. In that case, we are prepared to escalate further.
