I hear the positions clearly. Let me respond from the controlling shareholder’s perspective, because this is exactly the kind of moment where long-term ownership discipline matters.

First, where I agree:
- The company **does** need a structural answer, not a cosmetic one.
- The current cost base and capacity structure are **not sustainable**.
- A solution that simply preserves the status quo would erode value, investment capacity, and ultimately jobs.
- Any settlement that produces prolonged industrial conflict would be damaging for everyone, including the owners.

So I am aligned with management on the need for a **credible competitiveness reset**.

Where I disagree with the workforce side and, to a lesser extent, the state:
- A blanket “no closures, no compulsory redundancies, no meaningful structural change” is **not acceptable**.
- A deal that protects every site irrespective of economics is not stewardship; it is **deferred decline**.
- If the restructuring cannot produce durable savings, then the company will be weaker, not stronger, and that is unacceptable for the enterprise and the owners.

I also want to be direct on governance: this family holding will **not** support any outcome that permanently impairs the company’s value or strips management of the ability to restore competitiveness. At the same time, I am not looking for theatrics or a public confrontation. We expect this to be handled through the supervisory board and the proper governance channels, but the economic reality cannot be negotiated away.

That said, I do **not** read the situation as “closures or nothing.” If there is a genuine alternative package that delivers **comparable, durable savings** and restores margins, then it deserves serious consideration. But it must meet hard tests:
1. It must be **large enough** to close the competitiveness gap.
2. It must be **durable**, not a temporary deferral.
3. It must be **credible and enforceable**.
4. It must not simply shift the burden entirely onto the owners while preserving every legacy protection untouched.

On that basis, I would support a negotiated solution if it includes:
- a real and measurable labour-cost contribution,
- capacity reductions that actually eliminate overcapacity,
- flexibility in working time and deployment,
- voluntary and socially managed staffing reductions where possible,
- and a clear investment path into future products and technologies.

What I will not support:
- a settlement that preserves all plants as a sacred principle,
- a purely political compromise with no economic substance,
- or a deal that locks in the current structure and leaves the company uncompetitive.

To the works council and union: if you want site and job security, then you need to engage with the scale of the problem. That means accepting that some concessions are unavoidable. If you refuse every structural adjustment, then you are effectively choosing value destruction over adaptation. That is not a responsible position for long-term stakeholders.

To management: you need to be careful not to present the harshest possible version of restructuring as the only route. If there is a less destructive path that still achieves the economics, you should be prepared to take it. But if labor refuses any substantive contribution, then you should not hesitate to insist on stronger structural measures. The company cannot be made competitive by goodwill alone.

To the regional state: your role as anchor shareholder is important, but it cannot become a veto over economic reality. If the alternative to closures is only a symbolic compromise that leaves the cost base intact, that is not a responsible use of your position either. Protecting the regional economy in the long run requires a viable company, not merely preserved headcount.

So my position is this:
- **No to paralysis.**
- **No to a deal that is economically empty.**
- **Yes to a negotiated restructuring if it genuinely restores competitiveness and protects long-term enterprise value.**

If the discussion is serious, then bring forward a package that matches the scale of the challenge. If it is not serious, then we should be honest that the company is being pushed toward a weaker future.
