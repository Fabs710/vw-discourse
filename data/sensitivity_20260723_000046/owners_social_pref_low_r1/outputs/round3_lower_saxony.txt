I will be equally direct.

## Where I agree
I agree with all sides on one essential point: **this company has a structural competitiveness problem and needs a durable solution**.  
I also agree that we cannot settle this with slogans, and that a **site-by-site, quantified comparison** is now necessary.

The expert contributions were useful in one respect: they confirmed that temporary gestures alone will not solve a structural cost gap. So yes, there must be real capacity and cost adjustment somewhere in the system.

## Where I disagree
I do **not** accept that this automatically makes plant closures or compulsory redundancies the benchmark solution.

### 1) Closures are not “the” default answer
To management, the family, and investors: I understand the pressure for durability and margin improvement. But you have **not yet proven** that closures are unavoidable.

The expert was careful:  
- closures are powerful,  
- but they are **not the only route**,  
- and **partial consolidation** may capture much of the savings if it truly removes fixed cost and excess capacity.

That matters. It means the discussion should not start from “which plant to close,” but from “what is the minimum permanent savings gap, and which package closes it without destroying the regional industrial base?”

I will not endorse closure talk as a shortcut for convenience.

### 2) Forced redundancies remain unacceptable
To management: I hear your insistence that you cannot rule out workforce reduction in advance. I understand that you need flexibility.

But I am not moving on this point: **compulsory redundancies are not acceptable** as a restructuring instrument in this situation.  
If the company needs adjustment, then it must come through:
- voluntary exits,
- redeployment,
- working-time measures,
- qualification,
- and a fair, negotiated contribution.

Not through dismissals imposed on the workforce while others call that “hard restructuring.”

### 3) Co-determination is not a decorative feature
To management and capital: this company’s governance is not a formality.  
The state has a blocking position under the special statute, and the workforce has real co-determination rights. Those are not obstacles to be worked around; they are part of the company’s legitimacy.

Any deal that sidelines those rights will not be stable.  
If you want a durable settlement, it must be designed **with** the relevant stakeholders from the outset, not presented later as a near-finished package.

### 4) Job and site guarantees are not the problem
I disagree with the suggestion that binding site and job protections are inherently incompatible with restructuring. They are not.

They are the condition for trust.

If the company wants labor and the state to support difficult measures, then it must provide real protection against backdoor closures and later attrition. Otherwise, “shared sacrifice” becomes a one-way street.

## What I think the expert analysis actually implies
The expert did **not** say the company must close plants. What the expert said is:
- the company likely needs **real permanent fixed-cost reduction**, and
- **some form of hard capacity reduction** somewhere may be necessary,
- but that does **not automatically mean full closures**.

That is the key point.

So for me, the real test is:
- can the company achieve the required savings through **partial consolidation, shift reduction, product reallocation, overhead cuts, and a fair employee contribution**,
- while preserving plants and jobs in the state,
- and while maintaining co-determination and the company’s role as a regional anchor?

That is the route that still deserves serious examination.

## Where I am open
I remain open to:
- capacity reductions spread across the network,
- shift and utilization adjustments,
- internal redeployment,
- voluntary exits only,
- qualification and transformation programs,
- top-management and owner contributions,
- and a limited, fair labor contribution if it is part of a balanced package.

I am also open to discussing **partial consolidation**, if and only if it does not become a disguised closure by another name and if it preserves the state’s industrial base in a meaningful way.

## What I will not accept
Let me be clear about the boundary:

- **No plant closures in the state as the starting point or default solution.**
- **No compulsory redundancies.**
- **No outcome that guts regional employment.**
- **No weakening of co-determination or the state’s veto role.**

Those are not bargaining positions I will drift away from because the discussion is long or because others keep repeating “durable savings.”

## What I want next
I support the call for a **single neutral site-by-site comparison**. But it must compare not only savings, but also:
- regional employment impact,
- the ability to preserve key sites,
- the risk of long-term industrial decline,
- and whether the company remains a real anchor for the state.
