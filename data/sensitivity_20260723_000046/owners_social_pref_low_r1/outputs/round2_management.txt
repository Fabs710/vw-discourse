I’ll respond directly.

## To the Works Council
I agree with one thing: this company has to be transformed, not just patched. But I do **not** agree that we can declare “no closures, no forced redundancies, no meaningful structural change” and still expect a competitive business.

You keep saying plant closures are a choice, not a necessity. In one sense that is true. But if the only alternative is to leave us with an uncompetitive cost base, then that “choice” is really just choosing decline more slowly. I will not do that.

What I **do** accept:
- we should avoid closures if a serious alternative can deliver the same economics;
- any settlement must be negotiated;
- redeployment, qualification, and voluntary exits all belong on the table.

What I **do not** accept:
- binding job guarantees through 2030 if they block restructuring;
- a principle that every German plant must stay open regardless of economics;
- a framework where labour contributes only through reversible, symbolic measures while structural overcapacity remains.

If you want me to move away from closures, then I need a package that is not just social language, but hard numbers:
- durable capacity reduction,
- a material labour-cost contribution,
- and a fixed path to lower overhead and better margins.

Without that, I am not bargaining around the core issue.

## To the Trade Union
I understand your insistence that the workforce should not pay alone. On that point, I agree. Management, owners, and labour all have to contribute.

But I disagree with your red line on keeping the collective wage framework untouched if it means nothing can change materially. A “limited waiver of bonuses” or “temporary moderation” is not enough if the structural cost base remains too high. We need more than temporary optics.

You ask for proof that closures are necessary. Fair enough — but the burden is not only on management to prove the impossibility of every alternative. The burden is also on the union to show that its preferred alternative can actually deliver:
- comparable savings,
- durability,
- and credibility with owners, lenders, and markets.

So here is my challenge back:
present a package that produces real, permanent savings at the scale needed. Not abstract solidarity. Not a list of principles. A concrete mechanism.

If you want to avoid closures, then show how we permanently remove capacity and fixed costs without them.

## To the Regional State Government
I appreciate your recognition that the company must become more competitive. But I need to be equally clear: your position cannot simply be “restructuring yes, closures no” if you are not prepared to accept the cost logic that follows.

If the state wants to preserve industrial capacity, then it has to support a solution that actually pays for it. That means:
- real labour-cost contributions,
- real capacity reduction,
- real flexibility,
- and no blanket guarantee that freezes the structure in place.

I agree that the regional economy matters. I disagree that preserving every site as-is is the way to protect it. A company that remains uncompetitive eventually threatens far more jobs than any targeted restructuring would.

So yes, I am open to an alternative to closures — but not to a political veto that leaves the business structurally weakened.

## To the Controlling Family
You are right that this cannot be a cosmetic compromise. And I agree that management must not present the harshest possible version as the only version.

But I need to push back on the implication that management should simply wait for a labour-friendly solution to appear. The numbers are urgent. Time is not on our side. If we delay too long, we lose margin, investment capacity, and strategic credibility.

I will support a less destructive path **if it is real**. That means:
- measurable savings,
- enforceable commitments,
- and a structural shift in cost and capacity.

If that cannot be achieved, then yes, stronger measures remain on the table.

## To Institutional Investors
You are right on one point: the company cannot afford a symbolic peace deal. I agree. We need durable margin improvement.

Where I disagree is your tendency to treat labour cost reductions and capacity cuts as if they can be abstracted from the social and governance reality. They cannot. If we push through a settlement that lacks legitimacy, we get strikes, sabotage of execution, and a weaker company.

That said, I will not subordinate competitiveness to the comfort of legacy arrangements. If labour wants to avoid closures, it has to help close the economics gap in a serious way.

## Bottom line
Let me be explicit:

- I am **open** to a non-closure solution.
- I am **not open** to a deal that preserves all plants, preserves most costs, and calls that restructuring.
- I am **not open** to a blanket 2030 job guarantee if it freezes necessary change.
- I am **not open** to temporary measures that expire before the savings arrive.

If the employee side wants to avoid closures, then bring forward a package that achieves:
1. durable overcapacity reduction,
2. meaningful labour-cost relief,
3. lower fixed costs,
4. and a credible return to target margins.

If no such package exists, then we should stop pretending that closure avoidance by itself is a solution.
