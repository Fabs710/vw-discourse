I will be direct.

## Where I agree
I agree with the expert, management, and the institutional investors on the central point: **this is a structural competitiveness problem, not a temporary dip**. The current cost and capacity structure cannot remain unchanged and still deliver acceptable returns. A cosmetic compromise would be value-destructive.

I also agree that we need a **site-by-site, quantified comparison**. Without that, we are just trading slogans.

And I agree with the state and labor on one important procedural point: a settlement that is imposed rather than negotiated would be unstable and therefore bad for value.

## Where I disagree
### To the Works Council and union
I do **not** accept your framing that plant closures and compulsory redundancies are inherently unacceptable in advance.

You are treating the current footprint as if it were sacrosanct. It is not. If a site is structurally underutilized and cannot be made competitive within a credible network plan, then refusing even to discuss closure is not stewardship of jobs — it is postponing a larger and more damaging adjustment.

The expert was careful, and correctly so:
- closures are powerful but not the only tool,
- partial consolidation may capture much of the benefit,
- non-closure restructuring can work only if it removes enough fixed cost and capacity.

That does **not** support a blanket “no closures” line. It supports a disciplined comparison.

On compulsory redundancies: I prefer voluntary and socially managed exits, but I will not join an absolute prohibition if the numbers show that meaningful workforce reduction is unavoidable. The company cannot be trapped by a promise that prevents restructuring.

### To the Regional State Government
I respect your role and your concern for the regional economy. But I do **not** accept “no closures in our state” as a governing principle if the economics do not support it.

Protecting the region long term sometimes requires accepting hard restructuring now. Freezing an oversized industrial footprint may feel protective, but if it leaves the business weak, it ultimately threatens more jobs and less investment later.

Your blocking position is real. So is the responsibility to use it in a way that preserves the company’s long-term viability, not just the current map of sites.

### To management
You are right to insist on durable savings. But I also want to push back on any temptation to present closures as the only credible route before the model is complete.

The expert explicitly said that **partial consolidation** may be the most plausible compromise if it truly removes fixed cost and excess capacity. So management should be precise:
- What is the minimum savings gap?
- How much comes from labor cost?
- How much from overhead?
- How much from capacity removal?
- Which sites are truly redundant?
- What savings can be locked in without full closure?

If management cannot answer that, then closure advocacy is premature. If it can, then labor and the state must engage with the result rather than hiding behind principles.

### To the union and works council on guarantees
A blanket job or site guarantee through 2030 is not acceptable if it freezes the network before we know the required adjustment. Guarantees can be part of a settlement, but only if they do not block real restructuring.

I will not support a deal that preserves formal protections while leaving the company structurally uncompetitive.

## Where I stand
My position remains:

1. **The company needs permanent fixed-cost reduction.**
2. **Some form of hard capacity reduction is likely necessary somewhere in the system.**
3. **A non-closure solution is possible only if it is economically comparable, durable, and enforceable.**
4. **If that cannot be demonstrated, closures and stronger workforce measures remain on the table.**

I am not committed to closures as the first choice. I am committed to **whatever structure restores competitiveness and protects long-term value**.

## On what I expect next
I support a neutral comparison, but it must be honest and complete.

The model should answer one question clearly:
**Which package closes the cost gap permanently without simply shifting the pain into the future?**

## Bottom line
I will not endorse a blanket no-closure commitment.
I will not endorse a deal that preserves all sites and most of the cost base.
I will support a settlement only if it is **hard, durable, and credible** — and that may well require partial consolidation or, if the numbers force it, closures.

If the workforce side wants to avoid the harshest outcome, it needs to show a package that really closes the gap. Until then, we should stop pretending that “no closures” by itself is a solution.
