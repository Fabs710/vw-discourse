- excess installed capacity,
- low utilization,
- high fixed site overhead,
- labor-cost gap versus competitors,
- and EV-transition-related underabsorption.

In that context:

- A **pure non-closure package** can work only if the required savings gap is **moderate** and if the company can permanently remove enough fixed cost through utilization, shift, headcount, and overhead measures.
- A **full closure scenario** is the most powerful lever for durable fixed-cost reduction, but it has the largest one-time cost, highest execution risk, and largest workforce impact.
- A **partial consolidation** scenario is often the most economically balanced option: it can capture much of the closure economics while reducing political and operational disruption, but only if it truly removes capacity and fixed cost rather than merely deferring them.

My provisional judgment is that **some hard capacity reduction is likely necessary somewhere in the system**, but it does **not automatically follow that a full site closure is required**. The key question is whether the company can permanently reduce enough fixed cost through consolidation and labor-cost reset to restore target margins.

# 2) Scenario comparison

## Scenario A: Full plant closure
This means one or more German plants are shut and production is moved elsewhere or removed.

### Economic effect
**Best for durable savings** if the plant is truly underutilized.

Typical annual savings come from:
- direct labor elimination,
- site overhead elimination,
- maintenance and utilities reduction,
- logistics simplification,
- management/indirect cost removal,
- and depreciation avoidance on future underused assets.

### Typical financial profile
- **Annual recurring savings:** high
- **One-time costs:** very high
- **Savings durability:** high
- **Execution risk:** high
- **Workforce impact:** highest

### Indicative ranges
These are broad industrial ranges, not site-specific facts:

- **Annual recurring savings:** often substantial, roughly equivalent to the removed site’s fixed-cost burden, commonly in the **tens to low hundreds of millions of euros per site per year** for a major auto plant
- **One-time costs:** can also be **very large**, due to severance, decommissioning, contract breakage, remediation, logistics reconfiguration, and transfer costs
- **Timeline to most savings:** usually **12–36 months**, depending on product transfer and labor settlement

### When closure is economically justified
Closure tends to be justified if:
- utilization is chronically low,
- there is no credible future product allocation,
- fixed cost per vehicle is structurally uncompetitive,
- and alternative consolidation would not eliminate enough fixed cost.

### Main weakness
The main weakness is that closure creates:
- the largest social cost,
- the highest execution risk,
- and often the biggest political/governance obstacle.

From a financial standpoint, it is strong only if the plant is genuinely surplus to the network.

---

## Scenario B: Partial consolidation
This means reducing capacity through measures such as:
- shift elimination,
- line consolidation,
- product reallocation,
- site specialization,
- transfer of sub-assemblies,
- or partial footprint reduction without full site closure.

### Economic effect
This can capture a significant part of closure savings if it truly removes fixed cost and excess capacity.

### Typical financial profile
- **Annual recurring savings:** medium to high
- **One-time costs:** medium
- **Savings durability:** medium to high, depending on permanence
- **Execution risk:** medium
- **Workforce impact:** medium to high

### Indicative ranges
Again broad ranges:

- **Annual recurring savings:** often **material but below full closure**, potentially a large fraction of closure savings if enough lines/shifts/sites are removed from the cost base
- **One-time costs:** lower than closure, but still significant because of retooling, transfers, redeployment, and possible duplication during transition
- **Timeline:** typically **6–24 months** to realize meaningful savings, depending on model timing and plant transfer complexity

### When consolidation is attractive
Consolidation is attractive if:
- one plant is underutilized but not uniquely redundant,
- another site can absorb volume efficiently,
- the company can remove whole shifts or lines,
- and product allocation can be redesigned without major cost inflation.

### Main weakness
The main risk is **false consolidation**:
- capacity is reduced on paper,
- but overhead and labor remain,
- so savings are delayed or diluted.

This scenario only works if management is willing to take out real fixed cost, not just reshuffle production.

---

## Scenario C: Non-closure restructuring
This means no German plant closures. Savings come from:
- working-time adjustments,
- voluntary exits,
- internal redeployment,
- labor-cost contributions,
- bonus restraint,
- productivity improvements,
- procurement and overhead cuts,
- shift optimization,
- and perhaps some capacity reduction via utilization management rather than physical closure.

### Economic effect
This is the least disruptive but also the hardest to make durable at scale.

### Typical financial profile
- **Annual recurring savings:** low to medium, unless concessions are substantial and permanent
- **One-time costs:** lower than closure, but not negligible
- **Savings durability:** often weaker unless locked in contractually
- **Execution risk:** lower industrial disruption, but higher risk of insufficient savings
- **Workforce impact:** lower immediate job loss, but possibly slower attrition over time

### Indicative ranges
- **Annual recurring savings:** often limited unless the package includes real, enduring labor-cost reduction and genuine capacity rationalization
- **One-time costs:** generally below closure, but there can still be material transformation costs
- **Timeline:** savings can begin sooner if wage/bonus/flexibility measures are immediate, but structural savings may be **insufficient** if physical capacity remains unchanged

### When it can work
It can work if:
- the savings gap is not too large,
- productivity gains are credible and enforceable,
- labor-cost relief is durable,
- and some capacity is removed through utilization/shift reduction.

### Main weakness
The biggest weakness is that it may **buy time rather than solve the problem**. If overcapacity is real and persistent, non-closure restructuring can leave the fixed-cost base too high.

# 4) What is likely necessary for credibility

If management is saying the company needs a **durable return to target margin**, then from a modeling standpoint the following are usually needed:

### A. Some permanent fixed-cost reduction
Temporary concessions alone are rarely enough.

### B. Some form of capacity reduction
Not necessarily a full closure, but enough to improve utilization meaningfully.

### C. A labor-cost reset
If the German cost base is materially above competitors’, a durable package usually needs:
- wage moderation,
- bonus restraint,
- working-time flexibility,
- and/or permanent structural adjustments.

### D. Site-specific product allocation
The future product plan matters more than abstract plant counts.

If the next model cycle leaves a site underfilled, closure or consolidation becomes much harder to avoid.

# 5) Likely decision logic by site

A site should be considered for closure if most of the following are true:
- utilization is structurally low,
- no major future model is assigned,
- logistics duplication is high,
- fixed-cost absorption is poor,
- and another site can absorb volume at lower total cost.

A site is better suited for consolidation if:
- it is underutilized but adjacent capacity can absorb production,
- specialization can reduce complexity,
- and a line or shift can be removed cleanly.

A site is a better candidate for non-closure restructuring if:
- it has a strong product role,
- utilization can improve,
- and cost can be reduced through labor and overhead rather than footprint reduction.

# 6) Timing expectations

## Closure
- **Decision to savings:** often 12–36 months
- Savings ramp can be delayed by transfer and wind-down complexity.

## Consolidation
- **Decision to savings:** often 6–24 months
- Faster if a shift or line can be removed without major retooling.

## Non-closure restructuring
- **Decision to savings:** can begin in 0–12 months for compensation-related measures
- But full structural improvement may be weak unless accompanied by permanent capacity and overhead reduction.

---

# 7) Sensitivities

The outcome is highly sensitive to:

1. **Volume outlook**
   - If demand recovers, closure pressure eases.
   - If demand weakens, consolidation/closure pressure rises.

2. **Product allocation**
   - A new model assigned to a site can change the economics dramatically.

3. **Labor-cost contribution**
   - A meaningful and durable labor contribution can make non-closure or consolidation more viable.

4. **Overhead removal**
   - Management often underestimates how much non-production overhead must also be cut.

5. **Transition cost**
   - Moving production is expensive and can erode apparent savings.

6. **Utilization after restructuring**
   - A plant with poor post-restructuring utilization can still be uneconomic.
