I will be direct.

### Where I agree
I agree with the Works Council, the state, and even parts of management’s diagnosis:

- The company has a real competitiveness problem.
- The German cost base is too high.
- A purely symbolic compromise would be irresponsible.
- A durable settlement has to be economically credible.

So yes: this cannot be solved by slogans, and it cannot be solved by asking everyone to “hang in there” while the numbers stay broken.

### Where I disagree
I do **not** accept the logic that because there is a problem, the solution must be:

- plant closures,
- compulsory redundancies,
- and a one-sided transfer of the pain onto the workforce.

That is exactly where I draw the line.

Management keeps speaking as if closures are the natural benchmark and anything else is a concession. That is not acceptable. Closure is not a technical necessity until every serious alternative has been exhausted and proven insufficient. That burden has not been met.

To the controlling family and investors: I hear the insistence on durable savings. But if your definition of “durable” means tearing up job security and collective standards while keeping ownership and executive power untouched, then you are asking labour to pay for the restructuring alone. That will not produce consent, and it will not produce stability.

To the state: I appreciate the clear opposition to closures and forced layoffs. But support for “restructuring” only matters if it is backed by real resistance when closures are put on the table. Otherwise, the workforce is left to carry the fight alone.

### What we are prepared to discuss
We remain open to a **serious negotiated alternative** if — and only if — it satisfies all of the following:

1. **No plant closures.**
2. **No compulsory redundancies.**
3. **No dismantling of the collective wage framework.**
4. **Binding site and job security guarantees.**
5. **A fair contribution from management and owners as well as labor.**
6. **Measures that are reversible or time-limited where possible.**
7. **A credible industrial plan that protects future investment and transformation.**

That leaves room for real change:
- working-time reductions,
- capacity adjustments across sites,
- internal redeployment,
- qualification and transformation measures,
- voluntary exits only,
- bonus restraint or temporary concessions,
- and a negotiated employee contribution.

But let me be equally explicit: **we will not trade away collective protections just to buy management more room to reorganize later against the workforce.**

### On the “show us the numbers” point
I actually agree with the demand for numbers. So here is the position from the union side:

If management claims closures are necessary, then it must show:
- the savings target,
- the time horizon,
- the cost gap,
- the alternatives considered,
- and why a negotiated non-closure package cannot close the gap.

Until that is laid out transparently, closure talk is not a plan — it is pressure.

And if a non-closure package can deliver comparable economics, then it deserves serious consideration. But “comparable” cannot mean identical to management’s preferred maximum cut. It means enough to restore viability **without destroying the collective framework and workforce security**.

### My bottom line
We are not refusing change. We are refusing a crisis strategy that makes the workforce the sole adjustment variable.

If management wants our cooperation, then it must present a settlement in which:
- labor contributes fairly,
- owners and management contribute visibly,
- jobs and sites are protected,
- and the collective agreement remains intact.

If that is not on the table, then the conflict will deepen. And if needed, we will use the tools available to us.
