I hear the workforce side clearly, but I need to be equally clear in return: **“no closures, no compulsory redundancies, no meaningful labour-cost adjustment” is not a restructuring plan**. It is a request to preserve the current cost base in a market that is already punishing us.

We are not in a theoretical debate. We are facing:

- a shrinking European market,
- intensifying competition,
- structurally too high German factory costs,
- and a brand that is not meeting its return-on-sales target.

If we simply freeze the existing structure, we will not restore competitiveness. And if we do not restore competitiveness, there will be **less investment, weaker products, and a worse future for every site**.

Where I agree:
- I agree that the goal is not destruction for its own sake.
- I agree that a durable settlement must be negotiated, not imposed.
- I agree that transformation, qualification, redeployment, and voluntary exit routes all have a role.

Where I disagree:
- I do **not** accept that every German plant must remain open irrespective of economics.
- I do **not** accept a blanket guarantee through 2030 if it prevents necessary structural change.
- I do **not** accept that burden-sharing can come only from management and owners while labour costs remain essentially untouched.
- I do **not** accept a cosmetic deal that preserves formal peace but leaves us uncompetitive.

Let me be explicit: management needs a **structural and sustainable** reduction in both cost and capacity. That means the package must create real savings, not just defer the problem. That could include plant rationalisation, capacity reductions, and a genuine labour-cost contribution. If there is an alternative package that delivers savings of comparable scale and durability **without closures**, I am prepared to examine it seriously. But it must be credible, measurable, and binding.

What would move me:
1. **A durable labour-cost contribution** of meaningful scale.
2. **A credible capacity reduction** that removes overcapacity permanently.
3. **Real flexibility on working time and deployment** that lowers fixed costs.
4. **Voluntary and socially managed staffing reductions**, where possible.
5. **A settlement that protects future investment** in new products and technology.

What will not move me:
- promises of peace without economics,
- temporary measures that expire before the savings materialise,
- or a demand that management simply absorb the crisis while the cost base stays where it is.

To be direct: if labour insists on absolute site immutability, then the choice narrows to deeper conflict, prolonged uncertainty, and greater pressure on the company’s future. That is not my preferred path, but I will not pretend it is off the table if no serious structural solution is offered.

So the question is not whether there will be sacrifice. The question is **whether the sacrifice will be structured enough to save the business**. I am ready to discuss alternatives to closures — but only if they deliver the same hard outcome: a lasting reduction in overcapacity and labour cost, and a credible path back to competitive margins.
