From the perspective of dispersed capital-market shareholders, I want to be very clear: **we are not here to defend every legacy arrangement, and we are not impressed by symbolic peace if the economics remain broken**.

### Where we agree
We agree with management, and broadly with the controlling family, on the core diagnosis:

- the German cost base is too high,
- overcapacity has to be dealt with,
- the company must restore competitive margins,
- and a cosmetic compromise would destroy value.

We also agree that prolonged conflict is costly. A drawn-out strike, uncertainty, and drifting operational performance are all value-negative. The company needs a **credible, durable restructuring**, not a headline agreement that leaves the return-on-sales problem untouched.

### Where we disagree
We do **not** support a solution that is so politically constrained that it leaves the business structurally impaired.

That means:
- **No acceptance of an outcome that preserves uncompetitive capacity simply because closures are unpopular.**
- **No support for a deal that loads all of the adjustment onto capital while labour costs stay effectively unchanged.**
- **No support for a “temporary peace” that expires before the savings show up.**

To be blunt: if the final package does not produce **real, durable margin improvement**, we will view it as value destruction.

### On the workforce positions
I understand why the works council and union are resisting closures and compulsory redundancies. But from our standpoint, **a blanket veto on plant closures is not credible if the alternative is to keep an overbuilt, high-cost structure in place**. That would simply postpone the problem and weaken the company further.

At the same time, we are not demanding destruction for its own sake. If there is a **serious alternative package** that delivers comparable savings through:
- capacity reductions,
- working-time flexibility,
- internal redeployment,
- voluntary exits,
- and a meaningful employee contribution,

then we would view that positively.

But the key word is **comparable**. It must be:
1. **large enough** to close the competitiveness gap,
2. **durable**,
3. **binding**, and
4. **credible to markets**.

Without those elements, it is not a solution.

### On management
Management is right to insist on structural change, but it also has to avoid presenting the most aggressive restructuring as the only conceivable option. Markets will reward a disciplined plan, not just a hard one. If you can secure the same economics with less disruption, that is preferable.

But if the company accepts a settlement that protects every site while avoiding meaningful savings, then management should not expect investor patience indefinitely. We will read that as weak execution and weak governance.

### On the controlling family and state
We respect the role of the family holding and the state as anchor shareholders, but their stewardship duty is to protect **enterprise value**, not simply to preserve the appearance of industrial continuity.

A deal that avoids closures but leaves margins structurally impaired is not long-term stewardship. It is delayed decline.

### Our position
We would support:
- a **credible restructuring with real cost reduction**,
- a **durable reduction in overcapacity**,
- **measurable labour-cost contributions**,
- and a settlement that restores competitive margins without prolonged industrial disruption.

We would **not** support:
- a cosmetic compromise,
- a politically convenient but financially inadequate settlement,
- or a structure that leaves the company unable to compete.

So our message is simple: **show us the economics**. If the alternative to closures truly delivers equivalent savings and improves margins durably, we can back it. If not, then the company should not pretend that preserving every legacy constraint is compatible with shareholder value.
