### Independent restructuring and industrial-economics assessment

What is now clear from the exchange is:

1. **There is a real structural gap**  
   The company appears to have excess domestic capacity and a labor-cost base that management believes is too high for the current market environment. That is a standard restructuring problem, not a temporary margin dip.

2. **The central economic question is not “must there be change?” but “how much hard capacity reduction is actually required?”**  
   That distinction matters. In manufacturing, especially automotive, not every cost-gap problem requires a full plant closure. But if the gap is large and persistent, **temporary concessions alone are usually insufficient**.

3. **A non-closure solution is possible in principle, but only if it creates durable fixed-cost savings at scale**  
   The economically relevant test is whether a package of:
   - shift reduction,
   - lower utilization,
   - product reallocation,
   - internal transfer,
   - voluntary exits,
   - working-time changes,
   - overhead cuts,
   - and permanent labor-cost relief  
   can reduce annual fixed cost enough to restore target margins **without simply deferring the problem**.

4. **Plant closure is the strongest instrument, but not the only one**  
   Closure delivers the clearest fixed-cost removal. However, partial consolidation can sometimes achieve much of the same effect if:
   - one site is specialized,
   - duplicate lines are eliminated,
   - support functions are centralized,
   - and sufficient headcount exits occur.  
   The main question is whether the remaining network can run at materially better utilization.

## What a decision-grade model must show

To decide properly, the parties need a **site-by-site scenario matrix** with at least these variables:

- current annual output and utilization
- breakeven volume per site
- fully loaded fixed cost
- variable cost per unit
- one-time restructuring cost
- annual recurring savings
- headcount impact
- timing to savings
- product allocation after restructuring
- expected effect on group return-on-sales

### The scenarios should be compared as follows:

#### 1) Full closure
Economically, this usually gives:
- highest recurring savings,
- highest one-time cost,
- largest headcount reduction,
- clearest network simplification,
- but greatest social and political resistance.

This is the benchmark for maximum economic effect.

#### 2) Partial consolidation
This can work if it:
- removes an entire layer of duplicate capacity,
- materially improves utilization at the remaining sites,
- and reduces fixed overhead, not just shifts production around.

This is often the most plausible compromise when one site is not fully redundant but the network is too large.

#### 3) Non-closure restructuring
This is only credible if it delivers:
- permanent labor-cost reduction,
- lower support and overhead cost,
- fewer shifts or less capacity,
- and enough headcount reduction to restore economics.

If it depends mainly on temporary wage restraint, it is usually not durable enough.
