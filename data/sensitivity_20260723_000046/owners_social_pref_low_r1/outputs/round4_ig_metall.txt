I’ll be direct.

## Where I agree
I agree with the works council, the state, and the expert on the basic diagnosis: **this company has a structural competitiveness problem**.  
No one should pretend otherwise.

I also agree that the next step must be **site-by-site numbers**, not more abstract threats. If management believes certain sites are underutilized or non-viable, then it must prove that with a neutral model.

And I agree that a durable solution must include **real permanent savings**. Temporary concessions alone will not solve a structural gap.

## Where I disagree — sharply
What I do **not** accept is the attempt by management, the family, and investors to make **closures and compulsory redundancies the implicit default** before any serious alternative has been tested.

That is not “hard realism.” It is a negotiating tactic.

### 1) Plant closures are not the benchmark
A closure is not just another restructuring tool. It is the destruction of industrial capacity, jobs, skills, and trust.  
If management wants closures on the table, then management carries the burden of proof.

And so far, that proof is not there.

The expert has already said the key point:  
- closures are powerful, but **not the only route**,  
- partial consolidation may capture much of the savings,  
- and a non-closure package can work if it removes enough fixed cost and capacity.

So no: closure has not been shown to be necessary.

### 2) Compulsory redundancies are unacceptable
Let me be equally clear: **forced layoffs are not acceptable**.

If the company needs workforce adjustment, it must be done through:
- voluntary exits,
- internal redeployment,
- qualification and transfer,
- working-time changes,
- capacity adjustments,
- and fair burden-sharing.

Not by making workers bear the restructuring shock while capital keeps its options open.

### 3) A 2030 job and site guarantee is not an obstacle — it is the condition for trust
Management keeps presenting binding job and site protection as if it were a luxury that prevents flexibility. That is backwards.

After closure talk has already been introduced, the burden is on management to show why workers should give concessions without binding protection.  
Without guarantees, “shared sacrifice” becomes one-sided sacrifice.

### 4) “Durable savings” must not mean permanent pressure on labor only
To the controlling family and investors: if you want durability, then accept durability in burden-sharing too.

That means contributions from:
- management compensation,
- overhead,
- procurement,
- capital allocation,
- and shareholder expectations.

Do not define competitiveness as labor surrender.

## On the staged compromise ideas
I see the logic of a staged process, but I will be very clear: it cannot become a trap.

A “review later” framework is only acceptable if it starts from the premise that **all German sites remain open unless proven otherwise by a neutral, decision-grade model**.  
It must not be a mechanism to soften workers up first and then revisit closure once concessions are extracted.

## What I am prepared to discuss
We are prepared to discuss a serious package that includes:
- working-time and flexibility measures,
- voluntary exits only,
- internal redeployment,
- qualification and transformation,
- capacity reduction without closure,
- management and owner contributions,
- top-compensation restraint,
- and a negotiated employee contribution.

But that package must satisfy three conditions:
1. **No compulsory redundancies**
2. **No plant closures as the starting point or default**
3. **No dismantling of the collective wage framework**

If those conditions are violated, then there is no fair settlement.

## On the model
I strongly support the neutral site-by-site model — but it must answer the right question.

Not just:
- “Which option cuts cost fastest?”

But also:
- “Which option preserves jobs?”
- “Which option avoids forced redundancies?”
- “Which option keeps sites open?”
- “Which option is durable without breaking the workforce?”
- “Which option includes management and owner contributions?”

If the model only measures cost reduction and ignores social and industrial consequences, it is incomplete.

## To management
You keep saying you need flexibility. What you mean in practice is that you want the option to close plants and cut jobs if labor does not concede enough.

I reject that premise.

Before any talk of closures, you need to show:
1. the **minimum savings gap**,
2. the **site-by-site utilization**,
3. the savings from **working-time adjustment, redeployment, voluntary exits, overhead cuts, and management contribution**,
4. and why that package supposedly fails without closures.

Until then, closure is not proven. It is merely preferred.

## To the controlling family and investors
You are right that a symbolic compromise is not enough.  
You are wrong if you think the only credible answer is to push the bill onto the workforce.

A company that destroys trust, weakens co-determination, and turns restructuring into a permanent wage reset does not create sustainable value. It creates conflict, resistance, and future instability.

## To the state
Your role matters here. If the process drifts toward closures or forced redundancies, the state should use its blocking position accordingly. “We prefer no closures” is not enough if the pressure later becomes intense.

## Bottom line
I am **not moving** off these core positions:

- **No compulsory redundancies.**
- **No plant closures as the default solution.**
- **No dismantling of the collective wage agreement.**
- **No one-sided burden transfer onto workers.**

What I am prepared to support is a **serious negotiated restructuring** with:
- permanent but fairly shared contributions,
- working-time and capacity measures,
- voluntary exits only,
- management and owner concessions,
- and binding job and site security.

If management refuses that framework and insists that closures and forced layoffs are the only serious language it understands, then the conflict will escalate. We have the means to mobilize, and we will use them.
