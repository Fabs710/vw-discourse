We agree with the diagnosis, but not with any answer that leaves the company structurally uncompetitive.

## Where we agree
- **With management:** the current German cost base and capacity structure are too high. A cosmetic compromise would be value-destructive.
- **With the family:** this must be hard, durable, and economically credible. A deal that merely postpones the problem is worse than no deal.
- **With the expert:** temporary concessions alone are unlikely to solve a structural gap. Some form of permanent fixed-cost reduction and capacity adjustment is probably necessary.
- **With the state and labor:** numbers matter. A site-by-site comparison is the right next step.

## Where we disagree
### With the Works Council and union
We do **not** accept the idea that:
- no plant closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- and broad guarantees through 2030

can be treated as non-negotiable if they prevent the company from restoring competitiveness.

That position may be understandable politically, but it is not acceptable economically.

A package that preserves the full footprint and most of the cost base is **not** a restructuring package. It is a delay. And delay destroys value in the share price, in margins, and in future investment capacity.

### With the state
We respect the regional responsibility, but “no closures in our state” cannot be the final answer if a site is structurally non-viable. Protecting every current site at all costs is not protection; it may simply shift losses forward.

### With management
We agree with your insistence on durable savings, but we also want the discipline of numbers before rhetoric hardens into a predetermined closure program. If a non-closure package can genuinely close the gap, we would support it. But that has to be proven, not asserted.

## Our position
We will support **whatever package credibly restores competitive margins and protects enterprise value**. That means the package must demonstrate:

1. **Durable fixed-cost reduction**
2. **Meaningful capacity reduction**
3. **A measurable labor-cost contribution**
4. **A credible timeline to savings**
5. **A clear path back to target return-on-sales**
6. **No prolonged conflict that keeps destroying value**

That leaves us open to:
- full closure,
- partial consolidation,
- or a serious non-closure restructuring.

But we will not support:
- a symbolic compromise,
- a deal that preserves the cost structure,
- or an arrangement that asks capital to absorb the cost of inaction.

## On the current impasse
The employee side says closure is not proven. Fair enough. Then prove the alternative.

What we have heard so far is a list of preferences, not yet a fully credible economic substitute. If the non-closure camp wants to avoid closures, it needs to show:
- how much savings come from working-time changes,
- how much from redeployment and voluntary exits,
- how much from overhead and management cost,
- how much from product and capacity changes,
- and why that package is durable enough to hit the margin target.

If that cannot be shown, then closure remains a legitimate restructuring tool.

We now need a **single neutral comparison matrix** for each German site showing:
- current utilization,
- breakeven volume,
- annual recurring savings,
- one-time restructuring costs,
- headcount impact,
- timing to savings,
- and expected impact on return on sales,

under:
1. full closure,
2. partial consolidation,
3. non-closure restructuring.

The model should make one point unmistakably clear:
**which scenario actually closes the cost gap permanently rather than deferring it.**

## Bottom line
We are not here to choose the most comfortable outcome.
We are here to protect risk-adjusted capital value.

So:
- we support **hard restructuring**,
- we support **permanent cost and capacity reduction**,
- we do **not** support a cosmetic compromise,
- and we remain open on the method only if it is financially credible.

If the non-closure side can prove comparable economics, we will back it. If not, then stronger measures stay on the table.
