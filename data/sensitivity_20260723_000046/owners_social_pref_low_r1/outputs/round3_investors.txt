We broadly agree with management, the family, and the expert on the **economic diagnosis**: the business has a structural competitiveness problem, and **temporary concessions or a cosmetic peace deal will not be enough**. If the company fails to remove enough durable cost and capacity, margins stay weak, the share price stays under pressure, and capital remains trapped in an underperforming asset.

That said, we are **not** signing up to closures as an article of faith, nor to a politically convenient but economically shallow compromise.

## Where we agree

### With management
- The cost base is too high.
- The current structure is not sustainable unchanged.
- Durable, not temporary, savings are needed.
- A site-by-site comparison is the right way to judge the alternatives.

### With the controlling family
- This must be a hard, economically credible settlement.
- A cosmetic deal would destroy value.
- We should compare closure, consolidation, and non-closure scenarios on the same basis.

### With the expert
- Temporary labor concessions alone are unlikely to fix a real structural gap.
- Some permanent fixed-cost reduction is likely necessary.
- Some form of capacity reduction is probably needed somewhere in the network.
- Partial consolidation may be the most plausible compromise if it really removes fixed cost and excess capacity.

## Where we disagree

### With the Works Council and union
We do **not** accept the position that:
- no plant closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- and strong long-term guarantees

can be treated as non-negotiable if they prevent the company from restoring competitiveness.

We understand the social and industrial stakes. But from a capital perspective, a package that preserves the footprint, preserves most of the cost base, and calls that restructuring is not acceptable. That is not a turnaround; it is a delay.

We also disagree with the argument that closure talk is merely leverage. If the numbers justify it, closure is not “pressure”; it is a legitimate restructuring tool. The burden is on the non-closure camp to show a package that is economically comparable, not just socially preferable.

### With the state
We respect the regional and political responsibility. But “no closures in our state” is not, by itself, a financially credible position. If a site is structurally redundant, keeping it open for political reasons destroys value and ultimately risks more jobs later.

## Our position

We will support **whatever package credibly restores competitive margins and protects enterprise value**. That means the package must satisfy all of the following:

1. **Durable fixed-cost reduction**
2. **Meaningful capacity reduction**
3. **Measurable labor-cost contribution**
4. **A credible timeline to savings**
5. **No prolonged conflict that erodes value**
6. **A clear path back to target return-on-sales**

This leaves us open to:
- full closure,
- partial consolidation,
- or a genuinely strong non-closure restructuring.

But we will not support:
- a symbolic compromise,
- a deal that leaves the cost base structurally impaired,
- or a settlement that asks capital to absorb the cost of inaction.

## On the current bargaining line
The key issue is no longer whether the company should change. It clearly must. The real question is whether the employee side can show a **durable alternative** that achieves the same economics without closures.

So far, that has not been demonstrated.

The expert’s view is helpful here: a pure no-closure, no-forced-redundancy, no-major-reset package is unlikely to be sufficient if the cost gap is as large as management says. We think that is probably right. But we are also not insisting on closures if a partial consolidation or other non-closure package truly closes the gap.

That is the standard:
- **show us the numbers,**
- **show us the durability,**
- **show us the timing,**
- **show us the impact on margins.**

## What we want next
We support the call for a **single neutral site-by-site scenario matrix**. But it has to be decision-grade.

[SUMMON: Independent site-by-site financial and capacity modeler to compare full closure, partial consolidation, and non-closure restructuring by plant, including annual recurring savings, one-time costs, timing to savings, headcount impact, breakeven volume, and expected impact on return-on-sales.]

The model should answer one question:
**Which package actually closes the cost gap and restores competitiveness without merely deferring the problem?**

## Bottom line
We are not here to choose the most socially comfortable option.
We are here to protect risk-adjusted value.

So:
- we **support hard restructuring**,
- we **support permanent cost and capacity reduction**,
- we **do not support cosmetic compromise**,
- and we **remain open on the method** only if it is financially credible.

If the non-closure side can prove comparable economics, we will back it. If not, then stronger measures must remain on the table.
