I’ll be equally direct.

## Where I agree
I agree with everyone on the core diagnosis:
- the company has a **structural competitiveness problem**,
- the current German cost base is **too high**,
- and a **cosmetic compromise** will not solve it.

I also agree that we need **site-by-site numbers**. The experts have done the right thing by forcing this discussion away from slogans and toward economics.

## Where I disagree — and sharply
What I do **not** accept is the attempt to turn “no closures” and “no compulsory redundancies” into an absolute framework **before** we know whether the economics can actually be fixed that way.

That is not responsible restructuring. That is asking management to sign up to a solution first and only then see whether the company can afford it. I will not do that.

### 1) Closures are not the goal, but they remain a real option
Let me be clear: I am not saying closures are desirable. They are a last-resort tool.

But I am also not prepared to rule them out in advance.

If a plant is structurally underutilized, has no credible future product allocation, and keeps draining fixed cost, then keeping it open for political comfort is not stewardship. It is decline management. And decline management is exactly what got us into this crisis.

The works council keeps saying closure is “destruction.” Yes — but uncompetitive overcapacity is also destruction. It destroys margin, investment capacity, and future jobs across the whole network.

### 2) Forced redundancies are not my preferred route, but I will not rule out workforce reduction
I understand why the employee side rejects compulsory redundancies. But I am not going to make an unconditional promise that the company can restructure without meaningful workforce reduction.

If the model shows that capacity and cost must come out at scale, then the question becomes how, not whether. Voluntary exits, redeployment, and internal flexibility are preferred. But if they do not close the gap, then stronger measures remain on the table.

I will not pretend otherwise.

### 3) A 2030 job guarantee that freezes the structure is not acceptable
The employee side keeps presenting site and job guarantees as a trust measure. I understand the logic. But a blanket guarantee through 2030 is not trust-building if it prevents us from adapting the network to market reality.

We cannot promise stability for a structure that may already be too large and too expensive. If we do that, we would be protecting today’s footprint at the expense of tomorrow’s business.

That is not a credible basis for investment.

### 4) “Shared sacrifice” must be real, not symbolic
The union and works council are right that this cannot be one-sided. I agree. Management, owners, and labor all have to contribute.

But I reject the suggestion that the only acceptable labor contribution is limited, reversible, and temporary. That is not enough if the cost gap is structural.

We need a durable reset:
- lower labor cost,
- less rigid capacity,
- lower overhead,
- and a structure that actually improves return on sales.

If the employee side wants to avoid closures, then it needs to show how it will deliver **permanent** savings, not just temporary relief.

## On the expert assessments
The experts have been very useful, and they have also made something clear that the employee side keeps trying to blur:

- A pure **no-closure, no-compulsory-redundancy, no-major-reset** package is unlikely to be sufficient if the cost gap is as large as we believe.
- A **partial consolidation** package may be viable — but only if it truly removes fixed cost and excess capacity.
- Temporary labor concessions alone are not enough.

That aligns with management’s core position.

So I am prepared to work with:
- partial consolidation,
- shift elimination,
- product reallocation,
- site specialization,
- voluntary exits,
- redeployment,
- and durable labor-cost contributions.

But I am **not** prepared to lock in a deal that preserves the full footprint, preserves the cost base, and calls that restructuring.

## To the Works Council
You say management has not proved that non-closure cannot work. Fair enough.

But the burden is not only on management to prove impossibility. The burden is also on you to show that your preferred alternative can actually deliver:
- comparable savings,
- durability,
- and a credible path back to target margins.

Right now, your position is still: no closures, no compulsory redundancies, keep all sites open, protect job guarantees, and rely on voluntary and reversible measures. That is not a complete restructuring model. It is a protective framework around the existing structure.

That does not meet the company’s needs.

## To the Trade Union
I appreciate your willingness to talk numbers, but I disagree with your attempt to keep the collective framework essentially untouched while insisting that management and owners carry the burden.

That is not how a real turnaround works.

If the company is structurally uncompetitive, then labor cost must be part of the adjustment in a material and durable way. Not just top-end restraint. Not just bonus moderation. Not just temporary measures that disappear before the savings matter.

If you want to avoid closures, then show a package that permanently lowers the cost base enough to hit the brand’s return-on-sales target.

## To the Regional State Government
You are right to demand quantified comparison. But I need to be equally blunt: “no closures in our state” cannot be the final answer if the numbers show the network is oversized.

Protecting the regional economy is important. But protecting it by preserving uneconomic capacity is not actually protection. It just delays the problem and makes the eventual adjustment worse.

If there is a credible non-closure package that achieves the same economics, I will support it. If there is not, then you cannot expect management to accept political vetoes that leave the company uncompetitive.

## To the Controlling Family and Investors
I agree with you more than with labor on this point: the settlement must be hard, durable, and economically credible.

But I also want to be clear that the family and investors cannot simply demand structural change and leave management to absorb the blame. This will require a visible contribution across the company, including:
- overhead,
- capital allocation discipline,
- management cost,
- and labor cost.

Still, the key question is not who feels best about the outcome. It is whether the outcome restores competitiveness.

## The real test
So here is the test I am applying:

A package is acceptable only if it delivers:
1. **durable overcapacity reduction**,
2. **meaningful and lasting labor-cost relief**,
3. **lower fixed cost**,
4. **credible margin improvement**,
5. **and a clear investment path to 2030**.

If a non-closure solution can do that, then I am open to it.

If it cannot, then we should stop pretending that closure avoidance is a solution in itself.
