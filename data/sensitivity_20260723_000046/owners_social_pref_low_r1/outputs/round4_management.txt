I’ll be equally direct.

## Where I agree

I agree with the expert on the central economic point: **this is a structural problem, not a temporary one**.  
The company has too much capacity, too much fixed cost, and a labor-cost base that is not competitive enough for the current market.

I also agree that we now need a **site-by-site model**. We need numbers, not rhetoric.

And I agree with the labor side on one thing: a cosmetic deal would be worthless. If we are going to negotiate, it must produce **durable** savings, not just delay the adjustment.

## Where I disagree — fundamentally

What I do **not** accept is the repeated attempt to preclude plant closures and compulsory workforce reduction **before** the economics are proven.

That is not a restructuring strategy. That is a demand that management accept the desired outcome first and only then ask whether the business can live with it. I will not do that.

### 1) Closures are not the goal, but they remain on the table
Let me be precise: I am not arguing for closures as a first preference.  
I am saying that **if a site is structurally underutilized, lacks credible future product allocation, and keeps tying up fixed cost, then closure cannot be ruled out in advance**.

The works council keeps framing closure as if it were irrational by definition. It is not.  
A plant that cannot be made competitive within a credible network plan is not protected industrial heritage. It is a liability.

The expert has already made clear:
- closures are the strongest instrument,
- partial consolidation may be enough in some cases,
- but a serious non-closure package must actually remove fixed cost and capacity.

That means the question is not “why would you ever close a plant?”  
The question is “can you prove a non-closure option closes the savings gap permanently?”

So far, I have not seen that proof.

### 2) Compulsory redundancies cannot be ruled out as a matter of principle
I understand the labor side’s refusal. I understand it politically and socially.

But from a management perspective, I cannot sign up to a blanket commitment that says workforce reduction is off-limits no matter what the site economics show.

If the restructuring requires headcount to come out at scale, then the discussion is about the method, not about whether adjustment happens.  
Voluntary exits, redeployment, qualification, working-time changes: all preferred.  
But if they do not get us to the required cost base, stronger measures remain necessary.

I will not pretend otherwise.

### 3) A blanket 2030 job and site guarantee is not acceptable if it freezes the network
The workforce is presenting guarantees as the trust condition. I understand why. But a blanket guarantee through 2030 is not a trust-building measure if it prevents us from adjusting the footprint to demand reality.

We cannot promise permanence for a structure that may already be too large and too expensive. That would be irresponsible to the company, the owners, and the future investment program.

### 4) “Shared burden” must be real, but it must also be meaningful
I agree that management, owners, and labor should all contribute. That is not in dispute.

But I reject any framing in which labor contribution is limited to reversible, temporary, or symbolic measures while the structural cost base stays intact.

That is not a turnaround. That is a pause.

We need:
- durable labor-cost relief,
- meaningful capacity reduction,
- lower fixed cost,
- and a credible path to the brand’s return-on-sales target.

If those savings can be achieved without closures, fine. But they must be real.

## On the expert model request

I support the call for a neutral site-by-site comparison, but I want it to answer the right question:

Not just:
- “Which option cuts cost fastest?”

But:
- “Which option closes the cost gap permanently?”
- “Which option reduces capacity enough?”
- “Which option improves utilization enough?”
- “Which option restores margin and investment capacity to 2030?”

If the model shows that non-closure restructuring can do that, I will engage with it.  
If it shows that it cannot, then we should stop pretending closures are off-limits in a serious restructuring.

## To the Works Council

You keep saying closure is “not a normal tool.” I agree it is extreme.  
But extreme is sometimes necessary when the business structure is wrong.

You also keep saying management has not proven the need for closures. Fair enough. Then prove your alternative.

Show me:
1. the minimum savings gap,
2. the site-by-site utilization,
3. the permanent savings from working-time, redeployment, voluntary exits, overhead cuts, and labor-cost contribution,
4. and why that package actually reaches target margins without closures.

If you cannot show that, then your position is not a complete restructuring answer. It is a refusal to face the scale of the problem.

## To the trade union

I welcome the fact that you are willing to discuss numbers. But I reject the idea that the collective bargaining framework can remain essentially untouched while the company somehow becomes competitive again.

If the company is structurally too expensive, then labor cost must come down in a material and durable way.  
Not just top-end restraint. Not just temporary moderation. Not just reversible concessions.

If you want to avoid closures, then I need to see a permanent and substantial contribution.

## To the state government

I respect your role and your blocking position. But “no closures in our state” cannot be the end of the discussion.

If the numbers show a site is non-viable, then preserving it for political reasons simply moves the pain forward and makes the eventual adjustment worse. That is not industrial policy. That is postponement.

I am open to a solution that preserves sites if it truly restores competitiveness. I am not open to a political freeze on a network that cannot earn its keep.

## To the controlling family and investors

I agree more with you than with labor on the core issue: **the settlement must be hard enough to change the economics**.

But I also want to be clear that the family and capital cannot simply demand a reset and leave management to absorb the fallout. The package has to be visible across the system:
- overhead,
- procurement,
- management cost,
- capital allocation,
- and labor cost.

That said, the key question remains the same: does the package restore competitiveness or not?

## My bottom line

My position is unchanged:

1. **The company needs durable fixed-cost reduction.**
2. **The company needs meaningful capacity reduction.**
3. **Labor cost must come down materially.**
4. **A non-closure solution is acceptable only if it is economically comparable and durable.**
5. **If that cannot be shown, closures and stronger workforce measures remain on the table.**

I will not sign onto a deal that preserves the footprint, preserves the cost base, and calls that restructuring.

If the workforce side wants to avoid the harshest outcome, then it needs to show a package that really closes the gap. Until then, we should stop pretending that “no closures” is a solution in itself.
