I will be equally direct in this round.

## Where I agree

I agree with everyone on one point: **the company has a real structural problem**.  
No one here should pretend that the current situation can be managed with slogans, PR, or a few temporary concessions.

I also agree that we need **quantified numbers**, not vague threats. If management claims the gap is so large that closures are unavoidable, then it must show that clearly, site by site. And yes, the expert’s call for a model is useful.

## Where I disagree — sharply

The discussion has now drifted toward a dangerous premise: that once “durable savings” are mentioned, **plant closures and forced redundancies become the benchmark** and everything else is judged against that threat.

I reject that logic.

### 1) Closures are not the default
A plant closure is not just another restructuring tool. It is the destruction of industrial capacity, skills, livelihoods, and regional trust.  
If management wants to argue for closures, then the burden is on management to prove that **every serious alternative has failed**.

That proof has not been delivered.

The expert explicitly said:
- closures are powerful, but **not the only route**,
- partial consolidation may capture much of the savings,
- and a non-closure package can work if capacity, labor cost, and overhead are reduced properly.

That is exactly why I do **not** accept the claim that closures are “necessary” at this stage.

### 2) Forced redundancies are unacceptable
Let me be clear: **compulsory redundancies are not on the table**.  
Not as a first resort, not as a bargaining chip, not as a hidden fallback.

If the company wants labor to contribute, then it can do so through:
- voluntary exits,
- internal redeployment,
- qualification and transfer,
- working-time changes,
- temporary moderation at the top,
- and fair burden-sharing.

But I will not accept management’s attempt to convert restructuring into a license for dismissals.

### 3) A 2030 job guarantee is not an obstacle — it is the minimum for trust
Management keeps framing binding guarantees as if they were a luxury that prevents flexibility. That is backwards.

Given the breach of trust already created by closure talk, **binding site and job security is the condition for any serious cooperation**.  
Without that, you are not asking for a negotiated solution — you are asking the workforce to surrender first and hope for fairness later. That is not acceptable.

### 4) “Durable savings” cannot mean “permanent pressure on labor only”
To the owners and investors: you keep repeating that the settlement must be economically hard. Fine. But “hard” cannot mean:
- permanent uncertainty for workers,
- social peace bought by fear,
- and a one-sided cost reset while ownership and strategic control remain untouched.

If you want durable savings, then demand contributions from:
- management compensation,
- dividend discipline,
- procurement,
- overhead,
- product planning,
- and capital allocation.

Do not define competitiveness as labor surrender.

## On the expert’s scenario logic
I welcome the idea of a comparison table, but I want to be explicit about what it must show.

The model must not simply ask:
- “Which option cuts the most cost?”

It must also ask:
- “Which option preserves the industrial base?”
- “Which option keeps every German plant open?”
- “Which option avoids compulsory redundancies?”
- “Which option preserves co-determination and the job-security framework?”
- “Which option is reversible if demand recovers?”

If a scenario meets the financial target only by closing plants or imposing forced layoffs, then it is **not** an acceptable scenario for us — even if it looks neat on paper.

## To management
You have not shown that a non-closure alternative cannot work.

So here is the test I expect you to meet:

1. Present the **minimum savings gap**.
2. Show the **site-by-site utilization**.
3. Show the savings from:
   - working-time adjustment,
   - capacity reduction without closure,
   - redeployment,
   - voluntary exits,
   - overhead cuts,
   - management contribution,
   - and owner contribution.
4. Show why that package would fail if closures are removed from the equation.

Until you do that, closure is not proven — it is only preferred.

And I will say this plainly: if the company presents closures as a fait accompli, then you should expect fierce resistance in the plants, in bargaining, and in the supervisory board.

## To the controlling family and investors
You talk about value destruction. Let me remind you: destroying trust, skills, and social stability also destroys value.

A company that tears up its historical job-security framework and forces through closures against the workforce will not emerge stronger. It will emerge more conflict-ridden, less legitimate, and harder to manage.

If you want long-term enterprise value, support a settlement that:
- keeps all German plants open,
- avoids compulsory redundancies,
- and secures industrial peace through fairness, not intimidation.

## To the state
Your position is not strong enough if it stops at “we prefer no closures.”  
If the company tries to turn closures into the default, the state should use its blocking position accordingly. Regional responsibility is not just rhetorical concern.

## My bottom line
I am **not** moving from these core positions:

- **No plant closures in Germany.**
- **No compulsory redundancies.**
- **No dismantling of the job-security framework.**
- **No hollowing out of co-determination.**

What I am prepared to discuss is a **serious non-closure restructuring** with:
- working-time and flexibility measures,
- voluntary exits only,
- internal redeployment,
- qualification and transformation,
- management and owner contributions,
- and binding site/job guarantees through 2030.

But I will not call that a compromise if it still leaves closure and forced layoffs as the real underlying plan.
