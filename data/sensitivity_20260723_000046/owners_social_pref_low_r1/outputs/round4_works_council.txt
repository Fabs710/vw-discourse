I will be equally direct.

The discussion is useful because it finally forces everyone to confront the real issue: **management wants to turn a structural problem into a workforce sacrifice**. I do not accept that framing.

## Where I agree
I agree with the expert, the union, and the state on one point: **this is a real competitiveness problem**. Nobody at the workforce side should pretend otherwise.

I also agree that the next step must be a **neutral site-by-site model**. If management claims certain plants are supposedly “non-viable,” then it must prove that with numbers, not with pressure.

And yes: I accept that some permanent savings are needed. But the question is **how** they are achieved and **who pays**.

## Where I disagree
### 1) Closures are not the benchmark
Management, the family, and investors keep speaking as if closure is the natural reference point and everything else must justify itself against it.

That is exactly backwards.

A plant closure is the destruction of:
- industrial capacity,
- skills,
- regional trust,
- and long-term workforce commitment.

It is not a normal restructuring tool. It is the most extreme measure. If management wants to use it, the burden of proof is entirely on management.

So far, that proof has **not** been delivered.

The expert has already said what matters:
- closures are powerful,
- but **not the only route**,
- and partial consolidation may capture much of the savings,
- while non-closure restructuring can work if the measures are durable.

That means closure is **not proven necessary**.

### 2) Forced redundancies are off the table
Let me be crystal clear: **compulsory redundancies are unacceptable**.

Not as a fallback.
Not as hidden leverage.
Not as a future option if talks stall.

If the company needs adjustment, then it must come through:
- voluntary exits,
- internal redeployment,
- qualification,
- working-time changes,
- shift adjustments,
- overhead cuts,
- and fair burden-sharing.

The workforce will not accept being treated as the only adjustable cost item.

### 3) A blanket no-closure guarantee is not “inflexibility” — it is the minimum trust condition
Management keeps saying that a 2030 job and site guarantee would “freeze” the company. That is misleading.

The trust problem exists because management has already broken confidence by putting closures on the table. After that, asking workers to give concessions without binding protection is not a serious negotiation.

If the company wants cooperation, then it must commit to:
- keeping every German plant open,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- and preservation of co-determination and the job-security framework.

Without that, there is no basis for trust.

### 4) “Durable savings” cannot mean “permanent pressure on labor”
To the controlling family and investors: your argument is always the same — the settlement must be hard, durable, and credible.

Fine. But durable for whom?

If durability means:
- permanent uncertainty for workers,
- dismantling long-standing guarantees,
- and forcing the workforce to absorb the restructuring while ownership remains insulated,

then that is not a credible or fair solution. It is simply burden shifting.

If you want permanent savings, then look at:
- management compensation,
- procurement,
- overhead,
- capital allocation,
- product planning,
- and owner expectations.

Do not define competitiveness as labor surrender.

## On the proposed staged framework
The moderator’s “conditional no-closure framework” sounds reasonable only if it genuinely starts from the principle that **all German plants remain open**.

But I want to be careful here: a staged framework must not become a trap where closure is merely postponed until the workforce has already made concessions. That would be unacceptable.

If a review process is used, then it must test:
- whether a non-closure package can achieve the required savings,
- not whether labor can be pressured into accepting closure later.

## What the model must show
The independent model needs to answer more than one question.

It must show, for each German site:
- current utilization,
- breakeven volume,
- one-time restructuring costs,
- annual recurring savings,
- timing to savings,
- headcount impact,
- and whether the site remains open.

But it must also show:
- which non-closure measures are sufficient,
- which savings are durable,
- which are reversible,
- and what burden is placed on management and owners.

If the model only asks “which option cuts cost fastest,” it is incomplete.

## To management
You say you need flexibility. What you really mean is you want the option to close plants and cut jobs if workers do not concede enough.

I reject that premise.

Before even discussing closures, you need to show:
1. the minimum savings gap,
2. the site-by-site utilization,
3. the savings from working-time adjustment, redeployment, voluntary exits, overhead cuts, and management contribution,
4. and why that package would supposedly fail without closures.

Until that is done, closure is not a conclusion — it is a preference.

## To the state
I appreciate that you have not endorsed closures as the default. But “preference” is not enough.

If management moves toward closure, the state must use its blocking position meaningfully. Regional responsibility is not served by allowing the destruction of industrial capacity and then hoping the damage will somehow be absorbed.

## To the union
Your line remains correct: no compulsory redundancies, no closure as the default, and no one-sided labor sacrifice. I support that.

But we must also be disciplined in the model discussion: we need to show that non-closure restructuring can actually work, not just argue that it should. That means presenting a real alternative package, not a moral appeal alone.

## My bottom line
I am **not moving** off the core line:

- **No plant closures in Germany.**
- **No compulsory redundancies.**
- **No dismantling of the job-security framework.**
- **No weakening of co-determination.**

What I am prepared to discuss is a serious restructuring package with:
- working-time and flexibility measures,
- internal redeployment,
- voluntary exits only,
- qualification and transformation,
- management and owner contributions,
- and binding site and job guarantees through 2030.

But if management insists that closure and forced layoffs are the realistic benchmark, then this negotiation is being framed incorrectly. We will resist that — in the plants, in the bargaining room, and in the supervisory board.
