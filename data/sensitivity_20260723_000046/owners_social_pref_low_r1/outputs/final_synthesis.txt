## 1. Decision context
The company faces a structural competitiveness problem: excess capacity, high fixed costs, and a German cost base that management and capital judge uncompetitive. The live decision is whether to pursue deep restructuring through closures and compulsory redundancies, or to reach a negotiated package that preserves sites while still delivering durable savings.

Governance makes this unusually difficult. Co-determination gives labor an effective veto on major plant decisions, while the controlling family, regional state, and investors each hold blocking or influential positions. The negotiation therefore hinges less on abstract preferences than on whether a credible economic case can be built for a non-closure solution that is acceptable to the most powerful stakeholders.

## 2. Stakeholder assembly
- **Group & Brand Management**: leads restructuring and needs a credible path to margin recovery.
- **Works Council**: plant-level employee representation with co-determination power and strong legitimacy on workforce protection.
- **Industrial Union**: bargaining counterpart that can mobilize strikes and shape labor concessions.
- **Regional State Government**: anchor shareholder and political actor with veto leverage and regional employment interests.
- **Controlling Family Holding**: dominant governance shareholder, focused on long-term value and structural competitiveness.
- **Institutional Investors**: dispersed capital, favoring durable value-preserving restructuring.
- **Independent Expert**: neutral economic assessor clarifying the decision framework, but without decision power.

Higher-standing actors for weighting purposes are management, the controlling family, the regional state, and the union/works council bloc because they combine legitimacy with blocking or execution power. Investors are important but less decisive, and the expert is advisory.

## 3. Final positions

### Group & Brand Management
Management insists the company needs durable fixed-cost and capacity reduction, and keeps closures and compulsory redundancies on the table if alternatives fail. It is open to a non-closure settlement only if the model shows comparable, permanent economics.

### Works Council
The works council rejects closures as the benchmark and insists that management must prove any plant shutdown with numbers. It opposes compulsory redundancies outright and demands binding site and job guarantees, with burden-sharing also from management and owners.

### Industrial Union
The union aligns closely with the works council on no closures as a default and no forced layoffs. It is prepared to discuss permanent labor contributions, but only within a framework of voluntary exits, redeployment, working-time changes, and shared sacrifice.

### Regional State Government
The state accepts the structural problem and the need for durable savings, but resists closures and forced redundancies as starting points. It wants site and job protection, while still being open to partial consolidation or other non-closure measures if the numbers demand adjustment.

### Controlling Family Holding
The family holding agrees that the business needs hard restructuring and that symbolic compromise would destroy value. It will not pre-commit against closures or workforce reduction, but says it is open to non-closure restructuring if it can close the gap permanently.

### Institutional Investors
Investors support whatever package restores margins and protects enterprise value, including closure, consolidation, or a serious non-closure restructuring. They reject any deal that preserves the current footprint and cost base without credible long-term savings.

### Independent Expert
The expert does not endorse any site-specific outcome because the necessary plant-level data are missing. It argues that the correct comparison is full closure, partial consolidation, and non-closure restructuring on the same metrics: utilization, breakeven volume, recurring savings, one-time costs, timing, and headcount impact.

## 4. Genuine conflicts
1. **Closure as legitimate tool vs. closure as unacceptable starting point**  
   Management, family, and investors view closure as necessary if economics require it; labor and the state treat closures as the most extreme measure and reject them as a default.

2. **Compulsory redundancies**  
   Management/capital will not rule them out in principle; labor and the state reject them outright.

3. **Binding guarantees vs. restructuring flexibility**  
   Labor and the state want binding site/job guarantees to make concessions credible; management and family say blanket guarantees could freeze the network and prevent adjustment.

4. **Burden of proof**  
   Labor and the state demand that management prove closure necessity; management and capital argue that labor must prove non-closure alternatives can achieve comparable economics.

5. **Definition of durability**  
   Labor means durable savings through shared burden and non-closure measures; capital means permanent fixed-cost and capacity reduction, potentially including closure.

## 5. Areas of convergence
- All sides agree the problem is real and structural.
- All sides agree a symbolic or temporary fix is insufficient.
- All sides accept that decisions should be based on site-by-site economics rather than slogans.
- There is shared openness to a mixed package involving redeployment, qualification, capacity adjustment, and some form of contribution from multiple stakeholders.
- There is broad support for an independent, neutral comparison matrix.

## 6. Key trade-offs for the decision-maker
- **Preserving sites and jobs now vs. achieving durable competitiveness**.
- **Social peace and trust vs. management flexibility and speed**.
- **Legitimacy of co-determination vs. ability to impose structural change**.
- **Short-term savings from labor concessions vs. long-term savings from capacity reduction**.
- **Risk of under-adjustment if closures are ruled out too early vs. risk of conflict escalation if closures are kept too active.**

The decision-maker must also weigh whether a “non-closure first” sequence is a genuine test or merely a delay tactic, since the two sides interpret it very differently.

## 7. Integrative pathways
1. **Conditional no-closure review period**  
   Keep all sites open during a defined review window, prohibit compulsory redundancies in that period, and require a neutral model to test each site against closure, consolidation, and non-closure alternatives.

2. **Tiered restructuring sequence**  
   Start with voluntary exits, redeployment, working-time and shift changes, overhead reductions, and partial consolidation; only if agreed thresholds remain unmet does a second-stage review consider stronger measures.

3. **Burden-sharing package with safeguards**  
   Pair workforce contributions with visible management/owner concessions, time-limited employment protection, and targeted investment commitments to reduce the trust deficit.

4. **Decision-grade site matrix**  
   Use a single comparison table for each plant to determine whether non-closure restructuring can actually match the economics of closure.

## 8. Open questions
- What is the minimum savings gap that must be closed?
- For each site, what are current utilization, breakeven volume, and future product allocation?
- How much of the gap can be closed through non-closure measures alone?
- Which savings are permanent and which are reversible?
- What one-time restructuring costs would each scenario require?
- What level of labor contribution is considered meaningful by management and capital?
- Under what conditions, if any, would the state or works council accept a closure recommendation?
- What threshold makes a site “structurally non-viable” in decision terms?

## 9. Recommended next step
Commission a single neutral, site-by-site industrial economics model and use it as the basis for a structured Round 5 negotiation. The model should compare full closure, partial consolidation, and non-closure restructuring for each German site using the same assumptions and should explicitly test whether durable savings can be achieved without closures or compulsory redundancies.

Before that round, each stakeholder should be required to state in advance:
- its minimum acceptable economic threshold,
- what concessions it can make,
- and what outcome would cause it to move from principle to compromise.

## 10. Emergent insights that only appeared through multi-turn interaction
- The core issue is not simply “close or not close,” but **whether a non-closure package can be made decision-grade**. This only became clear once all sides were forced to speak against the same economic template.
- Labor’s position hardened into a **trust condition**: once closures entered the discussion, guarantees became central, not peripheral.
- Management and capital moved from broad concern about competitiveness to a sharper claim that **non-closure alternatives have not yet been proven**, shifting the burden of proof.
- The expert clarified that the debate cannot be resolved without **site-specific data on utilization, breakeven, and capex**, revealing that the current dispute is partly a data problem, not only a bargaining problem.
- A meaningful compromise likely requires **sequencing**: labor wants closure excluded up front, while management wants it retained as an off-ramp. The interaction showed that a staged review with explicit thresholds is the only plausible bridge.
- The state’s role is more than symbolic: it is not merely “anti-closure,” but a potential **stabilizer of social peace** if it can connect regional legitimacy to a disciplined restructuring process.
- The most important unresolved issue is not the existence of overcapacity, but **which instrument can remove it without destroying too much trust to execute the rest of the turnaround**.
