I will be direct.

## Where I agree
I agree with management, the expert, and the investors on the core diagnosis: **this is a structural competitiveness problem**.  
The company’s German cost base and capacity are not where they need to be. If we respond with symbolism, we will destroy value over time. That would be unacceptable for the family holding.

I also agree that we need **site-by-site, decision-grade numbers**.  
Without a neutral comparison of utilization, breakeven, fixed cost, and savings, this debate becomes political theatre. That helps no one.

And I agree with labor and the state on one procedural point: a solution imposed by brute force would be unstable and therefore value-destructive. We need a settlement that can actually be executed.

## Where I disagree
### To the Works Council and union
I do **not** accept your blanket positions that:
- no plant closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- and no meaningful restructuring of the footprint

can be treated as non-negotiable from the start.

That is not a realistic governing position for a company with this cost structure.

You are asking us to rule out the strongest restructuring tools **before** the numbers are shown. I will not accept that. If a site is structurally underutilized and cannot be made viable in a credible network plan, then refusing even to consider closure is not stewardship of jobs — it is a delay of a harder correction later.

The expert has already said the key thing: **closure is not the only route, but it remains the strongest one where capacity is clearly redundant**. Partial consolidation may work in some cases, but only if it truly removes fixed cost and excess capacity. I am prepared to test that. I am not prepared to pre-commit to preserving every site.

On compulsory redundancies: I prefer voluntary exits, redeployment, and socially managed adjustments. But I will not endorse an absolute ban if the economics show that workforce reduction is unavoidable. A blanket prohibition would hand the workforce a veto over any serious reset.

### To the Regional State Government
I respect the state’s role and its concern for the region. But I do **not** accept “no closures in our state” as a governing principle.

If the model shows that a site is structurally non-viable, protecting it for political reasons simply shifts the burden to the rest of the company and weakens the entire group. That is not regional responsibility; it is postponement.

Your blocking position matters. So does using it responsibly — in a way that preserves long-term industrial value, not just the current site map.

### To management
I agree with your insistence on durability, but I also want to push back on any temptation to present closure as the default answer before the model is complete.

The expert’s assessment supports a disciplined comparison, not a predetermined outcome. So management should be specific:
- What is the minimum savings gap?
- How much comes from labor cost?
- How much from overhead?
- How much from capacity removal?
- Which sites are truly redundant?
- What savings can be locked in without a full closure?

If those questions are not answered, then closure advocacy is premature. If they are answered, then labor and the state must engage with the numbers rather than hide behind principles.

### To the union on guarantees
A blanket job and site guarantee through 2030 is not acceptable if it freezes the network before we know the necessary adjustment.  
Guarantees can be part of a settlement, but only if they do not block real restructuring.

## What would move me
What can move the family holding is not rhetoric about fairness. It is a **credible plan** that:
1. restores margins,
2. reduces fixed cost permanently,
3. removes excess capacity where necessary,
4. protects long-term enterprise value,
5. and avoids prolonged conflict.

That means I am open to:
- partial consolidation,
- specialization,
- shift reductions,
- product reallocation,
- overhead cuts,
- voluntary exits,
- redeployment,
- and a meaningful labor contribution.

I am **not** open to:
- a cosmetic compromise,
- preserving the full footprint at the expense of competitiveness,
- or a structure that makes future value destruction inevitable.

## On the expert request
I support the call for a **single neutral site-by-site comparison**. It needs to be decision-grade, not illustrative.

The model should answer one question clearly:
**Which package actually closes the cost gap permanently without simply deferring the problem?**

## Bottom line
My position remains:
- the company needs **durable fixed-cost reduction**,
- it likely needs **meaningful capacity reduction**,
- a **non-closure solution is acceptable only if it is economically comparable and enforceable**,
- and **if that cannot be shown, closures and stronger workforce measures remain on the table**.

I will not endorse a blanket no-closure commitment.  
I will not endorse a deal that preserves the current cost base and calls it restructuring.

If the workforce side wants to avoid the harshest outcome, it needs to show a package that truly restores competitiveness. Until then, I will continue to treat all serious options as open.
