The expert’s framework is useful, and I agree with the central point: **we need durable savings, not slogans**. But I want to be equally clear that this does **not** mean the company should preserve every site and every legacy protection simply because closures are unpopular.

## Where I agree
- The problem is structural, not temporary.
- The debate must be grounded in site-by-site economics.
- A cosmetic peace deal would destroy value.
- A non-closure option is worth considering **if** it can deliver comparable, durable savings.

## Where I disagree
### To the Works Council and union
Your position still reads as: **no closures, no compulsory redundancies, no material labor reset, and strong guarantees through 2030**. That is not a restructuring solution; it is a demand to keep the current structure largely intact while expecting the owners to absorb the economic hit.

I do **not** accept that as a basis for a viable settlement.

If the model shows that a site is structurally redundant, then refusing even to discuss closure is not stewardship of jobs — it is postponing a larger problem. That is harmful to the company, and therefore harmful to the workforce as well.

### To the Regional State Government
I appreciate your insistence on quantified comparison and your willingness to support a negotiated alternative. But I do **not** accept a default position of “no closures in our state” if the numbers show that some capacity must be removed to restore competitiveness.

Your role is to protect the regional economy long term, not to freeze an industrial footprint that may already be too large. If the non-closure package cannot close the gap, then you cannot ask the owners to sacrifice enterprise value just to avoid difficult decisions.

### To management
You are right on the need for structural change, but you still need to be careful not to overstate closure as the only credible route. The expert is correct that **partial consolidation** may be the most plausible compromise if it truly removes fixed cost and excess capacity.

So I want management to be precise:
- What is the minimum savings bridge?
- How much of that comes from labor cost?
- How much from overhead?
- How much from capacity removal?
- Which sites are genuinely uneconomic?

If the answer is simply “closures because they are easier,” that is not enough. If the answer is “only closures can get us there,” then show it in the model.

## My position
The family holding will support a settlement only if it is **economically hard and durable**.

That means:
1. **real and quantified savings,**
2. **permanent capacity reduction somewhere in the system,**
3. **a meaningful labor-cost contribution,**
4. **credible flexibility and redeployment,**
5. **and no deal that permanently impairs future investment capacity.**

What I will **not** support:
- a purely political compromise,
- a package that preserves all sites regardless of economics,
- or a labor-only solution that leaves the cost structure unchanged.

## On the model
The expert’s conclusion is important: a pure non-closure package is unlikely to be enough if the cost gap is as large as management says. That is exactly why I do not accept categorical rejection of closures.

I am open to **partial consolidation** if it produces real, enforceable savings. I am also open to a non-closure solution if, and only if, it proves it can achieve the same economics with contractually durable measures.

But the burden is now on labor and the state to show that their preferred route is not just socially preferable, but financially sufficient.
