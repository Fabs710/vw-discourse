Yes. Here is a **practical threshold framework** that a neutral restructuring specialist would use in this German co-determined automotive setting.

## 1) Core principle
A **non-closure restructuring is credible** only if it can deliver:

1. **material structural savings**,  
2. **real capacity reduction**, and  
3. **implementation certainty** under German co-determination and collective-bargaining constraints.

If it delivers only labor moderation without fixing footprint economics, it is not durable.

---

## 2) Decision thresholds

### A. Non-closure remains credible if:
- it can deliver roughly **70–80% of the required structural savings**
- within **12–24 months** for most of the effect
- while keeping the network operationally balanced
- and without needing unrealistic assumptions about voluntary exits, wage restraint, or demand recovery

This means the remaining gap is still manageable through ordinary efficiency work, overhead cuts, product reallocation, and normal cycle improvement.

### B. A hybrid with limited closure becomes hard to avoid if:
After serious non-closure measures, there is still:

- a **persistent 20–30% or larger residual savings gap**
- or a **material capacity overhang**
- or one or more plants remain **structurally loss-making**
- or the plan depends on assumptions that are too fragile to govern on

In plain terms: if the non-closure route does not materially change the cost base, closures or consolidation become the economically harder-to-avoid answer.

---

## 3) Site-level tests
For each plant, I would ask:

### Preserve / keep open if:
- utilization can be lifted to a credible mid-cycle level
- fixed cost per unit can become competitive
- required capex has a reasonable return
- the site has a plausible future product assignment
- product complexity and logistics do not create chronic structural disadvantage

### Consolidate or reallocate if:
- utilization is weak but not hopeless
- some volumes can be transferred elsewhere
- the site needs rationalization, shift reduction, or line simplification
- capex is needed, but only after footprint simplification

### Close or prepare for closure if:
- utilization stays structurally low even after network reallocation
- the site needs repeated capex just to remain viable
- it remains a cost outlier after labor and overhead measures
- it has no credible future product path
- the economics depend on permanent underutilization being accepted by capital

---

## 4) What counts as “equivalent structural savings”?
Real savings are:

- permanent headcount reduction via voluntary exits and attrition
- shift reductions
- line consolidation
- permanent labor-cost moderation agreed in bargaining
- overhead and management-layer cuts
- logistics and complexity reduction tied to volume concentration

Weak savings are:

- one-off bonuses waived for a single year
- temporary freezes that do not change the base
- accounting effects without cash improvement
- productivity gains that are not enforceable in operations

---

## 5) Red-flag indicators
The non-closure route becomes weak if **two or more** of these apply:

1. residual gap remains above **20–30%**
2. one or more sites remain chronically underutilized
3. the plan depends on extraordinary labor concessions
4. the company must keep spending significant capex just to preserve weak sites
5. headcount reductions cannot be forecast with confidence
6. meaningful savings arrive only after **24 months or later**
7. strike or implementation risk offsets the expected savings

---

## 6) Practical conclusion
### Non-closure is still credible when:
- it closes most of the gap
- it is implementable in 12–24 months
- and no plant remains a chronic outlier

### Hybrid with limited closure becomes necessary when:
- the residual gap stays material
- one or more sites are structurally uncompetitive
- and keeping them open would damage the whole network

---

## 7) Governance reality in Germany
Even if a purely economic answer points one way, the plan still has to be:

- negotiable under co-determination
- legally implementable
- politically survivable
- operationally stable

So the real test is not only “what is cheapest,” but:
**what can actually be implemented and sustained.**
