From the perspective of dispersed capital-market shareholders, my threshold is simple: **the company must get back to a competitive, durable margin structure.** Everything else is secondary.

So I will be direct.

## Where I agree
I agree with management, the family, and partly with the state on one central point: **the current cost base is not acceptable**. If German operations remain structurally expensive and underutilized, then shareholders will continue to absorb margin erosion, weak returns, and a discount in the valuation.

I also agree that a cosmetic settlement would be value-destructive. A deal that merely postpones the problem, preserves excess capacity, and adds another layer of governance theatre would be worse than no deal at all.

## Where I disagree
### To the Works Council and Union
I understand the social and industrial logic of protecting jobs and sites. But from our side, **“no closures, no compulsory redundancies” as an absolute line is not financially credible unless you can show equivalent savings**.

If the outcome leaves:
- the cost base still above peers,
- capacity still too high,
- and margins still structurally impaired,

then that is not a solution. That is a managed decline.

I am open to alternatives to plant closures and forced layoffs, but only if they are **quantitatively sufficient**. We need:
- real capacity reduction,
- durable labour-cost relief,
- productivity gains,
- and enforceable implementation.

A promise of shared sacrifice is not enough unless it produces comparable economics.

### To the State Government
I respect the political role, but I will not support a position that effectively blocks any hard operational change. Public-interest arguments matter only if the business remains viable. If political constraints prevent the company from restoring competitiveness, then the long-term outcome for the region and the workforce will be worse, not better.

### To the Controlling Family
I appreciate the focus on long-term enterprise value. But that also means we cannot settle for an outcome that sounds decisive while leaving the economics unchanged. If you want investor support, then the package must be more than “stability.” It must show a clear path to **improved return on sales and free cash flow**.

## What would move us
We would support a deal if it credibly delivers all of the following:

1. **Structural cost reduction**
   - not symbolic savings, but enough to restore peer-competitive margins.

2. **Durable capacity adjustment**
   - if not closures, then an alternative that achieves the same effect in economics.

3. **Avoidance of prolonged disruption**
   - we dislike drawn-out strikes because they destroy value fast.

4. **Clear governance certainty**
   - a settlement that can actually be implemented, not one that is blocked later.

5. **Investment discipline**
   - capital must be preserved for the transition, but only if the core business is fixed.

## What will not move us
We will not support:
- a cosmetic compromise,
- indefinite job guarantees with no hard restructuring,
- a settlement that preserves all sites regardless of economics,
- or a plan that leaves margins structurally impaired.

## Bottom line
We are **not** here to defend management for its own sake, and we are **not** here to defend labour peace at any price. We are here to protect capital value.

If the employee side can produce an alternative package that genuinely matches the savings of closures and layoffs, we will back it. If not, then management’s deeper restructuring case becomes more compelling, and we would not oppose it in principle.

In short: **show us the numbers and the durability, or expect us to support the stronger cost-cutting path.**
