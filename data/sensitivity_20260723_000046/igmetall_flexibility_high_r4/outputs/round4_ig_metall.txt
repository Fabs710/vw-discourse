## First: what I agree with
I agree with the works council, the state, and the expert on one central point: **this is a real restructuring problem, not a cosmetic one**. We are not defending the status quo. We accept that the company needs lower cost, lower complexity, and a durable industrial plan.

I also agree that **numbers matter**. If the company wants sacrifices from the workforce, it must show a credible restructuring logic and not just use “competitiveness” as a slogan.

And yes, I agree with management and the owners on one point as well: **the package must deliver real structural savings**. A deal that leaves the company uncompetitive will not protect jobs in the medium term.

## Where I disagree
What I do **not** accept is the attempt to keep **plant closures and forced redundancies** permanently in reserve while asking the workforce to sign up to concessions first.

That is not a neutral sequencing choice. It is a bargaining posture that keeps all the pressure on labor and none on capital.

### To management
You say closures must remain available if the non-closure route fails. But you still have not shown that the non-closure route has actually been exhausted.

You keep demanding “equivalent structural savings,” but the expert has already outlined a real menu of alternatives:
- shift reductions,
- line consolidation,
- product allocation,
- voluntary exits,
- retirement pathways,
- wage moderation,
- bonus restraint,
- overhead cuts,
- executive restraint,
- investment sequencing.

So the real question is not whether restructuring is needed. It is whether management is willing to **test the non-closure package seriously before reaching for closures**.

Right now, your position is: “Give us concessions first, and keep closure on the table if you don’t like the result.”  
That is not shared sacrifice. That is a leverage strategy.

### To the family holding and investors
You are right that value destruction through conflict is a risk. But do not confuse that with a mandate for the hardest labor cut.

You want “economic reality.” Fine. Then the economic reality is also this: **a workforce treated as disposable does not deliver stable transformation**. Strike risk, trust erosion, and implementation failure are not side issues. They are direct economic risks.

If you insist on closure as a normal fallback from day one, you will get resistance, and that will cost more than the alternatives the expert described.

### To the state government
I appreciate your support for a non-closure path. But I need to be clear: a “conditional protection” model only works if the condition is not a disguised path back to closure.

If the state wants to defend industrial continuity, it must insist that the non-closure route is the **primary restructuring path**, not a probation period for eventual sacrifice.

### To the works council
We are aligned on the core red lines, and that matters.

But I need to draw a line within the line: voluntary instruments are only acceptable if they are **used to protect sites and jobs**, not to pave the way for closures later. We cannot let “voluntary” become the softer face of the same hard-cut agenda.

So yes, I agree with your insistence on:
- no forced layoffs,
- site protection,
- co-determination,
- and binding safeguards.

But I do **not** agree with treating every capacity adjustment as illegitimate just because it reduces output. Capacity reduction can happen without plant closure. The expert said that clearly.

## On the moderator’s compromise proposals

### Proposal 1: “Non-closure first, closure as triggered fallback”
I can only support this **if the trigger is truly exceptional and narrowly defined**, not a backdoor threat.

The union’s concern is legitimate: a fake protection phase is unacceptable. So if this route is to be discussed, the fallback cannot be a vague management discretion clause. It would need:
- transparent metrics,
- independent verification,
- joint monitoring,
- and a genuinely high threshold before any closure discussion reopens.

That said, I want to be blunt: if the fallback is written so loosely that it can be invoked whenever management feels uncomfortable, then it is not a protection phase. It is a postponement of the same conflict.

### Proposal 2: “Conditional protection in exchange for measurable labor contribution”
This is the only serious bargaining basis on the table.

We are open to:
- temporary wage moderation,
- reduced special payments,
- working-time flexibility,
- voluntary exit programs,
- redeployment,
- productivity improvements.

But only if these are matched by:
- **binding no-closure commitments for the agreement term**,
- **no compulsory redundancies**,
- **management and ownership contributions**,
- and a real site-by-site implementation plan.

No guarantees, no labor contribution. That is still our position.

### Proposal 3: “Hybrid package with protected core sites”
As a starting point, I reject this.

Why? Because it normalizes the idea that some sites are “protected” and others can be sacrificed. That is not a non-closure strategy. It is a managed shrinkage strategy with a nicer label.

If management wants to argue that a hybrid is unavoidable, then it must first prove why a serious non-closure package fails. That proof has still not been delivered.

## What I want to see next
I support the call for a **site-by-site quantitative model**, but I want it used correctly.

It must show:
- utilization,
- fixed costs,
- labor cost per unit,
- capex needs,
- headcount impact,
- and 3–5 year savings,

**for the non-closure route first**, not just as a menu of closure justifications.

And I want the model to include:
- management overhead reduction,
- executive restraint,
- investment sequencing,
- and the effect of voluntary labor measures.

If those numbers show a residual gap, then we talk about additional burden-sharing. We do **not** start from plant closure.

## My line on the red lines
Let me repeat the union’s position clearly:

- **No German plant closures.**
- **No compulsory redundancies.**
- **No erosion of the collective wage framework.**
- **No open-ended insecurity.**
- **No one-sided burden transfer onto workers.**

That is not a rhetorical posture. That is the minimum for any durable settlement.

## What flexibility exists
We remain prepared to discuss a **temporary, negotiated employee contribution**, including:
- wage restraint,
- bonus moderation,
- working-time flexibility,
- and voluntary workforce reduction,

but only if it is matched by:
- binding job and site guarantees,
- genuine management and ownership contributions,
- and a restructuring design that preserves all German plants.

## Bottom line
We are not refusing restructuring. We are refusing a restructuring logic that makes the workforce absorb the crisis while plant closures stay on the shelf as a threat.

If management wants a deal, it must stop treating closures as the default benchmark and bring a real non-closure plan with enforceable guarantees.

If it does that, we will negotiate seriously.  
If it does not, then there is no basis for expecting labor peace.
