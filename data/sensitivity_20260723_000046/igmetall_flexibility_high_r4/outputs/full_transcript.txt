[Works Council (Betriebsrat) (Plant-level employee representation under co-determination) — Round 1]

- keep every German plant open,
- protect every employee from forced layoffs,

- no closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- no unilateral downgrading of labour conditions,
- clear product and investment commitments for German sites,
- and a genuine role for employee representatives in shaping the future plan.

Without that, there is no basis for agreement. We are prepared to resist decisively, including through industrial action and by using every rights-based lever in co-determination.

   - temporary wage restraint, bonus waiver, or other negotiated measures,

## 1) The savings gap: what is likely required

Without the company’s internal data I cannot give a precise euro figure, but in a German mass-market OEM with the described symptoms, the turnaround typically needs a **material, permanent cost reset** in the order of:

- **low-to-mid single-digit percentage points of vehicle cost** on the German footprint, and
- a **capacity reduction large enough to remove fixed-cost absorption losses**, not just trim overtime or overhead.

In practical terms, if the German passenger-car footprint is carrying chronic underutilization, the real savings requirement is usually not “a few hundred million euros” of discretionary cuts. It is more often a **multi-year structural package** combining:

- workforce reduction,
- plant/output rationalization,
- supplier and logistics simplification,
- administrative overhead cuts,
- and operating-rule changes.

If management says the issue is “structural overcapacity,” then the savings gap is likely **too large to close by voluntary labour measures alone** unless those measures are unusually deep and the market recovers.

## 2) Site-by-site competitiveness: how this should be judged

The key metric is not whether a site is “expensive” in isolation, but whether it remains viable after adjusting for:

- model volume,
- product mix,
- automation level,
- logistics distance to suppliers and customers,
- energy and utility costs,
- labor productivity,
- capex required for the next platform cycle,
- and the strategic role of the site in the network.

A site is usually at risk if it has several of these characteristics at once:

- low or declining utilization,
- legacy product allocation with no successor model,
- high fixed labor content,
- significant indirect labor,
- weak transport economics,
- and high reinvestment need to stay current for EV production.

In German automotive restructurings, the sites that can usually be preserved are the ones that have:
- a **clear future product**,
- **high relevance in the platform network**,
- and a **credible path to productivity improvement**.

If a plant lacks a next-generation product mandate, then “no closure” quickly becomes a promise to preserve excess fixed cost indefinitely.

## 3) Can equivalent savings be achieved without closures?

### Short answer
**Sometimes, but not always.**  
In this scenario, I would treat it as **possible only if the footprint can be reduced enough through internal measures to match the fixed-cost effect of a closure**. That is a high bar.

### What can substitute for closure
The main non-closure levers are:

- **shift reductions / line balancing**
- **internal product reallocation**
- **voluntary exits and early retirement**
- **natural attrition**
- **temporary wage restraint / bonus moderation**
- **working-time flexibility**
- **redeployment across plants**
- **longer amortization periods / slower investment sequencing**
- **management overhead and SG&A cuts**

### Their limitation
These measures can produce meaningful savings, but they are usually **slower, more complex, and less certain** than a closure. More importantly:

- they often reduce **variable labour cost** more than **fixed site cost**,
- they do not fully remove duplicated infrastructure,
- and they may preserve too much capacity if the core problem is overproduction.

So if management needs a **durable fixed-cost reduction**, then purely “soft” measures often underdeliver unless paired with:
- a very large voluntary headcount reduction,
- major output reassignment,
- and real reduction in shifts or lines.

## 4) Forced redundancies: are they necessary?

Not necessarily as the first instrument, but they may become necessary if:
- voluntary exit numbers fall short,
- redeployment capacity is exhausted,
- and the required savings target is not met.

In German companies with strong co-determination, a no-compulsory-redundancy deal is feasible **if** the restructuring target is modest enough or the company can absorb the cost of a long transition. But if the company truly needs a sharp step-down in headcount, then refusing compulsory redundancies while also refusing closures creates a mathematical problem: **where does the fixed cost come out?**

So the answer is:

- **Operationally possible?** Yes, sometimes.
- **Guaranteed?** No.
- **Likely sufficient for a major overcapacity problem?** Often not.

## 5) The realistic option set

### Option A: Deep restructuring with one or more closures
**Most effective economically.**  
This gives the cleanest fixed-cost reset and is the strongest answer if one or more plants are structurally uncompetitive.

Typical features:
- closure or sale/repurposing of one site,
- headcount reduction through redundancy mix,
- consolidation of production,
- stronger near-term savings,
- highest labour conflict risk.

### Option B: No closures, but very deep internal rationalization
Possible only if the company can truly remove capacity without shutting a site. This usually requires:
- major volume transfer,
- one or more line shutdowns,
- shift eliminations,
- generous voluntary exit programs,
- and a long implementation horizon.

This is often **nearly as painful as closure**, just distributed differently. It can work, but it requires exceptional bargaining discipline and a willingness by capital to accept slower savings realization.

### Option C: Hybrid package
This is usually the most realistic in German governance settings.

It would combine:
- one limited closure or footprint reduction at the weakest node,
- guarantees for the remaining core sites,
- voluntary workforce reduction elsewhere,
- labour-cost concessions,
- and investment commitments for the retained plants.

From a restructuring standpoint, this is often the **highest-probability compromise** because it creates enough savings while preserving political legitimacy.

## 2) What “non-closure restructuring” must actually mean

If the workforce side wants a credible alternative to closure, the package must do more than promise goodwill. It should be built around five pillars:

### A. Capacity reduction without closure
Examples:
- removing one shift at selected plants
- consolidating overlapping product lines
- reducing variant complexity
- concentrating component production
- lowering annual output targets at weaker sites

This can reduce fixed-cost pressure without shuttering the plant.

### B. Workforce reduction mainly through voluntary means
Examples:
- natural attrition
- partial retirement
- early-retirement windows
- severance-based voluntary exit programs
- internal transfer to growth areas
- temporary agency labor reduction

This is slower than a forced reduction, but in Germany it is often the only politically and industrially workable route.

### C. Labor-cost contribution
Examples:
- temporary wage restraint
- deferred tariff increases
- shorter working time with partial compensation adjustment
- reduction of special payments for a limited period
- freezing certain allowances
- conversion of some cash pay into one-time retention or transformation payments

This must be time-limited and visibly shared across stakeholder groups if it is to be accepted.

### D. Investment and product commitment
If workers accept restraint, they will reasonably expect:
- allocation of future products
- capex commitments
- battery/e-mobility or platform work for selected sites
- qualification funding
- minimum utilization commitments where feasible

Without product allocation, a “no closure” promise is usually just a delay.

### E. Binding safeguards
A deal needs enforceability:
- site protection agreement
- employment protection agreement
- defined trigger mechanisms
- review clauses
- arbitration or mediation mechanism
- transparency on KPI milestones

## 3) What a realistic package could look like

A credible package usually has **three layers**:

### Layer 1: Immediate savings within 12 months
- management overhead reduction
- capex deferral on non-critical projects
- reduction in external consultants
- hiring freeze in non-core functions
- shift reduction at weak sites
- temporary labor-cost moderation

### Layer 2: Structural savings over 2–3 years
- consolidation of overlapping production
- internal redeployment
- qualification and re-skilling
- supplier and logistics simplification
- headcount reduction through voluntary exits
- footprint rationalization inside the existing site set

### Layer 3: Long-term competitiveness measures over 3–5 years
- product allocation to efficient sites
- automation and process redesign
- platform standardization
- battery/e-drive integration where economic
- labor agreement redesign for new product cycles

## 4) What counts as “equivalent savings” in practical terms

If closures are off the table, the company will usually need a combination of:

- **lower fixed personnel cost**
- **lower effective hourly cost**
- **higher utilization**
- **lower overhead**
- **lower complexity**
- **lower investment intensity in weak areas**

In plain terms: if you do not close a plant, you must still remove the cost that plant represents through other means.

A useful restructuring test is:

> **Does the non-closure package reduce the company’s annual cost base and structural capacity by enough to restore target margins within the planned horizon?**

If the answer is no, then the proposal is not an equivalent alternative; it is a delay mechanism.

## 5) Why compulsory redundancies are not the only way to reduce headcount

In Germany, large industrial restructurings often avoid compulsory dismissals by using a layered exit architecture:

- natural attrition
- retirement windows
- partial retirement
- voluntary severance
- transfer to sister units
- limited-time work-hour reductions
- internal labor pools

That can achieve substantial reductions, but it requires:
- time
- money
- trust
- and usually a binding “no forced redundancies” promise for a defined period

So the works council’s position is not economically impossible.  
But it is only workable if there is a **credible voluntary reduction budget** and if the company is allowed to reconfigure operations.

## 6) What the employee side should accept if it wants to avoid closures

If the objective is to keep plants open, the workforce side generally has to accept some combination of the following:

- **temporary wage moderation**
- **reduced special payments**
- **working-time flexibility**
- **less rigid location protection**
- **voluntary exit programs**
- **redeployment obligations**
- **productivity improvements**
- **temporary suspension of some legacy guarantees**

A strict “all plants stay open, no forced redundancies, no meaningful labor contribution” position is not a restructuring offer. It is a blocking position.

## 7) What management should avoid if it wants a deal

Management should avoid presenting closure as the first and only credible lever. That usually destroys trust and makes even a good compromise impossible.

Better practice in German industrial restructuring is:

1. show the gap clearly,
2. quantify the non-closure options,
3. specify the residual gap,
4. explain what additional burden-sharing is needed,
5. then reserve closure as a last-resort fallback, not the opening move.

If management cannot quantify the residual gap after non-closure measures, it will not be credible in a co-determined environment.

## 8) Site-level safeguards that are credible

A robust non-closure deal should include site-specific protections such as:

- minimum product allocation by site
- agreed shift volumes or capacity bands
- capex commitments by site
- employment corridors, not absolute employment certainty
- redeployment priority rules
- training guarantees
- annual review with joint monitoring
- trigger-based renegotiation if market conditions worsen materially

A pure blanket “no closure until 2030” guarantee is often too rigid unless the company is well-capitalized and the business case is very strong.  
A more workable approach is:

- **no closure for a defined period**
- **subject to agreed productivity and cost milestones**
- **with a joint review mechanism**
- **and pre-agreed fallback measures if targets are missed**

That is more durable than a slogan and less likely to collapse later.

## 9) The most realistic compromise architecture

If I were designing a neutral restructuring template, I would propose:

- no compulsory redundancies during the agreement term
- voluntary headcount reduction target
- temporary labor-cost contribution
- shift/capacity consolidation at weaker sites
- product and investment commitments for selected sites
- management overhead cuts
- defined governance and review process
- sunset clause after 3–5 years

**Tradeoff**
- Lower short-term conflict, higher trust, but slower savings and more implementation risk.

This is feasible **if** the savings target is realistic and the company is willing to make reciprocal commitments.

## 1) What is economically credible?

### A. Status quo
Not credible.

If European demand is weak, Chinese competition is intense, and German cost levels are above target, then doing little or nothing will usually leave:
- underutilized plants,
- excessive fixed overhead,
- too-high labor cost per vehicle,
- weak returns on sales,
- and growing pressure for more radical action later.

### B. Pure non-closure restructuring
**Credible only if the savings gap is modest to medium and if the company can extract enough structural relief from within the existing footprint.**

Typical mechanisms:
- one or more shift eliminations,
- consolidation of overlapping production,
- voluntary headcount reduction,
- wage moderation or deferred pay increases,
- bonus restraint,
- overhead cuts,
- capex deferral or reallocation,
- internal redeployment,
- productivity gains and line simplification.

This can work if the company can remove enough cost in a 2–3 year horizon. It is most plausible where:
- the weakest sites are not catastrophically underutilized,
- there is room to raise utilization through product allocation,
- and the workforce reduction can be largely voluntary.

### C. Hybrid restructuring with limited closure
This is, in many German automotive cases, the **most plausible economic outcome** if the savings requirement is large.

Why:
- if one or more sites are structurally too small, too specialized, or too underutilized, keeping all sites open may require excessive ongoing subsidy;
- a limited closure can remove a large block of fixed cost quickly;
- it can also simplify the network and make the remaining sites investable.

But this only works if:
- the closure target is genuinely the weakest node,
- the remaining footprint gets enough future product volume,
- and the labor/political conflict does not destroy the value gained from the savings.

## 2) What savings levers are realistically available?

Below is the practical menu, with rough industrial logic rather than company-specific figures.

### Labor-cost levers
These are often the fastest to negotiate, but not always enough on their own.

- temporary wage restraint
- delayed tariff step-ups
- reduced special payments
- bonus moderation
- lower allowance levels
- shorter working time with partial compensation changes

**Typical effect:** meaningful but temporary.  
**Risk:** if only temporary, it may defer rather than solve the structural issue.

### Workforce levers
These are important because headcount is usually the largest operating cost variable.

- natural attrition
- retirement windows / partial retirement
- voluntary severance
- internal transfer to growth areas
- limited hiring freezes
- agency labor reduction

**Typical effect:** real but slower.  
**Risk:** voluntary programs can be too slow or too expensive if the company needs rapid reduction.

### Capacity / footprint levers
These are the highest-impact structural measures.

- closing a shift
- consolidating lines
- transferring a product family
- reducing output at weak plants
- closing one structurally weak plant
- moving component production or logistics to fewer nodes

**Typical effect:** strongest and most durable fixed-cost reduction.  
**Risk:** politically hardest, but economically most decisive.

### Management / overhead levers
These matter, but they rarely solve a major manufacturing overcapacity issue alone.

- executive pay restraint
- management headcount reduction
- consultant and external service cuts
- non-essential travel and overhead reduction
- capex prioritization

**Typical effect:** useful signal and some cash savings, but not sufficient alone.

### Investment sequencing
This can support a restructuring but cannot replace it.

- defer low-return projects
- concentrate capex on future-product sites
- delay non-critical tooling
- sequence EV and platform investment more tightly

**Typical effect:** supports liquidity and focus.  
**Risk:** underinvestment can create future weakness if overused.

2. **No compulsory redundancies during the agreement term**
3. **Shift reductions and line consolidation at weaker sites**
4. **A voluntary workforce reduction target**
5. **Temporary labor-cost moderation**
6. **Management overhead reduction**
7. **Product and investment commitments for remaining sites**
8. **Joint monitoring and trigger clauses**
9. **A hard sunset date, typically 3–5 years**

### Key constraint
A non-closure deal is only credible if the company can show that it still reaches:
- lower annual fixed costs,
- lower labor cost per vehicle,
- improved utilization,
- and acceptable margin recovery within the planned horizon.

If those metrics do not improve enough, the deal is not structurally equivalent.

## 4) Why “no compulsory redundancies” is not the same as “no headcount reduction”

This point matters in Germany.

A restructuring can eliminate a large number of jobs without forced dismissals if it uses:
- voluntary severance,
- retirement pathways,
- attrition,
- redeployment,
- internal transfer,
- hiring freezes.

So from a restructuring perspective, the issue is not whether headcount falls. It is **how** it falls and how quickly.

That means the works council’s position is not automatically incompatible with restructuring. But it does require:
- enough time,
- enough money,
- and a credible voluntary exit budget.

If the company really needs a major fixed-cost reset, wage restraint and voluntary exits may not be enough.

### 2. Some sites may be too weak

With co-determination, family control, and state blocking rights, management may not be able to implement a clean “market” solution. That often pushes parties toward a compromise package that is economically less than ideal but politically executable.

So the most realistic compromise is often:
- a **non-closure-heavy package first**, plus
- one or more **limited closures or partial site rationalizations** if the numbers still do not close.

- permanent headcount reduction via voluntary exits and attrition
- shift reductions and line consolidation
- permanent reduction in allowances or special payments where legally and collectively agreed
- overhead and management-layer cuts
- footprint simplification
- procurement and logistics simplification tied to volume concentration

- temporary pay freezes with no lasting change
- deferrals of cost into future years
- one-off bonuses sacrificed in a single year
- accounting savings not matched by actual cash improvement

### Close or prepare for closure if:
- utilization stays structurally low even after network reallocation
- the site needs repeated capex just to remain viable
- it remains a cost outlier after labor and overhead measures
- it has no credible future product path
- the economics depend on permanent underutilization being accepted by capital

---

## 4) What counts as “equivalent structural savings”?
Real savings are:

- permanent headcount reduction via voluntary exits and attrition
- shift reductions
- line consolidation
- permanent labor-cost moderation agreed in bargaining
- overhead and management-layer cuts
- logistics and complexity reduction tied to volume concentration

Weak savings are:

- one-off bonuses waived for a single year
- temporary freezes that do not change the base
- accounting effects without cash improvement
- productivity gains that are not enforceable in operations

---

## 5) Red-flag indicators
The non-closure route becomes weak if **two or more** of these apply:

1. residual gap remains above **20–30%**
2. one or more sites remain chronically underutilized
3. the plan depends on extraordinary labor concessions
4. the company must keep spending significant capex just to preserve weak sites
5. headcount reductions cannot be forecast with confidence
6. meaningful savings arrive only after **24 months or later**
7. strike or implementation risk offsets the expected savings

---

## 6) Practical conclusion
### Non-closure is still credible when:
- it closes most of the gap
- it is implementable in 12–24 months
- and no plant remains a chronic outlier

### Hybrid with limited closure becomes necessary when:
- the residual gap stays material
- one or more sites are structurally uncompetitive
- and keeping them open would damage the whole network

---

## 7) Governance reality in Germany
Even if a purely economic answer points one way, the plan still has to be:

- negotiable under co-determination
- legally implementable
- politically survivable
- operationally stable

So the real test is not only “what is cheapest,” but:
**what can actually be implemented and sustained.**
