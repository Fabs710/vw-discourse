Yes. Based on the situation described, I can define **practical decision thresholds** for when a non-closure restructuring remains credible versus when the residual gap becomes too large and a limited-closure hybrid becomes economically hard to avoid.

I’ll stay within restructuring and industrial-relations realities, and I’ll distinguish **economically credible**, **negotiably credible**, and **not credible**.

## 1) The right test is not “closure or no closure”
The correct question is:

**Can the non-closure package close enough of the cost, capacity, and cash gap within a realistic implementation window, with acceptable execution risk?**

A non-closure path is credible only if it can deliver all three:

1. **Structural savings**, not one-off optics  
2. **Capacity reduction**, not just wage moderation  
3. **Execution certainty**, meaning the company can actually implement it under German labor and co-determination constraints

If one of these is missing, the package may be politically attractive but commercially weak.

---

## 2) Practical threshold framework

### A. Non-closure restructuring is still credible if:
A non-closure package can plausibly deliver:

- **at least ~70–80% of the required structural savings**
- **within 12–24 months for the bulk of the effect**
- while keeping the **industrial footprint operationally balanced**
- and without relying on assumptions that require perfect labor peace, extraordinary demand recovery, or unrealistic productivity jumps

In plain terms:  
If the gap left after labor, shift, product, and overhead measures is **small enough to be bridged by normal further efficiency work, demand recovery, or incremental management action**, then non-closure remains viable.

### B. A hybrid with limited closure becomes economically hard to avoid if:
After credible non-closure measures, there is still:

- a **persistent 20–30%+ residual savings gap**
- or a **material capacity overhang** that cannot be absorbed by working-time changes, product relocation, and voluntary reductions
- or **one or more plants remain structurally loss-making** even after all plausible non-closure levers are applied
- or the plan depends on assumptions that are too fragile, such as:
  - unusually high voluntary exit volumes,
  - sustained wage restraint far beyond contract norms,
  - permanent underutilization accepted by capital,
  - or management overhead cuts that are clearly insufficient to change the economics

At that point, closures are not “preferred”; they become a **structural correction**.

---

## 3) Site-level decision thresholds
For each plant, I would use the following rule-of-thumb tests.

### A plant is a candidate for preservation if:
- utilization can be lifted to a **credible mid-cycle level**
- fixed cost per unit can be brought near peer-competitive levels
- required capex is **justified by the future product pipeline**
- and the plant can be kept busy through **product allocation, shift rationalization, or internal transfer of volumes**

### A plant becomes a serious closure or consolidation candidate if:
- utilization remains structurally low even after network reallocation
- it needs repeated capex just to remain runnable
- logistics or product complexity make it a chronic cost outlier
- or it cannot reach competitive unit cost even after labor moderation and overhead allocation

In German automotive restructuring, the classic warning signs are:

- **chronic underutilization**
- **high fixed-cost burden**
- **poor model fit for future EV/product architecture**
- **large required capex with weak return**
- **repeatedly high unit labor cost**
- **limited alternative product assignment**

If several of those are present at the same site, non-closure becomes harder to defend economically.

---

## 4) What counts as “equivalent structural savings”?
This is often the decisive issue.

A non-closure plan is economically meaningful only if savings are:

- **permanent or long-dated**
- **large enough to change the cost base**
- **not dependent on temporary accounting effects**
- **not offset by hidden implementation costs**
- and **not erased by later rework, overtime, complexity, or logistics losses**

Examples of real structural savings:
- permanent headcount reduction via voluntary exits and attrition
- shift reductions and line consolidation
- permanent reduction in allowances or special payments where legally and collectively agreed
- overhead and management-layer cuts
- footprint simplification
- procurement and logistics simplification tied to volume concentration

Examples of weak or non-structural savings:
- temporary pay freezes with no lasting change
- deferrals of cost into future years
- one-off bonuses sacrificed in a single year
- accounting savings not matched by actual cash improvement
- productivity gains assumed without enforceable operating changes

A package can be “large” but still not “structural enough.”

---

## 5) My practical red-line indicators
I would treat the non-closure path as no longer credible if **two or more** of the following are true:

1. **Residual gap remains above ~20–30%** of required savings after all realistic non-closure levers  
2. **At least one plant remains materially underutilized** with no credible product or volume remedy  
3. **Implementation depends on extraordinary labor concessions** that are unlikely to be repeated or sustained  
4. **The plan requires continued high capex** just to keep weak sites open  
5. **The company cannot forecast headcount reductions with reasonable confidence** without closures  
6. **The restructuring timeline stretches beyond 24 months** before meaningful savings arrive  
7. **Industrial conflict risk becomes so high** that savings are offset by disruption, delayed launches, or supplier damage

If those indicators stack up, the non-closure route is likely too weak.

---

## 6) What would make a limited-closure hybrid acceptable?
A hybrid is economically defensible when:

- most of the footprint can be saved through non-closure measures
- but **one or a small number of sites** are clearly structurally disadvantaged
- and keeping them open would force the rest of the network to absorb an excessive burden

In that case, the least damaging option is often:

- preserve the core sites
- consolidate product lines
- move volumes out of the weakest plants
- and, if no viable alternative remains, close only the smallest number of sites necessary

That is a classic restructuring logic: **protect the network, not every node**.

---

## 7) German governance reality matters
In Germany, a technically sound restructuring is not enough. It must also be:

- **negotiable under co-determination**
- **legally implementable**
- **politically survivable**
- and **operationally stable**

So a plan that is economically neat but cannot win the necessary board, works council, and union alignment is not a real plan.

That means management should not ask only:
> “What is the cheapest theoretical solution?”

It should ask:
> “What solution can actually be implemented, sustained, and financed through the restructuring period?”

That often pushes companies toward a **hybrid compromise** rather than the purest economic answer.

---

## 8) My concise threshold conclusion
### Non-closure remains credible when:
- it covers **most of the structural gap**,
- it can be implemented within **12–24 months**,
- and no site remains a chronic outlier after reallocation and consolidation.

### A hybrid with limited closure becomes unavoidable when:
- a **material residual gap** remains after realistic non-closure levers,
- one or more sites are structurally uncompetitive,
- and keeping them open would jeopardize the viability of the broader German footprint.
