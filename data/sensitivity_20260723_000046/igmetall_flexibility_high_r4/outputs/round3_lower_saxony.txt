I will be direct.

## My position
As the regional state and anchor shareholder, I support a **real restructuring**. I do **not** support a restructuring that is simply a pretext for plant closures, forced redundancies, and the weakening of the state’s co-determination rights.

The company’s cost problem is real. The expert’s assessment confirms that. But the conclusion is **not** that closures are automatically the only credible path. It is that the burden of proof is now on management and capital to show, **site by site**, why less destructive options are insufficient.

## Where I agree

### With management
You are right that:
- the cost base is too high,
- overcapacity is a real issue,
- cosmetic fixes will fail,
- and the company needs durable savings.

I agree with all of that.

### With the expert
The expert has helpfully clarified that:
- a non-closure restructuring can be credible,
- but only if it delivers real structural savings,
- and only if it includes capacity reduction, voluntary workforce reduction, labor-cost moderation, and binding investment commitments.

That is the right framework.

### With the works council and union
You are right that:
- workers should not bear the burden alone,
- management and ownership must contribute,
- and a blunt closure-first approach would damage trust and the regional industrial base.

I support that.

## Where I disagree

### To management
I do **not** accept your framing that closures must remain a normal baseline option from the start.

A plant closure in this state is not an abstract efficiency lever. It has consequences for:
- regional employment,
- suppliers,
- municipalities,
- training,
- and the company’s social license.

So if you want me to even consider hard measures, you must first prove that:
- alternatives have been exhausted,
- non-closure capacity reduction is insufficient,
- and the same savings cannot be achieved through internal reorganization, working-time changes, redeployment, product shifts, and voluntary measures.

Right now, that proof is not there.

### To the family owners and investors
You are right that the package must be economically real. But you are **not** entitled to define “real” as “whatever produces the biggest labor cut fastest.”

A restructuring that guts German sites and then calls that “equivalent savings” is not balanced. It may be clean on a spreadsheet, but it is politically and industrially reckless.

### To the works council and union
I also need to be equally clear with you: a blanket “all plants stay open, no compulsory redundancies” line is only sustainable if it is matched by a **credible, quantified non-closure plan**.

Saying “no” to closures is not enough by itself. If the company needs material capacity reduction, then the question is how to achieve it without destroying the industrial base. That means you must engage on:
- shift reductions,
- product reallocations,
- voluntary exit volumes,
- working-time changes,
- productivity measures,
- and internal redeployment.

A pure blocking position is not enough to save the company.

## What I can support
I can support a **non-closure restructuring package** if it contains all of the following:

1. **A site-by-site quantified model**
   - utilization,
   - fixed costs,
   - labor cost per unit,
   - capex needs,
   - headcount impact,
   - and savings over 3–5 years.

2. **Capacity reduction without plant closures as the first option**
   - shift cuts,
   - line rationalization,
   - product consolidation,
   - internal footprint reduction,
   - and lower output at weak sites.

3. **Workforce reduction mainly through voluntary instruments**
   - natural attrition,
   - retirement pathways,
   - voluntary severance,
   - redeployment,
   - qualification.

4. **A fair labor contribution**
   - temporary wage restraint,
   - bonus moderation,
   - working-time flexibility,
   - productivity improvements.

5. **Meaningful management and ownership contribution**
   - overhead cuts,
   - executive restraint,
   - investment discipline.

6. **Binding job, site, and investment safeguards**
   - no closures as long as viable alternatives exist,
   - protection of the state’s veto and co-determination role,
   - clear commitments to German production and future products.

## What I cannot accept
I cannot accept:
- plant closures as the default answer,
- compulsory redundancies as the presumed solution,
- a deal that damages the regional economic anchor role of this company,
- or any attempt to sideline the state’s rights under the special statute.

Those are not bargaining preferences. They are core interests.

## My answer to the expert’s “hybrid” point
A hybrid with limited closure may be the most probable compromise in abstract economic terms. But probability is not legitimacy.

If a hybrid is to be discussed at all, it must only come **after** a serious non-closure model has been tested and shown to fail on the numbers. I will not start from closure and work backward.

## What should happen next
I support the proposed **site-by-site quantitative model** comparing:
- status quo,
- non-closure restructuring,
- and hybrid restructuring.

But I want that model to include not only economics, but also:
- regional employment impact,
- supplier impact,
- investment commitments,
- and the effect on co-determination and governance stability.

## Final word
I am open to a durable deal. I am not open to a settlement that treats the state, the workforce, and the regional economy as disposable.

If management can show a credible non-closure path with real savings, I will help broker it.
If not, then it must explain why it is choosing a route that destroys jobs and weakens the company’s long-term legitimacy.
