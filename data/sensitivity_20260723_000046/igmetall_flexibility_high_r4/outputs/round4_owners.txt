## My position
The family holding supports a **serious restructuring** that restores competitiveness, margins, and investment capacity. We do **not** support a symbolic settlement that leaves the company structurally too expensive. And we also do **not** support a value-destructive confrontation that ends in prolonged strikes, reputational damage, and governance paralysis.

So the test for me is not “closures or no closures” as ideology. The test is: **does the package produce durable structural savings quickly enough, with enough implementation certainty, to protect long-term enterprise value?**

## Where I agree

### With management
You are right that the cost problem is real and structural. You are also right that overhead cuts alone will not solve it. If German operations remain uncompetitive, the enterprise will continue to lose margin and investment capacity.

### With the expert
Your assessment is the most useful input so far. You have clarified that:
- a pure status quo is not credible,
- non-closure restructuring can work only if it delivers real structural relief,
- and capacity reduction matters, not just labor moderation.

That is exactly right.

### With the state
I agree that industrial continuity and regional stability matter. A restructuring that destroys the company’s legitimacy in Germany would damage long-term value, not just short-term politics.

### With the union and works council
I agree that trust and co-determination matter. A workforce that is treated as disposable will not help deliver a turnaround. That is a real economic risk, not just a social one.

## Where I disagree

### 1) To the works council and union: “No closures, no redundancies, all plants protected” is not enough
I respect the defensive logic, but I do not accept an absolute guarantee posture as a restructuring answer.

If a site is structurally weak, then the company cannot be forced to carry that weakness forever. I am not prepared to endorse a settlement that rules out every hard option before the numbers have been tested.

You say closures must be last resort. Fine. I can accept that sequencing.  
But “last resort” cannot mean “never, regardless of economics.”

### 2) To management: closure cannot be the opening move either
At the same time, I do **not** support using closure as the first negotiating position. That destroys trust and hardens resistance before alternatives are assessed.

If management wants serious support, it must first demonstrate:
- what non-closure measures deliver,
- what residual gap remains,
- and why any further step is necessary.

### 3) To the state: blocking rights protect the enterprise, not every site
I respect the state’s rights under the special statute. But those rights are not meant to freeze industrial reality. They exist to protect the long-term viability of the enterprise and the region.

If a plant is structurally unsustainable, simply preserving it politically may feel protective, but it can also postpone decline and reduce the company’s ability to invest elsewhere.

### 4) To institutional investors: value protection is not the same as labor maximalism
I understand your concern about margin recovery. But I do not accept the notion that the most value-maximizing route is automatically the hardest labor cut or the quickest footprint reduction.

A drawn-out conflict, strike risk, and implementation failure can destroy more value than a more balanced restructuring with credible labor contribution and stable execution.

## What I want to see
I support the call for a **site-by-site quantitative model**, but it must answer the real question:
**What package gets us to a durable margin reset with the least destructive governance and execution risk?**

For each site, I want:
- utilization,
- fixed costs,
- labor cost per unit,
- product allocation,
- capex need,
- headcount impact,
- and 3–5 year savings.

But I also want:
- implementation risk,
- strike risk,
- supplier impact,
- and governance feasibility.

## My view on the three broad paths

### 1) Status quo
Not acceptable.

### 2) Pure non-closure restructuring
This is acceptable only if it is not decorative. It must include:
- real capacity reduction,
- meaningful voluntary workforce reduction,
- temporary labor-cost contribution,
- management overhead reduction,
- executive restraint,
- and investment discipline.

If it cannot close the savings gap, then it is not enough.

### 3) Hybrid restructuring with limited closure
I do not treat this as desirable, but I do treat it as a credible fallback if the numbers prove that the non-closure route cannot deliver sufficient structural savings.

That is the key point: **I am not ideologically attached to closures, but I am also not willing to rule them out in advance.**

## What I will not support
I will not support:
- a preserve-everything deal,
- a package that leaves the company permanently uncompetitive,
- a deal that avoids hard choices by hiding behind vague guarantees,
- or a prolonged conflict that destroys enterprise value.

And I will say this clearly: if the non-closure route is presented without enough savings, I will not pretend it is sufficient. That would be irresponsible ownership.

## My bargaining logic
The right sequence is:

1. prove the non-closure package on the numbers,
2. identify the residual gap,
3. test whether that gap can still be closed through non-closure levers,
4. and only then determine whether limited closure or other hard measures are unavoidable.

That is not a threat. It is governance discipline.

## My position on guarantees
I am open to **time-limited, enforceable protections** if they are tied to measurable performance and real employee contributions.

I am **not** open to a blanket promise that permanently removes management’s ability to restructure if circumstances worsen. That would impair governance and future competitiveness.

## Bottom line
The family holding wants:
- a real cost reset,
- a credible path to margin recovery,
- durable labor peace,
- and stable implementation.

We will support a non-closure solution **if it truly delivers equivalent structural savings**.  
We will not support a cosmetic “no closure” promise that leaves the company weak.  
And we will not support a reckless closure-first strategy that triggers value-destructive conflict.

If the parties want this to move, the next step is not another round of principles. It is the **site-by-site model** with residual savings gap and implementation risk.
