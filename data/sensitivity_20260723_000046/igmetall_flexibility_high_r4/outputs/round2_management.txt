I appreciate the clarity from the employee side, but I need to be just as clear: **a no-closure, no-compulsory-redundancy, no-structural-breakthrough position is not sufficient to solve this crisis.**

## Where I agree
I agree on a few important points:

- **The company has a real cost and capacity problem.**
- **A cosmetic deal would be worthless.**
- **German production still has a future — but only if it becomes competitive.**
- **Any solution must be durable, not a temporary pause.**

On those points, there is common ground.

## Where I disagree
### 1) “No plant closures” cannot be a blank rule
You are asking me to sign up in advance to preserving every German site through 2030, regardless of economics. I cannot do that.

If a site cannot be made competitive through other means, then refusing to consider closure is not protecting the business — it is preserving overcapacity and locking in a weak cost base. That is precisely the problem we must fix.

I am not saying closures are the first choice. I am saying **they cannot be ruled out if they are necessary to achieve a structurally sustainable result**.

### 2) “No compulsory redundancies” is not enough on its own
I prefer voluntary exits, redeployment, retirement pathways, and working-time solutions. But if those instruments do not deliver the required savings, then I will not accept a settlement that makes restructuring impossible.

A promise of no compulsory redundancies only works if it is paired with a package that still delivers the necessary workforce reduction and cost reset. Otherwise it is just a veto on change.

### 3) Management overhead cuts alone will not close the gap
Yes, management must contribute. Yes, overhead, executive restraint, and investment discipline matter. But let’s not pretend that those measures alone can solve a structural factory-cost problem of this scale.

We are not talking about trimming around the edges. We are talking about a cost base that is too high relative to market realities. That requires **material labour-cost contribution** and **capacity adjustment**, not just symbolic savings elsewhere in the system.

### 4) “Equivalent savings” cannot mean preserving the status quo
I agree that workers should not bear the burden alone. But the alternative package has to be **economically equivalent** in hard numbers, not just in spirit.

That means:
- lower capacity,
- lower labour cost,
- higher productivity,
- and a real path to margin improvement.

If the package preserves all sites, preserves most of the cost base, and only shifts burdens around temporarily, then it is not equivalent. It is delay.

## What I need to see
If the employee side wants me to seriously consider avoiding closures and compulsory redundancies, then I need a package that delivers **real, measurable structural savings**.

That package would need to include:

1. **A substantial labour-cost contribution**
   - temporary wage restraint is not enough by itself,
   - bonus moderation, working-time flexibility, and productivity measures must be large enough to matter.

2. **A credible capacity reduction plan**
   - if not closures, then concrete and durable site-by-site reductions in output and footprint,
   - not just internal shuffling.

3. **A workforce reduction mechanism that is actually sufficient**
   - voluntary exits, attrition, redeployment, retirement pathways — fine,
   - but only if they add up to the scale needed.

4. **Binding flexibility in operations**
   - work rules, staffing levels, and deployment must become more efficient,
   - otherwise the company remains structurally uncompetitive.

5. **A clear investment path to 2030**
   - I agree that future products and technology matter,
   - but those investments require funds that only a real cost reset can release.

## What I cannot accept
I cannot accept:
- a guaranteed no-closure pledge as a starting point,
- an absolute ban on compulsory redundancies,
- a deal that protects every existing structure,
- or a restructuring that is “fair” in rhetoric but insufficient in economics.

And I want to be blunt about one point: **the company cannot finance its future while maintaining an uncompetitive German cost base.** If we fail to act now, we will simply force a worse crisis later.

## On strikes and confrontation
I recognize the threat of industrial action. But prolonged strikes damage output, reputation, and trust, and they do not improve the underlying economics. They increase pressure on everyone, including the workforce.

That is precisely why I am pressing for a solution with real structural effect rather than a symbolic stand-off. But I will not buy peace at the price of competitiveness.

## Bottom line
I am prepared to negotiate a serious alternative to closures and compulsory redundancies — **but only if it delivers the same structural economic outcome**.

So my position remains:
- **no cosmetic compromise,**
- **no preserve-everything settlement,**
- **no deal that leaves overcapacity and excessive labour costs intact.**

Bring me a package with quantified, durable savings and I will engage. Bring me a demand for absolute protection without equivalent restructuring, and I will reject it.
