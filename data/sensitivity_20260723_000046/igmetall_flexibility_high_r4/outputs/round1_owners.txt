I appreciate the seriousness of the positions already laid out. Let me respond plainly from the perspective of the controlling shareholder.

### First, where I agree
I agree with management that the company’s cost base and capacity situation are not sustainable as they stand. If we do nothing meaningful, we will destroy value over time, weaken competitiveness, and reduce the room to invest in future products and technology.

I also agree with the works council, the union, and the state that a purely one-sided solution would be the wrong answer. A settlement that simply dumps the entire burden on the workforce would be unstable, damage trust, and almost certainly fail in execution. That would be value-destructive.

### Where I disagree
What I cannot support is a position that treats the current structure as essentially sacrosanct.

- **To the works council:** I understand the insistence on avoiding closures and forced redundancies, but a blanket “no closures, no compulsory redundancies” line is not yet a solution. If certain sites or structures cannot be made competitive, then refusing any capacity change in advance does not protect the company; it postpones a worse outcome.
- **To the union:** I hear the call for shared sacrifice, and that is reasonable in principle. But a labour contribution must be large enough and durable enough to matter. Temporary gestures that do not shift the economics are not enough.
- **To the state government:** I respect the regional responsibility, but the state cannot treat every capacity adjustment as politically off-limits. The company’s long-term health has to remain central.

### The controlling shareholder’s position
My priority is not to preserve every historical structure. My priority is to preserve and strengthen the enterprise over the long term.

That means I will support a restructuring only if it delivers:
- a credible and durable improvement in margins,
- a real reduction in structural overcapacity,
- a competitive labour-cost position,
- and stability in governance and industrial relations.

I am **not** prepared to endorse an outcome that leaves the company permanently burdened by excess capacity and unaffordable costs just because it avoids confrontation today.

### On plant closures
I am not demanding closures as an end in themselves. But I do reject the idea that closures can be ruled out before the economics are tested site by site. If an alternative package can achieve the same structural savings without closures, that is preferable. If it cannot, then we need the honesty to say so.

### On compulsory redundancies
I prefer solutions that avoid compulsory redundancies. That is not just a social preference; it is also about preserving know-how and stability. But I will not make that a hard absolute if it prevents the company from restructuring effectively.

### What would move me
What could move me is a package that combines:
- meaningful capacity reduction,
- negotiated labour-cost relief,
- productivity and flexibility improvements,
- voluntary exits and redeployment where possible,
- and binding commitments that restore competitiveness and protect the core German industrial base.

That is the threshold. Not a symbolic compromise, but a real operational turnaround.

### What will not move me
What will not move me is:
- a guarantee of all sites regardless of economics,
- a plan that avoids all hard decisions,
- or a deal that preserves the appearance of peace while continuing value erosion underneath.

### Bottom line
I am open to an alternative to closures and forced redundancies — but only if it is economically equivalent. If the employee side and the state want to block the hard measures, then they must help produce a package that actually solves the problem. Otherwise, I will not support an agreement that trades away the company’s future for short-term calm.
