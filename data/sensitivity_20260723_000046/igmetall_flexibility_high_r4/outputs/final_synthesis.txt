## 1. Decision context
The company faces a genuine structural competitiveness problem: high German production costs, overcapacity, and pressure from EV-transition investments, weak European demand, and lower-cost competitors. The decision is not whether to restructure, but whether restructuring can be achieved primarily through non-closure measures or whether closures and hard headcount reductions must remain available.

Governance is unusually constraining. Co-determination, the controlling family, and the regional state all hold effective blocking power, so a technically optimal plan is not enough; the package must also be implementable under German labor, board, and political constraints.

## 2. Stakeholder assembly
- **Group & Brand Management** — drives restructuring and seeks material structural savings.
- **Works Council** — plant-level workforce representation with strong co-determination rights.
- **Industrial Union** — collective bargaining counterpart with strike capacity.
- **Regional State Government** — anchor shareholder and political guardian of regional employment.
- **Controlling Family Holding** — controlling owner focused on long-term value and governance control.
- **Institutional Investors** — capital-market shareholders prioritizing competitiveness and returns.
- **Independent Expert** — non-party restructuring specialist asked to define credibility thresholds.
- **Moderator** — facilitator identifying the sequencing impasse and compromise frames.

## 3. Final positions

### Group & Brand Management
Management supports a serious restructuring and insists it must deliver “equivalent structural savings,” not symbolic concessions. It will accept a serious non-closure path only if it is quantified and credible; otherwise, closures and compulsory redundancies must remain available as fallback options.

### Works Council
The works council firmly rejects plant closures, compulsory redundancies, and any “non-closure first, closure later” arrangement that leaves the axe in reserve. It is willing to discuss temporary employee contributions only if they are matched by binding guarantees through 2030, co-determination in implementation, and no erosion of the German site footprint.

### Industrial Union
The union shares the anti-closure and anti-forced-redundancy position, but is somewhat more explicit that a negotiated labor contribution is possible if the package contains real guarantees and management/owner burden-sharing. It accepts the need for restructuring, but refuses a model that front-loads pain on labor while leaving closure as an easy fallback.

### Regional State Government
The state supports durable restructuring and recognizes the cost problem, but resists closure-first logic because of regional employment, supplier, and political consequences. It wants non-closure to be the primary route and would only contemplate limited fallback measures under strict, measurable conditions.

### Controlling Family Holding
The family is pragmatically open to either non-closure or limited closure, but only if the package delivers durable margin recovery and implementation certainty. It will not accept a preserve-everything settlement, nor a cosmetic no-closure promise that fails to fix the cost base.

### Institutional Investors
Investors are aligned with management and the family on the need for real structural savings, but they want proof that non-closure measures can do the job before endorsing hardship measures. They will support a non-closure package only if it credibly closes most of the gap; otherwise, they accept limited closure as economically necessary.

### Independent Expert
The expert defines the decision threshold most clearly: non-closure remains credible if it can deliver roughly 70–80% of required structural savings within 12–24 months, with manageable execution risk. If a residual gap remains materially large, or if one or more sites remain structurally uncompetitive, a hybrid with limited closure becomes hard to avoid.

## 4. Genuine conflicts (structurally incompatible positions)
1. **Closure legitimacy**
   - Labor-side actors treat closure as unacceptable except perhaps in a deeply hypothetical last resort.
   - Management, family, and investors treat closure as a legitimate fallback if numbers do not close.
   - This is a real incompatibility about the structure of the bargain, not merely timing.

2. **Burden of proof**
   - Labor and state demand that management prove non-closure failure before closure is even considered.
   - Capital-side actors demand proof that non-closure can match the needed structural savings before closures are ruled out.

3. **Guarantees vs flexibility**
   - Labor wants binding no-closure/no-forced-redundancy commitments.
   - Management and capital refuse to surrender restructuring flexibility in advance.

4. **Meaning of “credible restructuring”**
   - Labor defines credibility as preserving all German plants and jobs through strong guarantees.
   - Capital defines credibility as achieving the required savings, even if footprint reduction is part of the answer.

## 5. Areas of convergence
- The situation is serious and cosmetic solutions are unacceptable.
- Structural savings must be real, not accounting artifacts.
- Industrial conflict would be costly and should be avoided if possible.
- Management overhead, executive restraint, and investment sequencing should contribute.
- Voluntary exits, redeployment, and working-time measures are preferable to forced redundancies.
- A site-by-site quantitative model is necessary.
- Any workable compromise must be enforceable, not symbolic.

## 6. Key trade-offs for the decision-maker
- **Speed vs legitimacy:** faster restructuring may require harder measures, but that increases strike risk and political resistance.
- **Savings vs implementation certainty:** the deepest theoretical savings may be unimplementable under co-determination.
- **Blanket site protection vs targeted adjustment:** protecting all German plants maximizes legitimacy but may leave overcapacity unresolved.
- **Temporary guarantees vs long-term flexibility:** stronger promises ease labor acceptance but may impair future governance if the market worsens.
- **Non-closure trial vs fallback closure option:** a genuine trial phase improves trust, but management fears being trapped in indefinite renegotiation.

## 7. Integrative pathways
### A. Time-limited non-closure phase with objective triggers
A fixed 12–24 month phase could prohibit closures and forced redundancies while a quantified restructuring package is implemented. Independent monitoring would determine whether pre-agreed thresholds are met; only then could limited fallback restructuring be reopened.

### B. Reciprocal bargain with binding protections
Labor would provide temporary wage moderation, working-time flexibility, bonus restraint, and voluntary exits. In exchange, management and owners would provide overhead cuts, executive restraint, investment sequencing, and binding no-closure/no-forced-redundancy guarantees for the agreement term.

### C. Site-by-site preserve/consolidate/review map
The footprint could be split into protected core sites and weaker sites subject first to consolidation, product reallocation, and shift rationalization. Closure would remain a last-resort outcome only after jointly verified failure of recovery tests.

## 8. Open questions
- What exact savings gap remains after realistic non-closure measures?
- Which sites are structurally underutilized, and can product allocation materially improve them?
- How much voluntary headcount reduction is realistic within 12–24 months?
- What independent mechanism can verify savings and trigger thresholds?
- What level of investment is needed to keep weaker sites viable?
- How much burden can management, owners, and the state realistically absorb?
- What is the acceptable residual risk of strike action if closure is kept in reserve?

## 9. Recommended next step
Commission the requested **independent site-by-site restructuring model** immediately, with shared assumptions and three scenarios: status quo, non-closure restructuring, and hybrid with limited closure. The model should include utilization, fixed costs, labor cost per unit, capex needs, headcount impact, 3–5 year savings, implementation risk, strike risk, supplier impact, and governance feasibility.

## 10. Emergent insights that only appeared through multi-turn interaction
1. **The real dispute shifted from “whether to restructure” to “who carries the burden of proof.”**  
   Early rounds looked like a disagreement about closure. By round 4, it became clear the deeper issue was sequencing: whether non-closure must be proven before closure is allowed, or whether closure remains a standing fallback.

2. **A purely economic answer is insufficient in this governance setting.**  
   The interaction showed that even a credible savings model must pass co-determination, board, ownership, and state legitimacy tests. Implementation risk is not secondary; it is part of the economics.

3. **The phrase “no closures” is not stable unless coupled with a credible savings mechanism.**  
   Labor’s insistence on full protection is understandable, but the conversation revealed that capital will not treat it as credible unless the non-closure path closes most of the gap.

4. **The parties are closer on instruments than on sequencing.**  
   Most actors accept voluntary exits, working-time moderation, overhead cuts, and product shifts. The impasse is not the menu of tools; it is whether those tools are tried under a protected phase or under the shadow of immediate closure fallback.

5. **Expert framing created a practical threshold that was missing at the start.**  
   The 70–80% savings rule of thumb and the 20–30% residual-gap warning gave the discussion a usable decision standard that neither labor nor capital had articulated on its own.

6. **Governance power shapes what “credible” means as much as cost data do.**  
   Because several parties can block a closure, the feasible solution is likely to be a negotiated hybrid, even if one side would prefer a cleaner economic design.

7. **The state’s role is not merely defensive.**  
   The state emerged as a possible broker of a protected non-closure phase, but only if it can specify measurable fallback conditions. That is more constructive than a simple veto posture.

8. **There is latent agreement on burden-sharing, but not on symmetry.**  
   Everyone now accepts that labor cannot carry the whole adjustment. What remains unresolved is how much burden owners and management must absorb to justify employee concessions.
