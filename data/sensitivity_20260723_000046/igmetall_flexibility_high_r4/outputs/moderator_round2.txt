### 1) What has been agreed
- The company has a **real cost, capacity, and competitiveness problem**.
- A **cosmetic deal** would not be acceptable to any side.
- Any restructuring must be **durable** and supported by **credible numbers**.
- There is broad recognition that **management, ownership, employees, and the state** all have some role in any solution.
- There is some openness to **employee contributions** if matched by **binding safeguards** and broader burden-sharing.
- All sides accept that **industrial conflict would be costly** and should be avoided if possible.

### 2) Where the core disagreements remain
- **Closures:**  
  Employee-side representatives and the state want closures treated as a last resort; management, family, and investors insist closures must remain available if needed.
- **Compulsory redundancies:**  
  The workforce side rejects them outright; management, family, and investors do not accept an absolute ban.
- **Scope of labor contribution:**  
  Employees accept temporary restraint/flexibility only with guarantees; capital-side actors argue labor must make a substantial structural contribution.
- **Meaning of “equivalent savings”:**  
  The employee side sees non-closure alternatives as potentially sufficient; the capital side argues only a package with real structural capacity reduction is credible.
- **Timing and guarantees:**  
  Employees want binding no-closure/no-redundancy commitments through 2030; management and investors refuse to pre-commit before alternatives are proven.

### 3) What key trade-offs are still unaddressed
- **How much savings are actually required** versus how much can realistically come from non-closure measures.
- **Whether voluntary exits, redeployment, working-time changes, and productivity gains** can close the gap without plant closures.
- **Which sites are structurally weakest** and whether any can be kept open with credible future product commitments.
- **How burden-sharing is split** between labor costs, management overhead, owner contributions, and capacity reduction.
- **What binding governance mechanism** could make any compromise enforceable without becoming symbolic.
- **How to balance regional/political constraints** against the need for a financially viable footprint.

### 4) Brief refocus for the next round
- Move from principles to a **site-by-site quantitative model**.
- Compare at least three scenarios: **status quo**, **non-closure restructuring**, and **hybrid restructuring with limited closure**.
- For each site, show: **utilization, fixed costs, labor cost per unit, required investment, headcount impact, and net savings over 3–5 years**.
- Ask each party to state **one non-negotiable red line** and **one area of possible flexibility**.
- Focus the next round on **verifiable numbers, enforceable safeguards, and a realistic burden-sharing formula**.
