I’ll respond directly.

## Where I agree
I agree with the Works Council on the core principles:

- **no compulsory redundancies**
- **no plant closures as the default answer**
- **no settlement that shifts the burden mainly onto workers**
- **binding guarantees must accompany any employee contribution**

I also agree with management, the family, and investors on one point: **the cost problem is real**. A cosmetic deal would not help anyone, and we are not here to pretend otherwise.

## Where I disagree
What I do **not** accept is the idea that the only “serious” restructuring is one that includes closures and job cuts. That is not a neutral economic truth; it is a management preference that still needs to be proven.

### To management
You say “show us the numbers.” Fine. But then the numbers must prove more than that the company has a problem — they must prove that **the proposed measures are necessary and proportionate**.

Right now, I do **not** see proof that:
- closures are unavoidable,
- compulsory redundancies are unavoidable,
- or that workers must absorb the main pain while management and owners retain their full room.

If you want the union to accept a labour contribution, then you must show a credible package with **shared sacrifice** and **site security**. Otherwise, your restructuring proposal is just a demand that employees pay for a strategic failure they did not cause.

### To the family and investors
You keep saying savings must be “equivalent.” I agree in principle. But equivalent to what? If “equivalent” means:
- permanent job destruction,
- plant closures,
- and the dismantling of wage standards,

then I reject that definition.

A real equivalent package can also come from:
- temporary wage restraint,
- bonus waivers,
- working-time reductions,
- internal redeployment,
- qualification,
- management overhead cuts,
- and negotiated productivity measures.

That is what a serious shared-sacrifice model looks like. Not workers alone paying the bill.

### To the state government
I support your resistance to closures. But I need to be very clear: “some adjustment is unavoidable” cannot become a coded approval for forced layoffs or closure-by-stealth.

If the state wants to back a restructuring, then it must insist on:
- no forced redundancies,
- no closure path disguised as “site optimization,”
- and binding commitments for investment and future production in Germany.

## What we are prepared to discuss
The union is **not** saying “nothing may change.” We are saying **the workforce will not finance the turnaround alone**.

We are prepared to discuss a negotiated package with the following elements:

1. **Temporary labour-cost contribution**
   - wage restraint,
   - bonus moderation or waiver,
   - working-time flexibility,
   - all limited in duration and linked to hard safeguards.

2. **Capacity reduction without closures**
   - shift reductions,
   - internal reallocation,
   - product adjustments,
   - use of natural attrition and voluntary exits,
   - qualification and redeployment.

3. **Binding site and job guarantees**
   - no plant closures through 2030,
   - no compulsory redundancies,
   - clear commitments that German production remains a core part of the group.

4. **Management and ownership contribution**
   - overhead reductions,
   - executive restraint,
   - investment discipline,
   - and a visible contribution from capital, not only labour.

5. **Enforceable collective-bargaining framework**
   - any contribution by workers must be embedded in a collective agreement,
   - not vague promises or one-sided management declarations.

## What is not acceptable
Let me state the red lines plainly:

- no compulsory redundancies
- no plant closures
- no unilateral tearing-up of collective wage standards
- no open-ended insecurity
- no “temporary” sacrifice for workers that becomes permanent for everyone else

If management insists that the only way forward is closures and forced job cuts, then we are not in a negotiation; we are in a confrontation. And the union is prepared for that. The peace obligation has ended, and our members expect us to defend them.

## On the “numbers” question
If management wants credibility, then bring a **non-closure, no-forced-redundancy model** to the table and quantify it:

- how much comes from labour-cost adjustments,
- how much comes from management savings,
- how much comes from voluntary measures,
- how much capacity can be reduced internally,
- and what investment commitments secure the future.

If that model is real, we will bargain on it. If it is just a path toward closures under another name, then it will fail.

## Bottom line
The union is ready to negotiate a durable settlement, but only on the basis of **fair burden-sharing**.

That means:
- employees contribute,
- management contributes,
- owners contribute,
- and jobs and sites are protected.

If that is on the table, we can work. If the workforce is expected to carry the pain alone, then the answer is no.
