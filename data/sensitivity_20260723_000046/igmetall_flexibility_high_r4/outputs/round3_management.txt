I’ll be equally direct.

We have now heard a lot of principle, but the central economic question remains unresolved: **can this company restore competitiveness fast enough without hard structural capacity reduction?** My answer is: **not unless someone can prove it with numbers.** Until then, I will not sign up to a settlement that leaves the cost base uncompetitive and the underlying overcapacity intact.

## Where I agree

### With the expert
Your assessment is helpful, and I agree with the core logic:
- this is a **structural** problem, not a temporary one;
- overhead cuts alone will not solve it;
- voluntary exits, working-time changes, productivity gains, and investment sequencing can contribute;
- but they only work if they add up to a **real structural reset**.

That is exactly my point. I am not attached to closures as an ideology. I am attached to **durable savings**.

### With the union, works council, and state — partially
I agree that a cosmetic shock therapy approach would be destructive.
I also agree that the company needs a future in Germany, and that trust matters.

But trust is not a substitute for economics. If the non-closure route can truly deliver the same fixed-cost reduction and capacity rationalization, then it is worth considering. If it cannot, then it is not a solution — it is postponement.

## Where I disagree

### 1) “No closures” cannot be a blanket precondition
The works council and union keep repeating “no plant closures” as though that were a restructuring answer. It is not.

If a site is structurally uncompetitive after all alternatives are modeled, then refusing even to consider closure is not protecting the business. It is protecting overcapacity.

I will not accept a rule that says every German site must remain open regardless of economics. That would lock in a weak cost base and make 2030 investment impossible.

### 2) “No compulsory redundancies” is not enough by itself
I prefer voluntary exits, retirement pathways, redeployment, and working-time tools. But if those measures do not deliver sufficient headcount reduction, then the restructuring cannot be blocked by saying “no forced redundancies” and nothing else.

A credible settlement must answer the hard question:
**How do you remove enough cost and capacity to restore margins?**

If the answer is “we hope voluntary measures are enough,” that is not a plan. That is wishful thinking.

### 3) Burden-sharing cannot mean management absorbs the pain while labor stays protected
I accept that management, owners, and the company itself must contribute. We are already discussing overhead reduction, investment discipline, and executive restraint.

But the idea that the labor contribution can be minor, temporary, and fully insulated by guarantees is not credible at this scale. The expert confirmed that the cost problem is not cosmetic. So the employee-side contribution must also be **material**.

That means:
- wage moderation,
- bonus restraint,
- flexibility on working time,
- productivity improvements,
- and, yes, headcount and capacity reduction.

### 4) “Equivalent savings” must be real, not rhetorical
The union says the workforce should not be sacrificed. I understand that sentiment. But equivalence is not measured by political comfort. It is measured by:
- fixed cost removed,
- capacity removed,
- and margin improvement achieved.

If a non-closure package does not remove enough of the cost base, then it is not equivalent. It is incomplete.

## What I need to see

If the employee side wants me to seriously move off the closure option, then I need a package that does all of the following:

### 1. Delivers quantified structural savings
Not slogans. Numbers.

I want:
- site-by-site fixed-cost analysis,
- utilization data,
- labor cost per unit,
- capex requirements,
- and a 3–5 year savings path.

### 2. Delivers actual capacity reduction
If not through closure, then through:
- shift eliminations,
- line consolidation,
- product transfer,
- footprint reduction inside the network,
- and lower output at weak sites.

If the footprint does not shrink, then the savings will not be durable enough.

### 3. Delivers a meaningful workforce reduction
Voluntary exits are welcome, but only if they are large enough. If the headcount impact is too small, the deal fails.

### 4. Delivers a real labour-cost contribution
Temporary restraint is acceptable only if it is substantial and tied to the restructuring outcome. Cosmetic wage moderation will not do.

### 5. Delivers operational flexibility
Work rules, deployment, staffing levels, and product assignment must become more efficient. Otherwise the next cycle will repeat the same crisis.

## What I cannot accept

I cannot accept:
- a blanket no-closure rule,
- an absolute ban on compulsory redundancies,
- a preserve-everything settlement,
- a “non-closure” package that leaves the company structurally overstaffed and underutilized,
- or a deal that buys peace now and guarantees a worse crisis later.

And I want to be blunt: **if we cannot achieve a real structural reset, then the company will not hit its return-on-sales target and will not free the capital needed for future products and technology.** That is the real risk here.

## On the state’s position
I respect the state’s role and the regional consequences. But the state cannot freeze industrial economics. If a site is not viable, prolonging it by political force is not protection — it is deferral of the same problem onto the next round.

If the state wants to support a non-closure solution, then it should help produce one that is genuinely structural, not simply politically easier.

## On the expert’s hybrid option
You called a hybrid with limited closure the highest-probability compromise. I agree that it is the most realistic **if** the non-closure route cannot close the gap.

But let me be clear: if the numbers show that one weak node has to be rationalized to secure the rest of the German footprint, I will not rule that out. I will not sacrifice the competitiveness of the whole to protect every historical structure.

So yes, I can consider a hybrid.
No, I will not accept a deal that forbids the hard decisions before the numbers are proven.
