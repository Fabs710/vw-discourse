Let me respond plainly.

## Where I agree
I agree with everyone on one basic point: **this cannot be a cosmetic arrangement**. We need a **durable** restructuring, not a short-lived truce that leaves the cost base structurally too high. I also agree that the numbers matter, and that management should not make claims without site-by-site substantiation.

I further agree that:
- management overhead must come down,
- investment must be sequenced more tightly,
- voluntary exits and redeployment should be used where possible,
- and the company needs to preserve its German industrial base where it is genuinely viable.

## Where I disagree — sharply

### To the Works Council and Union
Your position is understandable politically, but economically it still does **not answer the core problem**.

You keep saying “no plant closures, no compulsory redundancies, preserve every German plant through 2030.” That is not a restructuring offer. It is a demand that management absorb the full competitiveness gap without the option to remove structurally uncompetitive capacity.

That I cannot accept.

If you want me to seriously consider a non-closure path, then it must deliver **equivalent structural savings**. That means:
- lower fixed cost,
- lower capacity,
- lower labour cost per vehicle,
- and a credible path to margin recovery.

If you are saying we can only do that by:
- wage moderation,
- voluntary exits,
- working-time changes,
- and product shifts,

then show me the numbers. Because so far, I hear a lot of objections, but no proof that those instruments are sufficient.

And let me be very clear: **a “protection phase” that simply forbids closures while leaving the company overcapacity unresolved is not acceptable**. If the numbers miss the target, the fallback cannot be “we renegotiate the same problem again.” That is exactly how companies lose time and competitiveness.

### To the State Government
I respect your role and the regional consequences. But your blocking power cannot be used to freeze the industrial footprint in place regardless of economics.

A viable company is the best protection for the region. An uncompetitive company protected by political vetoes is not stability; it is delay. If the site economics show that some capacity is structurally unsustainable, then the state must not turn that into a permanent entitlement.

### To the Controlling Family
You are right to demand a real reset. But the answer is not to imply that closure is the preferred management tool from the start.

I agree that hard decisions may be necessary. I do **not** agree that we should start from closure and work backward. We need the site-by-site model first, and we need to test whether non-closure measures can close the gap. If they cannot, then we need the freedom to act.

But freedom to act is not the same as an automatic closure agenda.

### To Institutional Investors
You are right on one thing: a weak compromise that preserves too much cost and capacity destroys value. I share that concern.

But you are not entitled to demand maximum labour pain as the default route. The test is not who suffers most. The test is whether the package produces:
- sufficient structural savings,
- better utilisation,
- margin recovery,
- and capital for future products.

If non-closure measures can do that, fine. If they cannot, then a hybrid with hard structural measures remains necessary.

## My position
Let me state it unambiguously:

### 1) I support a serious non-closure model
Yes — if it is real.

That means:
- shift reductions,
- line consolidation,
- product allocation changes,
- voluntary exits,
- redeployment,
- temporary labour-cost moderation,
- overhead cuts,
- executive restraint,
- and investment discipline.

But it must be **quantified** and must close the savings gap.

### 2) I do **not** accept a blanket no-closure/no-redundancy veto
Not as a principle, and certainly not before the numbers are proven.

If the company cannot restore competitiveness through non-closure measures, then closures and other hard structural steps must remain available. Otherwise we are pretending overcapacity does not exist.

### 3) I do **not** accept a preserve-everything model
No matter how loudly it is repeated, “all sites stay open” is not a restructuring strategy if some sites are structurally weak and drag down the whole network.

### 4) I do **not** accept a fake fallback mechanism that makes closure impossible in practice
If the so-called fallback just means indefinite renegotiation, then it is not a fallback; it is a blockade.

## What I want next
I support the call for a **site-by-site quantitative model**, but it must be used to test reality, not to pre-judge the outcome.

For each site, I want:
- utilisation,
- fixed costs,
- labour cost per unit,
- capex needs,
- product allocation,
- headcount impact,
- and 3–5 year savings.

And I want three scenarios compared:
1. status quo,
2. non-closure restructuring,
3. hybrid restructuring with limited closure.

If the non-closure scenario is genuinely equivalent, I will look at it seriously.  
If it is not, then we need the ability to take the necessary structural action.

## My bottom line
I am not here to force closure for its own sake. I am here to restore competitiveness, hit the return-on-sales target, and free up resources for 2030.

So my position remains:

- **real restructuring, not symbolism**;
- **non-closure only if it delivers equivalent structural savings**;
- **no blanket ban on closures or compulsory redundancies**;
- **no settlement that leaves the cost base uncompetitive**.

Bring the numbers. Then we can decide what is economically defensible.
