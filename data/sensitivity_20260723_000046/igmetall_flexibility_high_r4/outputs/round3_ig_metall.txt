I’ll respond plainly.

## Where I agree
I agree with the expert, the state, and even management on one basic point: **this is a real structural problem**. There is overcapacity, there is a cost issue, and a cosmetic agreement would not save jobs in the medium term. So no one should mistake my position for denial.

I also agree that **numbers matter**. If we are going to make sacrifices, they must be tied to a real restructuring logic, not slogans.

## Where I disagree
What I do **not** accept is the way management and investors keep treating **closures and forced redundancies as the default benchmark** and then asking the workforce to prove why it should not be punished.

That reverses the burden of proof.

If you want the union to accept concessions, then you must first show that:
- closures are truly unavoidable,
- compulsory redundancies are truly unavoidable,
- and the required savings cannot be achieved through a serious non-closure package.

So far, I have not seen that proof. I have seen assumptions and preferences.

## To management
You say a no-closure, no-forced-redundancy position is not sufficient. That may be true **only if** you have already exhausted the alternatives. But you have not demonstrated that.

The expert has made one thing clear: a non-closure restructuring can be credible **if** it combines:
- capacity reduction,
- voluntary exits,
- working-time changes,
- labour-cost moderation,
- management overhead cuts,
- and binding product and investment commitments.

That is the route we are prepared to discuss.

What we are **not** prepared to discuss is a settlement that begins with:
- plant closures,
- forced dismissals,
- and permanent insecurity for the workforce.

That is not restructuring with shared sacrifice. That is shifting the burden onto labor.

## To the family and investors
You keep saying “economic reality.” Fine. Then be honest about the real choices.

The expert is correct that the company can pursue:
- shift reductions,
- consolidation of production,
- voluntary severance,
- retirement pathways,
- redeployment,
- wage restraint,
- bonus moderation,
- and investment sequencing.

If you dismiss those measures as insufficient before testing them seriously, then you are not asking for a balanced restructuring. You are demanding the workforce finance the turnaround while capital keeps the hard options open as leverage.

That is exactly what we reject.

## To the state government
I appreciate your support for a non-closure route, but let me be equally clear: “closure as a last resort” is only meaningful if you are prepared to **use your blocking position to stop premature closure logic**.

If the state is serious, then it should insist on:
- no German plant closures during the agreement term,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- binding investment commitments,
- and a real monitoring mechanism for capacity reduction and future product allocation.

Otherwise, “last resort” becomes a polite way of keeping the closure threat alive.

## To the expert
Your assessment is useful because it confirms something the union has been saying all along: a non-closure deal is not fantasy. It is a **real option**, but only if the company accepts genuine burden-sharing and measurable capacity reduction.

You also made clear that a blanket preserve-everything position with no labor contribution would not work. I agree with that. But that is **not** our position.

Our position is:
- employees contribute,
- management contributes,
- owners contribute,
- and the burden is not dumped on the workforce alone.

That is a very different thing.

## What we are prepared to discuss
A serious package can include:

1. **Temporary labor-cost contribution**
   - wage restraint,
   - bonus moderation,
   - working-time flexibility,
   - all time-limited and tied to guarantees.

2. **Voluntary workforce reduction**
   - natural attrition,
   - retirement pathways,
   - voluntary severance,
   - internal redeployment.

3. **Capacity reduction without closures**
   - shift cuts,
   - product consolidation,
   - line rationalization,
   - lower output at weak sites.

4. **Management and ownership contributions**
   - overhead reduction,
   - executive restraint,
   - investment discipline.

5. **Binding safeguards**
   - no plant closures through the agreement term,
   - no compulsory redundancies,
   - site-by-site guarantees,
   - enforceable co-determination in implementation.

## What is not acceptable
Let me repeat the line clearly:

- no compulsory redundancies,
- no German plant closures,
- no one-sided “equivalent savings” that really means layoffs,
- no open-ended insecurity,
- no settlement that leaves workers carrying the main pain.

If management and investors are unwilling to move off that logic, then there is no deal.
