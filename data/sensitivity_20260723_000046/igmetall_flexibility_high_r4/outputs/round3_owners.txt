## My position
The controlling family holding supports a **real restructuring**. We do **not** support a cosmetic compromise that preserves the current cost base, nor do we support a prolonged conflict that destroys value. The company needs a durable reset in competitiveness and margins, and that requires **structural savings**, not rhetoric.

At the same time, I am **not** willing to endorse a settlement that triggers unnecessary instability, damages the company’s social legitimacy, or needlessly destroys industrial value. A deal that looks “clean” on paper but leads to protracted strikes, implementation failure, or reputational damage is not an acceptable outcome for long-term owners.

## Where I agree

### With management
You are right that:
- the cost problem is real,
- overcapacity is real,
- and overhead cuts alone will not fix this.

The expert has reinforced that point. I agree that the company needs **material, durable savings** and a lower fixed-cost base.

### With the expert
Your assessment is useful and credible:
- a pure “do nothing” or “preserve everything” stance is not viable,
- non-closure restructuring can work only if it produces **real structural relief**,
- and capacity reduction matters as much as labour-cost moderation.

That is exactly the frame we should be using.

### With the state
I agree that industrial continuity, regional stability, and governance legitimacy matter. A solution that simply bulldozes the workforce and the region would be value-destructive over time.

### With the workforce side
I agree that the workforce should not bear the burden alone. A durable settlement needs **shared sacrifice** and credible commitments from management and ownership as well.

## Where I disagree

### To the works council and union
Your core red lines remain too absolute.

A blanket **“no plant closures, no compulsory redundancies, all sites protected through 2030”** is not an economic plan. It is a political demand. If a site is structurally uncompetitive, I will not support preserving it indefinitely at the expense of the whole company.

I do **not** accept the argument that because closures are painful, they must be off-limits. Pain is not the criterion. Viability is.

At the same time, I am not endorsing closures as the default answer. They must be **last resort measures**, not the opening move. But they must remain available if the numbers prove they are necessary.

### To management
You are right to insist on real structural savings, but I do not accept any attempt to use “necessary competitiveness” as a blanket justification before alternatives are genuinely tested.

If you want support for hard measures, you must show:
- site-by-site economics,
- the actual residual gap after non-closure measures,
- and why specific measures are not enough.

A closure-first posture without proof will not get my support.

### To the state government
I respect your statutory role, but I do **not** accept the idea that your blocking rights can freeze the footprint in place regardless of economics. Governance rights are there to protect the enterprise and the region, not to preserve every legacy structure.

If the company’s competitiveness cannot be restored otherwise, then the state must not help lock in structural losses.

## What would move me
I am prepared to back a serious package if it contains all of the following:

1. **A quantified structural savings target**
   - not a vague ambition, but a measured, durable improvement in margins and fixed costs.

2. **A site-by-site model**
   - utilization,
   - fixed costs,
   - labour cost per unit,
   - capex needs,
   - headcount impact,
   - and 3–5 year savings.

3. **A credible non-closure scenario**
   - if it truly delivers equivalent savings, I would prefer that to an unnecessary closure.

4. **A realistic fallback**
   - if the non-closure route does not close the gap, then one or more hard structural measures must remain available.

5. **Meaningful employee contribution**
   - temporary wage restraint,
   - bonus moderation,
   - working-time flexibility,
   - productivity improvements,
   - and voluntary workforce reduction.

6. **Management and ownership contribution**
   - overhead cuts,
   - executive restraint,
   - and disciplined investment sequencing.

7. **Execution stability**
   - no endless confrontation, no symbolic deal, no implementation that collapses under its own ambiguity.

## What I will not support
I will not support:
- a preserve-everything settlement,
- a deal that leaves the company permanently uncompetitive,
- a package that gives no real structural savings,
- or a prolonged strike war that destroys enterprise value.

I also will not support a “non-closure” package if it is just a way to avoid hard decisions while preserving too much cost and capacity.

## On the expert’s hybrid option
The expert’s point is important: a **hybrid** may be the most realistic compromise if the non-closure route cannot fully close the gap. I do not treat that as a mandate for closures, but I do treat it as a credible fallback if the numbers require it.

So my position is:
- **prefer non-closure if it is genuinely equivalent,**
- **accept hard structural measures if it is not,**
- **reject cosmetic compromise in either case.**
