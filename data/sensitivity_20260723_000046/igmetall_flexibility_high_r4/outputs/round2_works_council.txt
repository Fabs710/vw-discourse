Management, shareholders, and investors are still trying to frame this as if the only serious choice is “closures or denial.” I reject that framing.

We all agree there is a cost and competitiveness problem. We do **not** agree on who should pay for management’s strategic failures and market misjudgments. The workforce will not be made the shock absorber for every problem in this restructuring.

## Let me be specific

### To management
You keep saying “show us the numbers.” Fine — then show them transparently, site by site, line by line.

But I will be just as clear: **you have not yet shown that closures are unavoidable**. Saying a site is “not competitive” is not enough. If you want to shut a German plant, you must prove:
- that every alternative has been exhausted,
- that the same savings cannot be achieved through working-time changes, redeployment, product shifts, qualification, and voluntary instruments,
- and that the closure would not damage the wider network, supplier base, or future capacity.

Until then, closure is not a solution; it is a threat.

And on compulsory redundancies: I repeat, **we will not accept them**. If management cannot restructure without forcing people out, then management has not done its job properly.

### To the family owners and investors
You are all talking about “durability” and “equivalent savings,” but your version of durability seems to mean permanent damage to workers and sites, with no equivalent responsibility from capital.

If you want a serious alternative to closures, then you must accept that the savings package cannot come only from cutting jobs. There are other levers:
- management overhead,
- external spending,
- investment sequencing,
- executive compensation,
- working-time arrangements,
- internal flexibility,
- and the use of natural attrition and voluntary offers.

The burden cannot be loaded onto labour alone while ownership and management preserve their room to maneuver untouched.

### To the state government
I appreciate that you are resisting a crude closure logic, but I need to be blunt: your language about “some adjustment is unavoidable” can quickly become a back door to closures if you are not careful.

We can discuss internal flexibility and capacity adjustments **only if** they stay within the framework of:
- no German plant closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- binding site guarantees through 2030,
- and a genuine role for works council co-determination in implementation.

If those guarantees are not there, then there is no basis for us to treat “adjustment” as acceptable.

## What we are prepared to discuss

We are not refusing restructuring. We are refusing **destructive restructuring**.

A credible package would have to include all of the following:

1. **Binding no-closure commitment for German sites through 2030**
   - Not a vague intention, but a board-level commitment.

2. **Binding no compulsory redundancies**
   - Workforce reduction, if any, through voluntary exits, retirement pathways, redeployment, qualification, and internally managed working-time reductions.

3. **Fair employee contribution**
   - We are prepared to discuss temporary wage restraint, bonus moderation, or working-time flexibility.
   - But only in exchange for hard guarantees on sites and jobs.

4. **Management and ownership contribution**
   - overhead cuts,
   - executive restraint,
   - investment discipline,
   - and a transparent commitment that the workforce is not financing everybody else’s mistakes.

5. **Real co-determination in the restructuring**
   - The works council must not be handed a finished package and asked to rubber-stamp it.
   - We need a formal role in shaping implementation, site by site.

## What is not acceptable
Let me state this plainly:

- no German plant closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- no dismantling of job-security protections,
- no forced “equivalent savings” standard that is actually a code word for layoffs,
- no fait accompli presented as a negotiation.

If that is the direction, then there is no settlement. There will be resistance, including industrial action, and I am not backing away from that.

## On the claim that we need “numbers”
We do not oppose numbers. We oppose using numbers as a weapon to pre-decide the outcome.

If management is serious, put forward a **non-closure restructuring model**:
- quantify the required savings,
- identify how much comes from voluntary workforce measures,
- identify how much comes from management and ownership contributions,
- identify how capacity can be reduced without shutting plants,
- and show the investment and product commitments that make this credible to 2030.

If that model is real, we will examine it. If it is just a dressed-up path to closures and forced redundancies, we will reject it.

## Bottom line
The line remains the same:
- **all German plants stay open,**
- **no compulsory redundancies,**
- **job and site guarantees through 2030,**
- **real co-determination,**
- **fair burden-sharing.**

Management has not met the burden of proving that it needs to break these guarantees. Until it does, our answer is no.
