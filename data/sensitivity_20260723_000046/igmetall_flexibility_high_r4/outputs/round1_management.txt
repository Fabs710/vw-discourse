Let me be equally clear from management’s side.

I recognize the points you are making: this company does have a real cost problem, and we cannot pretend that a shrinking European market and tougher global competition will solve themselves. I also agree that a purely cosmetic restructuring would be useless.

But I must reject the central premise of your position: **“no closures, no compulsory redundancies, no meaningful labour contribution.”** That is not a viable answer to the scale of the problem we face.

### Where I agree
- **Yes, we need a future for German production.**
- **Yes, we need investment in products and technology.**
- **Yes, any solution must be durable, not just a short pause.**
- **Yes, we should avoid unnecessary destruction of industrial capability.**

### Where I disagree
What I cannot accept is a settlement that preserves every structure exactly as it is while expecting the company to become competitive anyway. We are carrying **too much capacity** and **too high a cost base**. If we leave that untouched, we will not hit our return-on-sales target, we will not free funds for future products, and we will continue to lose ground to lower-cost competitors.

That means:
- **some reduction in German capacity is unavoidable,**
- **a substantial labour-cost contribution is unavoidable,**
- and **all options must be on the table**, including plant closures if that is what it takes to produce a structurally sustainable result.

### On your “no closures” line
I understand why you are drawing a hard line. But from where I sit, a blanket refusal of closures is not a serious response to the economic reality. If a site cannot be made competitive through alternative measures, then keeping it open forever is not a strategy — it is deferring the problem and making it worse.

I am not saying closure is the first or only instrument. I am saying it cannot be ruled out in advance.

### On compulsory redundancies
I hear the concern, but I will also be direct: if we cannot achieve the necessary savings through negotiated measures, then job reductions will happen in some form. I would prefer that we find a socially managed solution — redeployment, attrition, working-time changes, voluntary exits, and agreed labour-cost adjustments. But I will not bind management to a promise that leaves us unable to restructure effectively.

### What could move me
I am prepared to work toward a negotiated package if it delivers **real, durable, structural savings**. That means:
- a genuine reduction in capacity,
- a meaningful and measurable labour-cost contribution,
- flexibility on work rules and staffing,
- and certainty that the company’s German operations will return to a competitive cost position.

If you want to avoid closures and forced redundancies, then you need to put forward an alternative that achieves the same economic effect. Not slogans, not aspirations — **numbers**.

### What will not move me
- A demand for full job security through 2030 with no structural changes.
- A promise of future investment without a credible cost reduction plan.
- A settlement that shifts the entire burden onto owners, management, or capital markets while leaving the workforce untouched.
- A deal that protects every site but leaves the company uncompetitive.

### Bottom line
I am open to negotiation, but not to denial. If the employee side wants to avoid the hardest measures, then it must help deliver a package that is **equally effective** in restoring competitiveness. Otherwise, management will continue to pursue the restructuring we believe is necessary.
