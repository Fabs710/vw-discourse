## My assessment

### 1) What is already clear
The company likely has a **structural European cost problem**, not just a cyclical one. In automotive manufacturing, when a brand is missing its ROS target and the footprint has excess fixed cost, the burden usually comes from some combination of:

- underutilized plants,
- duplicated overhead,
- high labor cost per vehicle,
- subscale functions,
- logistics inefficiency,
- and legacy terms that make flexible adjustment expensive.

If utilization is materially below efficient levels, the fixed-cost absorption loss alone can be very large. In a plant network, **10 percentage points of utilization shortfall** can easily mean a **mid-single-digit euros-per-vehicle** penalty on top of labor and overhead, depending on volume and product mix.

### 2) What the dispute is really about
The real question is not “closures or no closures.” It is:

> Can the company remove enough **recurring fixed cost** and **excess capacity** without shutting plants and without compulsory redundancies?

That is a quantifiable question. The answer depends on:
- current volume by plant,
- model allocation,
- line rates and shift patterns,
- available flexible labor instruments,
- wage and benefit structure,
- logistics distances and inter-plant transfers,
- and how much overhead can actually be taken out.

### 3) My preliminary industrial view
Without seeing the underlying data, my **base-case expectation** is:

- **Capacity reductions through working-time changes, redeployment, and voluntary exits can help materially**
- but they **rarely fully substitute** for closing a structurally underutilized plant if that plant has:
  - low line utilization,
  - high fixed overhead,
  - poor product outlook,
  - and significant logistics duplication.

In other words:  
**A no-closure package may be feasible if the problem is modest.**  
If the problem is a **large and durable structural overcapacity**, then a no-closure package usually leaves too much fixed cost in place unless the labor contribution is unusually large and durable.

### 4) What would need to be shown for “no closures” to work
For an alternative package to be economically credible, it would need to demonstrate, at minimum:

1. **Recurring cost savings**
   - not one-offs,
   - not deferred costs,
   - not accounting-only effects.

2. **Material capacity removal**
   - enough to align installed capacity with realistic European demand.

3. **Lower labor cost per vehicle**
   - through pay measures, working-time redesign, shift changes, or productivity gains.

4. **Overhead reduction**
   - plant-level and HQ/admin overhead must come down, not just direct labor.

5. **Implementation certainty**
   - because a theoretically good package that cannot be executed under co-determination is not a real package.

## What I would expect a proper model to contain

### A. Site-by-site plant table
For each German site:
- annual output capacity,
- current utilization,
- product assigned,
- direct labor hours per vehicle,
- logistics cost,
- fixed overhead,
- maintenance and energy burden,
- required capex to keep competitive,
- and scenario outcomes under:
  - status quo,
  - 1-shift / 2-shift changes,
  - volume reallocation,
  - partial line shutdown,
  - full closure.

### B. Cost bridge
A bridge from current to target economics:
- labor cost per vehicle,
- overhead per vehicle,
- depreciation / under-absorption,
- logistics,
- quality / scrap,
- and one-time restructuring charges.

### C. Scenario comparison
At least three cases:
1. **No closures, no involuntary layoffs**
2. **Selective closures / consolidation**
3. **Hybrid negotiated package**

For each case, the model should show:
- annual recurring savings,
- one-time costs,
- NPV over 5–7 years,
- employment impact,
- and break-even volume.

## Likely tradeoff
In most auto footprint restructurings, the tradeoff is:

- **Keeping all sites open** preserves labor peace and political acceptability,
- but often **reduces the achievable savings** enough that the company remains only marginally competitive.
- **Closing one site** typically delivers a sharper and more durable fixed-cost reduction,
- but creates major governance and industrial-relations risk.

So the question becomes whether the company can reach the needed savings by:
- reducing capacity enough elsewhere,
- extracting a real labor contribution,
- and cutting overhead aggressively.

If not, then the “no closures” position may be **socially preferable but financially insufficient**.

## My conclusion
At this stage, I would not say closures are definitely required. But I would also not say the no-closure option is credible without hard numbers.

### Decision-useful bottom line:
- **The workforce side has not yet proven that its preferred package can equal the recurring savings of closure-based restructuring.**
- **Management has not yet proven that closures are the only way to achieve the required savings.**
- An independent model is needed to determine:
  - how much capacity must come out,
  - which sites are structurally underutilized,
  - and whether a negotiated alternative can close the margin gap.
