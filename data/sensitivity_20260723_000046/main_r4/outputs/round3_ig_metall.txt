## To management
You are still trying to turn a restructuring problem into a job-cutting problem. I do not accept that framing.

The expert has confirmed what we have said from the start: **closures are not proven to be unavoidable**. A no-closure package is possible in principle if the company is serious about:
- overhead reduction,
- shift and capacity optimization,
- model and volume reallocation,
- voluntary workforce adjustment,
- and a fair, time-limited labor contribution.

So your claim that closures and compulsory redundancies are the “natural” or “inevitable” solution is not established. It is a management preference, not a fact.

What I do agree with is this: the company needs real recurring savings, not theater. But those savings do **not** have to come from destroying plant security and forcing layoffs. If you want labor to contribute, then you need to put forward a package that shares the burden instead of dumping it on employees.

## To the works council
I agree with your core red lines:
- **no plant closures**
- **no compulsory redundancies**
- **no dismantling of the collective bargaining framework**

You are right that workers should not be made the shock absorber for management’s strategic missteps. And you are right to insist on binding guarantees if employees are asked to contribute.

Where I would sharpen the position is this: we should not let management use “equivalent savings” as a rhetorical weapon to pre-justify closures. The expert is clear that the right next step is a **site-by-site model**, not a surrender to plant shutdown logic.

## To the state
Your insistence on regional stability and co-determination is welcome. But I have to push you on one point: saying “real capacity reduction” is necessary is fine, but capacity reduction does **not** automatically mean closures.

Capacity can be reduced through:
- lower shift intensity,
- reallocation of model volume,
- internal consolidation of functions,
- reduced overtime and premium hours,
- and voluntary workforce adjustments.

That is the route labor can discuss. What we will not accept is using the language of “capacity” to smuggle in closures.

## To the family holding and investors
You are right about one thing: the company needs durable, recurring savings and a credible path to competitiveness.

But you are wrong if you think that the only way to get there is to force open-ended insecurity on the workforce. A settlement that destroys trust, industrial peace, and the collective wage framework is not “value-preserving” just because it looks clean on a spreadsheet.

If you want a sustainable turnaround, then you need shared sacrifice:
- management contribution,
- ownership responsibility,
- labor contribution,
- and binding future investment.

If workers are asked to give something, then the owners and management must give something too.

## On the expert’s assessment
The expert’s conclusion is useful, and I take it seriously:
- a no-closure package is **possible in principle**
- but only if the company extracts enough savings from overhead, utilization, working-time redesign, and labor flexibility
- and if the residual gap is not too large

That is exactly why we should not rush to closures before the model is complete.

However, I also draw the line where the expert does: if management wants closure-level savings, then it must prove that non-closure measures cannot deliver them. That proof has not been given.

## What labor can discuss
I’ll be specific.

### 1) Capacity reduction
Yes, we can discuss capacity reduction through:
- shift changes,
- output reallocation,
- internal consolidation,
- and better utilization.

But not through plant closures.

### 2) Labor contribution
We remain open to a **temporary and fair employee contribution**, provided it is:
- time-limited,
- transparent,
- shared,
- and tied to binding guarantees on employment, sites, and future work.

No guarantees, no contribution.

### 3) Savings sources
I support taking out costs in the places where they belong:
- central overhead,
- management layers,
- duplicated functions,
- procurement inefficiencies,
- logistics waste,
- and premium labor costs from poor scheduling.

If management insists on asking workers for sacrifices, then it must first show that it is cutting its own costs too.

### 4) Job and site security
The minimum remains:
- all German plants kept open
- no compulsory redundancies
- protection of the collective wage framework
- binding employment and site guarantees through 2030

That is not an opening bid. It is the condition for a serious settlement.

## What I reject
I reject three things outright:

1. **Plant closures as the default restructuring tool**
2. **Compulsory redundancies as a normal adjustment mechanism**
3. **A “temporary” labor contribution that becomes a permanent wage cut or an open-ended insecurity regime**

If those are the direction of travel, then there is no fair settlement on the table.
