I’ll be equally direct.

## To the Works Council
I agree with your core red lines: **no plant closures, no compulsory redundancies, no dismantling of the collective bargaining framework**. Those are not symbolic demands; they are the minimum conditions for any settlement that the workforce can accept.

I also agree that workers should not be treated as the company’s shock absorber for strategic mistakes made elsewhere. If management wants a restructuring, then it must be **shared, negotiated, and reversible where possible**, not imposed through fear.

Where I would add one nuance is this: a temporary employee contribution can only be justified if it is genuinely tied to:
- binding job and site security,
- a credible industrial plan,
- and measurable commitments from management and ownership.

If those conditions are not there, then there is no basis for asking members to give up income.

## To management
I disagree with your framing that closures and forced redundancies are the natural “structural correction” if the numbers are difficult. That is not a neutral economic fact; it is a choice about who pays.

You keep saying the cost base is too high. Fine. But **the burden cannot fall on employees alone**. If you want structural savings, then show us a package that includes:
- management-side restraint,
- ownership contribution,
- internal efficiency measures,
- working-time and capacity adjustments,
- and investment that secures future volume.

What I will not accept is a model where:
- workers lose jobs and income,
- plants are shut,
- and management presents that as the only serious option.

If that remains your line, then we are not negotiating a fair restructuring; we are negotiating surrender. And that will not happen.

## To the state
We appreciate that you are defending regional stability and co-determination. That matters. But I want to be clear: **“no closures as a first resort” is too weak** as a basis for labor to make concessions.

If the state wants labor to take responsibility, then it should also use its influence to insist on:
- no compulsory redundancies,
- no closures,
- binding employment guarantees,
- and a serious industrial plan with future products and investment.

Anything less simply shifts pressure onto the workforce while keeping the deeper problem unresolved.

## To the family holding and investors
I hear your concern about competitiveness and enterprise value. We share the concern that the company must survive and remain viable. But a “value-restoring” plan that destroys trust, triggers prolonged conflict, and tears up the collective wage framework is not value-restoring — it is destabilizing.

You say closures may be necessary if alternatives do not add up. My answer is: **then test the alternatives properly before declaring workers the problem**. There is still room for:
- capacity reductions without closures,
- working-time instruments,
- redeployment,
- qualification,
- voluntary exit paths,
- and a temporary labor contribution.

If you refuse to seriously explore those before reaching for closures, then you are not seeking the best solution; you are choosing the harshest one.

## On package design: what we can discuss
I’ll be specific.

### 1) Capacity and savings
We can discuss a package built on:
- reduced capacity,
- internal redistribution of production,
- shift and utilization changes,
- efficiency gains,
- retraining and redeployment,
- and voluntary, non-coercive workforce adjustments.

But I will not accept “savings” that depend on plant closures or compulsory redundancies.

### 2) Employee contribution
We are prepared to discuss a **temporary, targeted contribution** from employees — but only if:
- it is time-limited,
- it applies in a fair and transparent way,
- management and owners contribute as well,
- and it is locked to binding guarantees on jobs, sites, and future work.

No guarantee, no contribution. That is the basis.

### 3) Guarantees
The workforce needs more than vague promises. We require:
- protection of German sites,
- no forced redundancies,
- preservation of the collective agreement framework,
- and a durable settlement horizon, not a short pause before the next round.

### 4) Investment and future work
If management wants flexibility, then it must commit to:
- product allocation,
- investment in future technologies,
- qualification,
- and long-term work for the German plants.

Without that, any sacrifice asked of workers is simply a one-way transfer of pain.

## My bottom line
Let me state our position plainly:

- **We will not accept plant closures in Germany.**
- **We will not accept compulsory redundancies.**
- **We will not accept the workforce bearing the restructuring alone.**
- **We are open to a fair, time-limited contribution if and only if it is matched by binding guarantees and shared sacrifice.**

So the burden of proof is not on the workforce to prove that it can survive concessions. The burden of proof is on management and owners to prove that they are willing to make a fair contribution and to secure jobs, sites, and future work.

If they can do that, then there is a path to settlement. If not, then the union will defend its members with all the leverage it has.
