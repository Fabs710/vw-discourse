I hear the message from both the Works Council and the union: you want to protect sites, avoid compulsory redundancies, and keep the industrial base in Germany intact. I understand why that is your starting point, and I agree on one point: **a purely short-term, cosmetic restructuring would be a mistake**. We need a durable industrial plan, not a delay tactic.

But I have to be equally clear: **the company’s current cost and capacity structure is not competitive enough to survive as it is**. German factory costs are too high, the European market is shrinking, and we are under pressure from lower-cost and Chinese competitors. If we do not structurally reduce the cost base, we will not reach the brand’s return-on-sales target, and we will not free the resources needed for the next product cycle and the technology investments to 2030.

So where I agree:
- We need a **credible long-term plan**.
- We need **investment in future products and technology**.
- We need **solutions that are sustainable**, not symbolic.

Where I strongly disagree:
- **A blanket “no plant closures” position is not realistic** if the numbers do not work.
- **A blanket “no compulsory redundancies” position is not enough** unless it is paired with equivalent structural savings.
- **Binding site guarantees through 2030** are not acceptable if they freeze an uncompetitive structure and make the turnaround impossible.

I am not raising closures as a rhetorical threat. I am saying plainly that **all options remain on the table** because the business requires a structural correction. If we can achieve the same durable effect through alternative measures — capacity reductions, working-time changes, meaningful labor-cost contributions, and sustained efficiency gains — then I am prepared to consider that. But it must add up to real, permanent savings at the scale the company needs.

That means I need answers on three points:

1. **Where exactly do the structural cost reductions come from?**  
   Not temporary savings. Not one-off sacrifices. Real, recurring improvements.

2. **What level of labor-cost contribution are you prepared to accept?**  
   If workers want to avoid closures, there has to be a genuine contribution to competitiveness.

3. **How do we reduce capacity in a way that is actually sufficient?**  
   We cannot preserve every site and every line if that leaves us with overcapacity and persistent losses.

I also want to be frank about the environment here: prolonged strikes would damage output, reputation, and confidence, and that helps nobody. But equally, a deal that merely postpones the problem would be worse. I need a settlement that the board, the owners, and investors can recognize as **structural and credible**.

So my position is this:  
- I am open to a negotiated solution.  
- I am **not** open to preserving the status quo under a different label.  
- And I will not accept a package that fails to reduce cost and capacity in a durable way.

If labor wants to avoid plant closures, then bring me a package that replaces them with **equivalent structural savings**. If not, we will have to proceed on the basis that the company’s future competitiveness comes first.
