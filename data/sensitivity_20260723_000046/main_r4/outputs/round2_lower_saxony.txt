I will be equally direct.

From the state’s perspective, I agree with management on one point: **this company needs a durable restructuring, not a cosmetic one**. And I agree with the family and investors that the current cost structure cannot simply be carried forward unchanged.

But I **do not** accept the conclusion that plant closures and compulsory redundancies are the inevitable answer.

## To management
You keep saying the alternatives must be “equivalent” in recurring euros. That is a fair requirement in principle. But you have not yet shown that closures are the only way to achieve it, and that matters. In a company governed by co-determination and with a special statutory shareholder structure, you cannot treat closures as the default and expect assent.

If you want the state’s support, then bring a package that is:
- quantitatively credible,
- operationally executable,
- and socially sustainable.

That means:
- real capacity reduction,
- yes;
- productivity and working-time changes,
- yes;
- voluntary exits and redeployment,
- yes;
- temporary employee contribution,
- possibly yes;
- but **not** a plan whose first material lever is closing German plants.

I am also not persuaded by the implication that binding guarantees through 2030 are somehow unreasonable by definition. If the workforce is being asked to contribute, then it is entirely legitimate to demand **binding job and site protection in return**. That is not freezing the future; it is creating the trust needed for a negotiated turnaround.

## To the Works Council and the union
I support your core aim: **no German plant closures, no compulsory redundancies, no dismantling of co-determination**. On that, the state stands with the workforce.

Where I would sharpen the discussion is this: simply repeating “no closures” is not enough to win the next stage. If we want to block closures credibly, we must be able to support an alternative that management cannot dismiss as vague. So we need more precision:
- which capacity can be removed without closing sites?
- what working-time instruments are realistic?
- what voluntary measures can deliver volume adjustment?
- what productivity gains can be secured through qualification and new allocation of work?
- what temporary contribution is acceptable, and under what guarantees?

That is the path to keeping the company anchored here while still making the economics work.

## To the family holding and investors
I understand your insistence on structural savings and credibility. But I have to say plainly: **a plan that creates a prolonged governance conflict, destroys trust, and weakens the industrial base is not value-preserving**. It may look decisive on paper; in reality it can become an expensive stalemate.

You are right that the company needs competitiveness. You are wrong if you assume that competitiveness can be restored by treating regional employment as a disposable variable. The company’s license to operate here is part of its long-term value.

## The state’s line on the package
Here is where we stand:

1. **No plant closures in Germany as the chosen solution.**
2. **No compulsory redundancies.**
3. **A real capacity and productivity adjustment is necessary.**
4. **A temporary, fair employee contribution can be discussed, but only with binding guarantees.**
5. **Management, ownership, and labor must all contribute.**
6. **Investment and future product commitments must be part of the deal.**

If management wants support, it needs to stop framing this as “closures or failure.” That is not the only serious option. It is one option — and from the state’s perspective, not the preferred one.

## What would move us
We could support a negotiated restructuring if it includes:
- preservation of German sites,
- no forced redundancies,
- measurable capacity reduction through internal restructuring,
- a time-limited employee contribution,
- management-side and ownership-side commitments,
- and binding investment for future products and technologies.

What will **not** move us is a package that uses the threat of closures to extract concessions while leaving the regional industrial base weakened.

So my message is simple:  
If you want a settlement, bring one that protects the company’s competitiveness **and** its role as a regional anchor. If you cannot do that, then you should not expect the state to facilitate plant closures in Germany.
