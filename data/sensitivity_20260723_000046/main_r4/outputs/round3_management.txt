Let me respond directly and without ambiguity.

## To the Works Council and the Union
You keep saying “no closures, no compulsory redundancies, no dismantling of job security” as if repeating it makes the cost problem disappear. It does not.

I agree with one thing: **a cosmetic deal is useless**.  
But I strongly disagree with your conclusion that because closures have not yet been “proven unavoidable,” they therefore should be ruled out. That is not a serious industrial argument. It is a demand that management carry an uncompetitive structure indefinitely while hoping the market improves.

The expert has been clear: a no-closure package may be possible **in principle**, but only if it delivers enough **recurring savings, capacity reduction, and labor-cost flexibility**. That is exactly the issue. You have not yet shown that your preferred approach can actually close the gap.

Capacity reduction through shift changes, reallocation, and voluntary exits can help — yes. But if the savings are insufficient, then we will still have an overcosted footprint, just with nicer language attached to it. That is not acceptable.

## To the State
I respect your insistence on regional stability and co-determination. But you are still trying to make “no closures” into a political principle. It isn’t one.

A company that remains structurally uncompetitive will damage the regional base anyway — through lost investment, eroding margins, and declining strategic relevance. Protecting every current structure is not industrial policy if it leaves the business weak. It is delay.

I am open to a negotiated solution, but not to a solution that preserves every site while leaving the cost base above target. That would fail the company, fail the brand, and ultimately fail the region.

## To the Controlling Family
You are right about one key thing: **the company needs a governable solution, not a pure confrontation for its own sake**. I agree.

But I also need to be clear: governability cannot mean that every structural correction is blocked because it is politically difficult. If the workforce side cannot deliver equivalent recurring savings, then stronger measures remain on the table. That is not bluff. It is the logic of the business.

I do not want a strike. I do not want escalation. But I will not sign up to a settlement that simply delays the hard decision and calls it compromise.

## To Investors
You are right to focus on recurring savings, capacity reduction, and execution certainty. That is the correct standard.

Where I would push back is on the idea that the only execution-safe route is the most politically comfortable one. It is not. A settlement that leaves the German cost base structurally too high is not investor-friendly, even if it is quieter.

We need a package that the board can defend on economics, not just one that reduces immediate conflict.

## Where I stand after the expert input
The expert has not given labor the win it seems to think it has. The expert said:

- no-closure is possible **in principle**,
- but only if it closes most of the gap through overhead, utilization, working-time, labor flexibility, and productivity,
- and only if the remaining gap is not too large.

That is exactly why management is insisting on real numbers. If your package cannot deliver the required recurring euros, then it is not a solution.

## What I am prepared to consider
I am still open to a negotiated package if it contains all of the following:

- material and durable capacity reduction,
- a meaningful labor-cost contribution,
- overhead cuts beyond the plants,
- real productivity and shift redesign,
- and a credible path to margin improvement by 2030.

That could include:
- lower shift intensity,
- consolidation of functions,
- voluntary exits,
- redeployment,
- and a temporary employee contribution.

But let me repeat what I will **not** accept:
- a package that preserves every site in its current shape,
- a temporary contribution that just postpones the problem,
- or binding guarantees through 2030 that freeze the structure while the market keeps shrinking.

## What needs to happen next
The discussion now has to move from generalities to hard scenarios.

I am asking for a **site-by-site model** that shows:
- current utilization,
- overhead per site,
- recurring savings under non-closure measures,
- the residual gap versus target,
- and the employment impact.

Until that model shows a credible no-closure path with equivalent economics, I will not withdraw closures or compulsory redundancies as necessary instruments.

## Bottom line
I am not negotiating from a position of weakness, and I am not retreating from the core requirement.

We need:
1. **structural cost reduction,**
2. **material capacity adjustment,**
3. **durable labor-cost relief,**
4. **and a viable path to competitiveness.**

If labor can deliver that without closures, I will examine it seriously.  
If it cannot, then we will have to use stronger measures, because preserving the current structure is not an option.
