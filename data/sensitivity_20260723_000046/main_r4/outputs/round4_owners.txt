I’ll be direct.

## My position
I support a **durable restructuring that restores competitiveness and margins**. I do **not** support a settlement that simply preserves the current cost structure and calls it compromise.

At the same time, I do **not** want a value-destructive confrontation that burns cash, destroys industrial peace, and weakens the company’s governability.

So my test is simple:
- **Does the package materially and durably improve the economics?**
- **Is it executable under the actual governance constraints?**
- **Does it avoid permanent damage to enterprise value?**

If the answer is no, I will not back it.

---

## To the works council
You are right on one point: a cosmetic solution is worthless.  
But I strongly disagree with your central line that **no closures, no compulsory redundancies, and binding guarantees through 2030** can be treated as a starting floor.

That is not restructuring. That is freezing the problem.

You keep saying the expert has shown closures are “not proven unavoidable.” Fine. But “not yet proven unavoidable” is not the same as “economically unnecessary.” The burden is not only on management to prove closure first; the burden is also on labor to show a package that actually closes the recurring savings gap.

I also reject the way you frame “equivalent savings” as if it were a trick. It is not a trick. It is the correct question. If non-closure measures cannot deliver the required structural cost reduction, then preserving every site becomes an argument for weaker competitiveness, not for stewardship.

### Where I agree
I agree that the first savings should come from:
- overhead reduction,
- management layers,
- duplicated functions,
- procurement and logistics waste,
- productivity gains,
- and better utilization.

I also agree that voluntary exits, retraining, and redeployment should be used aggressively before harder measures.

### Where I disagree
I do **not** accept:
- an absolute no-closure principle,
- a no-compulsory-redundancy principle as a permanent veto,
- or a 2030 guarantee that locks in an uncompetitive structure.

If your package can really deliver comparable recurring savings, then show it. If it cannot, then it is not a solution.

---

## To the union
You are right to insist that workers should not bear the burden alone.  
But you are wrong if you think “shared sacrifice” means the owners and management should concede the economics while the workforce keeps every structural entitlement untouched.

A temporary labor contribution may be part of a deal, but only if it is:
- meaningful,
- durable enough to matter,
- and not paired with a permanent freeze on restructuring.

I will not support a deal where the workforce contributes a little, management trims some overhead, and the company still carries structurally too much fixed cost. That would merely postpone the next crisis.

---

## To management
I support your insistence on hard numbers and execution certainty. That is the right discipline.

But I want to challenge one thing: do not treat closures as a default public posture if the real objective is a negotiable, governable outcome. In this structure, an over-aggressive stance can backfire and destroy the very value you are trying to protect.

You need to be able to defend the package on three grounds:
1. recurring savings,
2. capacity adjustment,
3. implementation risk.

If the best-risked outcome is a non-closure package, then pursue it.  
If it is not, then say so clearly and quantify the residual gap.

What I will not support is a vague threat of closures used as leverage without a verified gap analysis.

---

## To the state
I appreciate that you are focusing on regional stability and co-determination. That is appropriate.

I agree that closures should **not** be the first resort. But I do **not** agree that every German site must remain open as a principle regardless of economics. The company’s long-term stability is part of regional stability. If the business remains structurally weak, the region loses over time anyway.

So I can support your approach only if it is tied to:
- real capacity reduction,
- real overhead cuts,
- a credible labor contribution,
- and a demonstrable path to margin repair.

I will not support a political solution that protects headlines but not the company.

---

## To investors
You are right to focus on durable economics.  
I agree with you that a cosmetic settlement would be value-destructive.

Where I part company with you is this: a closure-based solution is not automatically superior if it creates major execution risk, labor conflict, and reputational damage. In this governance setup, a theoretically clean solution that cannot be implemented is not the right answer.

So yes, I want the numbers.  
But I want them to compare:
- recurring savings,
- one-time costs,
- execution certainty,
- and the risk of prolonged disruption.

That is what matters for long-term value.

---

## What I support next
The expert has already pointed to the correct next step: a **site-by-site model**.

That model must show, for each German site:
- utilization,
- fixed and variable cost,
- overhead burden,
- recurring savings from non-closure measures,
- one-time restructuring costs,
- employment impact,
- and residual gap to target.

And it must compare:
1. no closures / no compulsory redundancies,
2. selective consolidation,
3. hybrid restructuring.

[SUMMON: independent site-by-site restructuring analyst jointly acceptable to shareholder, management, and labor to validate utilization, overhead, capacity, savings, and employment assumptions]

---

## My bottom line
I am **not** backing a deal that:
- preserves every site in its current form,
- freezes the labor structure through 2030,
- or leaves the company uncompetitive.

I am also **not** backing a reckless showdown.

I will back the package that restores competitiveness, protects long-term enterprise value, and is actually executable. If labor can deliver that without closures, I will examine it seriously. If not, then stronger measures remain on the table.
