From the perspective of dispersed capital-market shareholders, the key question is not who wins the political argument; it is whether the company can get back to **competitive, risk-adjusted returns** without destroying execution certainty.

## Where I agree

I agree with management, and also with the family holding, on the central economic point: **the current cost base is too high and the status quo is not investable**. If German operations remain structurally uncompetitive, margins will stay under pressure, the return-on-sales target will remain out of reach, and the equity story will continue to weaken. That is not acceptable.

I also agree with the state and labor on one important issue: **a prolonged, destructive confrontation would be value-destructive**. Strikes, uncertainty, and reputational damage are not a substitute for restructuring. If this turns into a drawn-out industrial conflict, shareholders lose through missed production, higher costs, and delayed recovery.

## Where I disagree

I disagree with the Works Council and union on the blanket refusal of closures and compulsory redundancies. I understand the social and political logic, but from a shareholder perspective those are **inputs**, not sacred lines. If the business case requires genuine structural downsizing, then refusing every meaningful adjustment simply preserves an overcosted asset base. That is not protection; it is erosion.

I also disagree with any framing that treats **site and job guarantees through 2030** as acceptable if they freeze an uncompetitive structure. Guarantees are only valuable if they are paired with real competitiveness gains. Otherwise they merely defer the problem and make it bigger later.

At the same time, I do **not** support management if “all options on the table” becomes shorthand for a blunt closure strategy that provokes years of conflict and still does not deliver execution certainty. A plan that is theoretically strong but practically ungovernable is not a shareholder-friendly plan.

## What would move us

We would support a restructuring if it is clearly built around:

- **durable, recurring cost reductions**
- **credible capacity reduction**
- **a measurable path back to competitive margins**
- **limited industrial disruption**
- **deal certainty with enforceable commitments**

That means:
- no cosmetic savings,
- no one-off accounting fixes,
- no delayed decision disguised as compromise,
- and no package that leaves the cost base structurally impaired.

## On the current positions

### To management
Bring a package that can be defended on numbers and executed under this governance structure. If you cannot secure a durable cost reset without a prolonged fight, then the proposal is not yet good enough.

### To labor
If you want to avoid closures and layoffs, then the alternative must still add up economically. That requires a meaningful contribution on labor cost, flexibility, and capacity. Refusing every hard trade-off is not a solution shareholders can back.

### To the state
We appreciate the focus on regional stability, but regional protection cannot come at the expense of long-term enterprise value. If the compromise leaves the company undercompetitive, then the state is not protecting the industrial base; it is delaying its deterioration.

### To the controlling family
We agree that governance must deliver competitiveness. But “stronger measures” are only acceptable if they are actually executable and do not create permanent value destruction through conflict. Shareholders will not reward victory in a confrontation if the business itself is weakened.

## Our bottom line

We are open to supporting a negotiated settlement, but only if it produces:

1. **real structural savings**
2. **durable margin improvement**
3. **credible capacity adjustment**
4. **industrial peace with limited duration**
5. **a clear investment and product path**

What we will not support is:
- a cosmetic settlement,
- a prolonged strike-driven standoff,
- or a deal that preserves every site while leaving the company’s cost base uncompetitive.

If the outcome does not restore competitiveness, we will not regard it as a success, regardless of how politically comfortable it may seem.
