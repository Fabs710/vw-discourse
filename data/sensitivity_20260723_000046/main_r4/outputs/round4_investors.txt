From the capital markets’ perspective, the discussion is now finally focused on the right issue: **whether the restructuring will deliver durable, recurring savings with credible execution, or whether it will end in a politically comfortable but financially weak compromise.** We will not support the latter.

## Where we agree

### With management
We agree that:
- the current German cost base is too high,
- overcapacity is real,
- and a cosmetic settlement would destroy value.

We also agree that the company needs a **credible path back to competitive margins** and better return on sales. That is the standard. Not symbolism, not conflict avoidance.

### With the expert
The expert’s assessment is the right frame:
- a **no-closure package is possible in principle**,
- but only if it extracts enough recurring savings from overhead, utilization, productivity, shift redesign, labor flexibility, and volume reallocation,
- and only if the residual gap is not too large.

That is exactly the test we need.

### With the family holding
We agree that the company must remain **governable** and that a prolonged confrontation would damage enterprise value. A strike-driven or stalemate outcome would be bad for shareholders, bad for investment, and bad for the company’s strategic position.

### With the state
We agree that regional stability matters. But stability only matters if the company remains economically viable. A weak business eventually harms the region anyway.

---

## Where we disagree

### With the works council and union
Your red lines are understandable as bargaining positions, but as a final outcome they are **not acceptable** to capital markets:

- no plant closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- binding guarantees through 2030,
- preservation of the current structure.

That is not yet a restructuring; it is a request to freeze the problem and let shareholders carry the cost of low competitiveness.

I agree that closures are **not a goal in themselves**. But they also cannot be ruled out in advance if non-closure measures fail to close the recurring savings gap. If they cannot deliver the economics, then refusing closures becomes a veto on competitiveness.

I also reject the idea that management must prove closures first before labor engages seriously. The burden is shared: if you want to avoid closures, you need to show a package that is economically strong enough, not just socially preferred.

### With management
At the same time, I do **not** give management a blank check to declare closures “inevitable” without evidence.

That is not good enough for shareholders either.

We need:
- site-by-site utilization,
- recurring savings by scenario,
- one-time restructuring costs,
- employment impact,
- and the margin effect through 2030.

If management cannot demonstrate that a closure-based scenario delivers clearly superior risk-adjusted value, then it should not assume the harshest option is automatically the best one.

### With the state and labor on guarantees
We do not support indefinite guarantees that lock in an uncompetitive structure. A guarantee can be part of a deal, but only if it is matched by real, durable savings. Otherwise it just defers the next crisis.

## What would move us

We could support a settlement if it contains all of the following:

1. **Material recurring savings**
   - not one-offs,
   - not accounting effects,
   - not symbolic cuts.

2. **Real capacity reduction**
   - enough to address overcapacity,
   - not just a marginal trim.

3. **Meaningful overhead reduction**
   - management, administration, engineering duplication, and central functions must also come down.

4. **A substantial labor contribution**
   - temporary if needed, but large enough to matter for the cost base.

5. **Execution certainty**
   - a package that the board can actually pass and that survives the governance constraints.

6. **A real investment path**
   - savings without future product allocation just shrink the business.

## What we will not support

We will not support:
- a cosmetic settlement,
- a deal that leaves German factory costs structurally uncompetitive,
- a prolonged strike that destroys cash and confidence,
- or a “compromise” that simply postpones the hard decision.

We are also not prepared to back an outcome that leaves the cost base structurally impaired just because it avoids immediate conflict.

## Our position on closures and redundancies
We do **not** support closures as a first resort.  
But we also do **not** accept a blanket no-closure principle if it prevents the company from reaching competitive margins.

The same applies to compulsory redundancies: not our preferred route, but not automatically excluded if alternatives fail to deliver the required durable savings.

That is the hard truth. Shareholders care about the end result: margins, cash flow, and valuation.
