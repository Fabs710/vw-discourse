I will be equally direct.

## To management
You are still trying to shift the burden of your restructuring failure onto the workforce. That is the core problem here.

I agree with you on one point: **the company needs durable recurring savings**. Cosmetic measures are not enough. But I disagree with the way you keep defining the options. You present closures and compulsory redundancies as if they were the natural baseline unless labor proves a perfect alternative.

That is not a serious starting point. It is pressure politics.

The expert has said, again, that a **credible no-closure package is possible in principle**. That matters. It means closures are not “inevitable”; they are one option among others. If you want to argue they are necessary, then prove that the internal and negotiated measures are exhausted. So far, you have not.

And I will be blunt: if your plan depends on workers accepting job destruction while management and ownership preserve their own comfort, then it is not a restructuring. It is a transfer of pain downward.

## To the works council
I agree with your central line:
- **no plant closures**
- **no compulsory redundancies**
- **no dismantling of the collective bargaining framework**

You are right to insist that job security cannot be traded away as a convenience item. And you are right that the model must test alternatives, not manufacture a pretext for shutdowns.

Where I would sharpen your argument is this: we should not let management turn “equivalent savings” into a trap that requires labor to prove a perfect mirror image of a closure scenario before any no-closure option is considered. That is too high a bar and, frankly, a biased one.

The proper test is whether a negotiated package can:
- remove a large enough share of recurring costs,
- preserve all German sites,
- avoid forced redundancies,
- and remain operationally credible.

That is the right standard. Not “closure-equivalence at any social cost.”

## To the state
I appreciate that you are at least trying to balance competitiveness with regional stability. That is better than the pure cost-cutting logic from management and investors.

But I need to be firm: **capacity reduction does not mean closures**. Real restructuring can be done through:
- shift reductions,
- model reallocation,
- line consolidation within existing sites,
- productivity improvements,
- redeployment,
- voluntary exits,
- and temporary working-time measures.

If the state wants to defend the region, it should defend **all German sites remaining open**. Not pick which one gets sacrificed first under the label of “industrial realism.”

## To the controlling family and investors
You both keep saying the same thing in different language: the package must be “economically equivalent.” I understand that concern. But you are using it in a way that still privileges closure as the default benchmark.

I reject that. Why? Because a closure-based solution may look clean on a spreadsheet, but it can create durable damage through lost trust, weaker industrial peace, and a demoralized workforce. Those are real costs too.

Yes, the company needs recurring savings. No, those savings do not have to come from closing plants or forcing people out.

If you want a settlement that is value-preserving, then it must be **shared sacrifice**:
- management contributes,
- owners contribute,
- labor contributes,
- and the company commits to future investment and product allocation.

If only labor is expected to absorb the pain, then the deal is not fair, and it will not be durable.

## On the expert assessment
The expert has been useful. The key takeaway is simple:

- A **no-closure package is possible in principle**.
- It becomes harder if the savings gap is extremely large.
- The right question is how much can be removed through non-closure measures before anyone reaches for the hardest lever.

That is exactly why I refuse to accept closures as a foregone conclusion. The model is not a verdict. It is a test.

And let me say this clearly: **management also has a burden of proof**. It must show:
1. what internal overhead cuts are being made,
2. what volume and shift changes are being implemented,
3. what productivity measures are realistic,
4. and why those steps are insufficient.

Until that is shown, closures remain an overreach.

## What labor can discuss
I will be concrete.

### 1) Capacity reduction
Yes, we can discuss capacity reduction through:
- shift adjustments,
- volume reallocation,
- line consolidation,
- and better utilization.

But **not** through plant closures.

### 2) Workforce contribution
Yes, we can discuss a **temporary employee contribution**, but only if it is:
- time-limited,
- transparent,
- fair,
- and matched by binding guarantees on employment and sites.

No guarantees, no contribution.

### 3) Cost cuts outside production
Yes, take out overhead where it belongs:
- management layers,
- duplicated engineering,
- central functions,
- procurement inefficiencies,
- logistics waste,
- and unnecessary premium costs from poor scheduling.

The burden must not sit only on the shop floor.

### 4) Job and site security
The minimum remains:
- every German plant stays open,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- preservation of the collective wage framework,
- binding employment and site guarantees through 2030.

That is not negotiable downward.

## What I reject outright
I reject three things:
1. **Plant closures as the default restructuring tool**
2. **Compulsory redundancies as a normal adjustment mechanism**
3. **A temporary labor contribution that becomes a permanent wage cut or permanent insecurity**

If that is the direction, there is no fair settlement.

## Where this leaves us
We are prepared to negotiate a serious restructuring. We are **not** prepared to negotiate job destruction as if it were the only rational answer.

So the next step must be the site-by-site model. Not as a weapon, but as a test:
- utilization,
- overhead,
- recurring savings,
- one-time costs,
- employment impact,
- residual gap after non-closure measures.

And I want it assessed with union and works-council scrutiny, not just managerial assumptions.
