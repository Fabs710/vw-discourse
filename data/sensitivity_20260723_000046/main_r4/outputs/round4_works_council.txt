I will be equally direct in round 4.

## First: what has changed
The discussion has correctly moved onto the **independent site-by-site model**. That is useful. But let me be clear: **a model is not a mandate for closures**. It is a tool to test alternatives, and I will judge it by whether it seriously evaluates a no-closure path, not whether it is being used to manufacture consent for plant shutdowns.

## To management
You keep insisting that closures and compulsory redundancies remain on the table unless the workforce proves an “equivalent” package. I reject that framing.

Why?

Because it still treats job destruction as the default solution and forces the workforce to justify why its sites should live. That is backwards.

If management wants us to consider sacrifices, then management must first show:
- what has already been taken out at headquarters,
- what management layers are being cut,
- how much duplicated engineering and administration is being removed,
- what volume is being reallocated between existing sites,
- and why the remaining gap cannot be closed without destroying plants.

So far, I have heard lots of pressure and very little proof.

The expert has said clearly that a **credible no-closure package is possible in principle**. That is the key point. Not “unlikely.” Not “impossible.” Possible. That means closures are **not** a foregone conclusion. They are a choice.

And I want to state this plainly:  
**I do not accept plant closures in Germany as a restructuring instrument.**

## To the controlling family and investors
You are both trying to convert “durable savings” into a justification for breaking the social compact. I understand the financial logic you are applying, but I do not accept it as sufficient.

Yes, the company needs savings. Yes, it needs competitiveness. But not every cost gap can or should be solved by closing plants and threatening layoffs. If you destroy the long-standing job-security framework, you will also destroy trust, industrial peace, and the willingness of the workforce to carry any future transition.

That is not a side effect. It is the price you pay.

And I do not accept the claim that a no-closure solution must be “economically equivalent” to a closure scenario in every respect before it is considered legitimate. That is a false standard. The real question is whether the company can remain viable and competitive **without** closures and forced layoffs while sharing the burden fairly.

The expert has shown that this is at least plausible. That is enough to keep closures off the table, not to put them back on.

## To the state
I appreciate your position more than the others because you are at least arguing from industrial stability rather than pure cost cutting. But I must also be firm with you: “real capacity reduction” does **not** mean plant closures, and it does **not** mean opening the door to compulsory redundancies.

Capacity reduction can and must happen through:
- shift reductions,
- model reallocation,
- line consolidation within existing sites,
- working-time flexibility,
- voluntary exits,
- training and redeployment.

That is a real restructuring. It just does not sacrifice sites.

If the state wants to defend the region, it should defend **all German sites remaining open**, not negotiate which one gets abandoned first.

## To the union
We are aligned on the core red lines:
- no plant closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- no dismantling of the collective bargaining and job-security framework.

I also agree with your warning that a “temporary” labor contribution can easily become a permanent cut if not tightly bounded. That is exactly why any contribution must be tied to binding guarantees.

Where I would sharpen the line is this: we should not let management turn the no-closure discussion into a trap where labor is asked to concede first and prove later. No. The first obligation lies with management and shareholders to cut cost where they have the most control: central overhead, duplication, inefficient scheduling, and strategic waste.

## On the expert’s assessment
The expert has been genuinely useful, and I want to hold everyone to what was actually said:

- A no-closure package can be credible.
- It becomes harder only if the required savings gap is extremely large.
- The right question is the size of the gap and how much can be removed through non-closure measures.

That is not a green light for closures. It is a warning against pretending that closures are the only serious option before the numbers have been tested.

And let me say this plainly: **the burden of proof is not only on labor.**  
Management must also prove that:
1. all internal savings have been exhausted,
2. all overhead cuts are truly substantial,
3. all reallocation options have been used,
4. and no-closure measures cannot do the job.

Until that is shown, closures remain an unacceptable overreach.

## What I will support
I will support a serious model and a serious restructuring. But only within these boundaries:

### 1) Every German site stays open
That is the floor. Not a bargaining chip.

### 2) No compulsory redundancies
Workforce adjustment must come through:
- natural attrition,
- retirement corridors,
- voluntary exits,
- retraining,
- redeployment,
- and internal reorganization.

### 3) Real capacity reduction without closures
Yes to lower shifts, lower premiums, volume reallocation, and narrower site roles — but not to shutdowns.

### 4) Strong co-determination
The works council must be involved in shaping any restructuring, not just informed after decisions are made.

### 5) Binding site and employment guarantees through 2030
If workers are expected to contribute, then guarantees are not optional. They are the condition for any contribution.

### 6) Management and owner contributions
If labor is asked for restraint, then management and owners must also contribute through:
- central cost cuts,
- governance restraint,
- and investment commitments.

## What I reject outright
I reject the following:
- closures presented as “the only rational path” before the model is complete,
- compulsory redundancies as a normal adjustment tool,
- permanent insecurity disguised as restructuring,
- a temporary labor contribution without binding guarantees,
- and any attempt to use the model to pre-justify a fait accompli.

## On the next step
The model must not be a black box. It must show, for each German site:
- utilization,
- overhead,
- recurring savings,
- employment impact,
- one-time costs,
- and the residual gap after non-closure measures.

And it must compare:
1. no closures / no compulsory redundancies,
2. selective consolidation,
3. hybrid package.

But I repeat: I am **not** starting from the assumption that closures are acceptable if the numbers are difficult. They are not acceptable.
