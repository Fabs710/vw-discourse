I’ll be equally direct.

## My position
I **do not support plant closures in Germany as the restructuring instrument**, and I **do not support compulsory redundancies** as the default answer.  
That remains my clear position after this round.

I do, however, accept that the company needs:
- durable recurring savings,
- real capacity adjustment,
- and a credible path back to competitiveness.

So I am **not** defending the status quo. But I will not trade away jobs, sites, and regional industrial stability for a supposedly cleaner solution on paper.

---

## To management
I agree with you on the diagnosis:
- the cost base is too high,
- the business needs structural savings,
- and a cosmetic compromise would be unacceptable.

Where I disagree is your continued use of closures and forced redundancies as the implied baseline if labor does not “prove” equivalence.

That is too one-sided.

You keep asking for hard numbers, and that is legitimate. But the same demand applies to you:
- show what has already been removed from central overhead,
- show what management layers are being cut,
- show what duplication in engineering and administration is being eliminated,
- show what volume can be shifted between existing sites,
- and show why these measures, combined with productivity gains and voluntary tools, are insufficient.

Until that is done, closures remain an unproven choice, not a necessity.

And I will say this plainly: if the solution damages trust, industrial peace, and the company’s role as a regional anchor, then it may “save” something financially in the short term while destroying value in the longer term.

---

## To the works council and the union
I agree with your insistence that the workforce should not be made the sole shock absorber for restructuring.

I also agree that:
- no plant closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- and no dismantling of co-determination

are not luxuries. They are core conditions for a sustainable settlement.

Where I want to be precise is this: I support your line **because it is politically and industrially necessary**, not because I think the company can ignore economics. The company does need real adjustments. But those adjustments must come from:
- shift reductions,
- better capacity utilization,
- volume reallocation,
- voluntary exits,
- retraining and redeployment,
- and management-side overhead cuts.

That is the burden-sharing model I can defend.

I also support your call for union and works-council scrutiny of the model. That is exactly the right safeguard.

---

## To the controlling family
I understand your concern that a deal that merely preserves today’s structure would weaken the company. That concern is legitimate.

But I do not accept the implied conclusion that only closures can protect value.

There is a difference between:
- a managed reduction in capacity and cost, and
- abandoning sites and forcing redundancies.

If the company can get durable savings while preserving the German footprint and maintaining governability, that is the better long-term outcome. It protects not only jobs, but also the company’s social legitimacy and its ability to operate in Germany with workforce commitment intact.

So I am not following a “preserve everything at any cost” line. I am following a “restructure without breaking the company’s regional and social foundation” line.

---

## To investors
You are right to demand recurring savings and execution certainty.  
You are wrong if you believe that the only credible route is the harshest one before the alternatives are fully tested.

A settlement that keeps the company investable must also keep it socially workable. A conflict that detonates labor peace may look efficient on a spreadsheet, but it creates its own execution risk.

So yes: I accept the need for a hard economic test.  
No: I do not accept that the mere existence of a more disruptive option makes it preferable.

---

## Where I stand on the expert assessments
The expert is useful, and I take the core conclusion seriously:

- a no-closure package is **possible in principle**,
- but only if it removes enough recurring cost through overhead cuts, utilization changes, labor flexibility, and productivity gains,
- and if the residual gap is not too large.

That is exactly why I will not accept closures as a foregone conclusion.

The expert has **not** shown that closures are necessary.  
The expert has shown that the no-closure path must be quantified and disciplined.

That is a very different thing.

---

## What I am prepared to support
I can support a package if it contains all of the following:

### 1) Real capacity reduction without closure
That means:
- lower shift intensity,
- line rationalization within existing plants,
- model and volume reallocation,
- and tighter utilization.

Not shutdowns.

### 2) Significant overhead cuts
The first savings should come from:
- management layers,
- duplicated functions,
- central administration,
- procurement inefficiencies,
- and non-production waste.

### 3) Voluntary workforce adjustment
I am open to:
- retirement corridors,
- voluntary exits,
- redeployment,
- retraining,
- and hiring restraint.

### 4) A temporary and fair labor contribution
But only if it is:
- time-limited,
- shared,
- transparent,
- and tied to binding commitments on sites, employment, and future work.

### 5) Binding investment and product commitments
If workers are asked to contribute, then the company must commit to:
- future product allocation,
- EV and technology investment,
- and credible follow-on work for German sites.

---

## What I will not support
I will not support:
- plant closures in Germany as the chosen solution,
- compulsory redundancies as a normal restructuring tool,
- a settlement that makes German sites permanently insecure,
- or a “temporary” labor contribution that quietly becomes a permanent wage cut.

And I will also not support any arrangement that weakens the state’s co-determination and veto role under the special statute. That role is part of the governance balance that makes this company workable in the first place.

---

## What needs to happen next
We now need the model to do real work, not just provide cover for a preferred outcome.

I support the independent site-by-site model, but it must be rigorous and transparent. It should show, for each German site:
- utilization,
- fixed and variable cost,
- overhead burden,
- recurring savings under non-closure measures,
- one-time restructuring cost,
- employment impact,
- and residual gap to target.

And it must compare:
1. no closures / no compulsory redundancies,
2. selective consolidation,
3. hybrid negotiated restructuring.

## Bottom line
My position is unchanged, and it is not a reflexive “no” to restructuring.

It is this:
- **Yes** to durable savings.
- **Yes** to capacity adjustment.
- **Yes** to shared sacrifice.
- **Yes** to a serious independent model.
- **No** to plant closures as the default answer.
- **No** to compulsory redundancies.
- **No** to any settlement that guts regional employment or weakens co-determination.

If a negotiated package can deliver the savings while keeping the German sites open and preserving the company’s role as a regional anchor, I will work constructively for it.

If not, then I will use the leverage I have to block a solution that crosses those red lines.
