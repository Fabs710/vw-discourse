The key analytical point is now clear:

A **no-closure restructuring is possible in principle**, but it only works if the **residual savings gap** after all non-closure measures is small enough. The decisive issue is not whether closures are politically undesirable — they usually are — but whether keeping all German sites open still leaves the company with an uncompetitive fixed-cost base.

## What the site-by-site model must test
For each German site, you need:

- current output
- installed capacity
- utilization rate
- shift pattern
- fixed site costs
- direct labor cost
- overhead burden
- recurring savings from:
  - shift reductions
  - line consolidation
  - volume reallocation
  - management/administrative cuts
  - voluntary exits
  - procurement/logistics simplification
- one-time restructuring cost
- employment impact
- residual gap to target margin / ROS

## Practical interpretation
A no-closure package is credible if it can deliver:

- material recurring savings
- meaningful overhead reduction
- real capacity reduction through shifts and reallocation
- labor-cost relief that is durable enough to matter
- execution that survives co-determination and board constraints

If not, then the company likely needs **selective consolidation** because low-utilization sites tend to preserve too much fixed cost.

## What I would expect in a German OEM case
Typically:

- **non-closure measures can remove a meaningful share of the gap**
- but **not always the full structural burden** if one or more plants are underutilized
- closures become economically likely only if:
  - utilization stays structurally low,
  - volume cannot be reallocated,
  - and the residual gap remains too large after overhead and labor measures

## Bottom line
The burden of proof is now on the model:

- If non-closure measures close the gap, closures are not economically necessary.
- If they do not, then selective consolidation becomes the rational option.
