## Core analytical judgment

A **credible no-closure package is possible in principle**, but whether it is sufficient depends on the size of the recurring savings gap relative to the company’s fixed-cost burden and required margin improvement through 2030.

In practical automotive terms:

- If the problem is mainly **labor-cost excess, overhead duplication, and underutilized shifts**, then a negotiated package can often deliver a large share of the needed savings.
- If the problem is **structural overcapacity across multiple plants**, then keeping all German sites open usually leaves too much fixed cost in the system unless volume is sharply reallocated or the company accepts materially lower complexity, higher utilization, and more aggressive job reductions.
- The decisive question is not whether a no-closure package sounds acceptable, but whether it can **close the residual gap after all non-closure measures are fully loaded**.

## What the model must show

For each German site, the model should quantify:

1. **Current annual output and utilization**
   - installed capacity
   - actual volume
   - utilization rate
   - shift pattern

2. **Fixed cost burden**
   - plant overhead
   - maintenance and utilities
   - site administration
   - logistics and internal transport
   - depreciation if relevant to the decision case

3. **Variable cost / labor cost**
   - direct labor cost per vehicle
   - overtime and premium burden
   - cost of temporary labor or agency work if applicable

4. **Savings by measure**
   - shift reductions
   - line consolidation
   - headcount attrition / voluntary exits
   - management and overhead cuts
   - procurement / logistics simplification
   - model or volume reallocation

5. **One-time restructuring cost**
   - severance / social plan
   - retraining and redeployment
   - relocation and ramp-down cost
   - write-downs / idle asset costs
   - implementation cost

6. **Employment impact**
   - direct jobs
   - indirect jobs at the site
   - timing of reductions
   - voluntary vs. involuntary distinction

7. **Residual gap to target**
   - recurring savings achieved
   - savings still needed to reach target margin / ROS
   - sensitivity to volume downside

## Likely economic pattern

Without seeing the actual plant data, the most likely pattern in a German OEM of this scale is:

- **Some savings can be achieved without closures** through:
  - overhead reduction
  - fewer shifts
  - reduced premium pay
  - lower management duplication
  - voluntary exits
  - procurement and logistics simplification

- But **non-closure measures alone often do not fully remove the structural burden** of one or more low-utilization sites unless:
  - volumes are reallocated to achieve materially higher utilization at the remaining sites, or
  - the company accepts a much leaner product / complexity base.

So the key issue is not “can no-closure save money?” It can. The issue is whether it can save **enough recurring money** to offset the cost of preserving all sites.

## What would make a no-closure package credible

A no-closure package becomes credible if it delivers all of the following:

- **material recurring savings**, not just one-offs
- **durable labor-cost relief**
- **meaningful overhead cuts outside production**
- **real capacity reduction via shift and line rationalization**
- **clear volume reallocation**
- **execution that survives co-determination and board constraints**

If those conditions are met, then site closures are not economically necessary.

## What would make closures more likely

Selective closures or consolidation become more likely if the model shows:

- one or more plants operating at **persistently low utilization**
- insufficient volume to support three-shift or even two-shift economics
- fixed cost per vehicle staying uncompetitive even after overhead cuts
- a residual savings gap that is too large for labor concessions alone to bridge
- one-time restructuring costs that are justified by a large enough recurring benefit

In that case, the question becomes not whether closures are undesirable — they usually are — but whether they are the only way to get to a viable cost base.

## The labor-contribution issue

From a restructuring standpoint, a temporary labor contribution is economically useful only if it is:

- **large enough to matter**
- **time-limited**
- **paired with measurable management-side cuts**
- **not merely a deferred wage cut**
- **linked to verifiable investment and volume commitments**

If the labor contribution is small and temporary, it may help bridge a year or two, but it will not solve a structural overcapacity problem.
