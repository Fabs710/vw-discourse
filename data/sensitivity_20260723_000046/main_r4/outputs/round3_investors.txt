From the capital markets’ perspective, the discussion is now finally where it should have been from the start: on **durable economics, recurring savings, and execution certainty**. That is the right frame. But I need to be explicit: we are **not** prepared to back a settlement that preserves an uncompetitive cost base just because it is politically easier.

## Where we agree

### With management
We agree with the diagnosis:
- the German cost base is too high,
- overcapacity is real,
- and the current structure is not competitive enough to hit the return-on-sales target.

We also agree that a cosmetic compromise would be value-destructive. If the restructuring does not materially improve margins and fixed-cost absorption, capital markets will continue to discount the stock.

### With the family holding
We agree that the company must remain governable and investable. A prolonged conflict that burns cash, delays investment, and weakens strategic credibility would damage enterprise value.

### With the expert
The expert’s point is the most useful contribution so far: **a no-closure package may be possible in principle, but only if it removes enough recurring cost through utilization changes, overhead cuts, labor flexibility, and volume reallocation.** That is the correct test.

## Where we disagree

### With the Works Council and union
Your position is understandable as a bargaining stance, but from a shareholder perspective it is **not acceptable as a final outcome**:

- no plant closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- binding guarantees through 2030,
- and preservation of all current structures.

That is not yet a restructuring; it is a request to freeze the problem. If the company remains structurally overcosted, shareholders bear the downside through lower margins, lower valuation, and less capital for investment.

I do agree with one part of your argument: closures are **not a goal in themselves**. But that does **not** mean they can be ruled out in advance. If the non-closure package cannot close the recurring savings gap, then refusing closures becomes a veto on competitiveness.

### With management
At the same time, I do **not** give management a blank check to declare closures “inevitable” before proving that non-closure options have been fully modeled.

“Everything is on the table” is not enough. We need a comparison that shows:
- exact recurring savings,
- exact capacity reduction,
- exact employment impact,
- and implementation confidence under the actual governance constraints.

If management cannot show that a closure-based plan is superior on risk-adjusted value after execution risk is considered, then it should not assume the harshest route is automatically the best route.

### With the state
We understand the political and regional importance of preserving employment, but **regional stability does not trump competitiveness**. If the company remains structurally weak, the regional base will erode anyway — just more slowly.

So: yes to negotiated adjustment, no to a deal that preserves sites while leaving margins impaired.

## What would move us

We could support a settlement if it contains all of the following:

1. **Material recurring cost reduction**
   - not one-offs,
   - not accounting effects,
   - not temporary freeze measures.

2. **Credible capacity reduction**
   - enough to fix the overcapacity problem,
   - not just a symbolic trim.

3. **A meaningful labor contribution**
   - temporary if necessary,
   - but large enough to affect the cost base.

4. **Overhead reduction beyond the plants**
   - central functions and duplicated layers must also come down.

5. **Execution certainty**
   - a package the supervisory board can actually pass,
   - and that can survive resistance and still deliver savings.

6. **A real investment path**
   - because savings without future product allocation just shrink the business.

## What we will not support

We will not support:
- a cosmetic settlement,
- a deal that leaves German factory costs uncompetitive,
- a prolonged strike that destroys cash and confidence,
- or a “compromise” that merely postpones the hard decision.

## Our position on closures
We do **not** support closures as a first resort.  
But we also do **not** accept a blanket no-closure principle if it prevents the company from reaching competitive margins.

So the burden now is simple:
- show us, with numbers, that a no-closure package can deliver equivalent recurring savings and capacity reduction;
- if it cannot, then stronger footprint changes remain justified.

## What we want next
Before anyone claims victory on principles, we need a hard model:
- site-by-site utilization,
- recurring savings by scenario,
- one-time restructuring costs,
- employment impact,
- and margin effect through 2030.

## Bottom line
We are open to a negotiated solution. But our standard is clear:

**If the package restores competitiveness, delivers durable savings, and is executable under the governance structure, we can support it.**  
**If it only preserves employment politically while leaving the cost base broken, we will not back it.**

That is the capital markets’ position.
