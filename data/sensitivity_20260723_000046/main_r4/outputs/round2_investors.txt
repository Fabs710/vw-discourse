From the capital markets’ perspective, the discussion is now moving in the right direction in one respect: everyone is finally talking about **durable economics**, not slogans. That is necessary. But we are still not at a package we can support.

## Where we agree

### With management
We agree on the core diagnosis:
- the cost base is too high,
- the current structure is not competitive enough,
- and the brand’s return-on-sales target will not be met without a structural reset.

We also agree that **temporary fixes are not enough**. If the outcome leaves the company structurally uncompetitive, investors will continue to discount the shares and capital will remain constrained.

### With the family holding
We agree that the company must become **governable and investable**, not just politically survivable. We also agree that a prolonged value-destructive conflict would be bad for enterprise value.

### With the state and labor
We agree that a prolonged strike, reputational damage, and execution disruption would destroy value. And we agree that a cosmetic settlement would just postpone the problem.

## Where we disagree

### With the Works Council and union
Your repeated “no closures, no compulsory redundancies, no dismantling of job security” line is understandable politically, but from our side it is **not sufficient** and may be **incompatible with a real turnaround**.

What matters to shareholders is not the symbolism of preserving every site; it is whether the company gets to:
- a lower fixed-cost base,
- materially better margins,
- and a capacity structure that matches demand.

If you rule out the measures that may be necessary to get there, then you are asking capital to subsidize an uncompetitive structure. That is not acceptable.

At the same time, I will say this plainly: **closures are not a goal in themselves**. If you can produce a package that achieves equivalent recurring savings, capacity reduction, and margin improvement without closures, then that is preferable. But it must be *equivalent in hard numbers*, not equivalent in rhetoric.

### With management
We do **not** give management a blank check to use closures as the first and only serious lever. A plan that triggers a prolonged confrontation and still fails to deliver execution certainty is not shareholder-friendly.

If management wants support from the capital markets, it needs to demonstrate:
- exact recurring savings,
- exact capacity reduction,
- exact timeline,
- and a credible path to implementation under this governance structure.

“Everything is on the table” is not a plan. It is only acceptable if it is translated into a package that the board can actually execute without months of damaging conflict.

### With the state
We understand the political constraints, but state protection of jobs cannot come at the expense of a structurally impaired company. If the final outcome preserves sites but leaves the cost base uncompetitive, then the company will keep underperforming and the problem will come back worse.

So: yes to regional stability, no to permanent economic drift.

## What would move us

We could support a settlement if it includes all of the following:

1. **Material, recurring cost reduction**
   - not one-offs,
   - not accounting effects,
   - not temporary freeze measures.

2. **Credible capacity reduction**
   - enough to fix the overcapacity problem,
   - not just a symbolic trim.

3. **A meaningful labor contribution**
   - temporary if necessary,
   - but large enough to improve competitiveness,
   - and linked to productivity/flexibility gains.

4. **Execution certainty**
   - a package the supervisory board can actually approve,
   - and that can survive the expected resistance.

5. **A clear investment and product path**
   - because savings without future volume just shrink the business.

## What we will not support

We will not support:
- a cosmetic settlement,
- a deal that preserves every site while leaving margins impaired,
- a prolonged strike that destroys cash and confidence,
- or a “compromise” that merely delays closures while refusing real structural savings.

## On the current positions

### To the Works Council and union
If you want to avoid closures and compulsory redundancies, then you need to bring a **fully quantified alternative** that gives us the same recurring financial effect. Until then, your position is not a solution; it is a veto over restructuring.

### To management
Do not confuse shareholder patience with tolerance for an ungovernable confrontation. We are not asking you to protect every site. We are asking you to show that your plan is economically sufficient *and* executable.

### To the state and family
Your insistence on a negotiated, non-destructive outcome is sensible. But that outcome still has to restore competitiveness. If it does not, then it is not a viable compromise from our perspective.

## Bottom line

Our support is conditional on a package that **actually fixes the economics**.

That means:
- durable structural savings,
- real capacity reduction,
- a credible labor contribution,
- and enough implementation certainty to restore margins.

If the package is merely a political truce with uncompetitive economics, we will not back it.

If no one has yet quantified the savings alternatives site by site, then I would suggest we need a specialist operational restructuring advisor and an independent financial model before anyone claims that “no closures” can or cannot work.
