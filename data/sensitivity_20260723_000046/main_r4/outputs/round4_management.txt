Let me be equally direct.

## To the works council and the union
You keep repeating “no closures, no compulsory redundancies, no dismantling of job security” as if that were a restructuring strategy. It is not. It is a **constraint**. A very costly one.

I do **not** accept the premise that the company can preserve every German site, preserve the current labor-cost framework, and still close the competitiveness gap simply through “internal measures.” That is wishful thinking unless the model proves otherwise.

And that is precisely the point: **the burden is now on you to show the numbers**. Not slogans. Not moral pressure. Numbers.

The expert has said a no-closure package is possible **in principle** only if it removes enough recurring cost through overhead, utilization, working-time redesign, productivity, and labor-cost flexibility. Fine. Then let’s test whether your preferred path actually does that.

But I will not accept a position that says:
- closures are forbidden in advance,
- compulsory redundancies are forbidden in advance,
- guarantees through 2030 are mandatory in advance,
- and management must somehow make the economics work anyway.

That is not negotiation. That is asking management to accept structural uncompetitiveness.

## To the state
I respect your role and your concern for regional stability. But regional stability does not come from protecting every current structure at all costs. It comes from having a company that is competitive enough to invest, earn, and remain relevant through 2030.

You keep saying capacity reduction can happen without closures. Yes — **if** the result is real and durable. But if the sites remain structurally overcosted and underutilized, then you have merely postponed the problem.

I am prepared to support a negotiated solution that avoids closures **only if** it delivers:
- material recurring savings,
- credible capacity reduction,
- a real labor-cost contribution,
- and a clearly improved margin path.

Without that, “no closures” is just another word for delay.

## To the controlling family
I agree with you on one core point: a cosmetic deal would destroy value. We cannot afford a settlement that looks politically attractive but leaves the business structurally weak.

Where I push back is your apparent comfort with using closures as the default benchmark. I am not making closures a goal. I am making them a **necessary option** if the alternative does not close the gap.

What matters is not rhetoric about social compact. What matters is whether the package actually restores competitiveness.

So yes, I support the principle that the model must be rigorous and site-specific. But I will not accept the conclusion that the mere existence of a no-closure proposal means it should be considered equivalent. It must prove equivalence in hard economic terms.

## To the investors
You are right to focus on recurring savings, execution certainty, and margin recovery. That is exactly the right lens.

But you also need to be honest about execution risk. A package that sounds socially smoother but leaves the cost base too high is not “lower risk.” It is deferred risk.

I am not asking for blind acceptance of closures. I am saying that if non-closure measures cannot deliver the required structural effect, then stronger measures remain justified.

## On the expert’s assessment
The expert has not changed the core issue. The expert has sharpened it:

- No-closure is possible **in principle**.
- It is only credible if the recurring savings are large enough.
- If the savings gap is very large, the no-closure solution becomes thin.

That is the real test. And that test has not yet been met.

So let’s stop pretending the expert’s statement is a veto on closures. It is not. It is a demand for evidence.

## What management is prepared to consider
I remain open to a negotiated package if it contains all of the following:
- material capacity reduction,
- substantial overhead cuts,
- a genuine labor-cost contribution,
- productivity and shift redesign,
- and a credible path to restore return on sales by 2030.

That package can include:
- lower shift intensity,
- volume reallocation,
- voluntary exits,
- redeployment,
- and temporary labor contributions.

But I will not sign up to:
- a deal that freezes every site,
- guarantees the full current structure through 2030,
- or a “temporary” contribution that simply becomes a permanent subsidy of inefficiency.

## What I reject
I reject three things outright:
1. **A no-closure principle as a fixed red line**
2. **A no-redundancies principle that blocks all structural adjustment**
3. **A guarantees-first approach that makes competitiveness secondary**

If labor wants to avoid closures, it has to make the economics work. That means real recurring savings, not symbolic sacrifice.

## Where we go next
The next step must be the independent, site-by-site model — but with one clear objective:
**test whether non-closure measures can really close the gap.**

I want it to show, for each German site:
- utilization,
- fixed and variable cost,
- overhead burden,
- recurring savings under non-closure measures,
- one-time restructuring cost,
- employment impact,
- and residual gap to target.

And I want it to compare:
1. no closures / no compulsory redundancies,
2. selective consolidation,
3. a hybrid package.

## Bottom line
I am not retreating from the core requirement.

We need:
- **structural cost reduction**
- **material capacity adjustment**
- **durable labor-cost relief**
- **and a credible path to competitiveness by 2030**

If a no-closure solution can do that, I will support it.  
If it cannot, then closures and stronger measures remain on the table — because preserving an uncompetitive structure is not an option.
