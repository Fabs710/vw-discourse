I appreciate the clarity from both labor and management. From the state’s perspective, we are not here to defend an uncompetitive status quo — but we are equally not prepared to accept a restructuring that destroys the company’s role as a regional economic anchor.

Let me be direct.

### Where I agree with management
You are right that the company needs a **credible, durable restructuring**. Temporary fixes, symbolic savings, or simply delaying the decision would be irresponsible. We also recognize that the market environment has changed and that the company must secure room for investment in future products and technology.

### Where I disagree with management
I do **not** accept the idea that plant closures in Germany are simply the normal price of adjustment. Closing German sites, especially under conditions where there are still alternatives on the table, would have consequences far beyond the balance sheet:
- it would damage regional employment,
- weaken the industrial ecosystem,
- create political and social backlash,
- and undermine trust in the company’s long-term partnership model.

And I am equally clear that **freezing an uncompetitive structure is not acceptable either**. If the company can only survive by refusing any meaningful adjustment, then that is not a solution.

### Where I agree with labor
The Works Council and the union are right to insist that workers should not bear the burden alone. A restructuring that demands concessions only from employees, while management and ownership are insulated, would be neither fair nor sustainable. I also agree that **binding guarantees matter** — not as slogans, but as a condition for any negotiated contribution by the workforce.

### Where I disagree with labor
I cannot support an approach that simply says “no closures, no reductions, no hard trade-offs” without putting forward a credible industrial alternative that adds up financially. A state shareholder cannot sign off on wishful thinking. If the company truly needs durable savings, then we need a package that delivers them without gutting the regional base — but that means real adjustments, not just defending every existing structure unchanged.

### The state’s line
For us, the key tests are these:

1. **No decision that guts regional employment.**  
   Large-scale closures or mass displacement in Germany are not acceptable as a first resort.

2. **No sidelining of co-determination or the state’s veto role.**  
   The special statute and the governance balance are not decorative. Any attempt to push through a settlement by bypassing these structures will meet resistance.

3. **A negotiated restructuring must preserve sites and secure future work.**  
   That can include capacity adjustments, internal flexibility, redeployment, qualification, working-time instruments, and yes, a fair temporary contribution if it is tied to binding guarantees and investment commitments.

4. **The burden must be shared.**  
   Management, owners, and labor all need to contribute in proportion to their role and capacity.

### What would move us
We could support a restructuring if it is built around:
- preservation of German sites,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- a credible capacity and productivity adjustment,
- a time-limited employee contribution,
- management commitments on investment and product allocation,
- and binding safeguards for employment and co-determination.

What will **not** move us is a package that treats closures as the easy option or uses them as leverage to force one-sided concessions.

So my message is simple:  
If management wants our support, bring forward a real alternative to closures that is economically credible and socially responsible. If labor wants the company’s full backing, be prepared to discuss a meaningful contribution — but only within a framework that protects jobs, sites, and the regional industrial base.

We are prepared to broker a settlement. We are not prepared to endorse a dismantling.
