I’ll respond directly.

## To the Works Council and the union
I acknowledge one point clearly: **the company needs a durable solution, not a cosmetic one**. On that we agree.

But I do **not** accept the core line you are repeating as a fixed boundary:
- no plant closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- no dismantling of job security,
- and binding guarantees through 2030.

That package, as stated, **does not solve the structural problem**. It preserves the cost base while asking management to pretend the numbers will somehow improve. They won’t.

You say there are alternatives. Fine — but they must be **quantified and equivalent in effect**, not simply described in general terms. So far, I have not seen a package from labor that delivers:
- the same recurring cost reduction,
- the same durable capacity reduction,
- and the same margin impact
as a serious structural restructuring would.

Working-time changes, redeployment, voluntary measures, and efficiency programs are useful tools. They are **not enough on their own** if they leave overcapacity untouched and the labor-cost structure still above target. A temporary employee contribution is also not sufficient if it merely postpones the adjustment.

Let me be explicit:  
**If you want to rule out closures and forced redundancies, then you must show how the company still achieves equivalent permanent savings.** Not “we should try.” Not “maybe productivity will improve.” I mean actual, recurring euros on the cost base.

## To the state
I understand your concern about regional stability and industrial ecosystems. I do not dismiss that. But I have to say plainly: **protecting every existing site at any cost is not industrial policy; it is delay**.

You say closures are not acceptable as a first resort. I can accept that as a sequencing point. I cannot accept it as a substantive veto if the alternatives do not close the competitiveness gap. The company’s fiduciary responsibility is to the long-term viability of the business. If we leave the German structure uncompetitive, we will damage the regional base anyway — just more slowly.

If you want our support for a negotiated solution, then help bring forward something that **actually reduces structural cost and overcapacity**. Not a political holding pattern.

## To the controlling family holding
I agree with you on one central point: **we need a plan that can actually be executed under the governance structure**. A theoretical turnaround that cannot pass co-determination realities is not serious.

Where I disagree is on the idea that stronger measures should only come after every alternative has been exhausted in practice. We do not have unlimited time. The market is moving, the cost crisis is immediate, and every month of delay burns cash and credibility.

I am not asking for confrontation for its own sake. I am asking for **a credible structural reset**. If labor does not bring enough savings through negotiated means, then stronger measures remain necessary. That is not a threat for effect; it is the business logic.

## To institutional investors
You are right to insist on **durable, recurring savings** and **capacity reduction**. That is also my position. But I need to underline something: a deal that sounds politically neat but leaves the cost base uncompetitive is not a shareholder-friendly deal.

So on the numbers:
- We need a **material reduction in excess capacity**.
- We need a **substantial labor-cost contribution**, not symbolic restraint.
- We need **permanent structural savings**, not one-off concessions.
- We need a path that supports the brand’s return-on-sales target and frees capital for investment to 2030.

If those conditions are not met, then we have not solved the problem. We have only renamed it.

## What I can consider
I am open to a negotiated package if it contains real structural impact. That could include:
- capacity reductions across the German footprint,
- site-specific reorganization,
- working-time flexibility,
- voluntary exits and redeployment,
- a meaningful and time-limited employee contribution,
- and investment commitments linked to the new industrial plan.

But I will be blunt: **I am not open to a package whose only red line is protecting every plant and every job as they currently stand**. That is not a restructuring. It is a delay of the inevitable.

## What is not acceptable
These would be fundamentally unacceptable:
- a cosmetic deal that leaves German factory costs uncompetitive,
- a “temporary” contribution without durable savings,
- guarantees that freeze the structure through 2030 while the market keeps eroding,
- or a settlement that cannot be defended to the board, owners, and investors as structurally credible.

## Bottom line
I am prepared to negotiate a settlement. But the settlement must do three things:
1. **cut overcapacity materially,**
2. **reduce labor costs durably,**
3. **restore competitiveness fast enough to protect the 2030 investment agenda.**

If labor wants to avoid closures, it must bring a package that is **economically equivalent** in hard, recurring terms. If that is not possible, then we will have to pursue stronger restructuring measures, because the company cannot afford a compromise that preserves the problem.

If needed, I want the next round to focus on concrete numbers:
- how many units of capacity are coming out,
- how much recurring cost is being removed,
- what labor contribution is structurally secured,
- and what savings are guaranteed beyond 2026, not just this year.
