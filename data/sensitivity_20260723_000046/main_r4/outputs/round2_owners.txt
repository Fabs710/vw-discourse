I will respond directly.

## To management
I agree with your diagnosis on the core issue: **the current cost and capacity structure is not competitive enough**. If we do nothing structural, we will keep eroding margins and ultimately damage the company’s long-term value. That is not acceptable.

I also agree that we need **durable savings**, not cosmetic measures, and that the next product and technology cycle must be funded.

Where I do **not** fully agree is on the implied logic that closures and compulsory redundancies are the only credible route. That may be one route, but it is not yet proven to be the only route. Given the governance reality here — co-determination, the special statute, the state’s blocking position — a plan that depends on confrontation rather than execution is not a serious plan. It may be economically neat on paper and ungovernable in practice.

So my position is:

- I support a **hard structural reset**.
- I do **not** support a value-destructive showdown.
- I will not back a package that triggers prolonged industrial conflict unless there is no credible alternative.

If you want support, then bring a package that is **quantified, executable, and durable**:
- exact capacity reduction,
- exact recurring savings,
- exact contribution from labor,
- and a clear path to margin recovery.

## To the Works Council and union
I understand your red lines: no closures, no compulsory redundancies, no dismantling of bargaining protections. I also understand why you are defending them. But I need to be equally clear: **a blanket refusal of closures regardless of the economics is not, by itself, a viable answer**.

What I do agree with is that workers should not carry the entire burden. A settlement must involve **shared sacrifice**, and if labor is asked to contribute, that contribution must be tied to **binding and credible commitments** on employment, investment, and future work.

Where I disagree is the assumption that preserving every site in its current form is automatically value-preserving. If the company stays structurally overcosted, then we are simply postponing a bigger crisis. That helps nobody in the long run.

So if you want to avoid closures and forced layoffs, then the alternative must be **economically equivalent** in permanent effect. Not in principle, not emotionally — in actual recurring euros and actual capacity reduction.

## To the state
I appreciate your emphasis on regional stability and co-determination. Those are real factors, and I do not dismiss them.

But I do not accept the idea that the state’s role is to preserve the status quo or to rule out closures as a matter of principle. If the company remains structurally uncompetitive, the regional base will weaken anyway — just more slowly and with more damage to the balance sheet.

You are right that trust matters. You are wrong if you believe trust alone can substitute for hard economics. What would actually preserve the industrial base is a restructuring that restores competitiveness and keeps the company investable.

## To institutional investors
I agree with your insistence on **durable, recurring savings** and a credible path back to margins. That is the right standard.

I also agree that a prolonged strike or an ungovernable compromise would be value-destructive. However, I do not think shareholder value is served by demanding the harshest route automatically. The question is not whether cuts happen; the question is **which package can actually be executed and sustained**.

## My position as controlling shareholder
The family holding will support a solution that:

1. **Restores competitiveness durably**
2. **Cuts structural overcapacity materially**
3. **Improves margins in a way the board can defend**
4. **Preserves industrial peace if at all possible**
5. **Does not permanently impair governance control or enterprise value**

That means I am open to an alternative to closures, but only if it is not a euphemism for delay. If labor wants to rule out closures and compulsory redundancies, then the counterproposal must still deliver:
- real capacity reduction,
- real labor-cost contribution,
- and real recurring savings.

If that cannot be achieved, then I will not pretend otherwise: stronger measures remain necessary.

## What I would need next
I want the next round to move from principles to numbers:

- How much capacity comes out?
- How much recurring cost is removed?
- What is the labor contribution, and for how long?
- What investment and product commitments are tied to it?
- What level of employment and site security is actually compatible with a competitive cost base?

If there is a package that meets those tests without closures, I am prepared to consider it. If not, then we should stop pretending that a politically comfortable solution is automatically a viable one.

### Bottom line
I am **not** on the side of closures for their own sake.  
I am **not** on the side of protecting every existing structure at any cost either.

I am on the side of a solution that restores long-term value and keeps the company governable. If that requires deeper restructuring, so be it. If it can be done without closures, I am open — but only if the economics are real.
