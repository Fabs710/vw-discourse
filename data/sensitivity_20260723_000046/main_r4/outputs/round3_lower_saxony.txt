I will be direct.

## To management
I agree with you on one point: **the company needs durable, recurring savings and a credible path back to competitiveness**. I am not defending a status quo that leaves the brand permanently uncompetitive. That would also be a risk to jobs, investment, and the regional base.

But I disagree with your repeated implication that **plant closures and compulsory redundancies remain necessary unless proven otherwise**. In this governance setting, that is not just an economic claim; it is a political and social choice with very high costs. You have not yet shown that the same structural effect cannot be reached through a negotiated footprint adjustment that preserves sites and most jobs.

I also reject the tone that treats job protection and site security as if they were indulgences. For us, they are not side issues. They are central to the company’s role as a regional economic anchor.

What I could support is:
- substantial capacity reduction,
- real overhead cuts,
- shift and utilization changes,
- redeployment and qualification,
- voluntary exits,
- and a temporary, fair employee contribution,

**provided** this is tied to:
- no plant closures in the state as the chosen solution,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- binding investment commitments,
- and a credible long-term product allocation for the German sites.

If you cannot live with that, then we are not yet at a viable settlement.

## To the works council and the union
I agree with your core line that workers should not be made the sole buffer for strategic correction. I also agree that a temporary labor contribution only makes sense if there are **binding guarantees** and a real industrial plan behind it.

Where I am more cautious than you is on turning “no closures” into an absolute demand without any willingness to spell out how the necessary savings are achieved. To defend jobs credibly, we need to show that the alternative is not just politically preferable but operationally sufficient.

That means I support your insistence on:
- no forced redundancies,
- no closure-first strategy,
- preservation of co-determination,
- and serious use of internal alternatives.

But I also expect labor to engage constructively on:
- where capacity is being removed,
- how volume is redistributed,
- what productivity gains are realistic,
- and what temporary contribution is possible.

That is not surrender. It is what makes a no-closure line defendable.

## To the state
I appreciate that you are insisting on regional stability and the company’s industrial role. That is exactly the right perspective for an anchor shareholder.

I agree that closures should **not** be the first resort. I also agree that trust and social peace matter. But I would be careful not to make “no closures” sound like a blanket political veto before the alternatives are fully tested.

From the state’s perspective, the acceptable path is:
- protect German sites where possible,
- avoid compulsory redundancies,
- secure investment,
- and force a real restructuring of cost and capacity.

So I support your direction, but I want it to stay economically grounded. If the alternatives can deliver the required savings, then closures should not happen. If they cannot, then we need to know exactly why before anybody reaches for the hardest lever.

## To the family holding and investors
I understand your insistence on recurring savings and execution certainty. That is legitimate.

But I disagree with any framing that suggests the only meaningful test of seriousness is whether we accept closures up front. In a company with co-determination, a special statute, and a strong regional stake, a “technically optimal” solution that is politically ungovernable is not a serious solution.

You are right that value cannot be preserved by symbolism alone. But value also cannot be preserved by triggering a rupture in the company’s social compact. A durable settlement has to be defensible both economically and socially.

So my message is simple:
- yes to real savings,
- yes to capacity adjustment,
- yes to a labor contribution,
- no to treating plant closures as the default answer,
- and no to any deal that guts regional employment or weakens the state’s co-determination role.

## On the expert’s assessment
The expert’s conclusion is useful, but I read it carefully.

What it says is:
- a no-closure package is **possible in principle**,
- but only if it removes a substantial amount of recurring cost through overhead, utilization, working-time changes, labor flexibility, and productivity,
- and only if the remaining gap is not too large.

That is not a green light for closures. It is a warning that the numbers must be real.

So I support the independent modeling step. But I also note this: **the burden is not only on labor to prove perfection.** Management must also prove that closures are actually necessary, not just convenient.

## What the state can support
I am prepared to support a package if it contains all of the following:

1. **Preservation of German sites as the default**
   - no plant closures in Germany as the chosen restructuring path.

2. **No compulsory redundancies**
   - workforce reduction, if needed, through voluntary and negotiated means.

3. **Real capacity reduction**
   - through shifts, utilization, and volume reallocation.

4. **Durable savings**
   - including overhead and management-side cuts, not only labor sacrifices.

5. **A temporary employee contribution**
   - only if it is fair, time-limited, and matched by binding guarantees.

6. **Investment and product commitments**
   - to keep the German footprint viable beyond the current crisis.

## What I will not support
I will not support:
- closures presented as the first serious option,
- compulsory redundancies as a routine adjustment tool,
- a restructuring that destroys trust and industrial peace,
- or a deal that weakens co-determination and the company’s long-term regional role.

## My position
So let me be clear: I am **not** drifting toward a compromise for its own sake.

I am saying this:
- If management and the other shareholders can bring a package that achieves the required recurring savings **without** closures and without forced redundancies, I will work to support it.
- If they cannot, then they must show why all non-closure measures were truly exhausted before anyone talks about shutting sites in this state.

That is the point where I stand.
