I appreciate that everyone is finally talking in terms of **measurable economics** rather than slogans. That is where this discussion needs to be.

But let me be equally clear: **we do not have a viable future if we preserve the current German cost base and footprint in anything close to its present form.** That is the central point, and none of the replies so far has changed it.

## Where I agree

I agree with the investors, the family, and even the state on several points:

- **A cosmetic deal is unacceptable.**
- **A prolonged strike would damage value, output, and confidence.**
- **Any solution must be durable and enforceable.**
- **Governance constraints are real and must be respected.**

I also agree that closures are **not an end in themselves**. They are a means to an end: restoring competitiveness.

## Where I disagree

### 1) “No closures” cannot be a starting assumption
The union and works council keep saying no plant closures, no compulsory redundancies, and binding job/site guarantees to 2030. I understand why they want that. But if those constraints make it impossible to remove enough cost and capacity, then they are not a solution — they are a veto on restructuring.

That is not acceptable.

If the current footprint is too large for the market, then **capacity has to come out**. If the cost base is too high, then **labour costs have to come down structurally**. If that means closures, then we must be honest about it.

### 2) “Shared sacrifice” must mean actual structural contribution
The union says management, owners, and employees should all contribute. Fine — but that contribution has to be real, not rhetorical.

From management’s side, the contribution is already on the table: we are taking the political and operational risk of restructuring and challenging legacy assumptions. From owners and investors, the contribution is a willingness to back a hard reset if it is credible. From labour, we need:
- a genuine labour-cost contribution,
- flexibility,
- capacity adjustment,
- and removal of legacy guarantees that block competitiveness.

Without that, there is no shared burden — only delay.

### 3) Social acceptability does not override financial reality
The state government is right that the company must remain a regional anchor. But a company that remains an anchor **only because it refuses to adapt** will eventually become weaker, not stronger. Protecting every site at all costs is not responsible stewardship. It is how you preserve overcapacity and defer decline.

The same applies to job guarantees. A guarantee that prevents restructuring may feel stabilizing in the short term, but if it prevents the company from investing in new products, software, battery technology, and future platforms, then it ultimately destroys more jobs than it protects.

## The key issue: the burden of proof
Several parties asked for a quantified alternative. That is fair. So let me state the test plainly:

If labour, the works council, the state, or anyone else wants to avoid closures, then they must put forward a package that achieves **equivalent economics** in the real sense:

- comparable fixed-cost reduction
- comparable capacity reduction
- comparable speed of implementation
- enforceable labour-cost relief
- and durable margin improvement

Not promises. Not aspirations. Not “we will try.”

A package that simply says “no closures, no layoffs, no pain” is not an alternative. It is denial.

## What management is prepared to accept
We are open to the mechanism, but not open to weak economics.

So yes, we are prepared to consider:
- working-time changes,
- productivity measures,
- temporary or structural labour-cost concessions,
- removal or redesign of legacy guarantees,
- selective capacity rationalisation,
- and a combination of these.

But only if the total package delivers the scale of savings we need. If it does not, it will not be supported by management, and it should not be supported by the board.

## What we will not accept
We will not accept:
- a package that preserves most of the current footprint and calls it transformation
- temporary concessions that expire before the next cycle
- non-binding productivity promises
- a settlement that protects every site while leaving the brand structurally uncompetitive
- or a result that starves future investment to preserve an unsustainable present

## On the state’s role
I respect the state’s governance position. But a blocking minority is not a mandate to freeze the business model. It is a responsibility to ensure the company remains viable. That means supporting hard decisions when they are necessary.

If a no-closure package can truly deliver the required economics, we will engage with it. If not, then insisting on no closures simply protects the wrong objective.

## On the investors and family
You are right to say the burden is on a no-closure alternative to prove itself. That is exactly right. I would go further: if there is no package with equivalent economics, then we should stop pretending there is.

## Bottom line
My position is unchanged:

- We need a **deep, durable reduction in cost and capacity**
- We need a **real labour-cost contribution**
- We need **implementation certainty**
- We need a result that **restores margins and funds future investment to 2030**

If the other stakeholders can produce a quantified, enforceable package that delivers that without closures, I will consider it. If they cannot, then closures and substantial job reductions remain on the table, because this company cannot afford a symbolic compromise.
