We appreciate the move from principle to structure. That is the right direction, because what matters now is not rhetoric but whether the package can actually restore margins and value.

### Where we agree
- **A cosmetic settlement is unacceptable.**
- **Durable competitiveness is the objective.**
- **A prolonged strike would be value-destructive.**
- **Governance and implementation certainty matter.**

### Where we disagree
The union and works council are still framing the discussion as if **no plant closures, no compulsory redundancies, and binding job/site guarantees to 2030** can coexist with a serious cost reset. That is not a given. If those constraints materially block the needed savings, then they are not a solution — they are a veto on restructuring.

We do not support a package that preserves the current footprint and most of the cost structure while calling it a win. That would be exactly the cosmetic settlement we reject.

### On the key question: equivalent economics
“Equivalent economics” has to mean:
- a **measurable** reduction in fixed cost,
- a **real** capacity adjustment,
- and a **durable** improvement in return on sales.

If the alternative to closures can truly deliver that, fine. But the burden is on labour and the state to show how. So far, we have heard a lot about instruments, but not yet a quantified path to the same savings. Until that is provided, closure-based restructuring remains the benchmark, not a scare tactic.

### On the proposal space
We are prepared to consider a negotiated alternative **only if it is specific and enforceable**. For example:
- verified capacity reduction by site and shift,
- substantial working-time and flexibility changes,
- meaningful wage/labour-cost concessions,
- elimination or redesign of legacy guarantees,
- measurable productivity commitments with triggers and remedies,
- and a timetable that delivers savings quickly enough to matter.

What we will not back:
- broad job-security promises with no hard economics behind them,
- “temporary” contributions that simply defer the issue,
- vague productivity pledges,
- or any package that relies on hope rather than enforceable savings.

### On the union and works council demands
We understand the need for social protection, but **no compulsory redundancies and no plant closures** cannot be treated as the starting line if the company’s economics do not support them. If the workforce wants to avoid closures, it must help produce a package that makes closures unnecessary on financial grounds — not on political preference.

### On the state and family
We note your emphasis on regional and governance constraints. That matters, but it does not change the financial test. Political feasibility cannot be bought with shareholder returns if the end result is a structurally impaired business. A settlement that is socially comfortable but commercially weak will damage the share price, the cost of capital, and ultimately the company’s ability to invest.

### Our position for round 2
We would support one of two paths:
1. **A closure-based restructuring** if that is what is required to achieve the cost and capacity reduction; or
2. **A quantified, enforceable no-closure alternative** that delivers the same economics.

What we will not support is a middle-ground statement that avoids closures in language but leaves the cost base effectively intact in practice.

### What we need next
Before we move any further, management should specify the **required financial target** in concrete terms:
- how much cost reduction,
- how much capacity reduction,
- and over what timeframe.

Then labour/state should table a **numerically credible alternative** showing how the same target is met without structurally impairing margins.

Until then, our view remains unchanged: **no cosmetic deal, no prolonged conflict, and no settlement that fails to restore competitive economics.**
