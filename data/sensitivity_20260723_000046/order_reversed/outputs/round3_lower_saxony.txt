I will be equally direct.

We are finally discussing the right level of seriousness: hard economics, implementation risk, and what is actually governable. That is useful. But I need to be very clear that **I do not accept a closure-led solution as the default benchmark**, and I do not accept any package that **guts regional employment** or treats the state’s co-determination role as disposable.

## Where I agree

I agree with management, investors, the family, and the expert on one central point:  
**a cosmetic settlement is unacceptable.**

I also agree that:
- the company needs a **durable competitiveness reset**,
- the savings must be **measurable and enforceable**,
- a **prolonged strike** would damage everyone,
- and **vague productivity promises** are not enough.

I accept, too, the expert’s point that a no-closure package must be **deep, quantified, and fast enough** to matter. I am not arguing for delay dressed up as social policy.

## Where I disagree

### 1) I do not accept closures as the starting assumption
Management and investors keep treating closures as the benchmark if the alternative cannot “prove itself” against them. That may be convenient from a finance perspective, but it is not the only legitimate test.

For us, the benchmark is not “what is bluntest.”  
The benchmark is: **what preserves the company as a viable industrial anchor in this region while restoring competitiveness?**

A closure-first approach may be economically attractive on paper, but it creates permanent damage:
- loss of skilled jobs,
- loss of regional industrial capacity,
- loss of trust in co-determination,
- and political blowback that can make implementation unstable.

That is not a side issue. It affects long-term viability.

### 2) I do not accept that “equivalent economics” can ignore social and regional destruction
I understand the investors’ and family’s focus on margins. But if “equivalent economics” is interpreted as equivalent layoffs, equivalent site destruction, and equivalent damage to the region, then I reject that framing.

Equivalent economics must mean:
- real recurring savings,
- real capacity adjustment,
- real productivity gains,
- and a durable improvement in returns.

It does **not** automatically mean plant closures.

### 3) I do not accept a settlement that sidelines the state’s veto and co-determination role
Let me say this plainly:  
**No outcome that removes the state’s co-determination and veto role is acceptable.**  
That is part of the governance architecture here, not a negotiable afterthought.

If a restructuring is only “credible” when it bypasses the existing balance of power, then it is not credible in this company.

## On the expert’s assessment

The expert has been helpful in one important respect: they confirmed that **a no-closure package can work in principle** if it includes:
- quantified capacity reduction,
- material labour-cost relief,
- headcount reduction through non-compulsory means,
- binding enforcement,
- and investment commitments.

That matters. It means this is not a false choice between doing nothing and shutting plants.

But I also take the expert’s warning seriously: if the no-closure route is shallow, temporary, or weakly enforced, it will fail. I will not support that either.

So my position is not “no change.”  
My position is: **hard change, but not through default closures and mass layoffs.**

## On management

You say the current cost base and footprint are not viable. That may be true. But I need you to show why the answer must be plant closures rather than a serious negotiated package using other instruments.

I am prepared to consider:
- working-time adjustments,
- shift reductions,
- targeted labour-cost contributions,
- flexibility increases,
- productivity commitments,
- limited capacity rationalisation,
- voluntary departures,
- redeployment,
- and investment commitments tied to the deal.

What I am **not** prepared to accept is a plan that starts by writing off sites and then asks the state to absorb the political fallout.

If you insist closures are necessary, then show:
- why alternative capacity measures cannot deliver enough,
- what exact savings closures provide,
- and why those savings cannot be matched through a more balanced package.

Until then, I treat closure-first as a preference, not a necessity.

## On investors

You are right to insist on a quantified package. But I will push back on one point: you cannot simply say “if no-closure means no equivalent savings, then closures remain the benchmark” and stop there.

That logic ignores:
- the company’s regional embeddedness,
- the governance constraints,
- and the long-term value destruction that can follow from a socially explosive restructuring.

A settlement that technically maximizes short-term cost relief but destabilizes the company’s social legitimacy may not be the best long-term value path.

So yes, bring numbers.  
But do not pretend that numbers alone settle the question of legitimacy here.

## On the family holding

I appreciate that you are now speaking in terms of a real fallback, not symbolism. That is better. I also agree that endless renegotiation would be damaging.

But I need to be frank: if the fallback is effectively a hidden closure threat, then the “hybrid” becomes coercion by another name.

I can support a serious restructuring package only if it is genuinely designed to preserve sites and jobs **as far as possible**, not merely defer a closure decision until labour is weakened.

## On the union and works council

You are right that workers should not be asked to carry the full burden. I support that.  
But I also need to say this clearly: **binding no-closure and no compulsory redundancy guarantees cannot be absolute if they make any restructuring impossible.**

I am not prepared to bless a package that simply freezes the current structure and calls that social responsibility. That would be irresponsible to the company and the region alike.

So yes, there must be change. But it must be:
- site-sensitive,
- job-protective,
- enforceable,
- and shared.

## My position on the three bridge options

### 1) Hybrid restructuring with conditional closure deferral
This is the most promising path, but only if it is real.

I could support a deferral **if** it includes:
- hard quantified savings targets,
- binding site-specific capacity reductions,
- a clear review date,
- visible contributions from management and owners,
- and a backstop that is legally and politically executable if targets are missed.

If the fallback is designed only to pressure labour into conceding later, I will not support it.

### 2) Site-preserving adjustment package with hard economics
This is viable only if the numbers are genuine.  
It must produce:
- material fixed-cost reduction,
- real capacity adjustment,
- and durable labour-cost relief.

That means more than temporary wage restraint. It means a structural package. If you can show that, I am open.

### 3) Phased settlement with voluntary workforce reduction first
This is acceptable as a first phase, not as an alibi for inaction.

Voluntary departures, early retirement, redeployment, and retirement bridges can be part of the answer. But only if they deliver a meaningful structural result and are not just used to postpone the hard choice.

## What would move me

I would be prepared to support a settlement that contains:
- **no blanket plant closure decision now**
- **real capacity reductions by shift, line, and product allocation**
- **substantial voluntary workforce reduction**
- **working-time and flexibility changes**
- **targeted employee contribution**
- **management and owner contributions**
- **investment commitments tied to the restructuring**
- **binding milestones and review points**
- **preservation of co-determination and the state’s blocking role**

That is the kind of package that could protect the regional base while still restoring competitiveness.

## What I will not support

I will not support:
- plant closures as the first and only serious option,
- compulsory redundancies as the default tool,
- a deal that strips jobs from the state and calls it modernization,
- any attempt to bypass co-determination,
- or a paper agreement that leaves the company structurally weak.

## Bottom line

I am open to a serious restructuring.  
I am not open to a restructuring that treats regional employment as collateral damage.

So my position is:

- **No cosmetic compromise**
- **No blanket closure-first logic**
- **No compulsory redundancies as a starting point**
- **Yes to quantified, enforceable, site-preserving restructuring**
- **Yes to real shared sacrifice**
- **Yes to preserving the state’s governance rights and regional industrial role**

If management and the other shareholders want my support, bring a package that proves the economics **without** dismantling the regional anchor. If they cannot, then they should not expect the state to make closures easier.
