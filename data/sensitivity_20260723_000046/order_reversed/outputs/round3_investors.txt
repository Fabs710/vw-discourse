We are prepared to engage on the basis of the expert’s clarification, but our position remains anchored in one test only: **does the package credibly restore margins and competitive cost levels, fast enough and durably enough to protect equity value?**

## Where we agree
- **A cosmetic settlement is unacceptable.**
- **Prolonged strike action destroys value.**
- **A no-closure package can be acceptable in principle** if it is deep, quantified, enforceable, and quick enough.
- **Governance realities matter**, so any deal must be executable within the company’s framework.

## Where we disagree
### 1) We do not accept “no closures” or “no compulsory redundancies” as fixed starting assumptions
Those are outcomes, not premises. If they block the required savings, they become a veto on restructuring. That is not acceptable to shareholders.

### 2) We do not accept a package that preserves the footprint and calls it transformation
If the result is mostly wage restraint, temporary concessions, and vague flexibility with only limited capacity reduction, then this is exactly the cosmetic settlement we reject. The share price will not reward symbolism.

### 3) We do not accept that social legitimacy can substitute for economic effectiveness
A politically comfortable deal that leaves the German cost base structurally uncompetitive will destroy long-term value. That is not responsible stewardship; it is deferral.

## Our position on the bridge options
### Option 1: Hybrid restructuring with conditional closure deferral
This is the only one that sounds potentially credible, **but only if the fallback is real, not symbolic**.

We would support a deferral only if:
- there are **hard savings and capacity targets**,
- a **fixed review date** is set,
- underdelivery triggers a **pre-agreed structural consequence**,
- and the company does not get trapped in endless renegotiation.

If the fallback is politically impossible to execute, then the “hybrid” is just a delay mechanism.

### Option 2: Site-preserving adjustment package with hard economics
Potentially acceptable **only if the numbers are truly equivalent** to a closure-based plan.

That means:
- measurable fixed-cost reduction,
- real capacity removal,
- durable labour-cost relief,
- enforceable productivity gains,
- and implementation speed that matters to the earnings trajectory.

Without that, it is not equivalent.

### Option 3: Phased settlement with voluntary workforce reduction first
This can be a useful first step, but **voluntary measures alone are not enough** unless they produce a clear and sizable structural reset. We would not support using voluntary programs as a way to avoid hard decisions indefinitely.

## What we need to see from management
Management should specify, in hard numbers:
- the annual recurring cost reduction required,
- the capacity reduction required,
- the timeframe for delivery,
- and the minimum acceptable package elements.

Not broad statements. Numbers.

## What we need from labour/state
If you want investors to back a no-closure path, then bring:
- quantified savings,
- measurable capacity cuts,
- enforceable labour-cost concessions,
- and a governance structure that guarantees delivery.

General promises of flexibility and partnership will not move us.

## Our red lines
We will not support:
- a settlement that leaves the cost base uncompetitive,
- temporary concessions that merely postpone the problem,
- a “win” that protects every site but undermines margins,
- or a package with no credible enforcement.

## Bottom line
We are not attached to closures as ideology. We are attached to **value preservation**.

If a no-closure package truly matches the economics, fine. But until that is proven with hard, enforceable numbers, **closure-based restructuring remains the benchmark**, not a threat to be dismissed.
