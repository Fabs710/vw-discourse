We are now beyond slogans. Good. But let me be equally clear: **the company still needs a structural, enforceable cost and capacity reset, not a comfort package that preserves the current problem.**

## Where I agree

I agree with everyone on several fundamentals:
- A **cosmetic settlement is unacceptable**.
- A **prolonged strike would be damaging**.
- Any solution must be **quantified, enforceable, and executable** under the company’s governance.
- A no-closure or hybrid route is **not ruled out in principle** if it truly delivers the economics.

That is the right level of seriousness.

## Where I disagree

### 1) I do not accept “no closures” or “no compulsory redundancies” as fixed premises
Union and works council keep treating these as starting conditions. They are not. They are outcomes.

If the required cost reset cannot be achieved otherwise, then plant rationalization and substantial workforce reduction remain necessary tools. I will not accept a framework that rules out the only measures that may actually restore competitiveness.

### 2) I do not accept site preservation as a substitute for competitiveness
State government, I understand the regional role. But preserving every site is not the same as preserving the company’s future. If the German footprint remains too expensive and underutilized, then we are just financing decline.

### 3) I do not accept “possible in principle” as enough
The expert has said a no-closure package can work in principle. Fine. But “in principle” does not pay for the investment pipeline to 2030.

What matters is whether the package:
- cuts fixed costs durably,
- removes real capacity,
- lowers labour costs structurally,
- and can be enforced without endless renegotiation.

If it cannot, then it is not an equivalent alternative. It is delay.

## My response to the bridge options

### 1) Quantified hybrid with a hard backstop
This is the only bridge option that is remotely serious — **if** the backstop is real.

I could support this only if it contains:
- hard annual recurring savings,
- binding site- and line-level capacity reductions,
- a fixed review date,
- and a fallback that is actually executable, not politically decorative.

If the fallback is merely a threat that cannot be used, then the hybrid is weak from day one.

### 2) No-closure first phase with equivalence test
This is acceptable only as a **short test**, not as a permanent shield.

If you want this route, then the equivalence threshold must be:
- pre-agreed,
- quantified,
- time-limited,
- and tied to automatic consequences if missed.

Otherwise it becomes an open-ended postponement of restructuring.

### 3) Site-preserving restructuring with governance triggers
I am open to triggers, but only if they are automatic and meaningful. If they just create another round of bargaining later, they are not triggers. They are delay dressed up as control.

## What management needs
Let me say this plainly: if labour and the state want us to consider a no-closure path, then bring a package that shows, in hard numbers:

- annual recurring savings,
- capacity reduction by site and line,
- labour-cost contribution,
- headcount reduction path,
- implementation timetable,
- and enforcement if delivery fails.

Not general pledges. Not moral pressure. Numbers.

## What I will not accept
I will not accept:
- a deal that preserves the footprint while calling it transformation;
- temporary concessions that expire before the next cycle;
- a package that protects every site but leaves the brand uncompetitive;
- or a result that starves future investment to preserve today’s overcapacity.

## My position remains unchanged
We need:
- **deep, durable cost reduction**
- **real capacity adjustment**
- **a material employee contribution**
- **executability inside governance**
- **freed-up funds for future products and technology to 2030**

I remain open on the instrument. I am not open on a weak result.
