We are now at the point where the debate must be judged against a simple standard: **who carries the burden, and is the result a real restructuring or a disguised one-sided cut?**

## Where I agree
I agree with management, investors, the family, and the state on three things:
- this cannot be a cosmetic settlement;
- the company needs a durable reset;
- and a prolonged strike would hurt everyone.

I also accept the expert’s core point: a no-closure package must be **deep, quantified, enforceable, and fast enough** to matter. We are not disputing that.

## Where I disagree

### 1) “No closures” is not a veto on restructuring
Management and investors keep presenting plant closures and compulsory redundancies as if they were the only serious instruments. They are not.

The expert has said clearly that a **no-closure** route is possible in principle if it is real restructuring, not a soft compromise. That is exactly why we insist that closures and forced layoffs must not be the default answer.

If the numbers can be met through:
- shift reductions,
- working-time changes,
- product and line consolidation,
- wage moderation,
- bonus restraint,
- voluntary departures,
- and productivity gains,

then there is no justification for telling workers that closures are inevitable.

### 2) “Equivalent economics” cannot mean equivalent damage
I agree with investors that the package must deliver hard savings. But I reject the idea that “equivalent economics” automatically means closures are justified.

Equivalent economics must mean:
- equivalent recurring savings,
- equivalent capacity reduction,
- equivalent durability.

It does **not** mean equivalent destruction of jobs, sites, trust, and collective bargaining standards.

A deal that protects equity value by forcing workers to absorb all the pain is not a durable solution. It is a conflict waiting to happen.

### 3) Site preservation is not a luxury; it is part of the industrial base
To the state and the family: I understand the concern for long-term competitiveness. But a company that destroys its own skilled workforce and weakens industrial capacity in Germany will not become stronger by doing so.

A viable restructuring must preserve:
- the collective wage framework,
- core sites,
- skills,
- and future investment capacity.

That is not sentimental. It is the basis for long-term industrial performance.

## On management
You say the burden is on labour to prove that a no-closure path works. I reject that framing.

The burden is on management to prove that closures are necessary, not merely convenient. So far, what we have heard is:
- costs are too high,
- footprint is too large,
- closure remains on the table.

That is not a necessity argument. That is a preference.

If you want our support, then bring a quantified package that proves:
- how much savings are required,
- how much capacity must be removed,
- and why those objectives cannot be met without closures.

Until then, closure-first remains unproven.

## On investors
You keep saying that “no closures” and “no compulsory redundancies” cannot be fixed starting assumptions. For us, they are not slogans. They are the minimum boundaries of a fair restructuring.

We are prepared to contribute. But we will not accept a package where:
- only workers give up income,
- only workers accept flexibility,
- only workers bear job insecurity,
- while management and owners contribute mainly rhetoric.

If you want a credible deal, then support one that shares the burden fairly:
- wage restraint or delayed wage growth,
- partial bonus suspension,
- working-time flexibility,
- reduced temporary labor first,
- management contribution,
- owner contribution,
- and investment commitments.

That is a fair test. Not closures by default.

## On the controlling family
You say you are open on the instrument but not the outcome. That is fine. We are too.

But “open on the instrument” cannot mean that workers are only allowed to negotiate how they lose jobs. We need a package that keeps plants open and avoids compulsory layoffs if it is to be socially and industrially legitimate.

If the family wants a serious settlement, it should back:
- site preservation,
- voluntary workforce reduction,
- capacity cuts through shifts and lines,
- and visible contributions from ownership and management.

That is how you show stewardship, not by holding closures over the table as leverage.

## On the regional state government
You are right to insist that the company must remain viable and that change cannot be blocked forever. We agree.

But then use your blocking position to support a **site-preserving restructuring**, not a closure-led one. The state’s role is not to help make closures easier. It is to defend the regional industrial base while enabling real change.

That means:
- no blanket plant closures now,
- no compulsory redundancies as the starting tool,
- and a binding package that preserves jobs while reducing costs structurally.

If you want social peace, do not make the workforce pay for an outcome you could have shaped differently.

## On the expert
Your analysis was useful, but I want to be precise about what it proves and what it does not.

You proved that:
- a no-closure package can work in principle,
- but it must be deep and enforceable,
- and it must reduce fixed costs and capacity materially.

You did **not** prove that closures are necessary. That distinction matters.

So the question now is not whether change is needed. It is whether the company is prepared to accept a fair, site-preserving restructuring that shares the burden instead of dumping it on workers.

## Our position
We remain open to a hard package, but only under these conditions:

1. **No compulsory redundancies**
2. **No plant closures as the starting instrument**
3. **Binding job and site security**
4. **Real capacity reduction through non-closure measures**
5. **A measurable employee contribution**
6. **Visible management and owner contributions**
7. **Investment commitments for future products**
8. **Preservation of the collective bargaining framework**

We can discuss:
- wage moderation,
- temporary or partial bonus restraint,
- working-time changes,
- shift reductions,
- voluntary departures,
- reduced agency labor,
- productivity measures,
- and a time-limited contribution from employees.

What we will not accept is a settlement where workers carry the main burden while closures remain the quiet fallback.

## What would move us
A serious package would need to show, in hard numbers:
- recurring savings,
- capacity reduction by site and line,
- the share of the contribution from management and owners,
- and binding protections against compulsory layoffs and closures.

Bring that, and we can negotiate constructively.

Do not bring a package that merely repackages layoffs and site closures as “responsible restructuring.” That will be resisted — strongly, and with the tools we have.
