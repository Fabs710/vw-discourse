We are now in the real conflict, so let me speak plainly.

## Where I agree
I agree with everyone on one point: **a cosmetic settlement is unacceptable**.  
I also agree that:
- the company needs a durable competitiveness reset,
- a prolonged strike would hurt all sides,
- and any solution must be enforceable, not just announced.

I also acknowledge the expert’s core technical point: a no-closure package must be **deep, quantified, and binding** if it is to be credible at all.

## Where I fundamentally disagree

### 1) I do not accept closures as the benchmark
Management and investors keep speaking as if plant closures are the natural reference point and everything else must “prove itself” against them. I reject that framing.

For us, closures are **not** the default instrument. They are the last resort, and in this company they would be a historic breach of trust. You have not shown that closures are necessary — only that they are convenient for cutting faster and harder.

That is not enough.

### 2) I do not accept compulsory redundancies as a starting tool
The same applies to forced layoffs. If you begin restructuring by demanding closures and compulsory redundancies, then you are not searching for a fair solution. You are shifting the burden onto the workforce first and asking for acceptance later.

That is exactly what we will not do.

### 3) I do not accept “equivalent economics” as a code word for equivalent damage
Yes, the economics must work. Of course they must. But “equivalent economics” does **not** mean:
- equivalent closures,
- equivalent job destruction,
- equivalent weakening of co-determination,
- equivalent damage to regional industrial capacity.

A viable no-closure package must deliver real savings and real capacity adjustment — but **without** destroying plants or forcing people out.

## To management

The burden is on management to prove that closures are **necessary**, not merely preferred.

So far, I hear:
- costs are too high,
- the footprint is too large,
- closures remain on the table.

That is not a necessity argument. That is a pressure tactic.

If you want our support, bring a package that:
- keeps every German plant open,
- avoids compulsory redundancies,
- reduces capacity through shifts, lines, and working time,
- lowers labor costs structurally,
- and includes visible management and owner contributions.

Until then, you have not made the case.

## To investors
You insist on hard numbers. Fine — so do we. But do not pretend that numbers alone justify closures.

If a package achieves:
- recurring savings,
- capacity reduction,
- durable labor-cost relief,
- and enforceable milestones,

then it is a restructuring. It is not “cosmetic” simply because it avoids plant shutdowns.

What we will not accept is a deal where the workforce makes the sacrifice, management applauds the discipline, and shareholders call that stewardship.

## To the controlling family
You say you are open on the instrument but not the outcome. That sounds balanced, but in practice it still leaves closures hanging over the table as a threat.

If you mean what you say about long-term value, then back a solution that preserves the industrial base, preserves jobs, and still cuts costs through non-closure measures. That would be stewardship.

Holding closures in reserve as leverage while calling for trust is not stewardship. It is coercion.

## To the state government
You are right to say the company must remain viable. We agree. But viability does **not** require accepting closures as the starting point.

Use your blocking position to defend:
- every German site,
- co-determination,
- the collective framework,
- and a real industrial future for the region.

A state shareholder should not help normalize plant closures when a serious alternative remains on the table.

## To the union
We are aligned on the essentials: workers must not be made to carry the full burden. But let me be precise: our position is not “no change.” It is “no one-sided change.”

We are prepared to discuss:
- wage moderation,
- temporary or partial bonus restraint,
- working-time changes,
- shift reductions,
- reduced agency labour,
- voluntary departures,
- redeployment,
- and productivity improvements.

But only if the result includes:
- no closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- binding job and site security,
- and preservation of the collective framework.

That is the minimum basis for a fair settlement.

## On the expert’s assessment
The expert confirmed that a no-closure package can work **in principle** if it is deep and enforceable. That is important.

But let us be clear about what that means: it means the company has options other than closures. It does **not** mean workers must accept closures because management prefers the speed and convenience.

The expert did not say closure is inevitable. So stop presenting it that way.

## What we are prepared to table
If the others are serious about a fair restructuring, then we are prepared to discuss:
- shift and working-time adjustments,

- wage restraint or deferred increases,
- temporary bonus moderation,
- voluntary departure programs,

That is a real contribution. It is not surrender.

We will not accept:
- any German plant closure,
- any compulsory redundancy,
- any plan that weakens co-determination,
- any “hybrid” that uses closure as a hidden threat,
- or any settlement that preserves shareholder comfort by destroying workforce security.

The company does need restructuring.  
But restructuring is not the same as closures and forced layoffs.

Our line remains unchanged:
