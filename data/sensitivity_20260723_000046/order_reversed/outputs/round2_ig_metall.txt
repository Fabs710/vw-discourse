The expert’s answer is useful, and it confirms something we have said from the start: **a no-closure solution can be credible if, and only if, it is deep, quantified, enforceable, and genuinely shared**.

That is exactly why we will not accept the current framing that treats closures and forced layoffs as the benchmark and everything else as merely “delay.”

## Where I agree

I agree with management, investors, the family, and the state on these points:
- this cannot be a cosmetic settlement;
- the company needs a durable reset;
- vague productivity promises are worthless;
- and prolonged conflict would hurt everyone.

I also accept the expert’s point that a no-closure package has to be more than words. It must include:
- real capacity reduction,
- real labor-cost relief,
- headcount reduction through non-compulsory means,
- and binding milestones.

That is fair.

## Where I disagree

What I do **not** accept is the leap from “the company needs savings” to “therefore plant closures and compulsory redundancies are legitimate starting points.”

The expert did **not** say closures are the only viable route. In fact, the expert said the opposite: **a no-closure package can work in principle** if it is sufficiently deep and enforceable.

That matters. Because it means there is still a serious negotiated path that protects jobs and sites while delivering real economics.

So let me be precise:

- **No plant closures**
- **No compulsory redundancies**
- **No hollowing out of the collective bargaining framework**
- **No settlement where workers carry the full burden**

Those are not bargaining flourishes. They are our minimum line.

## On “equivalent economics”

The investors and management keep using that phrase as if it automatically justifies closures. It does not.

“Equivalent economics” means:
- equivalent savings,
- equivalent capacity adjustment,
- equivalent durability.

It does **not** mean equivalent destruction of industrial capacity or equivalent social damage.

If closures are proposed, then the question is not whether they are blunt and efficient in the abstract. The question is whether they are necessary **after** exhausting negotiated alternatives.

And the expert’s answer gives us a clear basis to say: **negotiated alternatives still exist**.

## What a serious labor-side package looks like

We are not denying the need for contribution. We are saying the contribution must be shared and structured.

We can consider a package with:
- wage restraint or delayed wage growth,
- temporary or partial bonus suspension,
- capacity reduction through shift and working-time changes,
- voluntary departure programs,
- reduction of agency and temporary labor first,
- productivity improvements,
- flexibility gains,
- and a temporary employee contribution if it is tied to site and job security.

But in exchange, we require:
- **binding no-closure commitments**
- **no compulsory redundancies**
- **site protection**
- **a collective framework that remains intact**
- **management and owner contributions visible in the package**
- **investment commitments for future products**

That is not “preserving the status quo.” That is shared sacrifice with a future.

## On the expert’s implementation risks

The expert is right that a no-closure package has risks. So does a closure-based package:
- legal deadlock,
- industrial action,
- political backlash,
- supply disruption,
- and damage to trust and skills.

The key difference is that closures create a permanent rupture in the workforce and regional base. That is why we oppose them.

If management wants certainty, then it should stop using closures as a threat and instead negotiate an agreement that workers can actually support and implement. Industrial peace is not obtained by intimidation.

## To management

You keep saying the burden of proof is on us. Fine — but the burden of proof is also on you to show that closures are truly necessary, not merely convenient.

If a credible alternative exists, then insisting on closures is not economics; it is a power move.

We will not accept that.

## To the investors and family

You are right to demand measurable savings. But you must also accept that there is a difference between:
- restructuring the company, and
- dismantling social partnership.

If you support a closure-first path when a negotiated no-closure route can still deliver the economics, then you are choosing conflict over viability.

That is not a prudent long-term ownership strategy.

## To the state government

You said no blanket closure decision and no outright veto on change. We can work with that. But then be consistent: if you recognize the governance realities and the social costs, you should support a settlement that explicitly rules out forced layoffs and plant closures while still delivering quantified savings.

That is the responsible middle ground — not between realism and denial, but between competitiveness and social cohesion.

## Our current position

We are prepared to negotiate a serious package, but only on this basis:
1. **No compulsory redundancies**
2. **No plant closures**
3. **Capacity reduction through agreed non-closure measures**
4. **Shared labor-cost contribution**
5. **Management and owner contribution**
6. **Binding job and site security**
7. **Durable collective-bargaining framework**
8. **Investment commitments tied to the deal**

If the others can bring a quantified package that meets those conditions, we will negotiate it in good faith.

If they cannot, then they should be honest: they are not seeking a fair restructuring, but a one-sided cost shift onto workers. In that case, they should expect resistance — including industrial action.

We are not blocking change. We are blocking a restructuring where employees are asked to absorb all the pain while others call that “inevitable.”
