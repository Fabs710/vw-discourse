I will be equally direct.

The expert’s input is useful, but it does **not** move me off the core line.

## Where I agree
I agree with everyone on three points:
- this cannot be a cosmetic settlement;
- the company needs durable competitiveness;
- prolonged conflict is costly and should be avoided if — and only if — there is a serious alternative.

I also accept the expert’s technical point that a no-closure package must be **deep, quantified, enforceable**, and not just a collection of hopes.

## Where I disagree
What I do **not** accept is the attempt to turn that technical finding into a political conclusion that plant closures and compulsory redundancies are therefore acceptable or inevitable.

They are **not** acceptable.

The expert said clearly that a no-closure solution can work in principle if it is deep enough. That matters. It means the discussion is **not** “closures versus fantasy.” It is “hard negotiated restructuring versus destructive one-sided restructuring.”

And on that point, I am not moving.

## On management
You keep saying “no closures” is a veto on restructuring. No.  
What is a veto on restructuring is insisting that the workforce must accept:
- plant closures,
- compulsory redundancies,
- and the dismantling of job security,
as the price of management’s strategic failures.

That is not a restructuring plan. That is a transfer of risk and pain onto employees.

If you want a serious deal, then bring one that achieves the required savings **without** closures and **without** forced layoffs. The expert showed that this is possible in principle. So stop presenting closure as the only adult answer.

## On investors
You keep repeating “equivalent economics” as if that justifies any means. It does not.

Equivalent economics cannot mean:
- equivalent destruction of sites,
- equivalent damage to trust,
- equivalent weakening of skills,
- equivalent breaking of co-determination.

We are prepared to discuss equivalent **savings**, not equivalent harm.

If you want real value protection, then support a package that preserves the industrial base and still reduces cost. A deal that triggers years of labour conflict and destroys trust is not value-preserving; it is self-defeating.

## On the controlling family
You say closures are not an end in themselves, and that is correct. Then treat them as what they are: a last resort, not a negotiating club.

If ownership wants durable competitiveness, it should back a settlement that keeps the plants open, protects jobs, and still delivers measurable savings through:
- capacity adjustments by shift and line,
- working-time flexibility,
- wage restraint,
- reduced temporary labour,
- management and owner contributions,
- and investment commitments.

That is a serious ownership strategy. Closure-first is not.

## On the regional state government
You are right to insist that the company must remain viable and that change cannot be blocked indefinitely. But you are wrong if you think this means accepting closures as the default benchmark.

The state has both a political and governance responsibility to defend the regional industrial base. That responsibility is not fulfilled by saying “show us the numbers” and then tolerating plant shutdowns if management puts enough pressure on the table.

You should be using your blocking position to insist on a settlement that:
- rules out plant closures,
- rules out compulsory redundancies,
- preserves co-determination,
- and still delivers a quantified cost reset.

That is not idealism. That is responsible stewardship.

## On the expert’s assessment
The expert was very clear on one critical point: a no-closure alternative can be credible if it includes:
- quantified capacity reduction,
- material labor-cost relief,
- headcount reduction through non-compulsory means,
- binding enforcement,
- and investment commitments.

That is the path we are prepared to negotiate.

What we are **not** prepared to negotiate is the idea that the only meaningful restructuring is one built on closures and forced layoffs. The expert did not say that. Management is pushing that interpretation because it is the easiest way to shift the burden.

## Our position remains unchanged
We are prepared to negotiate a hard package, but only under these conditions:

- **No German plant closures**
- **No compulsory redundancies**
- **Binding job and site guarantees to 2030**
- **Real capacity reduction through non-closure measures**
- **A measurable employee contribution**
- **Visible management and owner contribution**
- **Investment commitments for the future**
- **Preservation of co-determination and the collective framework**

That is the minimum basis for a settlement.

## What we will resist
If management comes back with a closure-led plan, we will resist it.
If investors try to force “equivalent economics” to mean equivalent layoffs, we will resist that.
If ownership treats co-determination as a hurdle to be bypassed, we will resist that.
If the state retreats from defending the industrial base, we will make that politically visible.
And if necessary, we will continue to mobilize the workforce.

## Bottom line
We are not blocking change. We are blocking a restructuring that treats workers and their sites as disposable.

The company has options. The expert confirmed that.  
So let’s stop pretending that plant closures and compulsory redundancies are the only serious path.

They are not. And we will not accept them.
