We agree on one thing: this cannot be a cosmetic settlement.

But let me be equally clear: **we will not accept a restructuring where workers carry the full burden while management, owners, and capital markets get a clean balance-sheet story.**

### Where we agree
- The company needs a **durable** solution, not a short pause.
- There must be a **real contribution to competitiveness**.
- Prolonged conflict is bad for everyone.
- Empty promises are not enough; if there are savings, they must be **verifiable and enforceable**.

### Where we disagree
The management framing is too one-sided. You talk about “structural correction,” but your model still points first to:
- **plant closures**
- **forced job cuts**
- **lower wages / removal of guarantees**

That is exactly the line we cannot accept as the default answer.

Why not?

Because if the restructuring means:
- jobs are destroyed,
- sites are shut,
- workers lose protections,
- and the pain is shifted almost entirely onto employees,

then that is not a fair transformation. It is simply cost dumping.

### On closures
We are not treating closures as a bargaining chip we can ignore, but we are saying clearly:  
**no compulsory redundancies and no plant closures as the solution.**

If management insists that closures are the only route, then management has not done its job in building a socially and industrially credible alternative. There are other instruments on the table:
- capacity reductions through agreed production shifts,
- working-time adjustments,
- temporary sacrifices in bonuses,
- productivity and flexibility measures,
- an employee contribution,
- and investment commitments tied to safeguarding sites.

That is the kind of package we sign on to, because it spreads the burden fairly.

### To the investors
You are right that vague promises are worthless. But “equivalent economics” does **not** mean equivalent destruction of jobs and sites. It means equivalent savings, achieved through a different mix of measures.

If you want durable competitiveness, then accept that there must be **shared sacrifice**:
- management must contribute,
- owners must accept lower near-term returns,
- and employees can contribute too.

What we will not accept is a deal where the workforce pays, and everyone else calls that realism.

### To the controlling family
You say closures are not an end in themselves. Good. Then do not make them the starting point. If there is a credible negotiated route that protects core sites and jobs while delivering real savings, then support it. That is what responsible ownership looks like.

### To management
You say weak compromise is worse than prolonged strikes. We disagree on the premise. A **fair compromise** is stronger than a unilateral cut plan that triggers years of conflict, erosion of trust, and industrial destabilization.

We are ready to negotiate a serious contribution:
- wage restraint,
- capacity reduction,
- flexibility,
- and perhaps a temporary employee financial contribution.

But only if the outcome includes:
- **site security**
- **job security**
- **no forced redundancies**
- **no plant closures**
- and a durable collective framework

### Our bottom line
We are open to pain-sharing. We are not open to workers being made the sole shock absorber.

If management wants a settlement, then bring a package that:
1. protects jobs and sites,
2. avoids compulsory redundancies,
3. avoids plant closures,
4. includes a real and measurable labour-cost contribution,
5. and includes owner/management contributions as well.

If that is not possible, then you should expect resistance — not because we are refusing reality, but because we are refusing to let the workforce pay for a crisis it did not create.
