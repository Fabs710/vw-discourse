As the requested independent restructuring and labour-economics specialist, I’ll stay on the technical question: **what minimum package would make a no-closure alternative economically credible versus closure-based restructuring?**

## Short answer
A no-closure package can be credible **only if it delivers most of the recurring benefit of closure-based restructuring through other instruments** and does so **quickly enough, durably enough, and with low enough execution risk**.

In practice, I would look for all of the following:

### 1) Recurring annual savings
A credible no-closure package must typically capture **roughly 70–100% of the recurring operating improvement** that management seeks from closures, depending on:
- the scale of the overcapacity,
- the existing fixed-cost burden,
- and whether the plants remain fully loaded or are partially consolidated.

If the gap is materially larger than that, the no-closure route is usually just deferral.

### 2) Material capacity reduction
It is not enough to reduce wages or add flexibility. A credible package needs **real capacity removal**, for example through:
- elimination of shifts,
- line consolidation,
- product rationalization,
- reduced overtime reliance,
- and lower build volumes at selected sites.

If capacity remains structurally above demand, the savings will leak away.

### 3) Durable labour-cost relief
The package must include structural rather than temporary relief:
- wage moderation or delayed wage growth,
- bonus restraint,
- removal or reduction of legacy protections,
- more flexible working time,
- lower staffing ratios,
- and lower agency/temporary labour use.

Temporary concessions alone usually do not change the long-run cost base enough.

### 4) Headcount reduction path
A no-closure package needs a **credible workforce reduction pathway**. If compulsory redundancies are excluded, then the package must show enough:
- voluntary departures,
- early retirement,
- internal redeployment,
- natural attrition,
- and hiring restraint.

Otherwise fixed labour costs will not fall sufficiently.

### 5) Implementation certainty
A technically good package can still fail if it is not executable. I would require:
- binding milestones,
- site-by-site targets,
- monitoring and audit rights,
- and a fallback mechanism if delivery misses plan.

Without that, investors will discount the savings heavily.

---

## Practical threshold view
If I were assessing a package on technical grounds, I would use this rough screen:

### No-closure package is plausible if:
- it delivers **most of the targeted recurring savings**,
- it reduces **effective capacity** meaningfully,
- it lowers **unit labour cost** structurally,
- and it can be implemented within **12–24 months** with low legal/governance friction.

### No-closure package is weak if:
- savings depend mostly on one-off items,
- capacity reductions are only nominal,
- there is no credible workforce reduction path,
- or the “fallback” is politically unlikely to ever be used.

### Closure-based restructuring is usually superior if:
- fixed-cost relief from closures is large and immediate,
- excess capacity is concentrated in one or two sites,
- and the alternative would require very deep concessions with high strike or non-delivery risk.

## Relative economics: closure vs hybrid vs no-closure

### Closure-based plan
**Pros**
- Largest and most certain fixed-cost removal
- Clear capacity reset
- Often strongest margin impact

**Cons**
- Highest one-off restructuring and social costs
- Higher execution/political risk
- Greater strike and governance friction
- Long lead time if product transfer is needed

### Hybrid plan
**Pros**
- Can deliver much of the economics with lower confrontation
- Keeps optionality
- Easier to phase in

**Cons**
- Can become a compromise that is neither fast nor deep
- Requires a strong fallback to avoid underdelivery
- Savings may be less certain than closure

### No-closure plan
**Pros**
- Lowest political and social disruption
- Preserves site capabilities and industrial footprint
- Often faster to agree initially

**Cons**
- Usually weaker on fixed-cost removal
- Requires unusually deep labour and productivity concessions
- Higher risk of savings erosion over time
- If not tightly enforced, it becomes delay rather than restructuring

---

## My technical judgment
A **true no-closure package** is economically credible only if it is not framed as “preserve everything and cut costs somehow.” It must instead function as a **capacity-and-cost redesign** with:
- fewer shifts,
- fewer positions,
- lower labour cost,
- lower volume at selected plants,
- and strict delivery controls.

If those elements are missing, then the package is unlikely to be equivalent to closures in lifetime economics.

If those elements are present, a no-closure route can be rational — especially if the company values:
- speed of labor peace,
- lower one-off costs,
- lower legal/gov­ernance friction,
- and preservation of future optionality.

## The minimum package components I would require
For a no-closure package to be judged genuinely equivalent, I would want:

1. **Annual recurring savings target** stated explicitly  
2. **Capacity reduction by site and model line**  
3. **Headcount reduction path** with quantified exit mechanisms  
4. **Labour-cost relief** that is structural, not temporary  
5. **Investment commitments** tied to delivery  
6. **Milestones and audit rights**  
7. **Automatic fallback** if targets are missed
