We are at the point where rhetoric has to give way to numbers.

## Where we agree
We agree with management, the family, the expert, and even the state on one basic point: **this cannot be a cosmetic settlement**. If the package does not restore competitiveness, it will destroy equity value.

We also agree that:
- a prolonged strike is value-destructive,
- any deal must be enforceable,
- and a “no-closure” label means nothing unless it delivers real recurring savings and capacity reduction.

## Where we disagree
### 1) “No closures” is not a principle we will accept as a constraint
We do **not** accept no-closures or no compulsory redundancies as fixed starting assumptions. Those are outcomes, not premises. If they block the required reset, they become a veto on restructuring.

That is not acceptable to shareholders.

### 2) Site preservation is not a substitute for margin recovery
We do **not** support a settlement that preserves the footprint while leaving the cost base structurally uncompetitive. A deal that looks socially balanced but fails economically is not a compromise; it is deferred loss.

### 3) Social legitimacy does not override lifetime economics
We understand the political and co-determination constraints. But governance constraints do not change the underlying economics. If the company cannot remove enough fixed cost and capacity, the market will punish it, whatever the social optics.

## On the current bridge options
### 1) Quantified hybrid with a hard backstop
This is the only route that sounds potentially credible to us, **but only if the backstop is real**.

We would consider it if it includes:
- hard annual recurring savings,
- meaningful capacity reduction,
- a fixed review date,
- and an enforceable fallback if delivery misses the target.

If the fallback is not legally and politically executable, then it is just a delay mechanism.

### 2) No-closure first phase with equivalence test
This is acceptable only as a **test**, not as a disguised permanent exemption from hard decisions.

We would back it only if:
- the equivalence threshold is quantified in advance,
- the time horizon is short enough to matter to earnings,
- and failure automatically reopens structural options.

If not, it becomes open-ended postponement.

### 3) Site-preserving restructuring with governance triggers
This is possible only if the triggers are automatic and meaningful. If they merely invite more negotiation later, they are not triggers; they are hope.

## What we need from management
Management must state, in hard numbers:
- annual recurring EBITDA improvement required,
- capacity reduction required,
- timing of savings,
- one-off restructuring cost,
- and the minimum package elements that make a no-closure path acceptable.

Not slogans. Numbers.

## What we need from labour and the state
If you want investors to accept a no-closure route, bring a package that is:
- quantified,
- enforceable,
- fast enough,
- and genuinely capable of restoring competitive margins.

That means real labour-cost relief, real capacity cuts, and clear implementation discipline.

General commitments to flexibility and partnership will not move us.

## What we will not support
We will not support:
- a cosmetic settlement,
- a footprint-preservation deal that leaves the cost base untouched,
- temporary concessions with no structural effect,
- or any package that forces shareholders to finance underperformance indefinitely.

## Bottom line
We are not attached to closures as ideology. We are attached to **risk-adjusted value preservation**.

If a no-closure or hybrid package genuinely matches the economics, is enforceable, and restores margins fast enough, we can support it. But until that is proven with hard numbers, **closure-based restructuring remains the benchmark**.
