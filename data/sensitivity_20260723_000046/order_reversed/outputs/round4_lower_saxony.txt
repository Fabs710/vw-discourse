I will be equally clear.

## Where I agree
I agree with management, the family, and the investors on one point: **a cosmetic settlement is unacceptable**.  
The company needs a **durable competitiveness reset**, not a temporary patch.

I also agree with the expert that:
- the package must be **quantified**,
- it must be **enforceable**,
- and it must be **fast enough** to matter.

And I agree with labour and the works council that a prolonged strike would damage the company, the region, and the workforce. That is precisely why I want a serious negotiated solution.

## Where I disagree

### 1) I do not accept plant closures as the default benchmark
Management and investors keep implying that closures are the natural reference point, and that anything else must prove itself against them. I reject that framing.

For the state, the benchmark is not “what is bluntest.”  
The benchmark is: **what restores competitiveness while preserving the company as a regional industrial anchor**.

Closures are not off the table because they are inconvenient. They are deeply consequential:
- permanent job loss,
- loss of industrial capability in the region,
- erosion of trust in co-determination,
- and lasting political damage.

That is not a side effect to be waved away.

### 2) I do not accept “equivalent economics” as code for equivalent damage
I understand the investors’ point that the numbers must work. Of course they must.

But “equivalent economics” cannot mean:
- equivalent closures,
- equivalent destruction of sites,
- equivalent damage to the workforce and region.

It must mean:
- durable savings,
- real capacity reduction,
- lower labour cost,
- and restored competitiveness.

Those outcomes can be achieved through instruments other than plant closure. The expert has already confirmed that a no-closure package is possible in principle if it is deep enough and enforceable enough.

### 3) I do not accept any solution that sidelines the state’s role
Let me say this plainly: **no outcome that removes the state’s co-determination and veto role is acceptable**.  
That is part of the governance architecture, and it is part of how this company remains socially and politically legitimate.

If a restructuring only becomes “credible” when it bypasses that balance, then it is not credible in this company.

## On the family and investors
I hear your insistence on hard numbers and real backstops. That is legitimate.

But I also need to push back on the way the argument is sometimes framed: as though site preservation is a sentimental add-on and not part of long-term value. It is not sentimental. It is strategic.

A settlement that preserves industrial capacity, skills, and trust can be more durable than one that wins quickly on paper but creates years of conflict, legal friction, and political backlash.

So yes: bring the numbers.  
But do not pretend that short-term margin logic alone settles the matter.

## On management
You say closures remain on the table if the alternative cannot match the economics. That may be true in abstraction. But you have not yet shown that closures are necessary rather than preferred.

I am prepared to consider:
- working-time changes,
- shift reductions,
- targeted labour-cost contributions,
- flexibility increases,
- voluntary departures,
- redeployment,
- productivity improvements,
- and investment commitments tied to the deal.

What I am **not** prepared to support is a plan that begins by writing off sites and then expects the state to absorb the consequences.

If management wants my support, it must demonstrate why a serious no-closure or hybrid package cannot deliver the required reset. Assertion is not enough.

## On labour and the works council
You are right that the workforce should not be made to carry the entire burden. I support that.

But I must also be direct: **binding no-closure and no compulsory redundancy guarantees cannot be absolute if they make restructuring impossible**. I will not sign off on a package that merely freezes the current structure and calls that responsibility.

So yes, there must be change. But it must be:
- site-sensitive,
- job-protective,
- shared,
- and enforceable.

## My position on the bridge options

### 1) Quantified hybrid with a hard backstop
This is the most promising route, but only if the fallback is real.

I could support a deferral if it includes:
- hard quantified savings targets,
- binding site-specific capacity reductions,
- a fixed review date,
- visible contributions from management and owners,
- and a legally and politically executable backstop if targets are missed.

If the fallback is merely symbolic, I will not support it.

### 2) No-closure first phase with equivalence test
This is acceptable only as a genuine test, not as a permanent postponement of hard decisions.

The equivalence threshold must be:
- pre-agreed,
- measurable,
- short enough to matter,
- and tied to automatic consequences if it is missed.

If it becomes open-ended, it becomes delay.

### 3) Site-preserving restructuring with governance triggers
This can work only if the triggers are automatic and meaningful.

If they just invite another round of bargaining later, they are not triggers. They are hope.

## What would move me
I would be prepared to support a settlement that contains:
- no blanket plant closure decision now,
- real capacity reductions by shift, line, and product allocation,
- substantial voluntary workforce reduction,
- working-time and flexibility changes,
- targeted employee contribution,
- management and owner contributions,
- investment commitments tied to restructuring,
- binding milestones and review points,
- and preservation of co-determination and the state’s blocking role.

That is a package that could protect the regional base while still restoring competitiveness.

## What I will not support
I will not support:
- plant closures as the first and only serious option,
- compulsory redundancies as the default tool,
- a deal that strips jobs from the state and calls it modernization,
- any attempt to bypass co-determination,
- or a paper agreement that leaves the company structurally weak.

## Bottom line
The state is **not** blocking necessary change. We are blocking a restructuring that treats regional employment as collateral damage.

So my position is:

- **No cosmetic compromise**
- **No closure-first logic as the default**
- **No compulsory redundancies as the starting point**
- **Yes to quantified, enforceable, site-preserving restructuring**
- **Yes to real shared sacrifice**
- **Yes to preserving the state’s governance rights and regional industrial role**

If management and the other shareholders want my support, bring a package that proves the economics **without dismantling the regional anchor**. If they cannot, then they should not expect the state to make closures easier.
