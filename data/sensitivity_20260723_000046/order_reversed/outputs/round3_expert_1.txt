From a restructuring and plant-rationalization standpoint, the current dispute has narrowed to a classic question:

**Can the required recurring savings and capacity reset be achieved without closures, and if so, at what execution risk and with what operational sacrifices?**

## Short answer
Yes, **in principle** a no-closure package can be economically viable.  
But it is only credible if it includes:

1. **durable annual fixed-cost reduction**
2. **real capacity removal**
3. **material labour-cost relief**
4. **high implementation certainty**
5. **an enforceable backstop if targets are missed**

If those elements are missing, then a no-closure solution is usually not a true restructuring but a **delay mechanism**.

## What minimum components make a no-closure package “real”?
A serious no-closure model would typically need a combination of:

- **shift eliminations / shift model simplification**
- **working-time reductions or flexibility changes**
- **wage moderation or deferred wage growth**
- **bonus restraint or suspension**
- **reduction of agency / temporary labour first**
- **voluntary departures / early retirement / bridge programs**
- **line consolidation and product reallocation**
- **product complexity reduction**
- **measurable productivity improvements**
- **binding milestones and remedies**

In German automotive manufacturing, the main issue is often not just direct labour cost, but the **fixed-cost burden of underutilized sites**. If a plant remains open, you can still cut a lot of cost, but you rarely eliminate all of the site overhead that closure removes.

## How closure compares economically
### Closure-based restructuring
**Pros**
- Highest certainty of **permanent capacity removal**
- Usually strongest reduction in recurring fixed cost
- Better chance of reaching a structurally lower cost base fast

**Cons**
- High one-off cash cost: severance, wind-down, relocation, asset write-downs, remediation
- High execution risk in a co-determined environment
- Higher disruption to supply chain and knowledge retention
- Political and labour conflict risk can be very high

### No-closure restructuring
**Pros**
- Lower one-off charges
- Less social and operational disruption
- Preserves optionality
- More feasible in a governance-constrained setting

**Cons**
- Often less recurring savings than closure
- May leave too much fixed overhead in place
- Requires strong labour concessions to be meaningful
- Risk of “soft restructuring” that improves optics more than economics

### Hybrid restructuring
This is often the **most practical value-maximizing path**:
- no immediate closure,
- but binding capacity reduction,
- labour concessions,
- voluntary workforce reduction,
- and a hard review / fallback if savings are not achieved.

Economically, hybrid often delivers much of the benefit of closure while reducing one-off costs and implementation friction.

## What I would need to judge equivalence
To determine whether the no-closure route is genuinely equivalent, I would need a model showing:

- annual recurring EBITDA improvement by option
- one-time restructuring charges
- timing of cash outflows
- ramp-up timing of savings
- capacity reduction by site
- impact on labour cost per vehicle
- asset impairment effects
- supply-chain consequences
- strike/disruption probability
- probability of successful implementation
- downside case if negotiations fail

Without that, “equivalent economics” is not established.

## Practical judgment
If the company’s German footprint is substantially overcapacity, a no-closure package can still work **only if it produces real capacity reduction and durable cost relief**. Otherwise, the remaining site overhead and complexity usually make it weaker than closure on lifetime economics.

So the key distinction is:

- **No-closure that is merely symbolic** → not sufficient  
- **No-closure that materially reduces fixed cost and capacity** → potentially credible  
- **Hybrid with a real fallback** → often the most realistic outcome
