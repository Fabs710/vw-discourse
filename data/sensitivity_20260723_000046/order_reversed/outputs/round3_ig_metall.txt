Let me be equally direct.

## Where I agree
I agree with management, investors, the family, and the state on three points:
- this cannot be a cosmetic settlement;
- the company needs a durable competitiveness reset;
- and a prolonged strike would damage everyone.

I also accept the expert’s technical point that a no-closure package must be **deep, quantified, enforceable, and fast enough** to matter. We are not denying that.

## Where I disagree
What I do **not** accept is the leap from “the company needs savings” to “therefore closures and forced layoffs are legitimate starting points.”

The expert did **not** say that closures are the only viable route. In fact, the expert said the opposite: **a no-closure package can work in principle** if it is sufficiently deep and enforceable.

That matters. Because it means the discussion is still about **which instruments are legitimate and fair**, not about accepting plant closures as the default.

## On management
You keep saying no closures and no compulsory redundancies are a veto on restructuring. No.  
What is a veto on restructuring is insisting that the workforce must absorb:
- plant closures,
- forced layoffs,
- and the dismantling of collective guarantees,
while management and owners present only words.

That is not shared sacrifice. That is one-sided pain.

If you want a serious deal, then bring one that achieves the required savings **without** closures and **without** compulsory redundancies. The expert showed that this is possible in principle. So stop presenting closure as the only adult answer.

## On investors
You keep repeating “equivalent economics” as if that automatically justifies closures. It does not.

Equivalent economics must mean:
- equivalent savings,
- equivalent capacity adjustment,
- equivalent durability.

It does **not** mean equivalent destruction of sites, trust, skills, or co-determination.

We are prepared to discuss equivalent **savings**, not equivalent damage.

If you want value protection, then support a package that preserves the industrial base and still reduces cost. A deal that triggers years of labour conflict and destroys trust is not value-preserving; it is self-defeating.

## On the controlling family
You say closures are not an end in themselves, and I agree. Then treat them as what they are: a **last resort**, not a negotiating club.

If ownership wants durable competitiveness, it should back a settlement that keeps plants open, protects jobs, and still delivers measurable savings through:
- capacity adjustments by shift and line,
- working-time flexibility,
- wage restraint,
- reduced temporary labour,
- management and owner contributions,
- and investment commitments.

That is a serious ownership strategy. Closure-first is not.

## On the regional state government
You are right to insist that the company must remain viable and that change cannot be blocked indefinitely. But you are wrong if you think that means accepting closures as the default benchmark.

The state has both a political and governance responsibility to defend the regional industrial base. That responsibility is not fulfilled by saying “show us the numbers” and then tolerating plant shutdowns if management puts enough pressure on the table.

Use your blocking position to insist on a settlement that:
- rules out plant closures,
- rules out compulsory redundancies,
- preserves co-determination,
- and still delivers quantified savings.

That is responsible stewardship.

## On the expert’s assessment
The expert was very clear on one critical point: a no-closure alternative can be credible if it includes:
- quantified capacity reduction,
- material labour-cost relief,
- headcount reduction through non-compulsory means,
- binding enforcement,
- and investment commitments.

That is the path we are prepared to negotiate.

What we are **not** prepared to negotiate is the idea that the only meaningful restructuring is one built on closures and forced layoffs. The expert did not say that. Management is pushing that interpretation because it is the easiest way to shift the burden.

## Our position remains unchanged
We are prepared to negotiate a hard package, but only under these conditions:

1. **No compulsory redundancies**
2. **No plant closures**
3. **Capacity reduction through agreed non-closure measures**
4. **A measurable employee contribution**
5. **Visible management and owner contribution**
6. **Binding job and site security**
7. **Durable collective-bargaining framework**
8. **Investment commitments tied to the deal**

If the others can bring a quantified package that meets those conditions, we will negotiate it in good faith.

If they cannot, then they should be honest: they are not seeking a fair restructuring, but a one-sided cost shift onto workers. In that case, they should expect resistance — including industrial action.

## What we are prepared to table now
We are not saying “no change.” We are saying “no unilateral pain.”

We can put these instruments on the table:
- wage restraint or delayed wage growth;
- temporary or partial bonus suspension;
- capacity reduction through shift and working-time changes;
- voluntary departure programs;
- reduction of agency and temporary labor first;
- productivity improvements with measurable milestones;
- flexibility gains;
- and a temporary employee contribution if it is tied to site and job security.

That is a real contribution. It is not a blank cheque.

## What would move us
A package can move us only if it includes:
- **binding no-closure commitments**
- **no compulsory redundancies**
- **site protection**
- **management and owner contributions**
- **investment commitments for future products**
- **enforceable milestones**
- **and a collective bargaining result that remains intact**

Outside those conditions, there is no basis for us to shift.

## Bottom line
We are not blocking change. We are blocking a restructuring where employees are asked to absorb all the pain while others call that “inevitable.”

The company has options. The expert confirmed that.  
So let’s stop pretending that closures and compulsory redundancies are the only serious path.

They are not. And we will not accept them.
