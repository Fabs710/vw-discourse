From a restructuring and corporate-finance perspective, the right way to assess this is not “closures vs no closures” as an ideological choice, but **which package most credibly delivers the required lifetime economics after all one-off and recurring effects are included**.

## Bottom line
A **no-closure** solution is economically possible in principle, but only if it delivers:
1. **durable annual fixed-cost reduction**,  
2. **real capacity removal or production migration**,  
3. **material labour-cost relief**,  
4. **high implementation certainty**, and  
5. **a credible enforcement mechanism**.

If those are not achieved, then a no-closure package is usually just a **delay mechanism** and will underperform a closure-based or hybrid restructuring on lifetime value.

## How the economics typically compare

### 1) Closure-based restructuring
**Economic strengths**
- Usually provides the **largest and most certain recurring savings**.
- Removes fixed cost structurally: plant overhead, utilities, maintenance, local logistics, and often a portion of indirect labour.
- Can improve asset utilization at remaining sites.
- Sends a strong signal to the market if executed cleanly.

**Economic costs**
- High one-time cash outflow: severance, site wind-down, environmental remediation, contract termination, relocation, write-downs, and transition costs.
- Execution risk is significant because of co-determination, legal process, and labour conflict.
- Can trigger supply-chain disruption and loss of productive know-how.
- May destroy option value if demand recovers or EV product allocation changes.

**When it wins economically**
- When the cost gap is large and persistent.
- When the plant is structurally underutilized.
- When alternative cost cuts cannot close the gap fast enough.
- When management needs a decisive balance-sheet and margin reset.

### 2) Hybrid restructuring
This is usually the **most realistic value-maximizing path** in a constrained governance environment.

Typical features:
- No immediate closure.
- Capacity reduction through one or more of: shift removal, line consolidation, product reassignment, SKU simplification, reduced overtime, lower staffing intensity.
- Labour contribution through wage moderation, working-time flexibility, removal of legacy guarantees, and voluntary workforce reduction.
- A hard review date and fallback if savings do not materialize.

**Economic strengths**
- Captures a meaningful part of closure economics without fully paying the closure cost upfront.
- Often has lower execution friction than immediate closure.
- Preserves strategic optionality while still delivering structural savings.
- Can be phased to align with product-cycle timing and capex plans.

**Economic risks**
- If the fallback is weak or politically unexecutable, the package becomes non-credible.
- Savings can be partially offset by complexity, lower plant efficiency, and ongoing fixed overhead.
- Requires tight milestone tracking and governance discipline.

**When it wins economically**
- When management can quantify the required savings and labour can deliver enough recurring concessions to reach them.
- When a closure would be too costly, too slow, or too risky relative to the value at stake.
- When the company wants to preserve some industrial flexibility while resetting costs.

### 3) No-closure restructuring
This can work, but only under strict conditions.

**What must be true**
- The company must be able to **remove enough cost base without closing a site**.
- Capacity must still fall in a real way: fewer shifts, less product complexity, lower labour intensity, and possibly transfer of volume to lower-cost locations.
- The employee package must be durable, not temporary.
- The accounting savings must translate into **cash savings**, not just headline P&L improvement.

**Economic strengths**
- Avoids closure-related one-time charges.
- Reduces political, social, and operational disruption.
- Preserves plant optionality and future product allocation.

**Economic weaknesses**
- Often produces **less total recurring savings** than closure.
- May leave too much fixed cost in place.
- Can be vulnerable to renegotiation every time results disappoint.
- Risk of “soft restructuring” that improves optics more than economics.

**When it wins economically**
- If the plants are not truly excess to the network, but need redesign.
- If the needed savings are mostly labour-cost related rather than site-fixed-cost related.
- If the company can achieve a lasting productivity step-up and volume reallocation without plant shutdown.

## What matters most: lifetime economics
The correct comparison is:

**NPV of recurring savings**
minus
**one-time restructuring costs**
minus
**execution risk discount**
minus
**strategic optionality loss**
plus
**value of preserved flexibility**

In practice, closure has:
- higher upfront cost,
- higher political and implementation risk,
- but often stronger certainty of recurring savings.

No-closure has:
- lower upfront cost,
- lower social friction,
- but often weaker and less durable savings.

Hybrid often balances the two.

## Key financial judgment
If I were evaluating this for a board, I would ask for a model that shows, for each option:

- annual recurring EBITDA improvement,
- one-time cash cost,
- timing of cash outflow,
- timing of savings ramp,
- asset write-downs and balance-sheet impact,
- working-capital and supply-chain effects,
- strike/disruption probability,
- probability of successful implementation,
- and downside case if negotiations fail.

Without that, “equivalent economics” cannot be established.

## Practical assessment of the investor position
The investor view is financially coherent: they are right to insist that a no-closure package must be **quantitatively equivalent** and not just politically easier. They are also right that symbolic concessions do not restore equity value.

But from a restructuring standpoint, they should not treat closures as the only benchmark. A **credible hybrid package** can outperform a closure plan on lifetime value if:
- closure charges are very high,
- execution risk is high,
- remaining sites can absorb volume efficiently,
- and labour concessions are durable.

## Practical assessment of the labour-side position
Labour’s position is economically viable only if it can produce:
- substantial recurring cost relief,
- measurable capacity reduction,
- and enforceable flexibility.

Pure job preservation without structural savings is not financeable in a competitively stressed business.

## My conclusion
A **closure-based plan** is usually the strongest tool for maximum structural cost reduction, but it is also the most disruptive and costly to execute.

A **no-closure plan** is only credible if it is essentially a **hard restructuring by another name**: real capacity reduction, hard labour-cost concessions, and enforceable milestones.

A **hybrid package** is the most likely route to a value-maximizing outcome in this governance setting, because it can preserve some industrial footprint while still delivering genuine recurring savings and preserving optionality.
