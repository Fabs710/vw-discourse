We have now established the real issue: **whether the company can restore competitiveness without removing enough cost and capacity from the German base**.

I will be direct.

## Where I agree
I agree with the expert, investors, and the family on the central financial test:
- a **cosmetic settlement is worthless**;
- the package must be **durable, measurable, and enforceable**;
- and we need **real savings, not slogans**.

I also agree with the state and the works council on one point:
- a prolonged strike would be damaging for everyone.

So yes, we all want to avoid an open-ended conflict. But avoidance is not a strategy if the result leaves the company structurally uncompetitive.

## Where I disagree

### 1) “No closures” cannot be treated as a fixed premise
The union and works council continue to insist on:
- no plant closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- binding job and site guarantees to 2030.

I understand why they want that. I do **not** accept it as a starting constraint if it blocks the required reset.

If the current footprint is too large for the market, then **capacity has to come out**.  
If the cost base is too high, then **labour costs have to come down structurally**.

If the only way to get there is by closing one or more plants, then that is not ideology — that is the economics.

### 2) “Equivalent economics” means equivalent savings, not equivalent comfort
I agree with investors and the family that any alternative to closures must be judged on hard numbers. But let me be equally clear: the burden of proof is not on management to disprove every no-closure idea in the abstract. The burden is on those rejecting closures to show a package that really matches the economics.

That means:
- durable fixed-cost reduction,
- real capacity reduction,
- measurable labour-cost contribution,
- implementation speed,
- and enforceability.

General references to flexibility, productivity, and shared sacrifice are **not enough**.

### 3) Governance constraints do not change the financial reality
The state is right that co-determination and the special statute matter. Of course they do. But governance is not a shield against reality.

A blocking minority cannot turn an uncompetitive cost base into a viable one.  
A job guarantee cannot substitute for margin improvement.  
A politically comfortable deal that leaves the brand underperforming will still destroy value.

## On the expert’s assessment
The expert confirmed what management has been saying from the start:
- a **no-closure package can work in principle**,
- but only if it is **deep, quantified, enforceable, and fast enough**.

That is the key point. “Can work in principle” is not the same as “should be assumed sufficient.” If the package is shallow or slow, it is not a real alternative.

## On the bridge options

### 1) Hybrid restructuring with conditional closure deferral
This is the only bridge option that comes close to serious, and only if the fallback is real.

I could support a deferral **only if**:
- there are hard savings targets,
- site-specific capacity reductions are binding,
- milestones are fixed,
- and the fallback is executable if delivery fails.

If the fallback is politically blocked or endlessly renegotiable, then this is just delay dressed up as prudence.

### 2) Site-preserving adjustment package with hard economics
This can be acceptable **only if** it produces the same economics in substance:
- lower fixed costs,
- lower capacity,
- lower labour costs,
- and durable margin improvement.

If the package mostly consists of temporary concessions and good intentions, then it does not solve the crisis. It postpones it.

### 3) Phased settlement with voluntary workforce reduction first
This is useful as a first phase. It is **not** enough by itself.

Voluntary departures, early retirement, redeployment, and retirement bridges can contribute to the reset. But if they do not deliver enough structural change, then they are simply a softer way of missing the target.

## What management will not accept
Let me be explicit about our limit.

We will **not** accept:
- a package that preserves most of the current footprint and calls that transformation;
- temporary concessions that expire before the next cycle;
- vague productivity promises without enforcement;
- or a result that starves future investment to preserve an unsustainable present.

We need to hit the brand’s return-on-sales target and free funds for future products and technology to 2030. That is not negotiable.

## What we are prepared to accept
We remain open on the mechanism, but not on weak economics. So yes, we are prepared to consider:
- working-time changes,
- productivity measures,
- labor-cost concessions,
- redesign of legacy guarantees,
- selective capacity rationalisation,
- voluntary workforce reduction,
- and a hybrid package with a real backstop.

But only if the total package delivers the scale of savings required.

## To the union and works council
You keep saying closures and forced redundancies are unacceptable. I understand that. But what you have not yet done is show a package that actually matches the required economics without them.

If you want to avoid closures, then you must help produce a restructuring that makes closures unnecessary on financial grounds — not on political preference.

That means real sacrifice, not just protected language.

## To the state government
You are right to defend the regional base. But defending the region does **not** mean preserving every site and every legacy constraint regardless of economics.

If you want to be part of the solution, then support a package that:
- reduces capacity in a meaningful way,
- lowers labor costs structurally,
- and restores the company’s ability to invest.

If you insist that no closure can ever be considered, then you are not shaping the solution — you are narrowing it before the numbers are even tested.

## To the investors and family
You are right to demand hard numbers. I agree with that completely.

But I will also say this: if a no-closure package can truly deliver the required economics, then I am prepared to pursue it. I am not attached to closures as an ideology. I am attached to the outcome.

## My position
So the position of management remains:

- **We need deep, durable cost and capacity reduction**
- **We need a real labor-cost contribution**
- **We need enforceable implementation**
- **We need a settlement that restores competitiveness and funds future investment**
- **We are open to a hybrid or no-closure route only if it meets the economics**

If it does not, then closures and substantial job reductions remain on the table, because this company cannot afford a symbolic compromise.
