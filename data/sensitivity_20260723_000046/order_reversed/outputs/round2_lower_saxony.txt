We are now at the point where slogans are no longer enough. I will be equally direct.

### Where I agree
I agree with the investors, the family, and management on one core point: **this cannot be a cosmetic settlement**. If the cost base remains structurally uncompetitive, the company will keep bleeding value and eventually jobs. I also agree that **a prolonged strike is damaging** and that any solution must be **durable, measurable, and executable**.

### Where I disagree
I do **not** accept the framing that plant closures and deep job cuts are the default benchmark and everything else must merely prove itself against that threat.

For us, the question is not whether change is needed. The question is **what kind of change** is compatible with:
- the company’s long-term health,
- the regional industrial base,
- and the governance structure that legally exists here.

And on that point, I need to be clear:  
**We will not support an outcome that guts regional employment or sidelines the state’s co-determination and veto role.**

### On the investors’ position
You keep saying “equivalent economics,” and that is legitimate. But equivalent for whom?

If “equivalent economics” means the same savings delivered by destroying sites, breaking trust, and hollowing out the regional base, then no — we do not accept that as the only meaningful test.

If, however, there is a **quantified, enforceable alternative** that achieves:
- real capacity adjustment,
- real cost reduction,
- and durable competitiveness,

then we are prepared to work with that. But the burden is not on labour alone. It is also on management and owners to show that they are not using closures as the easiest instrument simply because it is politically harder for others to resist.

### On the family’s position
I hear your point that closures are not an end in themselves. Fine. Then I expect you to treat them as a **last resort**, not as the implicit pressure point in every negotiation.

You are right that a weak compromise is not acceptable. But an arrangement that preserves the company while crushing its social legitimacy and regional footprint is also not acceptable. Long-term ownership means more than quarterly margin discipline.

### On management
Your diagnosis may be serious, but your conclusions are still too one-dimensional.

We are not denying overcapacity or cost pressure. We are saying that the answer must be **socially and politically executable** and must not permanently damage the company’s role as a regional anchor.

If you insist that plant closures are necessary, then show us:
- why alternative capacity measures cannot work,
- what exact savings closures deliver,
- and why those savings cannot be matched through a negotiated package of:
  - working-time adjustments,
  - flexibility,
  - targeted labour-cost contributions,
  - productivity commitments,
  - investment obligations,
  - and selective capacity rationalisation without closure.

If you cannot do that, then your closure-first position is not a necessity claim — it is a preference.

### To the union and works council
I appreciate that you are insisting on fairness and shared burden. That is important, and I agree with much of your concern about one-sided cost dumping.

But I also need to say this clearly: **binding job and site guarantees cannot be absolute if they make any restructuring impossible.** If the alternative package is just “no closures, no redundancies, and no real economic adjustment,” then we will not support that either. That would simply postpone the crisis and leave everyone worse off later.

So the state’s position is not to defend every status quo element. It is to defend:
- the regional industrial base,
- meaningful employment,
- the company’s long-term viability,
- and a negotiated solution that preserves both competitiveness and social cohesion.

### What would move us
A package could gain our support if it contains:
- **no blanket closure decision**
- **site-preserving capacity reductions where possible**
- **a measurable employee contribution**
- **management and owner contributions visible in the package**
- **investment commitments tied to the restructuring**
- **binding review points and reversibility if targets are not met**
- **preservation of the state’s co-determination and blocking minority role**

That is the kind of settlement that can be defended politically and economically.

### What we will not support
- plant closures presented as the first and only serious option;
- a settlement that strips jobs from the state and calls it modernization;
- any attempt to force through restructuring while bypassing governance realities;
- a deal that leaves workers carrying the burden alone;
- or a paper agreement that cannot survive implementation.

### My bottom line
We are prepared to back a hard restructuring **if** it is balanced, enforceable, and preserves the company’s role as a regional economic anchor. We are **not** prepared to endorse a closure-led solution simply because it is the bluntest instrument on the table.

If management wants our support, bring a **quantified alternative** that protects sites and jobs as far as possible while still delivering real savings. If the only answer offered is closures and mass layoffs, then you should not expect us to make that easier.
