We are still anchored to the same test: **does the package credibly restore competitive margins, or does it merely postpone the adjustment?**

## Where we agree

### To management
We agree that a cosmetic settlement is unacceptable. If German operations remain structurally overcosted, the equity story does not improve. We also agree that capacity has to come down in some form, and that the package must be enforceable.

### To the controlling family
We agree that prolonged industrial conflict destroys value. A long strike or an unstable settlement would damage earnings, investment capacity, and ultimately the share price.

### To the union and works council
We agree that labor peace and industrial know-how matter. We do not want reckless disruption. We also accept that non-closure measures can be meaningful and that the adjustment should not fall only on workers.

### To the state government
We agree that a deindustrialized outcome would be bad for the company and the region. We are not asking for a scorched-earth solution.

## Where we disagree

### 1) “No closures” is not, by itself, a restructuring plan
The union and works council keep saying plant closures are not acceptable. That may be politically understandable, but financially it is not sufficient.

The question is not whether closures are ideologically pleasant. The question is whether the alternative delivers:
- **durable fixed-cost reduction**
- **real capacity reduction**
- **margin improvement**
- **implementation certainty**

If it does not, then it is not equivalent.

### 2) Job guarantees through 2030 are only acceptable if they do not lock in a weak cost base
A long employment guarantee is not valuable to us if it simply preserves structural inefficiency. We can support time-limited stability **only if** it is paired with front-loaded, measurable savings and a real footprint reduction.

### 3) Non-closure alternatives need numbers, not assertions
The expert has already made the benchmark clear: internal measures can help, but they usually do not fully replicate closure economics. That means the burden is now on the non-closure camp to show:
- how much savings are delivered,
- how durable they are,
- how much capacity is actually removed,
- and what backstop exists if targets are missed.

So far, we still see rhetoric more than proof.

## Our view of the current compromise paths

### Balanced site-preservation package with hard triggers
This is the only route worth discussing seriously, but only if it contains:
- quantified annual savings,
- permanent overhead cuts,
- meaningful workforce contribution,
- measurable capacity reduction,
- and automatic corrective measures if performance slips.

If the “guarantees” are open-ended and the savings are vague, we would oppose it.

### Two-stage restructuring
Potentially acceptable, but only if stage 2 is a real backstop, not a fiction. Investors will not accept a deal where stage 1 is the whole plan and stage 2 is politically impossible to activate.

### Partial footprint adjustment without full closure
This is promising only if it removes enough fixed cost and capacity to matter. Shutting a line is not the same as removing a plant. If the footprint remains bloated, margins remain weak.

## Direct response to the other parties

### To management
If you think closures are necessary, prove that the alternatives are insufficient. Put numbers on:
- current overcapacity,
- site-level fixed costs,
- savings from internal rationalization,
- and the gap that only a closure would close.

Do not use closure language as leverage if you have not exhausted credible alternatives.

### To the union
If you want to avoid closures and forced redundancies, then your package must be economically real, not just socially preferable. We need to see:
- how much labor cost comes out,
- how capacity is removed,
- what duration the concessions have,
- and how the company avoids drifting back into the same cost base.

A promise of peace without structural savings is not enough.

### To the works council
Site security cannot become a shield for permanent underperformance. If the company can stay open only by preserving too much overhead and too much capacity, then that is not a sustainable arrangement. We are willing to back site preservation only if it is tied to hard operating improvements.

### To the state government
We hear your political constraints. But if you want to block closures, you need to help produce a financially credible alternative, not just veto the obvious one. That means supporting a package that is actually large enough to restore competitiveness.

## Our threshold for support

We could support a closure-minimized package if it clearly delivers all of the following:

1. **Material annual savings**
2. **Durable reduction in the cost base**
3. **Real capacity reduction**
4. **Enforceable milestones and backstops**
5. **No cosmetic preservation of legacy structures**
6. **Protection of future investment capacity**

If those conditions are met, we are open.

If they are not, then we will not support a settlement simply because it avoids conflict. A deal that preserves the headline of stability while leaving the company uncompetitive is value destructive.

## Bottom line

We are not demanding closures for their own sake. We are demanding **credible economics**.

If the parties can produce a closure-minimized plan that is genuinely equivalent in scale, durability, and certainty, we will consider it. If not, then the company should stop pretending that a weak compromise is a competitive restructuring.
