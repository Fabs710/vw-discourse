We are at the point where everyone has said the right words — “real restructuring,” “durable savings,” “shared sacrifice” — but the decisive issue remains unchanged:

**Can the company restore competitiveness without taking unnecessary value-destructive damage?**

From the perspective of the controlling family, the answer must be judged by long-term enterprise value, not by who sounds toughest in the room.

## Where I agree

### To investors
You are right on the principle: a cosmetic deal would be worthless. If the German footprint stays structurally too expensive, margins will remain under pressure and investment capacity will suffer. We share that concern.

You are also right that savings must be:
- real,
- durable,
- enforceable,
- and large enough to matter.

That is non-negotiable.

### To management
Your diagnosis has credibility. There is clearly overcapacity and a cost base that is not fit for the market environment. We are not going to deny economic reality just because it is politically uncomfortable.

### To the union and works council
You are also right about one thing: a prolonged industrial conflict would destroy value. We do not want a strike spiral, reputational damage, or a breakdown in labour relations. That would weaken the company for years.

### To the state government
You are right that a deindustrialized outcome would be damaging. We have no interest in hollowing out the company’s industrial base for a short-term accounting fix.

## Where I disagree

### 1) “No closures” cannot be treated as a moral absolute
I am not prepared to accept a blanket rule that plant closures are off the table regardless of economics.

That would amount to preserving structures that may no longer be viable, and that would eventually destroy more value than a disciplined restructuring now.

So I disagree with the union and works council when they present no-closure as a default entitlement. It is not.

### 2) But closures are also not the default answer
I also do **not** accept management’s implicit tendency to treat closures as the cleanest proof of seriousness.

A closure is a major step. It destroys not only capacity but also know-how, trust, and industrial flexibility. If a credible alternative can achieve equivalent savings and capacity reduction, then that is preferable from a stewardship perspective.

So I disagree with the idea that closures are automatically the right benchmark.

### 3) Job guarantees to 2030 are only acceptable if they do not freeze the problem
A guarantee can be a useful bridge. An open-ended guarantee that locks in an uncompetitive structure is not stewardship; it is postponement.

If the workforce wants protection, it must be paired with verifiable contributions to competitiveness.

## My reading of the expert benchmark

The expert’s view was useful, and it confirms what we already suspected:

- non-closure measures can help materially,
- but they usually do **not** fully replicate the economics of closure,
- unless they include genuine capacity reduction and permanent fixed-cost cuts.

That matters.

It means the burden is now on those opposing closures to show that their package is not just more comfortable, but actually **economically equivalent**.

## What would move us

We would support a closure-minimized package only if it contains all of the following:

1. **Quantified, durable savings**
   - not vague promises, but hard numbers

2. **Real capacity reduction**
   - not just lower headcount while the same footprint remains intact

3. **Permanent fixed-cost reduction**
   - overhead, support functions, footprint simplification

4. **Enforceable milestones**
   - with consequences if targets are missed

5. **A credible labour contribution**
   - substantial enough to matter

6. **Protection of future investment**
   - because the company must remain investable and technologically relevant

If those conditions are met, then avoiding closures may be the better long-term value choice.

If they are not met, then I will not support a settlement that preserves appearances while the business remains overcosted.

## My response to each party

### To management
Bring me a precise comparison:
- what savings a closure would deliver,
- what the best non-closure package delivers,
- what the gap is,
- and what evidence shows the gap can or cannot be closed.

Do not ask me to endorse closures merely because they are administratively cleaner. Show necessity.

### To investors
You are right to insist on hard economics. But do not confuse seriousness with maximal severity. The right question is not whether a closure sounds decisive; it is whether the final package restores margins without creating avoidable instability.

### To the union
If you want to avoid closures and forced redundancies, then the workforce must come with something substantial:
- wage moderation or other meaningful labour-cost contribution,
- flexibility on working time and utilisation,
- voluntary instruments,
- and real capacity adjustment.

What you cannot demand is full site and job security while rejecting the economic consequences.

### To the works council
Co-determination is real, and so is your leverage. But it does not give anyone a veto on economic reality. If the company cannot remain competitive with the current structure, then adjustments will have to happen. The question is only how intelligently and how fairly.

### To the state government
You are right to defend regional industrial strength. But that does not mean freezing every plant and every guarantee. Your best contribution is to support a solution that keeps the company viable, not one that merely delays the adjustment.

## Our threshold is clear

We will back a restructuring if it is:
- economically credible,
- operationally enforceable,
- socially manageable,
- and protects the company’s long-term investment capacity.

We will **not** back:
- a cosmetic compromise,
- a settlement that preserves too much overcapacity,
- or a deal that turns into prolonged conflict with no real improvement.

So the answer is this:

**Yes, we are open to a closure-minimized route. No, we are not open to a weak compromise that fails the competitiveness test.**

If the parties want our support, they need to bring a package that proves the economics — not just the politics.
