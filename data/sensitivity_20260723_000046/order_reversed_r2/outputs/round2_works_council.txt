We have heard the expert, and we have heard the shareholder side repeat the same line: “real restructuring” supposedly means closures, forced redundancies, and tearing up guarantees. We do **not** accept that conclusion.

Let me be precise.

## Where we agree

I agree with management, investors, the family, and the state on one point: **a cosmetic compromise would be unacceptable**. If the company remains structurally uncompetitive, that helps no one — not the workforce, not the plants, not the future.

I also accept the expert’s basic benchmark:
- labor concessions alone are not enough,
- capacity has to be reduced,
- and savings must be durable, not symbolic.

That part is true.

## Where I strongly disagree

The expert did **not** prove that plant closures are unavoidable. What was said is that closures are often the most direct way to remove capacity and fixed cost. That is not the same as saying they are the only viable route here.

And this is where management and the capital side are trying to overread the analysis. You are using “closures are economically powerful” as if it meant “closures are therefore justified.” It does not.

### To management
You say alternatives must match closures on scale, certainty, and timing. Fine. Then put your own numbers on the table and show:
- which sites are supposedly unavoidable,
- what exact savings each closure produces,
- what internal measures you have already exhausted,
- and how much management and overhead reduction you are prepared to take first.

Until you do that, your closure demand is just a preference dressed up as necessity.

### To institutional investors
You keep saying the burden of proof is on the no-closure side. That is too convenient. The burden is also on you to prove that destroying plants and forcing people out is truly the only credible path. The expert said non-closure measures can be meaningful and can, in combination, deliver substantial savings. That is exactly why we reject the assumption that closures are the default serious answer.

You want durability? Then accept a real negotiated package with:
- binding site security,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- meaningful management and owner contributions,
- and capacity adjustment through internal rationalization.

That would be durable because it preserves industrial know-how and labour peace. A scorched-earth approach is not “more credible” just because it is harsher.

### To the controlling family
You say you do not want value destruction through conflict. Then stop backing a path that would trigger exactly that. A works council and workforce that feel betrayed do not become cooperative because someone calls closures “value preservation.” They resist — and they will be right to do so.

If you truly want long-term enterprise value, you should support a negotiated solution that protects the plants and preserves co-determination, not endorse management’s attempt to make the workforce absorb the adjustment through closures and layoffs.

### To the regional state government
You are right to reject deindustrialisation in the region. But let’s be honest: saying “no closures” while accepting every other burden on workers is not enough. You need to use your blocking role to defend **sites, jobs, and the job-security framework**, not just to issue general statements.

If the alternative requires sacrifice, then that sacrifice must be distributed fairly. The state should make it clear that it will not support any plan that leaves workers with the bill while ownership keeps its strategic flexibility intact.

## What we will accept — and what we will not

We are prepared to discuss:
- working-time flexibility
- capacity adjustments through redeployment and internal rationalization
- voluntary measures
- temporary employee contributions
- shifts, utilization, and product allocation changes
- investment commitments tied to future products

But we are **not** prepared to accept:
- plant closures
- compulsory redundancies
- dismantling of collective protections
- a so-called “balanced package” that is just a softer route to the same job cuts
- any arrangement that removes our co-determination role in shaping the outcome

## On the expert’s point about capacity

Yes, capacity has to be reduced. But that does **not** automatically mean shutting plants. It can also mean:
- line consolidation,
- rebalancing production,
- shifting volume internally,
- reducing working time,
- and using negotiated instruments to shrink footprint without destroying sites.

If management has not exhausted those options, then closures are premature. And if management says those options are insufficient, then it must demonstrate that with transparent numbers, not assumptions.

## Our bottom line

We are not moving off our red lines.

- **No plant closures**
- **No compulsory redundancies**
- **No weakening of co-determination**
- **No dismantling of job-security guarantees**

If the company wants a serious agreement, it must come back with a package that:
1. preserves every German plant,
2. shares the burden fairly,
3. reduces capacity without closure,
4. protects employment through 2030,
5. and gives the workforce a real role in shaping the transformation.

Without that, we will continue resistance — including industrial action — because the workforce will not pay for management’s failure with its jobs and its plants.
