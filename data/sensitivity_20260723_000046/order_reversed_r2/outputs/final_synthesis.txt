## 1. Decision context

The company faces a classic restructuring dilemma under unusually constrained governance: it needs a durable cost and capacity reset, but any major plant closure or relocation faces strong institutional resistance from co-determination, the controlling family, and the regional state. Management argues that the German footprint is structurally overcosted and that real competitiveness requires deep fixed-cost reduction, potentially including closures and compulsory workforce reductions. Employee-side actors accept the need for change but reject closures and forced redundancies, while investors require economically credible, enforceable savings.

The key decision is therefore not whether to restructure, but whether the company can achieve an economically equivalent outcome without plant closures, and if so, under what enforceable terms.

## 2. Stakeholder assembly

- **Group & brand management:** Driving the restructuring and arguing for durable competitiveness restoration.
- **Works council:** Plant-level employee representation with supervisory-board power; prioritizes site and job protection.
- **Industrial union:** Negotiates wages and can call strikes; insists on no closures and no compulsory redundancies.
- **Regional state government:** Political anchor shareholder with a blocking role; opposes deindustrialization.
- **Controlling family holding:** Majority voting control through governance; focused on long-term enterprise value and labour peace.
- **Institutional investors:** Dispersed capital-market shareholders; demand credible, durable, enforceable savings and margin recovery.
- **Independent restructuring expert:** Benchmarks whether a non-closure package is actually closure-equivalent in fixed-cost and capacity terms.

## 3. Final positions

### Group & brand management
Management remains committed to a hard restructuring and does not accept “no closures” as a strategy in itself. It is open to closure-minimized options only if they remove enough fixed cost, capacity, and future cash drain to close the competitiveness gap. Otherwise, closures stay on the table.

### Works council
The works council rejects plant closures and compulsory redundancies and insists that co-determination and job security are not negotiable sidelines. It is open to internal measures—working-time changes, voluntary exits, redeployment, and line consolidation—but only if the burden is shared fairly and all German sites remain open.

### Industrial union
The union is prepared to discuss labour-cost moderation, flexibility, voluntary exits, and capacity reduction, but only without closures or forced layoffs. It views management’s closure framing as pressure rather than proof, and says the workforce should not carry the main burden of adjustment.

### Regional state government
The state rejects deindustrialization and will not support closures as the default answer. It is prepared to back a deep, enforceable non-closure package, but only if it preserves sites, jobs, and the state’s governance role while still delivering real competitiveness gains.

### Controlling family holding
The family prioritizes long-term enterprise value and labour peace, and does not support closures as an ideology or as an automatic first resort. It will back a closure-minimized restructuring if it is genuinely deep, durable, and enforceable; otherwise it will accept harder footprint adjustment as a value-preserving necessity.

### Institutional investors
Investors want proof that any no-closure package is economically equivalent to closure in material respects: fixed-cost reduction, capacity reduction, durability, enforceability, and margin impact. They are willing to support site preservation only if the package removes enough structural cost to be credible on a present-value basis.

### Independent restructuring expert
The expert’s position is the strictest: non-closure measures can substitute for closure only if they remove comparable permanent fixed cost, true productive capacity, and future cash drain, and do so on a durable, enforceable basis. Temporary concessions, symbolic site preservation, or partial internal rationalization are not enough.

## 4. Genuine conflicts (structurally incompatible positions)

1. **Plant closures vs. site preservation**
   - Management/investors/family treat closures as a legitimate benchmark if no-closure options fall short.
   - Union/works council/state treat closures as unacceptable first-line solutions.
   - This is structurally incompatible unless a closure-equivalent non-closure package is proven.

2. **Durable savings vs. job-security guarantees**
   - Capital-side actors want front-loaded, enforceable, permanent savings.
   - Labour-side actors want site and job guarantees through 2030 and beyond.
   - The harder the guarantee, the harder it is to preserve restructuring flexibility.

3. **Burden-sharing**
   - Labour wants management, ownership, and the state to contribute materially.
   - Management wants labour contributions that are measurable and structural.
   - The conflict is over who absorbs the adjustment first and most deeply.

4. **Meaning of equivalence**
   - Investors and the expert define equivalence in financial and operational terms.
   - Labour and the state want equivalence to include trust, co-determination, and social legitimacy.
   - These are not the same metric, and the gap is unresolved.

## 5. Areas of convergence

- All sides agree the company needs a real restructuring.
- All sides reject a cosmetic or symbolic settlement.
- All sides accept that prolonged industrial conflict would destroy value.
- All sides now accept that a non-closure package is only credible if it removes real fixed cost and capacity.
- All sides are open to a mix of labour-cost moderation, working-time changes, voluntary exits, line consolidation, and overhead cuts.
- All sides recognize that enforcement, milestones, and backstops matter.

## 6. Key trade-offs for the decision-maker

1. **Economic credibility vs. social acceptability**
   - Closures are financially clean but socially explosive.
   - No-closure packages are politically easier but must be deeply structural to be credible.

2. **Speed vs. legitimacy**
   - Closures may deliver faster fixed-cost removal.
   - Negotiated alternatives may preserve industrial peace but risk delay, dilution, and reversal.

3. **Certainty vs. flexibility**
   - Investors want certainty of savings.
   - Labour wants protection against irreversible commitments.
   - The more flexible the package, the less certain the savings.

4. **Short-term conflict vs. long-term trust**
   - A hard closure path may improve near-term economics but damage trust and governance legitimacy.
   - A compromise path may preserve trust but only if it is economically real.

## 7. Integrative pathways

### A. Phased footprint adjustment with a binding backstop
Implement immediate non-closure measures first: shift reductions, line consolidation, overhead cuts, working-time changes, voluntary exits. Set quantified savings targets and a fixed review date; if targets are missed, a pre-agreed second-stage footprint reduction activates automatically.

### B. Site-preservation package tied to real capacity cuts
Keep sites open for a defined period, but require permanent surrender of specific capacity, product lines, shifts, and overhead layers. Pair this with durable labour-cost contributions and management-side simplification so the footprint shrinks in substance, not just language.

### C. Joint burden-sharing compact
Create a package where:
- labour contributes through moderation, flexibility, and exits;
- management contributes through executive restraint and overhead cuts;
- owners contribute through deferred distributions or capital support tied to milestones;
- the state facilitates and conditions its support on verified outcomes.

## 8. Open questions

- What is the plant-level fixed-cost base by site?
- How much capacity can be removed without closures?
- What annual savings are guaranteed under the best non-closure package?
- What management and owner contributions are actually available?
- What enforcement mechanism prevents backsliding?
- What backstop is politically and legally credible if the first phase underdelivers?
- What duration of site/job guarantees would labour accept in exchange for deeper internal restructuring?
- Can the governance actors agree on a shared definition of “equivalence”?

## 9. Recommended next step

Move immediately to a **quantified comparison model** of three cases:
1. closure,
2. partial footprint adjustment,
3. no-closure package.

The model should compare fixed-cost reduction, capacity reduction, transition costs, capex implications, execution risk, and present value of free cash flow impact. Negotiation should then focus only on the smallest package that clears the economic threshold while remaining politically and institutionally feasible.

## 10. Emergent insights that only appeared through multi-turn interaction

- The dispute is not fundamentally about restructuring vs. no restructuring; it is about **what counts as credible restructuring**.
- “No closures” is not merely an emotional demand; it functions as a **governance constraint** shaped by co-determination and state veto power.
- The expert clarified that the real test is not whether a package feels like closure, but whether it removes **the same three burdens**: fixed cost, capacity, and future cash drain.
- Investor and family positions converged over the rounds: both became more explicit that they are open to closure-minimized solutions, but only if those solutions are quantified and enforceable.
- Labour-side actors also evolved: they did not become pro-closure, but they moved from simple refusal toward offering internal instruments—working-time changes, voluntary exits, line consolidation, and labour-cost contributions—if closures and forced layoffs are excluded.
- The discussion revealed that the real bargaining unit is not a plant, but a **portfolio of costs, capacity, and governance legitimacy**.
- The strongest unresolved issue is not economics alone, but whether a package can be made both **financially equivalent** and **institutionally acceptable** under a multi-veto system.
