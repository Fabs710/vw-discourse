We will be blunt.

Our support still hinges on one test: **does the package credibly and durably restore competitive margins, or does it merely postpone the adjustment while preserving an overcosted footprint?**

## Where we agree

### To management
You are right that the current cost base is not competitive and that the company needs a real restructuring, not a symbolic one. We also agree that some form of capacity reduction is necessary. If savings are vague, delayed, or reversible, the market will not reward them.

### To the family
You are right that prolonged conflict destroys value. A long strike or an unstable settlement would hurt earnings, investment capacity, and the share price.

### To the union and works council
You are right that industrial peace matters, and we do not want reckless disruption. We also accept that labor should not carry the full burden alone.

### To the state
You are right that the company is an industrial anchor and that deindustrialization would be damaging.

## Where we disagree

### 1) “No closures” is not a financial strategy
The repeated insistence that every site must stay open is not, by itself, a restructuring plan. It is a constraint.

If a no-closure package is to be acceptable, it must prove that it is economically equivalent in all material respects:
- fixed-cost reduction
- capacity reduction
- durability
- enforceability
- margin impact

So far, we have not seen that proof.

### 2) Social value is not a substitute for earnings recovery
The works council argues that trust, co-determination, and job security must be part of the comparison. We do not dismiss that, but capital markets do not price sentiment. They price future cash flows and risk-adjusted returns.

If the package protects every institutional legacy but leaves the company structurally overcosted, then it is value destructive.

### 3) “Internal rationalization” must be large enough to matter
Line consolidation, shift changes, voluntary exits, and overhead cuts can all help. But the expert already made clear that these measures often do not fully replicate closure economics unless they remove real capacity and permanent fixed cost at scale.

That means the burden is on the closure-avoidance camp to show:
- how much fixed cost comes out permanently,
- how much capacity is actually removed,
- how quickly the benefit arrives,
- and what happens if savings fall short.

## Our position on the current options

### Conditional no-closure package
This is the only closure-minimized route we would consider, and only if it includes:
- hard annual savings targets,
- durable labor-cost contribution,
- real capacity reduction,
- permanent overhead simplification,
- and automatic corrective measures if targets are missed.

If it is only a softer version of the status quo, we will not support it.

### Two-stage restructuring
Potentially acceptable, but only if stage two is a real backstop and not politically impossible. A backstop that cannot be activated is not a backstop.

### Partial footprint adjustment
This is the most promising route if it genuinely removes fixed cost. Closing a line or consolidating shifts can be acceptable if it materially improves the economics. Keeping the same bloated structure under a different label is not.

## Direct response to each party

### To management
Stop arguing in generalities. Put numbers on the table:
- current overcapacity by site
- fixed cost by site
- savings from internal rationalization
- savings from closure
- timeline to realization
- and the gap that only closure closes

If the gap is small, say so. If it is large, say so. We need the comparison, not the rhetoric.

### To the union
If you want no closures and no forced redundancies, then your package must be financially real. That means:
- durable labor-cost contribution
- flexibility on working time and utilization
- voluntary instruments
- real capacity shrinkage
- and a mechanism to prevent the cost base from creeping back

A promise of peace without structural savings is not enough.

### To the works council
You say every plant must remain open. That may be your political line, but it is not automatically a value-preserving line. If a site must be kept open only by preserving too much fixed cost, then you are defending an uncompetitive structure.

We are open to site preservation only if the footprint still shrinks enough to change the economics.

### To the state government
If you want to block closures, then you must support a package that is large enough to restore competitiveness. A veto without an alternative is not stewardship.

### To the controlling family
You are right to care about long-term value, but that does not mean treating closures as inherently bad or inherently good. The right answer is the one that restores margins with the least avoidable disruption.

## Our threshold remains clear

We can support a deal if it delivers all of the following:
1. material annual savings
2. durable cost-base reduction
3. real capacity reduction
4. enforceable milestones and backstops
5. protection of future investment capacity
6. no cosmetic settlement dressed up as reform

If these conditions are not met, then we will not endorse a weak compromise just because it avoids conflict in the short run.

## What we need next

If the parties want our backing, we need a quantified answer to four questions:
- What savings are guaranteed?
- What capacity is removed?
- What is the enforcement mechanism?
- What is the fallback if the package underdelivers?

Without that, the market will assume the restructuring is incomplete.

## Bottom line

We are open to a closure-minimized solution.

We are **not** open to an arrangement that preserves the existing cost disease under a softer label.

If there is no economically credible alternative, then closures remain a legitimate option. If there is one, show it — with numbers, durability, and enforceability.
