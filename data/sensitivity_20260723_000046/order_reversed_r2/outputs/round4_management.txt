## Where I agree

### To the investors
You are right that a cosmetic deal is useless. If we do not reduce the cost base materially and durably, we will simply postpone the crisis. I agree that the package must be enforceable, front-loaded, and big enough to matter.

### To the controlling family
You are right that prolonged industrial conflict destroys value. A long strike would hurt output, reputation, and our ability to invest. That risk is real.

### To the state government
You are right that the company must remain a strong industrial anchor in Germany. I do not dispute the need to preserve long-term industrial capability.

### To the expert
Your benchmark confirms the core economic point: non-closure measures only count if they remove **real fixed cost, real capacity, and future cash drain**. That is exactly the standard I am applying.

## Where I disagree

### 1) “No closures” is not a strategy
To the union, works council, and state: repeating “no closures” does not solve the structural problem.

If a site is underutilized, duplicative, or too expensive to sustain, then preserving it for political comfort simply locks in weakness. That is not social responsibility. It is managed decline.

I will not endorse a plan that leaves the company structurally overcosted and then calls that a success because it avoided difficult decisions.

### 2) Job guarantees cannot freeze competitiveness
A guarantee through 2030 is only acceptable if it sits on top of a credible restructuring that actually changes the economics.

If it becomes a shield against real adjustment, then it shifts the burden onto future investment, margins, and ultimately the survival of the business. That is not acceptable.

### 3) Internal rationalization is not enough unless it is deep
I am not rejecting non-closure measures. I am rejecting the claim that modest internal changes can fully substitute for major footprint adjustment.

The expert was clear: if the company wants a no-closure route, it must remove comparable fixed cost and capacity. That means:
- permanent overhead cuts,
- line and shift consolidation,
- headcount reduction,
- labor-cost contribution,
- and a meaningful reduction in productive capacity.

If those elements are not large enough, then the package is not equivalent.

## The economic reality

We are facing:
- a shrinking European market,
- intense low-cost competition,
- German factory costs above target,
- and a brand that is missing its return-on-sales objective.

That means we need:
1. **durable cost reduction**
2. **real capacity reduction**
3. **meaningful labor-cost contribution**
4. **protection of future investment capacity**

Anything short of that leaves the company too weak to compete.

## My view of the current alternatives

### Conditional no-closure package
This is the only closure-minimized route I am willing to consider seriously, but only if it delivers:
- quantified annual savings,
- durable fixed-cost reduction,
- real capacity reduction,
- enforceable milestones,
- and a credible backstop if targets are missed.

If it is just a softer version of the status quo, I will not support it.

### Two-stage restructuring
Potentially acceptable, but only if the second stage is a real backstop and not a political fiction. A backstop that cannot be activated is not a backstop.

### Partial footprint adjustment
This is the most promising non-closure route, because it can preserve core sites while still removing meaningful fixed cost and capacity. But again: it must be deep enough to matter.
