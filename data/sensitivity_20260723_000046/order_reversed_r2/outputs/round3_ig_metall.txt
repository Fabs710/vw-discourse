We have now had the expert benchmark, and it clarified the real issue: **non-closure measures must be judged by their capacity to deliver durable savings and real footprint reduction — not by whether management prefers the bluntest instrument.** That is a useful clarification. It does **not** move us off our core line.

## Where I agree

### To the expert
Your assessment confirms what we have said from the beginning:
- labor concessions alone are not enough,
- the package must be durable,
- and capacity has to come down in a meaningful way.

Agreed. We are not defending a frozen status quo. We are prepared to contribute if the restructuring is fair and balanced.

### To management, investors, and the family
You are right on one point: a cosmetic deal would be useless. If the company remains structurally uncompetitive, the crisis will return. We do not want that either.

### To the state government
You are right that deindustrialization would be harmful. We share the goal of keeping the company a strong industrial anchor in Germany.

## Where I disagree

### 1) You keep turning “capacity reduction” into “closures”
That is the central disagreement.

The expert did **not** say plant closures are the only credible route. The expert said closures are often the most direct way to remove fixed cost and capacity. That is not the same thing.

So when management says “if alternatives are not equivalent, then closures remain on the table,” my answer is: **then prove equivalence first, and do not use closure threats as a substitute for a fair bargaining process.**

We will not accept closures as the default solution.

### 2) You are underestimating what can be done without plant closures
You keep speaking as if the choice is either:
- full closures and layoffs, or
- no real restructuring.

That is false.

A serious package can include:
- working-time adjustments,
- shift and utilization changes,
- internal redeployment,
- voluntary departures,
- wage moderation or temporary labour-cost contributions,
- bonus suspension,
- management overhead cuts,
- procurement and indirect-cost reductions,
- line consolidation,
- and targeted capacity reduction inside the existing footprint.

That can be substantial. It is not “cosmetic” if it is actually negotiated, enforced, and broad enough.

### 3) Workers will not carry the whole burden
This is where your arguments become unacceptable.

If the only “durable” adjustments are:
- wage restraint,
- job reductions,
- and pressure on the workforce,
while management and owners mostly preserve their side of the equation, then this is not shared sacrifice. It is a one-sided extraction.

We will not accept that.

## Our position remains clear

We are prepared to negotiate a durable restructuring, and we have already shown that with our counter-proposal. But the conditions are still the same:

- **no compulsory redundancies**
- **no plant closures**
- **preservation of the collective wage framework**
- **site security**
- **a fair contribution from management and ownership**

If the company needs fewer jobs and lower costs, then the path must run through:
- voluntary instruments,
- internal redeployment,
- working-time adjustments,
- negotiated labour-cost contributions,
- and genuine management-side savings.

Not through closures and forced layoffs.

## To management

You ask for numbers. Fine — but let’s be honest about the burden of proof.

If you want to claim that closures are unavoidable, then you must show:
1. which internal measures you have exhausted,
2. what savings each closure actually delivers,
3. what management cuts you are taking first,
4. what owner contribution is on the table,
5. and why a negotiated footprint reduction inside the current sites cannot close the gap.

Until then, closures are not “necessary”; they are a preferred option.

And let me be equally direct: if management continues to present closures as the only serious answer, then it is inviting conflict, not solving the crisis. We know how to respond to that. Warning strikes are still available, and we are prepared to escalate if needed.

You keep saying “show us the numbers.” That is fair. But you also need to answer the same question in reverse:

**What exact savings are you prepared to accept as sufficient to avoid closures and forced redundancies?**

You cannot demand a no-closure package be “equivalent” in the abstract while refusing to define what equivalence means. If you want a deal, then commit to the concrete threshold you would accept.

And if your threshold still requires plant closures, say so plainly. Do not dress it up as neutral financial logic while the social cost lands entirely on employees.

## To the controlling family

You keep saying you want long-term enterprise value and less conflict. Then support a package that actually preserves industrial peace.

A settlement that:
- cuts jobs compulsorily,
- closes plants,
- and destabilizes labour relations
is not the only way to protect value. It may destroy more value through conflict than it creates through savings.

If you want legitimacy and stability, then back a negotiated solution that protects the workforce and shares the burden fairly.

## To the state government

You are right to defend the regional industrial base. But that means you must do more than reject closures in words.

Use your blocking role to insist on a package with:
- no compulsory redundancies,
- no plant closures,
- investment commitments,
- and balanced burden-sharing.

Do not let the burden be shifted onto the workforce under the banner of “competitiveness.” Competitiveness must not become a code word for rolling back employment standards.

## The crucial point

The company does **not** need to prove that every plant can remain untouched forever.

It does need to prove that:
- there is no fairer way to achieve the required savings,
- all internal alternatives have been seriously exhausted,
- and the restructuring burden is not being dumped primarily on employees.

At this stage, that proof has **not** been delivered.

So our line stands:

**No compulsory redundancies. No plant closures. Real shared sacrifice.**  
If that is impossible for management, then the problem is not that labour is inflexible. The problem is that management is choosing the most destructive route instead of the most balanced one.
