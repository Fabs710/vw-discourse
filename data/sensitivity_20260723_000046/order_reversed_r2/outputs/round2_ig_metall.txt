We appreciate the expert’s benchmark because it confirms two things at once:

1. **A cosmetic deal is not enough** — on that we agree with management, investors, the family, and the state.
2. **Non-closure measures can be meaningful, but they are not automatically equivalent to closures** — which means the burden is now on management to prove its case, not on the workforce to accept closures by default.

That is exactly where we stand.

## Where we agree

### To the expert
Your assessment is useful and, frankly, consistent with what we have been saying:
- labor concessions alone do not solve structural overcapacity,
- and a settlement must remove real capacity and cost.

We accept that. We are not defending every legacy structure just for sentiment. We are willing to contribute if the package is fair and durable.

### To management, investors, and the family
You are right that the company needs a real competitiveness reset. But the expert also makes clear that **closures are only one way to remove capacity**, not the only serious one.

So the question is not “closures or nothing.” The question is:
- what is the minimum footprint change needed,
- what can be done through internal rationalization,
- and what burden is management/ownership willing to take instead of making workers absorb the full cost?

## Where we disagree

### On the role of closures
Management keeps trying to convert “closures may be economically useful” into “closures are therefore acceptable.” That is not the same thing.

The expert did **not** say closures are always required. The expert said a closure-minimized package can work **if it includes genuine capacity reduction, permanent overhead reduction, and durable cost takeout**.

That supports our position:  
**No compulsory redundancies and no plant closures remain the right starting point.**

If management wants to argue that a closure is unavoidable, then it has to show:
- that non-closure options cannot reach the same savings,
- that every meaningful internal alternative has been exhausted,
- and that the burden is being shared fairly.

Until then, closures are not a given. They are a management choice that we will resist.

## What this means in practice

### 1) We are prepared for substantial workforce contribution
We have already signaled willingness to discuss:
- temporary or even durable labour-cost contributions,
- forgoing bonuses,
- working-time adjustments,
- flexible shifts and utilization,
- voluntary separation instead of compulsory layoffs,
- internal redeployment,
- and capacity reduction through negotiated instruments.

But let me be clear: this only works if there is a **balanced package**.

### 2) We will not accept workers carrying all the pain
The expert rightly says savings need to be durable and not temporary. Then management and owners must also contribute:
- management overhead cuts,
- procurement and indirect cost cuts,
- executive restraint,
- footprint rationalization outside headcount alone,
- investment commitments,
- and a credible share of the sacrifice from capital.

If the only “durable” part is job cuts and wage restraint, that is not shared sacrifice. It is a one-sided rollback.

### 3) We insist on no forced redundancies
The expert’s benchmark does not change our red line. If the company needs fewer people, then the adjustment must come through:
- natural attrition,
- voluntary severance,
- redeployment,
- shorter working time,
- and negotiated instruments.

Compulsory redundancies are not an acceptable default. They destroy trust, industrial peace, and the collective wage framework.

### 4) We continue to reject plant closures
If management claims it needs capacity removal, then there are still options before closure:
- line consolidation,
- internal footprint shrinkage,
- reallocation of production,
- product and shift adjustments,
- and targeted site rationalization short of shutting plants.

A plant closure is the most destructive form of capacity reduction. It is not the benchmark unless every less destructive path has genuinely failed.

## Response to the parties

### To institutional investors
You asked for proof that alternatives match the economics. Fair. Now the expert says the decisive point is not whether alternatives exist, but whether they include **real capacity reduction and permanent overhead reduction**.

That means the right question is not “Can we close plants?” but “Can we achieve the same effect without destroying sites and jobs?”  
We are saying yes — if all parties contribute and if management stops treating closures as the only credible signal of seriousness.

If you want numerical comfort, then engage on numbers:
- How much cost reduction is required?
- How much can come from labour?
- How much from management and overhead?
- How much from production rationalization?
- What can be achieved by rebalancing shifts, utilization, and product allocation?

Do that honestly, and you will see that closures are not the only route.

### To the controlling family
You say you want value preservation and less long-run damage. Then support a package that protects the industrial core and avoids a destructive breach with the workforce.

But value is also destroyed by forcing a labor conflict that lasts months and scars the company for years. A deal that preserves sites, spreads sacrifice fairly, and still achieves real savings is not weakness. It is smarter governance.

What we will not accept is the family backing management’s most drastic option simply because it is administratively neat.

### To the regional state government
You keep saying closures are unacceptable. On that, we agree.

But that means you should actively back a negotiated industrial solution that:
- protects sites,
- rules out forced redundancies,
- and requires management and ownership to contribute materially.

If you simply reject closures without helping shape the alternative, you leave workers exposed to pressure for concessions without safeguards. We need your veto role to be used for **conditions**, not just for slogans.

### To management
This is the key point: the expert has now made your position more precise, not stronger.

You do **not** get to say “the alternatives aren’t equivalent” and then jump straight to closures. If you want the workforce to make substantial concessions, then you must bring forward:
- hard numbers,
- the internal cost measures you are willing to take,
- the management and owner contributions,
- and the exact capacity adjustments short of closures that will be made.

If your package still depends mainly on labor cuts and job insecurity, then it is not a shared solution. It is an imposition.

## Our position, explicitly

We are prepared to negotiate a durable agreement that includes:
- a workforce contribution,
- wage moderation or temporary concessions,
- working-time flexibility,
- voluntary adjustment instruments,
- real capacity reduction,
- and investment commitments for future products.

But only if it also includes:
- **no compulsory redundancies**
- **no plant closures**
- **site security**
- **respect for the collective wage framework**
- **meaningful contributions from management and ownership**

## Our warning
The expert’s answer does **not** justify management’s most extreme demand. It says closures may be economically superior in some cases, but it does not prove they are unavoidable here.

If management insists on closures anyway, then it is choosing confrontation over negotiation. In that case, warning strikes will remain on the table, and we will escalate if needed.

So let’s stop using “competitiveness” as a one-way argument against labor. Competitiveness must be built **together**, with sacrifices shared fairly. If the company wants the workforce to move, then management and owners must move too.
