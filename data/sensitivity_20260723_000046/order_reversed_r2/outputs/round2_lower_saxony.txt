We are listening carefully, and we agree on one important point: **this must be a real restructuring, not a cosmetic compromise**. If the company remains structurally uncompetitive, that would hurt the business, investment capacity, and ultimately jobs as well. On that diagnosis, there is broad agreement.

But I need to be equally clear: **we will not accept “real restructuring” as a code word for plant closures in Germany, mass layoffs, and the hollowing out of the regional industrial base**. That would violate the very purpose of our role as anchor shareholder and political actor.

### To institutional investors
You are right that the company needs durable savings and not temporary peace. We do **not** dispute the need for a material cost reduction.

Where we disagree is your implication that closures are acceptable if they are the easiest way to model savings. For us, the benchmark is not a spreadsheet alone. It is whether the company remains a strong industrial anchor in this state and whether the restructuring is socially and politically sustainable over time.

If there is a package of:
- capacity reductions short of closures,
- working-time and utilization adjustments,
- labour-cost contributions,
- internal redeployment,
- and binding investment commitments,

that can deliver comparable durability, then that is the route we want. If someone claims closures are unavoidable, they must prove that less destructive alternatives cannot achieve the same effect.

### To the controlling family
We appreciate that you do not want a value-destroying conflict. On that, we agree. A long strike would be bad for the company and for the region.

But you are too quick to treat closures as an acceptable fallback. In a company with co-determination, a special statute, and major regional responsibilities, **implementability is not just about force; it is about legitimacy**. A “technically optimal” solution that blows up industrial relations and regional trust is not a good solution.

So yes: durable savings matter. But no: we will not endorse a path that treats German sites as disposable simply because the financial model is cleaner.

### To management
Your diagnosis may be correct in part, but your remedy is too narrow.

We do **not** accept that plant closures and compulsory redundancies are the default proof of seriousness. Before any closure logic can even be considered, you need to show that you have exhausted alternatives that preserve the company’s industrial core.

That means:
- deeper internal efficiency measures,
- rationalization of overhead and procurement,
- productivity improvements,
- phased capacity adjustments,
- redeployment and voluntary instruments,
- and a negotiated labour contribution.

If you cannot demonstrate that these are insufficient, then pushing closures is premature and irresponsible.

### To the union and works council
We agree with you that the workforce must not carry the burden alone. We also agree that the company’s strength comes from its industrial know-how and social partnership.

But I also have to say this directly: **a blanket refusal of any site adjustment, any capacity reduction, or any time-limited employment compromise is not enough to get to a viable settlement**. If the company’s economics have genuinely worsened, then some contribution from labour will be necessary.

What we will support is a deal that:
- avoids forced redundancies,
- avoids plant closures,
- protects the regional industrial base,
- and still delivers real savings through negotiated, reversible, and socially responsible measures.

That is the line we will defend.

### To the moderator’s compromise paths
Of the three routes, the only one that looks viable to us is the **balanced restructuring package with conditional guarantees** — but only if the guarantees are real, the savings are substantial, and the company’s sites in the state remain protected.

We would not accept an open-ended “stage 1” that simply postpones hard decisions, nor a backstop that quietly reintroduces closures later without serious exhaustion of alternatives. If there is a staged approach, then the first stage must be sufficient to preserve the core sites and the state’s economic role.

### Our threshold
Any acceptable settlement must:
1. **preserve the core German sites and a substantial employment base,**
2. **deliver real and durable competitiveness gains,**
3. **avoid forced redundancies and plant closures if at all possible,**
4. **respect co-determination and our statutory veto role,**
5. **keep the company investable for the long term.**

If a proposal does not meet those conditions, we will oppose it. We are not here to approve deindustrialization in exchange for a cleaner financial narrative.

### Bottom line
We are open to a serious restructuring. We are **not** open to a restructuring that sacrifices regional jobs and plants as the price of credibility.

If management, investors, and labour can bring forward a package that genuinely restores competitiveness **while protecting the company’s industrial anchor in this state**, then we are ready to work on it. If not, then the answer is no.
