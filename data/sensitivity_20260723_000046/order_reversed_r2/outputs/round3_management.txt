We are at the point where the lines are clear, and I will be equally clear.

## First: what I agree with

### To investors
You are right about one thing: **a cosmetic deal is worthless**. If we sign something that leaves German operations structurally too expensive, then we have not solved the crisis — we have postponed it.

### To the family
You are right that a prolonged strike spiral would destroy value. I do not want that either. A breakdown in industrial relations is costly for everyone.

### To the state government
You are right that the company cannot simply be reduced to a short-term financial exercise. We need a viable industrial base in Germany.

### To the union and works council
I also accept one part of your argument: labor cannot be the only side making concessions. I agree that management, overhead, and industrial footprint must also be addressed.

So yes — there is shared ground on the diagnosis. But there is no shared ground yet on the remedy.

---

## Where I disagree, sharply

### 1) “No closures” is not a credible strategy
To the union, works council, and state: your repeated insistence on preserving every site sounds politically understandable, but it does **not** answer the economic problem.

If a site is structurally too expensive, underutilized, or duplicative, then preserving it for the sake of stability just locks in weakness. That is not a solution. It is managed decline.

I am not prepared to sign up to a plan that protects the headline of employment while leaving the cost base uncompetitive.

### 2) Job guarantees through 2030 cannot freeze the adjustment
A guarantee can only be part of the answer if it is paired with **real, measurable, front-loaded savings**.

If a guarantee becomes a shield against meaningful restructuring, then it simply transfers the burden onto shareholders, future investment, and the company’s long-term competitiveness. That is unacceptable.

### 3) “Internal rationalization” is not enough unless it removes real capacity
To the state and works council: line consolidation, shift changes, redeployment, and internal footprint shrinkage may help. But unless they remove a **large enough amount of capacity and fixed cost**, they do not solve the overcapacity problem.

That is exactly what the expert benchmark confirmed:
- non-closure measures can be meaningful,
- but they usually do **not** match closure economics unless they are very deep and very durable.

So if you want me to accept no closures, you need to show that the alternative is not just gentler, but **economically equivalent**. So far, I do not see that.

---

## The key reality

This company has:
- a shrinking European market,
- intense low-cost competition,
- German factory costs above target,
- and a brand that is missing its return-on-sales goal.

That means we need:
1. **permanent cost reduction**
2. **real capacity reduction**
3. **durable labor-cost contribution**
4. **protection of future investment capacity**

If a proposal fails on any of those four points, then it does not meet the business need.

---

## On the expert assessment

The expert was useful, and the conclusion is straightforward: **plant closures are not the only route in theory, but they are the cleanest way to remove fixed cost and capacity**.

That does **not** mean I insist on closures as an ideology. It means:
- if a non-closure package can truly match the savings and capacity reduction, I am open to it;
- but if it cannot, then closures remain a legitimate and necessary option.

So I reject the idea that closures are off the table in principle.

---

## To the union

You say you are prepared to contribute. Good. Then bring a package that is actually meaningful.

That means:
- **durable labor-cost reductions**
- **flexibility on working time and utilization**
- **voluntary exits where needed**
- **real capacity adjustment**
- **and acceptance that some sites may not remain in their current form**

What I will not accept is:
- no closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- no erosion of guarantees,
- and yet somehow the same cost base magically disappears.

That arithmetic does not work.

If you want to avoid the hardest measures, then show me the numbers. Show me how the same savings are achieved, how fast, and how they are enforced.

---

## To the works council

Co-determination is real. But co-determination is not a veto on economic reality.

You say closures are unacceptable. I hear that. But the company’s survival and competitiveness do not bow to slogans.

If you want to protect sites, then you need to accept a restructuring package that actually changes the economics:
- deeper workforce contribution,
- productivity improvement,
- capacity reduction,
- and, if necessary, site changes.

Anything less leaves the company too weak to invest in the future.

---

## To the state government

You are right to defend the regional industrial base. But you are wrong if you think that preserving every current site is the same thing as preserving industry.

A company that keeps too much capacity, too many fixed costs, and too many legacy burdens will underinvest and continue to miss targets. That eventually harms the region more than a disciplined restructuring now.

So I am not asking you to endorse deindustrialization. I am asking you to support a restructuring that makes the company viable. If that requires difficult footprint decisions, then those decisions must remain available.

---

## To the controlling family and investors

You both want credibility. So do I.

But credibility does not come from grandstanding or from pretending that a difficult package can be avoided through vague promises of shared sacrifice. Credibility comes from:
- hard numbers,
- enforceable measures,
- real capacity reduction,
- and a cost base that is actually competitive.

If a no-closure package can deliver that, I am open.
If not, then we must be honest: it is insufficient.

---

## My position, plainly stated

I am prepared to negotiate a closure-minimized deal **only if** it delivers all of the following:

1. **material and durable cost reduction**
2. **real capacity reduction**
3. **meaningful labor-cost contribution**
4. **enforceable implementation**
5. **protection of future investment capacity**

If those conditions are not met, then I will not back a soft compromise just because it is more comfortable politically.

---

## What I want next

Before we go in circles again, I want a concrete answer from the employee side and the state:

- What exact annual savings are you offering?
- What specific capacity will be removed?
- What labor-cost contribution is on the table?
- What is the enforcement mechanism if targets are missed?
- And what evidence do you have that this closes the competitiveness gap without closures?

If you cannot answer that, then the reality is simple: **closures and deeper structural measures remain on the table**.

We need a solution that restores competitiveness, not a settlement that merely delays the crisis to 2027 or 2028.
