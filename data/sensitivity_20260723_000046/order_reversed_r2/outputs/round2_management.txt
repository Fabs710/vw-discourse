I’ll be blunt: **we are still far apart on the means, but not on the diagnosis**.

Everybody here now agrees that the company cannot live with a structurally uncompetitive German cost base. Good. That is the starting point, not the finish line. The question is whether we are prepared to make the hard decisions that actually change the economics, or whether we settle for language that sounds responsible while leaving the cost base intact.

## To the institutional investors
I agree with your core principle: **no cosmetic deal**.

You are right that the objective is durable margin repair, not conflict avoidance. You are also right that capacity has to come down if the market no longer supports current output. And you are right that the workforce contribution must be real, not symbolic.

Where I will not follow you is on pretending that a no-closure package is automatically equivalent if it lacks hard numbers. I am not interested in a theoretical alternative. I need a package that produces **the same structural savings, with the same certainty, on the same timetable**. If it cannot do that, then it is not an alternative; it is delay.

So yes: we need measurable savings, front-loaded where possible, and enforceable. But if alternatives fail to close the gap, then closures remain on the table.

## To the controlling family
I agree with you on one point: **value destruction through a prolonged conflict is unacceptable**. A strike spiral helps nobody.

But let’s be honest: the greater value destruction would be to preserve overcapacity and legacy cost burdens until the business keeps missing its targets. That is not prudence. That is slow decline.

You say you do not want closures as a default. Neither do I. But I will not accept a process where closures are treated as taboo regardless of economics. If we cannot get equivalent savings through a negotiated package, then we must be able to act on the footprint.

## To the regional state government
I understand the political reality, but I need to be equally direct: **the company is not a regional preservation scheme**.

We can and should protect a strong industrial base. We cannot protect every site and every job if the economics no longer support them. If we do that, we end up losing much more through weak margins, underinvestment, and declining competitiveness.

You say closures are not acceptable to you. I hear that. But then you need to be clear: what exact combination of measures do you support that actually closes the cost gap? “No closures” is not a restructuring plan.

If the state wants to keep industrial capacity in the region, it should back a deal that restores profitability, not one that freezes the current cost structure in place.

## To the union
You say you are prepared to discuss labour contributions. Good — but then let’s be concrete.

What we need is not a general commitment to fairness. We need:
- a **material labour-cost contribution**
- flexibility on working time and utilization
- a reduction in legacy burdens
- voluntary instruments where possible, but not as a substitute for scale
- and a package that delivers real savings, not just industrial peace

What I cannot accept is “no closures, no compulsory redundancies, no site changes, no erosion of guarantees” while also expecting the company to become competitive. Those conditions do not add up.

If you want to avoid the hardest measures, then show how you reach the required savings without them. Until then, you are asking management to accept a weaker company.

## To the works council
I respect the role of co-determination. But co-determination is not a veto on economic reality.

You are demanding site security and employment security through 2030. I understand why. But if that is to be more than a slogan, it must be matched by a **credible, durable, and measurable cost-down**. Otherwise you are asking us to sign off on a locked-in cost structure that we already know is not competitive.

We are not going to commit to guarantees that prevent necessary restructuring unless the package on the table genuinely restores competitiveness. A promise to preserve everything while hoping the market improves is not responsible governance.

## On the compromise paths
The moderator’s three routes are sensible as categories, but only one of them can work for us: **a balanced restructuring package with conditional guarantees**.

However, let me define the condition sharply:
- guarantees must be **time-limited and conditional**
- savings must be **quantified and enforceable**
- capacity reduction must be **real**
- and there must be a **credible backstop** if targets are missed

If stage one is just a postponement mechanism, it fails. If “site preservation” means preserving too much cost, it fails. If the backstop is never usable in practice, it fails.

## My position, plainly
We are prepared to negotiate a solution that avoids closures **if and only if** it produces:
1. a **material reduction in the cost base**
2. **durable labour-cost improvement**
3. **real capacity adjustment**
4. **enforceable implementation**
5. protection of investment capacity for future products and technology

If those conditions are not met, then I will say it openly: **the alternative is not sufficient**.

So let’s stop talking in generalities. If the employee side believes it can deliver the same economic effect without closures, bring the numbers and the mechanisms. If it cannot, then we need to be honest that tougher structural measures are unavoidable.
