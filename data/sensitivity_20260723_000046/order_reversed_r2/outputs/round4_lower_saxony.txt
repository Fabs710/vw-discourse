We have now reached the point where the discussion must stop circling and confront the real issue: **can the restructuring be made credible without sacrificing sites, jobs, and the state’s co-determination role?**

My answer remains: **yes, if it is genuinely deep, enforceable, and shared fairly; no, if it is simply closures in softer language.**

## Where I agree

### To management
You are right that this company has a structural cost problem and overcapacity. I do not dispute that. A cosmetic deal would merely postpone the crisis.

### To investors
You are right to insist on durability, enforceability, and real fixed-cost reduction. The market will not reward vague promises.

### To the family
You are right that prolonged industrial conflict would destroy value. A strike spiral helps no one.

### To the expert
Your benchmark is useful: a no-closure package is only credible if it removes the same real burdens that closure would remove — fixed cost, capacity, and future cash drain. I accept that standard.

## Where I disagree

### 1) I do **not** accept closures as the default or first answer
Management’s line that closures are the “cleanest” solution may be technically true, but politically and socially it is not acceptable as the starting point. For this company, in this state, plant closure is not a neutral efficiency measure. It is a major rupture with long-term consequences for jobs, trust, and the regional economy.

### 2) I do **not** accept a restructuring that weakens the state’s role or co-determination
Any package that sidelines the regional state, undermines our blocking position, or erodes co-determination is a non-starter. Those governance protections are not incidental; they are part of how this company remains anchored here.

### 3) I do **not** accept dumping the adjustment primarily on labour
The union is right on this point. If there are wage moderation, flexibility, voluntary exits, or working-time changes, then management and ownership must also contribute materially. That includes overhead cuts, executive restraint, procurement savings, and footprint simplification from the top.

## What I take from the expert’s benchmark

The expert confirmed what we already knew: **non-closure measures can be substantial, but they are only closure-equivalent if they remove comparable fixed cost and capacity on a durable basis**.

That means two things:

- If the package merely avoids closures while keeping the same bloated structure, I will oppose it.
- If the package truly shrinks the footprint, removes real capacity, and locks in permanent savings without plant closures, then it is worth serious consideration.

That is the standard. Not slogans. Numbers.

## My position on the current options

### Conditional no-closure package
This is the only route I am prepared to support — but only if it contains:
- quantified annual savings,
- real capacity reduction,
- permanent overhead cuts,
- a meaningful management and ownership contribution,
- and enforceable milestones with consequences.

If those elements are not there, then “no closure” is just a political label for postponement.

### Two-stage restructuring
Potentially acceptable, but only if stage two is a genuine backstop and not a hidden threat used to force labour into surrender. A backstop that cannot be activated is not a backstop.

### Partial footprint adjustment
This is the most promising path, if — and only if — it removes enough capacity and fixed cost to matter, while keeping the company’s German core sites alive.

## What I need to see next

I want a concrete comparison from management and the expert on three points:

1. **What exactly would closure save?**
2. **What exactly would the best no-closure package save?**
3. **What is the remaining gap, and can it be closed without plant closures?**

If you cannot answer that in numbers, then the claim that closures are “necessary” is not proven.

## To the union and works council
You have made your core line clear: no closures, no compulsory redundancies, no weakening of job security. I understand why. But you must also accept that preserving every current structure in place is not enough.

If you want our support for avoiding closures, then the package must include:
- real workforce contributions,
- internal redeployment,
- working-time and utilisation changes,
- voluntary exits,
- and genuine capacity reduction inside the existing footprint.

That is not capitulation; that is the price of credibility.

## To management and investors
Do not tell us that the only credible route is closure unless you can prove the no-closure alternative is insufficient. The burden of proof is on the side that wants the more destructive option.

## Bottom line

Our line is simple:

- **We support a serious restructuring.**
- **We do not support deindustrialisation.**
- **We do not support plant closures as the default answer.**
- **We do not support a weak compromise that leaves the company uncompetitive.**
- **We are open to a deep, enforceable, closure-minimized package that protects sites, jobs, co-determination, and long-term competitiveness.**

If the numbers show that such a package can work, we will back it.

If they do not, then management will have to explain why it failed to exhaust the less destructive options before reaching for closures.
