We agree on one central point: **the company needs a real restructuring, not a cosmetic one**. If the German cost base stays structurally uncompetitive, that threatens investment, margins, and ultimately jobs. I will not deny that.

But I must be equally clear: **we will not accept “real restructuring” as a euphemism for plant closures, mass layoffs, and weakening the state’s co-determination role**. That is not a line we can cross. Our mandate is to protect the regional industrial base and the company’s role as an anchor employer in this state.

## To management
Your diagnosis may be broadly right, but your remedy is still too one-dimensional.

You keep implying that closures are the natural proof of seriousness. They are not. Before anyone claims closures are unavoidable, we need to see:
- which fixed costs can be removed without shutting plants,
- what capacity can be reduced through line and shift rationalisation,
- what management overhead and procurement savings you will take,
- and what investment commitments you are prepared to make for the future footprint.

If you have not exhausted those options, then closure is premature.

## To institutional investors
You are right to demand durable savings and enforceability. I agree that a purely symbolic deal would be unacceptable.

But I disagree with the way you frame the choice. It is not “closures or no restructuring.” There is a serious middle ground:
- working-time and utilisation adjustments,
- voluntary rather than forced reduction,
- internal redeployment,
- capacity rationalisation short of closure,
- labour-cost contributions,
- and management-side cost cuts.

If that package is substantial enough, it can be a credible restructuring without gutting sites and jobs.

## To the controlling family
You are right to warn against value destruction through prolonged conflict. A strike spiral would hurt everyone.

But you are too quick to treat closures as a respectable fallback. A family owner with long-term responsibility should not prefer the administratively neat solution if it destroys trust, industrial know-how, and regional stability. Long-term value is not just about the cost line; it is also about legitimacy and labour peace.

## To the union and works council
I also need to be direct with you: a blanket refusal of any capacity reduction, any labour contribution, and any adjustment through 2030 is not enough.

If we want to avoid closures and forced redundancies, then the workforce side must be prepared to contribute meaningfully. That can include:
- wage moderation or temporary concessions,
- flexibility on shifts and working time,
- voluntary instruments,
- redeployment,
- and internal footprint shrinkage.

But those contributions must be matched by management and ownership. Workers cannot be the only ones paying for the adjustment.

## On the expert’s assessment
The expert has helped clarify the facts: **non-closure measures can save a lot, but they usually do not fully replicate closure economics unless they also remove real capacity and permanent overhead**.

That is important. It means I cannot support a purely soft package that preserves too much of the old structure.

But it does **not** mean closures are the default answer here. It means any acceptable alternative must be:
- large enough,
- durable enough,
- and precise enough
to genuinely change the footprint.

That is the standard.

## Where I stand on the compromise paths

### 1) Conditional site-preservation package
This is the only route I consider workable, and only if:
- the savings are quantified,
- the capacity reduction is real,
- the contributions are shared,
- and there are enforceable milestones.

### 2) Two-stage restructuring
Possible, but only if stage two is a real backstop and not a threat used to force labour into surrender. We will not accept a stage-one compromise that quietly makes stage two automatic and politically irreversible.

### 3) Partial footprint adjustment without full closure
This is the most promising practical route. If the company can shut lines, consolidate production, and reduce fixed overhead while keeping the core sites in this state open, that is the kind of solution we can work with.

## Our threshold is not hidden
We can support a negotiated restructuring if it:
1. protects core sites in the state,
2. avoids compulsory redundancies,
3. delivers real and durable cost reduction,
4. preserves co-determination and our veto role,
5. and keeps the company investable for the long term.

We cannot support a plan that:
- hollows out regional employment,
- removes the state from its role,
- or turns “competitiveness” into a pretext for deindustrialisation.

## Bottom line
So let me be plain:

- **Yes**, we are open to a serious restructuring.
- **No**, we are not open to closures as the first or default answer.
- **No**, we are not open to forced redundancies as the normal mechanism.
- **Yes**, we expect real contributions from management, ownership, and labour alike.
- **Yes**, we will back a solution that preserves the company as a strong regional industrial anchor.

If the others want our support, they need to bring forward a package that proves it can restore competitiveness **without sacrificing the state’s industrial base**. If they cannot do that, then we will oppose it.
