I will be equally clear: **we do not accept closures, and we do not accept compulsory redundancies.** Not as a starting point, not as a “necessary” fallback, and not as the price of being seen as serious.

The discussion has now become much too comfortable with the idea that management’s preferred instrument is therefore the only credible one. It is not.

## To management
You keep saying “show me the numbers,” but you are using that demand to push the burden entirely onto labour while keeping your own options vague.

So here is my answer:

- If you want a serious restructuring, **start with management and ownership contributions**.
- Show the overhead reductions, executive restraint, procurement savings, footprint simplification, and internal reallocation you are prepared to make.
- Show how much capacity can be taken out **without closing plants**.
- Show that you have exhausted every internal alternative before you come back and ask workers to surrender jobs and sites.

What I hear from you is still: “accept lower guarantees, accept lower security, accept more pressure, and trust us.” We have been down that road before. We will not walk it again.

## To institutional investors
You say closures are not ideological, only economically justified if alternatives are not equivalent. Fine. But you are treating equivalence as if it were only a financial spreadsheet question.

It is not.

A package is not equivalent if it destroys trust, industrial know-how, co-determination, and the job-security framework that made this company strong for decades. You cannot strip out the social and governance cost and then pretend the comparison is neutral.

You want durability? Then support a durable workforce settlement, not a one-sided cost-cutting exercise that triggers permanent conflict. A “credible” package that cannot be implemented because the workforce resists it is not credible at all.

## To the controlling family
You say you care about long-term enterprise value. Then act like long-term owners.

Long-term value is not protected by attacking the historic guarantees that underpin trust in this company. If you break the job-security framework, you break the foundation of social partnership. That will cost you more than any short-term savings.

I do not accept the argument that closures are somehow the responsible default. They are not. They are the most destructive option, and they should stay off the table unless you can prove that every less destructive route has failed — and you have not proven that.

## To the regional state government
You are right to reject deindustrialisation. But you cannot do that in words only.

If you truly want to protect the regional industrial base, then use your blocking power to enforce:
- no plant closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- binding investment commitments,
- and a restructuring plan that keeps every German site open.

Do not fall for the idea that “some sites must go” is just a neutral economic conclusion. It is a political choice, and one that sacrifices workers and regions to make the balance sheet look cleaner.

## To the union
On the core line, we stand together:
- no compulsory redundancies,
- no closures,
- real shared sacrifice.

Where I want to be precise is this: the workforce side should not be set up as the only actor taking risk. Any contribution from labour must be matched by real contributions from management and capital. That means:
- management overhead cuts,
- executive and corporate burden reductions,
- investment protection,
- and a binding commitment not to use the restructuring as a vehicle to dismantle co-determination or the job-security framework.

If the package does not protect that framework, it is not a fair package.

## On the expert benchmark
The expert said non-closure measures can be meaningful but often do not fully replicate closure economics unless they also remove real capacity and permanent overhead.

We heard that. Our conclusion is not to surrender; it is to insist on the right design:

- **Yes** to real capacity reduction through internal rationalization
- **Yes** to line consolidation
- **Yes** to shift and working-time changes
- **Yes** to voluntary instruments
- **Yes** to management and owner contributions

But **no** to plant closures.  
And **no** to compulsory layoffs.

If management says that is not enough, then management must show:
1. which non-closure measures it has already exhausted,
2. what it is prepared to cut itself,
3. why the burden cannot be shared more fairly,
4. and why the company’s future requires sacrificing plants rather than reorganizing the footprint internally.

Until then, closures are not justified. They are simply the harshest available tool.

## Our bottom line
Let me restate it without ambiguity:

- **Every German plant must remain open.**
- **No compulsory redundancies.**
- **No weakening of co-determination.**
- **No dismantling of the job-security framework.**

If the company needs to adjust, then it must do so through:
- negotiated work-time and utilisation changes,
- voluntary measures,
- internal redeployment,
- management-side savings,
- and a fair contribution from ownership.

If that is not acceptable to management and shareholders, then the conflict is not caused by our rigidity. It is caused by your decision to put the burden of restructuring on the workforce instead of sharing it.

And if you try to force that path, then yes — we will resist it with all the means available to us.
