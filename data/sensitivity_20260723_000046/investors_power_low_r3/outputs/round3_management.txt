Let me be equally direct.

We are now beyond the phase of repeating red lines. The company needs a restructuring that is **material, durable, and executable**. That means real cost reduction and real capacity adjustment, not a package that leaves the German cost base structurally uncompetitive and simply renames delay as compromise.

## Where I agree
I agree with all sides on a few points:
- the status quo is not viable,
- the solution must be durable, not cosmetic,
- future investment, products, and skills matter,
- and a prolonged conflict would damage the company.

I also agree that employees should contribute. That is not in dispute.

## Where I fundamentally disagree

### 1) “No closures” is not a serious answer by itself
To the Works Council and the Union: you keep saying “no plant closures, no compulsory redundancies, job security through 2030” as if repeating that makes it economically workable.

It does not.

If German overcapacity remains in place and the labor cost base stays above target, then we do **not** restore competitiveness. We postpone the crisis and call it social responsibility. That is not acceptable.

You have not shown a credible, quantified alternative that closes the gap to the level needed for the brand’s return-on-sales target. Until you do, your position is a refusal to confront the problem, not a solution.

### 2) Capacity reduction must be real, not rhetorical
I have heard a lot about working-time changes, voluntary exits, redeployment, bonus restraint, and productivity measures. Those may all be part of the answer.

But they are only acceptable if they produce:
- **material savings,**
- **durable savings,**
- and **actual capacity reduction.**

If they merely soften the blow while leaving too much fixed cost and too much output capacity in place, then they do not solve anything. They just delay difficult choices.

### 3) A blanket job guarantee through 2030 is incompatible with the scale of restructuring
I will say this plainly: I cannot support a commitment that freezes every site and every role through 2030 regardless of business reality.

We need the freedom to adjust production, headcount, and site footprints to market conditions. A blanket guarantee would lock in the current structure and deprive the company of the ability to restore competitiveness.

That is not stewardship. That is managed decline.

## To the Works Council
You say closures are not proven necessary. Then prove the alternative.

Bring a **site-by-site, measure-by-measure** counterproposal showing:
- the exact cost gap,
- the savings from each alternative instrument,
- the residual gap after those measures,
- and how that residual gap is closed without closures or compulsory redundancies.

Until then, “no closures” is a political stance, not an economic plan.

And let me be clear: co-determination gives you a powerful role in shaping the outcome. It does **not** give you a right to freeze the business model while the market moves on.

## To the Union
You are right that a cosmetic compromise would be useless. I agree.

But you are still underestimating the scale of the labor-cost contribution required. Temporary moderation and flexibility alone are not enough unless they are substantial and durable.

A real settlement needs:
- wage moderation,
- bonus restraint,
- working-time flexibility,
- voluntary exits,
- redeployment,
- reskilling,
- and potentially other instruments that reduce structural labor cost.

If industrial action escalates, it will not strengthen your case. It will increase the pressure for harder measures, because the company cannot carry indefinite disruption while the competitiveness gap remains open.

## To the State Government
I respect your role and your concern for the regional industrial base. But you cannot defend every site automatically and still expect the company to compete globally.

If you use your blocking power to prevent any meaningful adjustment, then you may preserve structure on paper while weakening the enterprise in reality. That would hurt the region more over time.

If you want to protect the German footprint, support a package that actually restores profitability and investment capacity. That may require accepting structural reductions where necessary.

## To the Controlling Family
I agree with you on long-term enterprise value. That is exactly what I am trying to protect.

But long-term value is not preserved by keeping all sites open at any price. It is preserved by ensuring:
- a competitive cost base,
- enough cash to invest,
- and a product portfolio that can compete in 2030.

So yes, stewardship matters. But stewardship also means accepting that the current structure may need deep adjustment.

## To Investors
You are right to ask for deal certainty and durable savings. I share that demand.

But I also need you to be realistic: a solution that leaves overcapacity intact is not durable. Nor is one that depends only on goodwill and promises.

We need a package that gives:
- savings,
- capacity reduction,
- execution certainty,
- and a viable path to margins.

## What I can support
I remain open to a negotiated solution that **avoids closures only if** it delivers all of the following:
1. **Material and durable labor-cost reductions**
2. **Real capacity reduction**
3. **A credible employee contribution**
4. **A binding investment and product plan for German sites**
5. **A structurally competitive cost base by 2030**

What I cannot support is:
- a cosmetic no-closure package,
- an absolute prohibition on compulsory reductions,
- or a blanket job guarantee that prevents the company from adapting.

## The practical test
If the employee side believes a no-closure path can work, then bring the numbers. Not slogans. Not principles. Numbers.

Show how you close the cost and capacity gap in a way that is:
- durable,
- economically equivalent,
- and executable.

If that can be shown, I will engage seriously. If it cannot, then management will have to pursue the harder restructuring path that the company’s competitiveness requires.

## Bottom line
We are not debating whether change is needed. We are debating whether the workforce side is willing to accept enough change to actually fix the business.

My answer remains the same:
- deep structural cost reduction,
- meaningful capacity adjustment,
- and a durable recovery of competitiveness.

Without that, there is no solution — only delay.
