I will respond bluntly, because the situation requires it.

## First: what I agree with
I agree with everyone on one point: **the status quo is not viable**.

I also agree that:
- the solution must be **durable**, not cosmetic;
- we need a **competitive cost base** by 2030;
- future **investment, products, and skills** matter;
- and a purely confrontational spiral would damage the company.

So yes, there is common ground on the need for restructuring.

## Where I fundamentally disagree
### 1) A blanket “no closures, no redundancies” line is not credible
To the Works Council and the Union: you keep repeating red lines as if repetition makes them economically valid. It does not.

If we preserve every German site, forbid compulsory reductions entirely, and keep the current cost structure broadly intact, then we do **not** fix the problem — we postpone it.

That is unacceptable to me because:
- the German cost base is structurally too high,
- European demand is shrinking,
- competition is intensifying,
- and the brand is missing its return target.

I am not prepared to sign up to a deal that leaves overcapacity in place and calls it transformation.

### 2) A job guarantee through 2030 is too broad
A binding guarantee for every German plant and every current job through 2030 is not compatible with the scale of realignment needed.

I will say it clearly: if the employee side insists that no site may close and no compulsory job reduction may occur under any circumstances, then you are asking management to accept a structurally uncompetitive business model and hoping the market will somehow reward that. It will not.

### 3) “Alternative measures” must be equivalent in economics, not just more comfortable politically
I am open to avoiding closures **if** the savings are truly equivalent and durable.

But “working-time changes,” “temporary moderation,” and “voluntary measures” are not enough on their own unless they deliver:
- real cost reduction,
- real capacity reduction,
- and real execution certainty.

If they do not, then they are a delay tactic.

## To the Works Council
You say closures are a choice, not a necessity. That may be true in the abstract, but it is not enough.

Then prove the alternative.

Show me a package that closes the cost gap **without** closures and **without** forced layoffs, while also restoring margin and freeing investment capacity. Not slogans — numbers. Until then, your position is not a solution; it is a refusal to confront reality.

I also reject the idea that co-determination means a right to freeze the business model. Co-determination is important, but it does not override economics.

## To the Union
You say employees should not “pay first and trust later.” On that point, I agree in principle: any employee contribution must be paired with a credible industrial plan.

But you are still not acknowledging the scale of the burden-sharing needed. Temporary wage moderation, bonus adjustments, working-time flexibility, and voluntary exits may all be part of the answer — but only if they are substantial and durable.

I am not looking for symbolic concessions. I am looking for a **material labor-cost contribution**.

And let me be equally direct: industrial action that damages output and reputation will not strengthen your case. It will make a structural solution harder, not easier.

## To the State Government
I appreciate your concern for the regional industrial base. That is legitimate.

But your position cannot be: “protect every site, use veto power if needed, and expect the company to become competitive anyway.” That is not stewardship; it is obstruction if it blocks necessary adjustment.

If you want to protect the company’s long-term industrial footprint, then you need to support a deal that actually restores competitiveness. That means being open to difficult choices, including structural capacity reductions where necessary.

I will not accept a process where governance rights are used to preserve inefficiency indefinitely.

## To the Controlling Family
I agree with your emphasis on long-term enterprise value. That is exactly the lens I am using.

Where I would push harder is this: long-term value is not preserved by keeping every site open at all costs. It is preserved by ensuring the company has a viable cost structure, enough cash to invest, and a product portfolio that can compete in 2030.

So yes, stewardship matters. But stewardship also means accepting that some structural adjustments may be unavoidable.

## On what I can support
I am still open to a negotiated solution that avoids closures **if** it includes all of the following:
1. **Material and durable labor-cost reductions**
2. **Real capacity reduction**
3. **Voluntary instruments first, but not only voluntary instruments**
4. **A serious reduction in structural overcapacity**
5. **A credible investment and product plan for German sites**
6. **A settlement that restores the brand’s return-on-sales trajectory**

What I cannot support is:
- a cosmetic no-closure package,
- a blanket job guarantee through 2030,
- or a deal that leaves the cost base uncompetitive.

## The practical reality
We are not debating whether change is needed. We are debating whether the workforce side is willing to accept a level of change that actually fixes the business.

If the answer is yes, then bring forward a **quantified counterproposal**:
- how much savings you can deliver,
- from which measures,
- over what time frame,
- and how that gets us to a competitive cost base.

If the answer is no, then management will have to pursue the harder restructuring path, because I will not let the company drift into a managed decline.

## Bottom line
I am prepared to negotiate hard and to consider alternatives to closures.

But I am **not** prepared to abandon the core objective:
- deep structural cost reduction,
- meaningful capacity adjustment,
- and a durable recovery of competitiveness.

If your proposals do not meet that test, they do not solve the crisis.
