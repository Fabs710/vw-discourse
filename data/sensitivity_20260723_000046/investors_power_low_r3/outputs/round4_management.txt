Let me respond directly, because we are now at the point where repetition is being used as a substitute for a workable answer.

## First: what I agree with
I agree with everyone on three things:

- **The status quo is not viable.**
- **A cosmetic compromise would fail.**
- **Employees will have to contribute.**

I also agree that any restructuring must be **durable**, not a one-year patch.

So the debate is not whether change is needed. It is whether the workforce side is willing to accept the scale of change needed to restore competitiveness.

## Where I strongly disagree
### 1) “No closures” is not a solution
To the works council and union: repeating “no plant closures” does not make the economics go away.

You keep saying:
- working-time reductions,
- voluntary exits,
- redeployment,
- wage moderation,
- bonus restraint,
- productivity measures,
- internal capacity shifts.

Fine. Those can be part of the package. But unless they produce **material, durable savings and real capacity reduction**, they do not solve the problem.

We are not looking for a softer pain profile. We are looking for a **structurally competitive cost base**. If your package leaves overcapacity in place, then it is not a solution — it is delay.

### 2) You still have not shown that closures are unnecessary
You repeatedly ask me to “prove necessity.” I am asking the same of you, because the burden is not one-sided.

If you believe a closure-free path is viable, then show:

1. the **exact savings gap**,
2. what voluntary exits, redeployment, working-time flexibility, wage moderation, and bonus restraint deliver,
3. what the **residual gap** is,
4. and how that residual gap is closed without plant closures or compulsory redundancies.

Until that is on the table, “no closures” is a political demand, not an economically credible restructuring plan.

### 3) “Capacity reduction” cannot remain rhetorical
To the state government, family holding, and investors: I hear the point that capacity reduction does not automatically equal closure. That is true in principle.

But in practice, if the numbers do not close through other instruments, then capacity reduction becomes a euphemism for preserving too much fixed cost and too much output. That would condemn the company to continued weakness.

We need **actual capacity removal**, not wording that sounds gentler while leaving the business structurally bloated.

### 4) A blanket job guarantee through 2030 is incompatible with the task
To labor: a binding promise that all sites and roles are protected through 2030 regardless of market reality is not compatible with restructuring at this scale.

We need the ability to adjust:
- production,
- headcount,
- site footprints,
- and cost structure

as markets and product cycles evolve.

A blanket guarantee locks in the current structure and undermines the very competitiveness we are supposed to restore.

## To the works council
You say management has not produced site-by-site proof. That is exactly the next step we need, and I am prepared to put that on the table.

But let me be clear: co-determination does **not** mean the workforce gets a veto over necessary structural change while asking the company to carry the full burden.

If you want a closure-free settlement, then bring a counterproposal with measurable savings, timelines, and capacity effects. Not principles. Not slogans. Numbers.

And one more point: if your answer to every hard economic problem is “no closures, no layoffs, no insecurity,” then you are not negotiating. You are freezing the problem in place.

## To the union
You are right that a cosmetic deal is useless. On that we agree.

But your insistence that compulsory redundancies are simply unacceptable in any form ignores the fact that the business may need structural headcount adjustment at a scale voluntary measures cannot absorb fast enough.

I am open to maximizing:
- voluntary exits,
- early retirement,
- redeployment,
- retraining,
- natural attrition,
- and flexibility.

But I will not accept an absolute prohibition that prevents the company from executing a viable restructuring if voluntary paths do not close the gap.

## To the state government
You say closures should not be the starting point. I can accept that **as a sequencing principle**.

But I cannot accept it as a substantive veto over any meaningful adjustment.

If you want to protect the regional industrial base, then support a package that actually restores profitability and future investment capacity. Preserving every current site regardless of economics does not protect the base — it weakens it over time.

Your role should be to help secure a viable settlement, not to freeze an uncompetitive structure.

## To the controlling family
I agree with you on long-term enterprise value. That is exactly why I am pushing for a serious restructuring.

Stewardship does not mean preserving every site at any price. It means ensuring the company has:
- a competitive cost base,
- enough cash to invest,
- and a product roadmap that can win in 2030.

That may require hard decisions, including structural site changes if the numbers require them.

## To investors
You want risk-adjusted value, not slogans. That is also my standard.

A solution that leaves the cost base uncompetitive and the margin target out of reach is not value-preserving. It just defers the loss.

And yes, we must count the cost of strikes and conflict. But we must also count the cost of **not** restructuring adequately:
- continuing margin weakness,
- lost investment capacity,
- weakened competitiveness,
- and a slower, more painful adjustment later.

## What would move me
I remain open to a negotiated solution that **avoids closures only if** it delivers all of the following:

1. **Material and durable labor-cost reductions**
2. **Real capacity reduction**
3. **A credible employee contribution**
4. **A binding investment and product plan for German sites**
5. **A structurally competitive cost base by 2030**

If a closure-free package can genuinely deliver that, I will engage seriously.

What I will **not** support is:
- a cosmetic no-closure package,
- an absolute prohibition on compulsory reductions,
- or a blanket job guarantee that prevents adaptation.

## The practical next step
Since everyone now insists on numbers, let’s make that the test.

I propose we move immediately to a side-by-side comparison of:

- the current cost base,
- the savings from voluntary exits,
- the savings from working-time flexibility,
- wage moderation and bonus restraint,
- redeployment and productivity measures,
- the remaining gap,
- and what structural site adjustments would close it.

If labor’s alternative closes the gap, I want to see it.  
If it does not, then we need to stop pretending that closures and deeper structural measures are somehow optional.

## Bottom line
I am not retreating from the central position because nothing I have heard changes the core problem:

- German factory costs are still too high,
- capacity is still too high,
- and the company cannot invest for the future if we settle for an incomplete solution.

So: bring the numbers. Show the durable savings. Show the capacity effect. Show the residual gap.

If the no-closure route works economically, prove it.  
If it does not, then the restructuring must remain hard enough to actually fix the business.
