We are still circling the same point, so let me state it plainly:

**I agree that restructuring is needed. I do not agree that plant closures or compulsory redundancies have been shown to be necessary.**  
And until that is proven with transparent numbers, I will continue to oppose them in full.

## To management
You keep saying “durable,” “material,” “executable,” and “structural.” Fine. Those are not arguments. They are labels.

What you still have **not** provided is:
- the exact savings gap,
- a site-by-site breakdown,
- what each alternative measure delivers,
- what residual gap remains,
- and why that gap cannot be closed without closing plants or forcing layoffs.

Until then, closure is not necessity. It is a chosen strategy.

I also reject the idea that a blanket job guarantee through 2030 is somehow unreasonable from the workforce side. If you want workers to accept wage moderation, flexibility, bonus restraint, and voluntary exits, then management must give something real back:
- **no closures,**
- **no compulsory redundancies,**
- **binding job security,**
- **binding site security,**
- **binding investment commitments.**

Without that, you are asking the workforce to pay for management’s failure while keeping the right to cut jobs later. That is unacceptable.

## To the family holding
You talk about stewardship. Then stop using closure pressure as a shortcut to force acceptance.

Stewardship means:
- protecting the industrial base,
- preserving trust,
- respecting co-determination,
- and avoiding irreversible damage.

If you support closures before the alternatives are fully quantified, then you are not preserving long-term value — you are destroying it and calling it discipline.

I agree with you on one point: a cosmetic deal is worthless.  
But a closure-heavy deal is not automatically “serious.” It may just be the fastest way to break the social compact.

## To institutional investors
You keep demanding “economic equivalence.” I accept that principle. But you are applying it selectively.

You cannot say:
- only closure-based savings count,
- only hard cuts are credible,
- and then ignore the costs of strikes, conflict, skill loss, legal disputes, and reputational damage.

A closure-free package can be economically credible if it includes:
- wage moderation,
- bonus restraint,
- working-time flexibility,
- voluntary exits,
- redeployment,
- retraining,
- productivity measures,
- and capacity adjustment without shutting plants.

So do not pretend the spreadsheet is the whole economy. It is not.

## To the state government
I appreciate your position more than some others, but your caution must become a real line, not just a preference.

If you say closures are not the starting point, then use your blocking power to ensure they are not the backdoor outcome either. The state should not wait until management creates a fait accompli and then call it “necessary.”

Your responsibility is to defend:
- the regional industrial base,
- co-determination,
- and jobs in Germany.

That means backing a settlement that keeps all German plants open unless and until management proves, with hard numbers, that no alternative works.

## To the union
We are aligned on the essentials:
- no closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- no dismantling of the collective framework,
- and no “pay first, trust later” deal.

I support your position. I also support industrial action if management keeps using closure threats as leverage instead of bringing facts.

## On the numbers
Everyone keeps asking for quantified alternatives. Good. Then here is what must be put on the table now:

1. **Exact savings gap**
2. **Savings from voluntary exits and early retirement**
3. **Savings from working-time flexibility**
4. **Savings from wage moderation and bonus restraint**
5. **Savings from redeployment and reskilling**
6. **Savings from productivity measures**
7. **Residual gap after those measures**
8. **Why that residual gap supposedly requires closures or forced redundancies**

Until management puts that on the table, the claim that closures are unavoidable is not proven.

We are prepared to discuss a serious package, including:

- bonus restraint,
- flexible working time,
- voluntary exit instruments,
- redeployment,
- training and reskilling,

1. **No plant closures**
2. **No compulsory redundancies**
3. **Binding job security through 2030**
4. **Binding site security**
5. **Real co-determination in implementation**

That is the minimum basis for any fair settlement.

## What we reject
We reject:
- closure threats as leverage,
- forced redundancies as “last resort” language,
- open-ended insecurity,
- and a restructuring that makes workers bear the whole burden while management keeps all options open.

## Bottom line
If you want movement, bring numbers and abandon the coercive posture.  
If you cannot prove that closures and compulsory layoffs are truly unavoidable, then you have no legitimate basis to demand them.

We are not blocking change.  
We are blocking **unnecessary destruction of jobs, plants, and trust**.

And let me be clear: if this room still cannot produce a closure-free, no-layoff path, then the conflict will continue — in the boardroom, at the bargaining table, and in the plants.
