From the perspective of dispersed capital-market shareholders, the test is not whether one side “wins” the social argument. The test is whether the company gets back to a credible, durable, competitive cost base with enough certainty to support margins, investment, and valuation.

### Where I agree
I agree with management, and to a degree with the family holding, that **doing nothing is not an option**. The current cost base and return profile are clearly not good enough. If the German operations remain structurally overcosted, that will continue to pressure earnings, cash generation, and the share price.

I also agree with the state government and the employee side on one important point: **a cosmetic settlement would be worse than no settlement at all**. If the result merely postpones the adjustment while preserving overcapacity and rigid costs, then shareholders will still be left with impaired margins and recurring restructuring risk.

### Where I disagree
I do **not** support a position that makes plant closures the default answer before every credible alternative has been tested.

Why? Because from an investor point of view, closures are not a virtue in themselves. They are acceptable only if they produce:
- durable savings,
- clear capacity alignment,
- lower execution risk over time,
- and a path back to target margins.

If the company can get comparable savings through negotiated labour-cost relief, working-time changes, capacity reductions, redeployment, and other structural measures, that is preferable to a prolonged conflict that destroys value through strikes, legal blockages, and operational disruption.

That said, I also do **not** accept the works council’s and union’s blanket red lines as a credible basis for a final deal. A permanent guarantee against closures and compulsory redundancies can easily become a guarantee of underperformance if the business case is real. Shareholders cannot support an outcome that leaves the cost base uncompetitive or margins structurally impaired.

### On management
Management is right to insist on a **structural** solution, not a symbolic one. But “structural” has to be measured by results, not by the harshness of the headline measures.

If management wants support from capital markets, it needs to show:
1. the size of the gap to competitive margins,
2. how much savings are required,
3. which measures deliver those savings with the least long-term damage,
4. and how execution risk is being contained.

If plant closures are truly necessary, management should say exactly why. If they are not necessary, then management should not use them as leverage for a deal that could be achieved otherwise.

### On the works council and union
I understand the political logic of saying “no closures, no compulsory redundancies.” But from an equity holder’s standpoint, that is only sustainable if the alternative package is genuinely equivalent on economics.

So the question is not whether workers contribute. They likely must. The question is whether the contribution is:
- meaningful,
- durable,
- enforceable,
- and sufficient to restore competitiveness.

If the answer is yes, investors can support a negotiated solution. If the answer is no, then the company should not pretend that a soft settlement is enough.

### On the state government
I respect the state’s role and the importance of industrial stability. But as an investor, I would caution against using veto power to block necessary restructuring while expecting the market to finance a weak margin profile indefinitely.

Political protection of every site is not a substitute for competitiveness. If the state wants to defend the company’s industrial base, the best way is to help secure a solution that restores returns and protects future investment capacity. That means being open to difficult changes if they are economically justified.

### On the family holding
I largely agree with the family holding’s emphasis on long-term enterprise value. But I would add one caveat: stewardship also means not forcing through a value-destructive confrontation just to prove toughness.

A protracted standoff, strike risk, and governance deadlock would be harmful to all shareholders. The family should use its control to drive a deal that is:
- economically credible,
- operationally executable,
- and not hostage to symbolic positions from either side.

### What would move us
Dispersed institutional investors would support an outcome if it clearly delivers:
- a credible path back to competitive margins,
- durable cost reduction,
- enforceable capacity alignment,
- and deal certainty.

We are open to a negotiated package that avoids closures **if** it produces equivalent savings and does not leave structural overcapacity in place. But we will not support a settlement that preserves all the current constraints and hopes for a better result later. That is not transformation; that is delay.

### Bottom line
Our position is pragmatic but firm:

- **Yes** to a durable restructuring.
- **Yes** to employee contributions if they are real and measurable.
- **Yes** to avoiding closures if savings are equivalent and credible.
- **No** to cosmetic compromise.
- **No** to prolonged conflict that destroys value.
- **No** to an outcome that leaves margins structurally impaired.

If the current red lines from labor make that impossible, then they need to be revised. If management cannot prove that closures are necessary, then it should not insist on them. The burden is on all sides to produce a financially credible path to competitiveness.
