I will be very clear.

## My position
The state government supports a **durable restructuring**, but **not** a restructuring that starts from plant closures and forced redundancies in Germany. That is still my line.

I am prepared to support a serious package if it:
- protects German employment to the maximum extent possible,
- preserves all co-determination rights,
- avoids plant closures unless they are proven absolutely unavoidable,
- avoids compulsory redundancies if there are negotiated alternatives,
- and ties any employee contribution to binding investment, product, and training commitments.

Anything else would put the regional economy, social peace, and the company’s legitimacy at unacceptable risk.

---

## Where I agree with management
Management is right on the basic diagnosis:
- the status quo is not viable,
- the cost base is too high,
- the company needs future investment and product renewal,
- and a cosmetic solution would be worthless.

I also agree that the company must remain competitive by 2030. That is not in dispute.

But agreement on the diagnosis does **not** mean I accept your preferred remedy.

### What I do not accept
You still have **not** shown that closures and compulsory redundancies are necessary rather than simply the fastest way to force acceptance.

That distinction matters enormously.

If closures are truly unavoidable, then prove it with:
- a transparent savings gap,
- a site-by-site assessment,
- a comparison of alternatives,
- and a clear explanation of why working-time changes, redeployment, voluntary exits, productivity measures, and wage moderation are insufficient.

Until that is done, I will treat closures as **an option management prefers**, not a necessity the state must endorse.

---

## Where I agree with the Works Council and Union
I agree with them on the central political and industrial point:
- employees should not be asked to “pay first and trust later,”
- co-determination is not decorative,
- and German sites are not disposable assets.

I also agree that alternatives exist and must be fully tested:
- voluntary exits,
- early retirement,
- redeployment,
- reskilling,
- temporary working-time reductions,
- targeted wage moderation,
- bonus restraint,
- and capacity adjustment without site shutdowns.

That is the right order of priorities.

### What I do not accept from labor
However, I also need to be direct with labor:
a blanket “no closures, no compulsory redundancies, guaranteed jobs through 2030” is only credible if it is backed by a **quantified alternative package** that closes the cost gap.

So far, I hear the right principles. I do **not** yet see the full numbers.

If labor wants the state to back a no-closure settlement, then it must help produce a package that is economically convincing, not just morally compelling.

---

## To the controlling family
You speak the language of stewardship, and I take that seriously. But stewardship means more than demanding adjustment.

It means:
- preserving long-term enterprise value,
- maintaining trust in the workforce,
- and avoiding irreversible damage to the company’s home region.

A strategy that destabilizes industrial relations, damages the regional base, and sidelines co-determination may look decisive, but it can destroy value in the medium term.

So yes, some adjustment may be unavoidable. But no, I will not support a logic that treats closure as the default proof of seriousness.

---

## To investors
You are right that the company needs a credible path back to margins and execution certainty. I share that concern.

But I also caution you: a closure-heavy approach can carry major hidden costs:
- strikes,
- legal conflict,
- loss of skills,
- reputational damage,
- and long-term erosion of the company’s role as an industrial anchor.

Those are not side effects. They are part of the economics.

If a negotiated alternative can deliver real savings and stability without destroying the German footprint, that is not “soft.” That is value-preserving.

---

## What I need to see next
I want the debate to move from positions to **numbers and conditions**.

### Specifically:
1. **What is the exact savings gap?**
2. **What savings come from labor-side measures without closures?**
3. **What savings come from capacity reductions short of closures?**
4. **What residual gap remains?**
5. **Why does that residual gap truly require closures or forced layoffs?**
6. **What investment and product commitments are management and owners prepared to make in return?**

Without that, nobody can honestly claim that the hard-line option is proven.

---

## My practical proposal for the next step
I am prepared to use the state’s role to broker a settlement, but only on these terms:

- **No closure as the base case**
- **No forced redundancies as the first resort**
- **Binding investment commitments for German sites**
- **Binding retraining and redeployment guarantees**
- **A quantified employee contribution**
- **Substantive involvement of co-determination in implementation**
- **A clear test of necessity before any irreversible step**

If the numbers show that a closure-free path is impossible, then we can discuss what that means. But we are not there.

---

## Bottom line
I will not give political cover to a restructuring that guts jobs and plants in Germany before all reversible options have been exhausted.

If the company wants the state’s support, bring forward a package that:
- protects the regional industrial base,
- restores competitiveness,
- and shares the burden fairly.

If it cannot do that, then it should not expect our assent — and it should be prepared for resistance in the boardroom and beyond.
