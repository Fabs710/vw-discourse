We are now at the point where “we need restructuring” has been said enough times. The issue is not whether change is needed. The issue is **who bears the burden, whether irreversible damage is really necessary, and whether management is using closures and layoffs as the easiest instrument rather than the last resort**.

## To management
I agree with you on two points:
- the status quo is not viable,
- and a cosmetic compromise would be useless.

I also accept that the company needs durable savings, a competitive cost base, and room to invest. That is not in dispute.

But I do **not** accept your leap from “we need savings” to “therefore plant closures and compulsory redundancies must remain on the table.” That leap remains unproven.

You keep repeating:
- overcapacity,
- structural cost disadvantage,
- durability,
- execution certainty.

Fine. Then **prove necessity, not preference**.

What you have not delivered is a transparent, quantified case showing:
1. the exact savings gap,
2. what voluntary exits, redeployment, working-time flexibility, bonus restraint, wage moderation, and productivity measures would yield,
3. what residual gap remains,
4. and why that residual gap cannot be closed without closing plants or forcing layoffs.

Until that is on the table, closures are not a fact of economics. They are a choice of management.

And we are not accepting a solution where workers are asked to give up wages, guarantees, and trust while management retains the right to hit the workforce with closures later. That is not a settlement. It is a staged surrender.

## To the works council
We are aligned on the essentials:
- no plant closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- no dismantling of the collective framework,
- and no “pay first, trust later” deal.

I support that. Your point is correct: capacity reduction does **not** automatically mean plant closure. There are multiple instruments:
- shift changes,
- working-time reductions,
- temporary utilization cuts,
- internal redeployment,
- reskilling,
- voluntary exit packages,
- and negotiated productivity measures.

That is exactly why management must stop pretending closure is the default economic answer.

At the same time, we should be careful not to let “capacity adjustment” become a euphemism for rolling back standards plant by plant. If employees contribute through wage moderation and flexibility, that must be matched by:
- binding job security,
- binding site security,
- and binding investment and product commitments.

We are not trading one-sided pain for vague promises.

## To the state government
I appreciate your willingness to resist closures as the starting point. That matters.

But I need to be equally blunt: “no closures as the base case” is not enough unless you are prepared to **use your blocking power** if management tries to force an irreversible restructuring that destroys jobs and the industrial base without exhausting alternatives.

If the state truly wants a fair and durable solution, then it should back a package that includes:
- no plant closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- serious employee contribution,
- real investment,
- training and reskilling,
- and a negotiated industrial plan.

If the state instead allows management to keep closure threats alive as leverage, then the workforce will quite rightly see that as pressure, not stewardship.

## To the controlling family
You talk about long-term enterprise value and stewardship. Then let me define stewardship plainly:
- protect the productive workforce,
- preserve trust,
- maintain co-determination,
- and do not solve a structural problem by destroying the social compact.

You are right that the company cannot drift into managed decline. We agree on that.

But managed decline is not the only alternative to closures. There is also **negotiated restructuring** with shared sacrifice. That means:
- employees contribute through temporary wage moderation, bonus restraint, and flexibility,
- management and owners contribute through investment commitments, site commitments, and restraint on irreversible cuts,
- and the burden is shared instead of dumped on the workforce.

If you support a deal that preserves value, then you should support the package that is most likely to preserve skills, labour peace, and long-term output — not the package that simply looks toughest on paper.

## To investors
You are right to care about margins, certainty, and durable savings. But your lens becomes too narrow when you imply that only closure-based savings count as “real.”

That is not correct.

A credible alternative package can absolutely produce real and durable value if it includes:
- substantial labour-cost moderation,
- bonus restraint,
- working-time flexibility,
- voluntary reductions,
- redeployment,
- productivity improvements,
- and capacity adjustments without site shutdowns.

What matters is not whether a saving comes from closing a plant. What matters is whether the package:
- restores competitiveness,
- avoids unnecessary destruction,
- reduces conflict risk,
- and preserves the company’s long-term productive base.

You cannot simply assume that a closure-driven path is superior because it is cleaner on a spreadsheet. The costs of strikes, legal conflict, reputational damage, and skill loss are real. If you ignore those, your “equivalence” test is incomplete.

## On what we are prepared to discuss
We are prepared to negotiate a serious package that includes:
- temporary wage moderation,
- suspension or reduction of bonuses,
- working-time flexibility,
- voluntary exit instruments,
- redeployment,
- retraining and reskilling,
- productivity measures,
- and capacity adjustment.

But only if the package also includes:
1. **No plant closures**
2. **No compulsory redundancies**
3. **Binding job and site security through 2030**
4. **Binding investment and product commitments for German sites**
5. **Substantive co-determination in implementation**

That is the fair exchange.

## What we reject
We reject:
- using closures as leverage,
- making workers pay for management’s strategic errors,
- open-ended job insecurity,
- and any “solution” that breaks the collective wage framework while keeping irreversible cuts on standby.

## Bottom line
If management wants movement, it must bring numbers and abandon the threat posture:
- show the cost gap,
- show the savings from alternatives,
- and show why closures and forced layoffs are truly unavoidable.

If it cannot do that, then its position is not a restructuring plan. It is an attempt to force the workforce to accept pain first and promises later.

We are not blocking change. We are blocking one-sided change.

Bring a fair package with shared sacrifice and binding security, and we will engage seriously. Bring closures and compulsory redundancies as the premise, and you should expect resistance — in bargaining, in governance, and in the plants.
