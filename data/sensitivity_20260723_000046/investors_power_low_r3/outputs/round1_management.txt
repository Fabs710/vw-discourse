Let me be equally clear: the status quo is not an option.

We are facing a structural cost crisis, shrinking demand in Europe, and intensified competition that we are not currently set up to absorb. Our German factory cost base is materially above target. The brand is not meeting its return-on-sales objective. If we do not act now, we will lose room to invest in the products, software, and technology needed for 2030.

So I agree with one part of what you both say: this must be durable, not cosmetic. A short-term patch that preserves today’s overcapacity and cost structure would fail everyone, including the workforce, because it would simply postpone a much harsher adjustment later.

Where I disagree is on your red lines as stated.

### On plant closures and compulsory redundancies
I understand the political and social weight of plant closures, but I will not pretend that every German site can be preserved unchanged. If the cost base remains structurally uncompetitive, then capacity must come out of the system. That may include closures, significant volume shifts, and workforce reductions. I am not going to rule those out in advance.

I also cannot accept a framework that excludes compulsory reductions entirely if that prevents a viable restructuring. We should exhaust negotiated solutions first, but I will not accept a deal that leaves us without credible capacity to right-size the business.

### On job guarantees through 2030
A blanket job guarantee through 2030 is not compatible with the scale of change we need. What I can discuss is a package that offers transition security, reskilling, internal redeployment, and a structured adjustment path — but not an unconditional guarantee that locks in every current location and role regardless of competitiveness.

### On employee contribution
If the workforce wants to avoid harsher structural measures, then there must be a real labour-cost contribution. That means meaningful savings, not symbolic concessions. I am talking about measurable and durable improvements in labour cost, working-time arrangements, and flexibility. Anything less will not close the gap.

### On the industrial future
On this point, there is common ground: if the company is to make this transition successfully, we need investment in future products, technology, and skills. But that investment depends on freeing up cash now. We cannot promise more future investment while leaving the cost base untouched.

### My position
So my position is this:
- We need a **structural** reduction in cost and capacity.
- We need a **material** employee contribution.
- We need a settlement that improves margins and restores competitiveness.
- We are open on the exact mix of closures, capacity reduction, and labour-cost measures.
- We are **not** open to a cosmetic compromise that preserves overcapacity and permanent cost weakness.

If the employee side wants to avoid closures, then it must bring forward a package that achieves equivalent savings and capacity reduction in a credible, durable way. If you can do that, I will engage seriously. If not, then we will have to pursue the harder restructuring path.

So I am asking both of you directly: what concrete combination of capacity reduction, labour-cost contribution, and structural savings are you prepared to put on the table that actually gets us to a competitive 2030 cost base?
