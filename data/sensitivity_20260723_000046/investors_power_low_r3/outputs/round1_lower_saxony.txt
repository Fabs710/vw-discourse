I appreciate the clarity from all sides, and I will be equally clear.

The state government does not deny the severity of the company’s competitive challenge. We understand that the German cost base is under pressure and that the transition to electric vehicles requires capital, flexibility, and a viable industrial plan. On that point, I agree with management: doing nothing is not an option.

But I disagree very firmly with the implication that the only credible path is plant closures, mass job cuts, and a unilateral erosion of protections.

### Where I agree
- The restructuring must be **durable**, not cosmetic.
- The company needs a **competitive cost base** and room for **future investment**.
- Employee contributions can be part of a settlement.
- Capacity will likely have to be adjusted somewhere.

### Where I do not agree
I do **not** accept that Germany must pay for competitiveness through closures and structural damage to the regional industrial base.

As a shareholder and as a public actor, I have a responsibility to protect:
- industrial employment in the region,
- the company’s role as a regional anchor,
- and the co-determination and veto structure that has helped stabilize this company for decades.

A plan that closes plants in Germany, creates lasting unemployment, and sidelines the state and workforce would not be a responsible restructuring. It would be a high-risk political and industrial rupture.

### On management’s position
Management is right to insist on real savings. But savings are not the same as plant closures. Before anyone talks about breaking sites, I want to see a package that explores:
- working-time adjustments,
- temporary wage moderation or targeted labor-cost relief,
- internal redeployment,
- retraining and reskilling,
- product allocation and volume commitments to German sites,
- and differentiated capacity reduction that avoids irreversible closures where possible.

If management says closures are “not ruled out,” then I will say just as clearly: **neither are blockages and vetoes ruled out**. Under the special statute and the governance rules, you do not get to treat that as a technical detail. It is a real power center, and it will be used if the red lines are crossed.

### On the workforce position
I strongly welcome the works council’s and union’s willingness to discuss burden sharing, investment, and a negotiated solution. That is the right direction.

At the same time, I would urge the employee side to remain open to **measurable adjustments** if they are paired with:
- binding site commitments,
- no forced redundancies,
- and a credible long-term industrial plan.

The state can support a settlement that asks employees to contribute, but only if that contribution is part of a fair exchange, not a pretext for hollowing out the company.

### What I can support
I could support a restructuring package that:
- preserves German sites to the maximum extent possible,
- avoids compulsory redundancies,
- uses voluntary, negotiated, and socially responsible instruments first,
- includes a serious employee contribution,
- and locks in investment in future products and skills.

What I cannot support is a one-sided “competitiveness” program that treats plant closures as the default solution and regards the regional economy as collateral damage.

### My question back to management
If the objective is truly a competitive 2030 cost base, then bring forward a plan that demonstrates:
1. how much savings can be achieved without closures,
2. what investment is committed to German sites,
3. how jobs will be protected,
4. and what role the workforce and state will have in governing the transition.

Without that, there is no basis for asking us to consider irreversible measures.

So let me be explicit: I am open to restructuring, but only to a **social-partnership restructuring** that preserves the company’s industrial footprint and respects the governance rights of the state and the workforce. Anything that cuts through those foundations will meet resistance.
