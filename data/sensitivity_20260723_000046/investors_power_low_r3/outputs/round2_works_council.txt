I will be equally direct in Round 2.

Management says the status quo is not an option. On that point, yes: the company needs a viable future. But what you are still not doing is proving that **plant closures and forced redundancies are necessary** rather than simply being the harshest option you prefer.

That distinction matters. Because for us, it is the difference between a restructuring and an attack on the workforce.

## Where I agree
I agree that:
- the solution must be durable, not cosmetic,
- the company needs a competitive cost base,
- investment in future products and skills is essential,
- and employees can contribute to a settlement.

But “employee contribution” does **not** mean surrendering job security, plant security, and co-determination.

## Where I disagree
### 1) On plant closures
We reject closures outright. Not as a bargaining trick — as a principle.

You have not demonstrated that every German plant is economically impossible. What you have done is present closures as if they were inevitable, while still refusing to fully explore alternative capacity tools:
- internal volume shifts,
- working-time adjustments,
- temporary utilization reductions,
- voluntary programs,
- retraining and redeployment,
- targeted cost moderation,
- and governance-led productivity measures.

If there is a savings gap, then show it transparently and prove that it cannot be closed without destroying sites. Until then, closures are a choice, not a necessity.

### 2) On compulsory redundancies
We will not accept forced layoffs. Period.

If management wants to reduce headcount, then it must do so through:
- natural attrition,
- early retirement,
- voluntary exits,
- internal redeployment,
- qualification and transfer,
- and negotiated instruments that preserve dignity and social responsibility.

A company of this size does not need to start by threatening people with the boot. That is a political decision, not an economic law.

### 3) On the “competitive 2030 cost base”
You keep saying “durable” and “credible,” but you are not giving a serious quantified alternative.

So here is our position:
- **Binding job and site guarantees through 2030**
- **No closures**
- **No forced redundancies**
- **Real employee contribution only in exchange for real security**
- **Co-determination involved in shaping the restructuring, not just informed afterward**

If management wants labour cost relief, then it must also accept binding guarantees on:
- site allocation,
- product commitments,
- investment,
- and workforce planning.

Without that, you are asking the workforce to pay first and trust later. We have seen how that ends.

## To the state government
You are right to resist a plan that damages the regional industrial base. But let me be clear: “preserving the industrial footprint” is not compatible with allowing one plant closure after another to become the new normal.

If the state wants to use its influence responsibly, then it should back a package that:
- protects all German sites,
- prevents forced redundancies,
- preserves co-determination,
- and secures future investment at the locations.

Your veto power is real. Use it if needed.

## To the controlling family
You say stewardship means long-term value. I agree. Then act like a steward, not a short-term enforcer.

Long-term value is not created by breaking trust with the workforce. It is created by:
- stable industrial relations,
- skilled employees,
- secure sites,
- and a credible transformation plan.

If you insist on closures and forced job cuts, you will not get trust, and without trust there will be no stable transformation. Only conflict.

## To investors
You say you want equivalence and durability. Fine. Then compare the true costs:
- strike risk,
- legal disputes,
- governance blockages,
- reputational damage,
- loss of skills,
- and the destruction of site morale

against a negotiated settlement that preserves jobs and sites.

A closure-driven “solution” may look clean on a spreadsheet and still destroy value in practice. Do not pretend otherwise.

## Our minimum terms
We are not moving off these:
1. Every German plant stays open.
2. No compulsory redundancies.
3. Binding job security through 2030.
4. Co-determination remains substantive, not ceremonial.
5. Any employee contribution is tied to guaranteed investment, training, product allocation, and workload protection.

If management can present a serious plan that meets those conditions and actually closes the cost gap, then we will discuss the details. But if the proposal still depends on closures or forced layoffs, then there is no basis for agreement.

## Final point
Do not mistake our firmness for inflexibility. We are prepared to negotiate hard on instruments, timing, and burden-sharing.

What we will not negotiate away are the workforce’s basic protections and the existence of the German plants themselves.

If you want a settlement, bring a plan that protects jobs and sites. If you cannot do that, then you should expect resistance — in the supervisory board, in the plants, and in the public arena.
