We are hearing the right diagnosis from most sides, but still not yet a sufficiently credible route to restore margins.

## Where we agree
We agree with management, the family holding, and the state on the central point:
- the status quo is not viable,
- the German cost base is too high,
- durable restructuring is necessary,
- and a cosmetic settlement would destroy value.

We also agree with labor on one point that matters financially:
- a prolonged strike, legal deadlock, or governance paralysis would be expensive and value-destructive.

## Where we disagree

### 1) Labor’s position is not yet an economically sufficient substitute
We do **not** support a settlement that simply says:
- no plant closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- job guarantees through 2030,

unless it is backed by a package that is **clearly equivalent** in economics and durability.

At this stage, that equivalence has **not** been demonstrated.

Working-time changes, voluntary exits, bonus restraint, redeployment, and wage moderation may all help. But we have not seen proof that they can close the gap to a structurally competitive margin profile by themselves.

### 2) Management still has not fully quantified necessity
At the same time, management is not yet off the hook.

If closures or deeper structural measures are being used as the benchmark, then show the numbers:
- exact savings gap,
- what each alternative measure delivers,
- what residual gap remains,
- and why that residual gap truly requires closures or compulsory redundancies.

Until that is transparent, closure threats are still leverage, not proof.

### 3) The state’s caution is understandable, but it cannot be a veto on economics
We respect the state’s concern for jobs, skills, and regional legitimacy. But shareholders cannot support a structure that preserves every current footprint while leaving margins structurally impaired.

A blocking stance is only defensible if there is a **credible alternative** that restores competitiveness. Otherwise, it just postpones value destruction.

### 4) The family’s stewardship test has to be outcomes-based
We agree that stewardship should not mean a fire sale of the industrial base. But it also cannot mean preserving current structure at any price.

Stewardship, from a shareholder perspective, means:
- competitive margins,
- sufficient cash for future investment,
- and an executable restructuring with deal certainty.

## What would move us
We could support a closure-free solution only if it is **economically equivalent** and **durable**. That means:

1. **Material labor-cost reduction**
   - not symbolic moderation, but recurring savings.

2. **Real capacity reduction**
   - not just softer utilization, but actual fixed-cost and output reduction.

3. **A credible and enforceable employee contribution**
   - with measurable timing and savings.

4. **Binding investment and product commitments**
   - so concessions are tied to future competitiveness.

5. **Execution certainty**
   - lower strike risk, lower legal risk, and lower governance risk.

## What is unacceptable
We will not support an outcome that:
- leaves the cost base uncompetitive,
- keeps margins structurally impaired,
- or uses “no closures” as a slogan without showing how competitiveness is restored.

We are also not interested in blank-check job guarantees if the economics do not support them.

## Our practical position
So our position remains:

- **Open to avoiding plant closures**, but only if the alternative is genuinely equivalent in economics and durability.
- **Open to negotiated labor concessions**, if they are substantial and recurring.
- **Not open to a cosmetic compromise** that keeps overcapacity in place.
- **Not open to a settlement that defers the hard choices and calls that stewardship.**

## What we want next
We want the parties to stop arguing in abstractions and put forward a side-by-side comparison:
- current cost base,
- savings from each alternative measure,
- residual gap,
- and the structural adjustment needed to close it.

If labor can show a closure-free package that truly restores competitiveness, we will support it.

If management cannot show that closures are genuinely necessary, it should stop using them as a bargaining threat.

But if no one can produce a credible path back to competitive margins, then we will back the measures that most clearly restore capital discipline and long-term value.

That is the standard we are using.
