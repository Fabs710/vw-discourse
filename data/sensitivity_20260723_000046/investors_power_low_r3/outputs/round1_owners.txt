I will be direct.

I agree with management on the diagnosis: doing nothing is not an option. If the German cost base remains structurally too high and the brand misses its return targets, then the enterprise will lose flexibility to invest and the long-term value of the core asset will erode. That would be unacceptable.

I also agree with the state government on one important point: a reckless, one-sided shock to the German industrial footprint would be destructive. We have a responsibility to preserve the company’s long-term legitimacy, not just extract short-term savings.

Where I do **not** agree is with the current employee red lines if they are treated as absolute.

### To the Works Council and Union
A blanket rejection of plant closures and compulsory redundancies is not a viable starting point if the company truly has structural overcapacity. If savings cannot be found in a durable way, then the burden simply gets pushed onto future investment, margins, and ultimately more jobs. That is not protection; it is delay.

I am not asking for “closures for their own sake.” But I am saying clearly: if equivalent savings and capacity reduction cannot be achieved by negotiated means, then selective structural action must remain on the table. Otherwise the restructuring is not credible.

At the same time, I do hear your point that employees should not bear the full burden. That is fair. If the workforce is asked for meaningful concessions, then those concessions must be paired with a serious industrial plan, not just promises. I would expect:
- targeted and measurable labour-cost relief,
- strong use of voluntary instruments first,
- retraining and internal redeployment,
- and a clear commitment to future product allocation and investment in German sites.

But I will not support a package that locks in every site and every job regardless of economics.

### To the State Government
I respect the role of the state as anchor shareholder and the importance of regional stability. But I would caution against treating every closure or capacity adjustment as a governance breach. The special statute and co-determination matter, yes — but they cannot become a veto over reality.

If the company’s competitiveness problem is real, then the response cannot be limited to working-time tweaks and symbolic sacrifices. You are right that the solution must be socially responsible. You are wrong if you think that means avoiding irreversible structural decisions altogether.

### My position
The family holding’s priority is long-term enterprise value and stewardship. That means:
- no cosmetic compromise,
- no open-ended conflict,
- no permanent impairment of the business,
- and no settlement that leaves the company uncompetitive.

So I would support a package only if it delivers:
1. a **credible and durable cost reduction**,
2. **real capacity adjustment**,
3. a **material employee contribution**,
4. **stability and labour peace**,
5. and a **clear investment path** for the future.

If a negotiated solution can achieve those outcomes without closures, then that is preferable. But if not, then I will not back a settlement that preserves overcapacity just to avoid hard decisions.

### The practical test
So the real question is simple: can the employee side and the state present a package that closes the competitiveness gap on a durable basis? If yes, I will consider it seriously. If no, then management’s case for more structural measures becomes much stronger.

I am not here to endorse conflict, but I am also not here to protect the status quo at the expense of the company’s future.
