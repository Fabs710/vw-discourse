I will respond plainly.

## To the Works Council
We are aligned on the non-negotiables:
- no plant closures in Germany,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- no dismantling of the collective framework.

That is still our line.

I also agree with your insistence that employees should not “pay first and trust later.” If the workforce is asked for concessions, they must be tied to binding guarantees on investment, product allocation, training, and site security.

Where I would sharpen the point is this: the trade union cannot accept a settlement that merely postpones the crisis by draining wages and protections while leaving management’s strategic choices untouched. If there is to be any labour contribution, it must be part of a real industrial plan, not a bridge to later cuts.

## To Management
Your argument that the status quo is not viable is not contested. What is contested is your leap from “we need restructuring” to “therefore closures and forced redundancies must remain on the table.”

You still have not demonstrated that:
1. every alternative to closure has been exhausted,
2. the cost gap cannot be closed through negotiated instruments,
3. and the company cannot achieve the required savings without breaking the social compact.

That burden is on management, and it remains unmet.

I also reject the framing that a binding job guarantee through 2030 is automatically incompatible with restructuring. That may be true for a management plan built around plant closures. It is not true for a negotiated plan built around capacity adjustment, working-time instruments, voluntary exit, redeployment, and measured labour-cost relief.

Your repeated invocation of “durability” is fair in principle, but durability cannot mean permanent insecurity for employees. A settlement that relies on fear and unilateral pressure will not be stable anyway — it will produce strikes, conflict, and governance deadlock.

## To the State Government
I appreciate your position more than management’s, because you are at least acknowledging that a restructuring must be socially and industrially responsible.

But I need to be direct: saying “we do not accept closures” is not enough unless you are prepared to use your blocking power to stop them if necessary. If the workforce is being asked to contribute, then the state should insist on a genuine exchange:
- no forced redundancies,
- no plant closures,
- binding investment,
- and a real future for the German sites.

That is the only kind of burden-sharing that could be defended as fair.

## To the Controlling Family
You talk about stewardship. Then the test of stewardship is simple: do not solve a structural problem by breaking the collective agreement and destabilizing the workforce.

I agree that the company needs a durable cost base. I do **not** agree that the only path is to keep closure threats alive until labor accepts permanent concessions. That is coercion, not stewardship.

If you want a long-term enterprise value solution, then back a negotiated package with:
- site security,
- no forced redundancies,
- fair employee contributions,
- and a commitment from owners and management to share the burden.

## To Investors
You are right that cosmetic compromise is useless. We do not support that either.

But you underestimate the cost of a closure-and-layoff strategy:
- industrial conflict,
- legal disputes,
- loss of trust,
- skill destruction,
- and lasting damage to the collective wage framework.

Those are not externalities. They are economic costs. Any “equivalent savings” calculation that ignores them is incomplete.

## Our current position
We are prepared to discuss a package that includes:
- temporary wage moderation,
- suspension or reduction of bonuses,
- working-time flexibility,
- a solidarity contribution from employees,
- redeployment and reskilling,
- and capacity reductions **short of** closures,
**if and only if** there is:
- no plant closure,
- no compulsory redundancy,
- binding job security,
- and a firm investment and product commitment to German sites.

That is a fair exchange. Anything else is workers paying for management’s errors.

## What we need next
If management wants movement, then put numbers on the table:
- how much savings are required,
- how much can be achieved without closures,
- what concrete employee contribution you expect,
- and what you are prepared to guarantee in return.

If the answer is still “closures may be necessary,” then you should also expect that our answer remains “not acceptable.”

We are not here to rubber-stamp plant closures or forced layoffs. We are here to negotiate a serious settlement that protects jobs, sites, and the collective framework while contributing fairly to the company’s recovery.
