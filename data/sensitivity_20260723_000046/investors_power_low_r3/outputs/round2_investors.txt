As dispersed capital-market shareholders, our lens is simple: **does this get the company back to a credible, durable, competitive margin structure with deal certainty?** If yes, we can support it. If not, we cannot.

## Where we agree

We agree with management, the family holding, and to a degree the state government that **the status quo is not viable**. The current cost base and return-on-sales profile are not acceptable. A cosmetic settlement that preserves structural overcapacity would be value-destructive.

We also agree with the labour side on one point: **a prolonged, disruptive conflict would destroy value**. Strike escalation, legal deadlock, and governance stalemate all hurt shareholders directly.

## Where we disagree

### On the labour red lines
I do **not** accept “no closures, no compulsory redundancies, binding job guarantees through 2030” as a viable end-state if the economics do not support it.

Those positions may be understandable politically, but they are only acceptable to shareholders if the alternative package is **economically equivalent**. So far, I do not see that proof.

If the company remains structurally overcosted, then preserving every site and freezing labour adjustment would simply lock in weaker margins and lower valuation.

### On management’s posture
I also do **not** support using closures as a negotiating bludgeon unless management can show that they are truly necessary.

If management says closures are required, then it needs to quantify:
- the margin gap,
- the savings gap,
- what measures have been tested,
- and why alternatives cannot close the gap.

A hard line without quantified justification is not credible. Shareholders need evidence, not leverage rhetoric.

### On the state government
We respect the state’s role and its concern for the industrial base. But shareholder capital cannot be asked to finance an uncompetitive structure indefinitely.

If the state’s influence is used to block all meaningful adjustment, that would risk turning governance protections into value destruction. The state should push for a settlement that restores competitiveness, not simply preserve every current footprint.

### On the controlling family
We broadly agree with the family’s emphasis on long-term enterprise value. But stewardship means **delivering** a viable cost base, not preserving symbolic completeness.

A deal that avoids hard choices today but leaves the company structurally impaired later is not stewardship from our perspective.

## What we need to see

We would back a negotiated solution if it clearly delivers all of the following:

1. **A credible path back to competitive margins**
2. **Material and durable cost reduction**
3. **Real capacity alignment**
4. **Enforceable employee contributions**
5. **Lower execution risk than prolonged conflict**
6. **Deal certainty**

That means the burden is on the parties to produce a package with real numbers:
- how much cost reduction comes from labour measures,
- how much from capacity changes,
- whether voluntary exits and redeployment are enough,
- and what the residual gap is.

## Our bottom line

We are **open to avoiding plant closures** only if the replacement package is genuinely equivalent in economics and durability. We are **not** open to an outcome that leaves the cost base uncompetitive or margins structurally impaired.

If labour’s position makes a competitive restructuring impossible, then it must move. If management cannot prove closures are necessary, then it should not insist on them. But the final test for us is the same either way:

**Does the package restore competitiveness, protect margins, and provide sufficient certainty for capital?**

Until that is answered convincingly, we remain cautious and supportive of only those measures that demonstrably improve risk-adjusted shareholder value.
