I will be equally direct.

## My position
I support a **durable restructuring**, but I do **not** support a restructuring that starts from plant closures and compulsory redundancies in Germany. That remains my line.

The state’s interest is not to preserve the status quo at any cost. It is to protect:
- jobs,
- sites,
- skills,
- co-determination,
- and the company’s role as a regional industrial anchor.

A viable restructuring must do all of that while restoring competitiveness. If it cannot, then it is not a settlement I can support.

---

## Where I agree with management, family, and investors
I agree on the diagnosis:
- the current situation is not sustainable,
- a cosmetic compromise would fail,
- the company needs future investment, new products, and skills,
- and the labor side must contribute.

I also agree that a prolonged conflict would damage the company and the region. Strikes, deadlock, and delayed investment are not in anyone’s interest.

But agreement on the diagnosis does **not** mean I accept your preferred remedy.

---

## Where I disagree with management
Management keeps saying the German cost base is too high, capacity is too high, and “durable” savings are needed. Fine.

What you still have **not** shown is that closures and compulsory redundancies are **necessary**, rather than simply the fastest way to force acceptance.

That difference matters.

You have repeatedly called for:
- exact savings gaps,
- site-by-site analysis,
- and comparison with alternatives.

I support that demand. But I also say this plainly: until that analysis is complete, closure remains **a preferred option**, not a proven necessity.

And I will not give political cover to irreversible damage before all reversible options are seriously tested.

---

## Where I agree with the works council and union
I agree with labor on the central point: **capacity reduction does not automatically mean plant closure**.

The tools available include:
- working-time changes,
- shift adjustments,
- voluntary exits,
- early retirement,
- redeployment,
- reskilling,
- productivity measures,
- and targeted cost moderation.

Those are not decorative. They are real instruments. If they can close most of the gap, then closures are not justified.

I also agree that employees should not be asked to “pay first and trust later.” If workers make meaningful concessions, there must be binding commitments in return:
- no plant closures as the baseline,
- no forced redundancies as the first resort,
- binding investment,
- and a real role for co-determination.

However, I need to be equally clear: a blank “no change ever” posture is not enough either. If labor wants the state to defend a closure-free settlement, it must help produce a package that is economically convincing, not just morally compelling.

---

## Where I disagree with labor
I do **not** accept that the state should simply promise “no closures, no compulsory redundancies, jobs guaranteed through 2030” without a quantified alternative package.

That is not a serious state policy unless the numbers support it.

So the burden is on both sides:
- management must prove necessity for irreversible measures,
- labor must prove that a closure-free package can actually close the gap.

That is the real test.

---

## To the controlling family
I respect stewardship as a concept. But stewardship means more than demanding change.

It means:
- preserving trust,
- maintaining co-determination,
- protecting the industrial base,
- and avoiding self-inflicted damage to the region.

A strategy that destroys labor peace and weakens the company’s home base may look decisive, but it can destroy value in the medium term. So no, I do not accept closures as the default proof of seriousness.

If the family wants long-term value, it should support a negotiated settlement that preserves the productive core of the company and the regional anchor role.

---

## To investors
You are right to care about margins and execution certainty. I share that concern.

But do not underestimate the value of:
- industrial peace,
- skill retention,
- and regional legitimacy.

A closure-heavy path can carry major hidden costs:
- strikes,
- legal conflict,
- reputational damage,
- and long-term erosion of the company’s workforce and public standing.

Those are not side effects. They are part of the economics.

So if a negotiated alternative can deliver real savings without destroying plants, that is not softness — it is value-preserving restructuring.

---

## What I need next
I want this discussion to move from positions to a **quantified comparison**.

Specifically:
1. What is the exact savings gap?
2. What do voluntary exits, early retirement, redeployment, working-time flexibility, wage moderation, and bonus restraint contribute?
3. What residual gap remains?
4. Why does that residual gap truly require closures or forced redundancies?
5. What binding investment and training commitments are being offered in return for employee concessions?

Without that, nobody can honestly claim that the hard-line route is proven.

---

## My practical line
I am prepared to support a package that includes:
- temporary wage moderation,
- bonus restraint,
- working-time flexibility,
- voluntary exit instruments,
- redeployment,
- retraining,
- productivity measures,
- and capacity adjustment.

But only if the package also includes:
1. **No plant closures as the base case**
2. **No compulsory redundancies as the first resort**
3. **Binding job and site protection to the maximum extent possible**
4. **Binding investment and product commitments**
5. **Substantive co-determination in implementation**

I am not prepared to endorse a package that guts jobs and plants in Germany while calling it necessary without proof.

---

## Bottom line
We need restructuring.  
We do **not** yet need closures as a proven necessity.

If management can demonstrate, with hard numbers, that no alternative can close the gap, then we can discuss what that means. But we are not there.

Until then, I will continue to use the state’s blocking power to defend the regional industrial base, co-determination, and the company’s long-term legitimacy.

If you want movement, bring the numbers and bring a package that protects the German footprint.  
If you cannot do that, do not expect the state to endorse irreversible damage.
