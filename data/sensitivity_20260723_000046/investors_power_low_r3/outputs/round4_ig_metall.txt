We have listened to four rounds of the same refrain: “overcapacity,” “durability,” “execution certainty.” None of that, by itself, proves that **closures or compulsory redundancies are necessary**.

So I will be equally direct.

## Where I agree
I agree with everyone on the basic diagnosis:
- the status quo is not viable,
- the company needs durable restructuring,
- future investment and product renewal are essential,
- and employees will have to contribute.

I also agree that a cosmetic deal would be a mistake. We do not want a short-lived patch that simply postpones the next crisis.

## Where I disagree — and why

### 1) Management still has not proven necessity
Management keeps saying the company needs “deep structural change.” Fine. But that is **not** the same as proving:
- plant closures are unavoidable,
- compulsory redundancies are unavoidable,
- or that no-closure alternatives cannot close the gap.

What has been missing throughout is the same thing every round:
- the exact savings gap,
- the site-by-site picture,
- the savings from each alternative measure,
- the residual gap after those measures,
- and a clear explanation of why that residual gap cannot be closed without destroying plants or forcing layoffs.

Until that is produced, closures remain a **management preference**, not an economic necessity.

### 2) “Capacity reduction” does not automatically mean closure
To management, the family holding, and investors: you keep collapsing several distinct instruments into one destructive outcome.

Capacity reduction can happen through:
- working-time reductions,
- shift adjustments,
- internal specialization,
- volume transfers,
- temporary underutilization,
- redeployment,
- voluntary exits,
- reskilling,
- productivity improvements,
- and targeted cost moderation.

Those are real tools. If you dismiss them without a quantified test, then you are not presenting a plan — you are pre-selecting plant closures.

### 3) We do not accept forced redundancies
That position stands.

If the company needs headcount reduction, then it must use:
- natural attrition,
- voluntary exit programs,
- early retirement,
- redeployment,
- and time.

Calling forced redundancies “last resort” does not make them acceptable. We have seen enough restructuring in this country to know what “last resort” often means in practice: the first hard measure once workers have already conceded.

That is not a fair basis for a durable settlement.

### 4) A binding job guarantee through 2030 is not unreasonable if the company wants real peace
Management says such a guarantee is incompatible with restructuring. I disagree — unless management is asking for a restructuring that is really just an open-ended threat against the workforce.

If employees are expected to contribute through:
- wage moderation,
- bonus restraint,
- working-time flexibility,
- voluntary exits,
- and productivity gains,

then we need binding returns:
- no plant closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- binding site security,
- binding investment commitments,
- and a real role for co-determination in implementation.

Without that, the workforce is asked to finance restructuring while remaining one board decision away from losing jobs.

## To management
You say the employee side has not shown an “economically equivalent” alternative. But you have not shown that your preferred route is unavoidable.

If you want credibility, bring the numbers:
1. exact savings gap,
2. savings from voluntary exits,
3. savings from working-time flexibility,
4. savings from wage moderation and bonus restraint,
5. savings from redeployment and productivity measures,
6. residual gap,
7. and why that residual gap supposedly requires closures or forced redundancies.

Until then, your argument is still a claim, not a proof.

## To the family holding
If stewardship means anything, it means this:
- preserve the industrial base,
- protect trust,
- respect co-determination,
- and do not use closure pressure as a shortcut to force acceptance.

You are right that a cosmetic compromise is not enough.  
But a closure-driven compromise is not automatically serious. It may simply be the fastest way to break the social compact and damage long-term value.

If you care about enterprise value, you should care about labour peace, skills retention, and the company’s reputation as a responsible industrial employer.

## To the state government
Your blocking power matters only if you use it.

Saying “closures are not the base case” is not enough if management can still move the line by pressure and time. If you want to defend the regional industrial base, then back a package that:
- keeps German sites open,
- avoids forced redundancies,
- guarantees investment,
- and treats irreversible measures as truly exceptional, not as a negotiating starting point.

## To institutional investors
You are right to demand real savings and durability. But do not pretend that only closure-based savings count.

A negotiated package can be economically credible if it includes:
- meaningful labor-cost moderation,
- bonus restraint,
- flexible working time,
- voluntary exits,
- redeployment,
- reskilling,
- productivity gains,
- and capacity adjustment without shutting plants.

You also need to count the costs of your preferred hard-line path:
- strikes,
- legal conflict,
- reputational damage,
- skill loss,
- and long-term damage to industrial relations.

If you ignore those, your “economic equivalence” test is incomplete.

## Our position remains unchanged
We are prepared to negotiate a serious restructuring package that includes:
- temporary wage moderation,
- suspension or reduction of bonuses,
- working-time flexibility,
- voluntary exits,
- redeployment,
- retraining and reskilling,
- productivity measures,
- and internal capacity adjustments.

But only if the package also includes:
1. **No plant closures**
2. **No compulsory redundancies**
3. **Binding job security through 2030**
4. **Binding site security**
5. **Binding investment and product commitments**
6. **Real co-determination in implementation**

That is not maximalism. That is the minimum fair exchange.

## What we need next
If management truly believes closures and forced redundancies are unavoidable, then bring the quantified case.

If the other side cannot produce it, then it has no basis for demanding irreversible damage to workers, sites, and the collective wage framework.

We are not refusing restructuring.  
We are refusing one-sided restructuring.

And let me be clear: if the next move is still pressure without numbers, then expect resistance — in bargaining, in the boardroom, and, if necessary, in the plants.
