I will be straightforward.

## Where I agree
I agree with management, the investors, and the state government on the central diagnosis: **the status quo is not viable**. The company needs a durable restructuring that restores competitiveness, improves margins, and protects the capacity to invest in the future. A cosmetic settlement that leaves the cost base structurally uncompetitive would be a bad outcome for everyone, including the workforce.

I also agree that this must be **socially and operationally durable**. A prolonged confrontation, strike escalation, or governance deadlock would destroy value and weaken the company’s standing. That is not acceptable from an ownership perspective.

## Where I disagree
I do **not** accept the current labor-side position as a complete answer.

### On plant closures
The Works Council and Union keep repeating “no closures” as if that were enough. It is not.

If the company has real structural overcapacity and German production costs remain materially above competitive levels, then **some form of capacity reduction has to happen**. That may or may not mean closures, but the employee side cannot simply rule them out categorically and expect the economics to disappear.

I do not want closures for their own sake. But I will not support a settlement that preserves overcapacity indefinitely just to avoid hard choices. That would be value-destructive delay.

### On compulsory redundancies
I prefer voluntary and socially responsible instruments first. That should be the default. But I also will not make an absolute promise that no compulsory reductions can ever occur if they are the only way to restore viability. A blanket prohibition is too rigid for the scale of the challenge.

### On job guarantees through 2030
A blanket guarantee through 2030 for all current sites and roles is, in my view, not credible if the company is to regain competitiveness. We can discuss transition security, redeployment, retraining, and structured adjustment. But not a promise that freezes the industrial structure regardless of business reality.

## To the Works Council and Union
You say employees should not “pay first and trust later.” On that principle, I agree. Any employee contribution must be matched by a serious industrial plan and real investment commitments.

But you have not yet put forward a package that demonstrates how you close the competitiveness gap **without** structural change. If you want to avoid closures, then show the numbers:
- how much cost reduction you can deliver,
- how much capacity adjustment you can achieve,
- and how durable that is.

At the moment, I hear principles, not a fully credible alternative.

## To the State Government
I respect your role and your concern for the regional industrial base. That matters. But I need to be equally direct: the state cannot defend every site automatically and still expect the company to compete globally. If the state uses its veto rights to block all meaningful adjustment, then it risks preserving a weak structure rather than protecting a strong industrial future.

If you want to defend the German footprint, the best way is to help secure a package that actually restores profitability and investment capacity. That may require accepting some structural reductions if alternatives do not add up economically.

## To management
Management is right not to accept a cosmetic solution. But I would caution against framing closures as the only serious option before the alternatives have been fully tested.

If closures are necessary, demonstrate it clearly. If not, then do not use them as leverage to force a deal that could be achieved through negotiated measures. The burden is on management to show the gap and the economics.

## My position as controlling shareholder
My focus is long-term enterprise value and the preservation of governance stability. That means:

- no managed decline,
- no open-ended conflict,
- no permanently impaired competitiveness,
- and no outcome that strips the company’s ability to invest.

So I am prepared to support a **hard but negotiated restructuring** if it delivers:
1. **material and durable cost reduction,**
2. **credible capacity adjustment,**
3. **real employee contribution,**
4. **investment in future products and skills,**
5. **and industrial peace.**

What I will not support is a package that simply preserves the current structure and calls it compromise.

## The practical test
If the employee side wants to avoid closures, then bring a quantified counterproposal that closes the cost gap in a durable way. If the state wants to avoid closures, it should help shape such a package rather than merely vetoing outcomes. If management wants structural change, it should quantify precisely what is required and why.

I am not here to endorse conflict, but I am also not here to protect an uncompetitive status quo.

If no one can produce a credible alternative that meets the company’s economic needs, then I will not stand in the way of necessary restructuring.
