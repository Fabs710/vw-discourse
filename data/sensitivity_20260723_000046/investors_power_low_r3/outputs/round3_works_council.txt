We are now at the point where every side has stated its preferred narrative. That is not enough.

Let me be equally clear in Round 3: **nothing has been shown that justifies plant closures or compulsory redundancies as necessary**. Repeating “overcapacity” does not make closures economically proven, and repeating “durability” does not make job destruction acceptable.

## Where I agree
I agree with everyone on three things:
- the current situation is not sustainable,
- the company needs a durable restructuring with investment and future products,
- and a cosmetic compromise would be a mistake.

I also accept that employees must contribute. We never denied that. But contribution is not the same as surrender.

## Where I disagree — and why

### 1) Management has not proven closures are necessary
Management says the cost base is too high and alternatives are not enough. Fine. Then prove it.

What we have heard is:
- “closures may be necessary,”
- “voluntary measures are not enough,”
- “the current structure is uncompetitive.”

What we have **not** seen is a transparent, site-by-site case showing:
- the exact savings gap,
- what each alternative measure delivers,
- what residual gap remains after voluntary exits, working-time flexibility, redeployment, bonus restraint, wage moderation, and capacity shifts,
- and why that residual gap cannot be closed without destroying plants.

Until that is on the table, closures remain a **preference**, not a necessity.

### 2) “Capacity reduction” does not mean closure
To the family holding and investors: you keep saying capacity must come down. We do not dispute that some capacity adjustment may be needed.

But capacity reduction can be achieved through:
- volume transfers,
- working-time reductions,
- shift changes,
- internal specialization,
- product reallocation,
- temporary underutilization,
- redeployment,
- and negotiated efficiency programs.

It does **not** automatically mean shutting German plants. You are collapsing several different tools into one hard-line outcome, and that is exactly what we reject.

### 3) Compulsory redundancies are not acceptable
We are not moving on this. Not now, not under pressure, not because it is called “last resort.”

If the company needs workforce reduction, then it must use:
- natural attrition,
- voluntary exit programs,
- early retirement,
- redeployment,
- qualification and transfer,
- and time.

If management says that is too slow, then I say management is still choosing the easiest socially brutal path instead of the right one.

### 4) A job guarantee through 2030 is not unrealistic if management wants peace
To the accusation that binding job and site protection through 2030 is “too broad”: no, it is the minimum basis for trust.

You want the workforce to give up pay, flexibility, and certainty. Then the company must give something real in return:
- binding site guarantees,
- no closures,
- no forced layoffs,
- investment commitments,
- and a substantive role for co-determination in shaping implementation.

Without that, you are asking us to finance your restructuring with our livelihoods.

## To management
You speak as if the only serious answer is to keep closure threats alive until labor folds. That is not a strategy; it is coercion.

If you truly want a durable settlement, then do what you have not yet done:
1. publish the quantified savings gap,
2. show what each alternative measure contributes,
3. state the exact residual gap,
4. and demonstrate why that gap cannot be closed without closures or forced redundancies.

Until then, your “hard choices” language is just a way to normalise irreversible damage.

## To the controlling family
If stewardship is your word, then act on it.

Stewardship means:
- protecting the industrial base,
- preserving trust,
- respecting co-determination,
- and avoiding self-inflicted conflict.

It does **not** mean backing management into a corner and hoping the workforce accepts a bad deal. It also does **not** mean treating closures as a convenient proof of seriousness.

If you support closures now without proof of necessity, then you are not preserving long-term value; you are gambling with it.

## To the state government
You say you do not accept closures as the starting point. Good. Then use that blocking power constructively.

The state should not wait for management to impose a fait accompli and then react. It should set a clear line now:
- no closures as the base case,
- no forced redundancies,
- binding investment for German sites,
- and a negotiated restructuring that preserves the regional industrial base.

If the state wants to be taken seriously, it should not merely “prefer” this outcome — it should be prepared to stop the opposite.

## To investors
You keep demanding “economic equivalence.” Fair enough. Then stop treating closures as the only credible benchmark.

An economically equivalent package can include:
- durable labour-cost moderation,
- working-time flexibility,
- temporary sacrifice of bonuses,
- internal reallocation,
- productivity measures,
- and capacity adjustment without site shutdowns.

If you only count savings that come from closure, you are not evaluating the full business case. You are pre-selecting the answer.

## Our position remains unchanged
We are prepared to discuss a serious restructuring package that includes:
- temporary wage moderation,
- bonus restraint,
- working-time flexibility,
- voluntary exits,
- redeployment,
- reskilling,
- productivity measures,
- and capacity adjustment.

But only under these conditions:
1. **No plant closures**
2. **No compulsory redundancies**
3. **Binding job and site security through 2030**
4. **Real co-determination in shaping the plan**
5. **Binding investment and product commitments**

That is not maximalism. That is the minimum to keep social peace and protect the company’s industrial future.

If management believes our position is impossible, then bring the numbers. Not slogans, not pressure, not abstract threats.

Bring:
- the cost gap,
- the alternative savings,
- the timing,

Until that happens, we will continue to treat closure threats as what they are: a unilateral attack on the workforce that we will resist in the supervisory board, in bargaining, and if necessary in industrial action.

If you want a settlement, come with a plan that keeps all German plants open and protects jobs. If you cannot do that, then do not expect us to negotiate away the livelihoods of the people we represent.
