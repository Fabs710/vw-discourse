I will be direct.

## Where I agree
I agree with management, investors, and the state on the central fact: **the status quo is not viable**. The company needs a restructuring that is **durable**, not cosmetic, and it must restore competitiveness, margins, and room to invest.

I also agree with the labor side on one important point: **a prolonged conflict would destroy value**. A strike spiral, governance paralysis, and reputational damage would hurt the company and the family holding materially. So yes, industrial peace matters.

## Where I disagree with labor
The works council and union keep insisting on:
- no plant closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- and binding job guarantees through 2030.

I do **not** accept that as a sufficient answer.

That position may be understandable politically, but it does not yet solve the business problem. If the German cost base remains structurally too high and capacity remains above what the market can absorb, then the company cannot simply wish that away. Some form of structural adjustment is necessary.

That said, I am **not** saying closures are the preferred first move. I am saying they cannot be ruled out categorically unless an alternative is shown to close the gap in a durable, executable way.

## Where I disagree with management
Management is right to reject a cosmetic compromise. But I also think management must be careful not to treat closures as the only serious option before alternatives are fully tested.

If you want support for a hard restructuring, then the case has to be **quantified and transparent**:
- the exact savings gap,
- the contribution from labor measures,
- the contribution from capacity measures short of closures,
- and the residual gap after those measures.

Without that, closure threats are leverage, not proof.

So I am not giving management a blank check either.

## Where I disagree with the state government
I respect the state’s role and its blocking power. But I do not support using that power to preserve every current structure automatically.

Protecting the regional industrial base is important. Preserving an uncompetitive structure is not. If the state wants to defend jobs in the long run, it has to support a package that actually restores profitability and investment capacity.

A veto used to freeze necessary change would be value-destructive.

## My view on the works council’s position
I understand the argument that capacity reduction does not automatically mean plant closure. That is true.

But it is not enough to say “find another way” without showing the numbers. If you want to avoid closures, then bring a package that is:
- materially cheaper,
- durable,
- and operationally credible.

Possible instruments may include:
- wage moderation,
- bonus restraint,
- working-time flexibility,
- voluntary exits,
- redeployment,
- retraining,
- productivity measures,
- and capacity shifts.

I am open to those. But only if they actually close the gap.

## My position as controlling shareholder
My core interest is long-term enterprise value. That means:
- no managed decline,
- no permanently impaired margins,
- no open-ended conflict,
- no outcome that strips the company of future investment capacity.

So I am prepared to support a hard but negotiated restructuring if it delivers:
1. material and durable cost reduction,
2. real capacity adjustment,
3. a credible employee contribution,
4. investment in future products and skills,
5. and industrial peace.

What I will not support is a settlement that simply preserves the current structure and calls it compromise.

## What I want to see next
I want a **quantified counterproposal** on the table, from labor and management together if possible:
- how much savings each measure delivers,
- what capacity reduction is achieved,
- what the residual gap is,
- and what binding commitments are exchanged for employee concessions.

If the no-closure route really works economically, show it.

If it does not, then the parties should stop pretending that categorical red lines are a substitute for a viable plan.

## Bottom line
I am **open to avoiding closures**, but only if the alternative is genuinely equivalent in economics and durability.

I am **not open** to:
- a cosmetic no-closure package,
- a blanket job guarantee through 2030 regardless of business reality,
- or any outcome that leaves the company structurally uncompetitive.

The burden now is on the parties to prove that a closure-free solution can restore competitiveness. If that proof is not forthcoming, then I will not block necessary restructuring simply to avoid hard decisions.
