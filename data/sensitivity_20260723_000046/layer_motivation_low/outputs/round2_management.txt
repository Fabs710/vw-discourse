I’ll respond directly.

### To the works council
I agree with you on one point: a cosmetic restructuring is useless. We are not here to manage headlines; we are here to fix a cost base that is too high and a production footprint that is too large for the market we face.

Where I **strongly disagree** is your insistence that the answer must be **no closures, no compulsory redundancies, and binding job guarantees through 2030**. That combination is not a turnaround plan unless you can show me equivalent, permanent savings elsewhere. So far, you have not.

You keep saying management has chosen the blunt instrument. No — management has identified the scale of the problem. If the numbers require structural capacity reduction, then refusing closures in advance is not realism, it is denial.

I am willing to discuss:
- capacity reductions without closure where they are genuinely sufficient,
- redeployment,
- training,
- natural attrition,
- working-time flexibility,
- and a temporary employee contribution.

But I will not pretend that those measures automatically add up to a durable fix. If they do not, then they do not.

And let me be equally clear: **job guarantees through 2030 cannot be unconditional** if the company is expected to carry an uncompetitive cost burden. If you want stronger security, then the package must deliver stronger savings. That is the trade-off.

### To the union
I appreciate that you are at least talking about time-limited contributions and structural effect. That is more serious than saying “no” to everything. But your position still contains a fundamental contradiction.

You say:
- no plant closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- no permanent wage erosion,
- and preserve the collective framework.

At the same time, you want a restructuring that materially improves margins and competitiveness. Those goals can only coexist if the alternative package produces **real, durable economic impact**. Temporary concessions alone will not be enough if the structural gap is large.

So here is my answer to your question: **yes, capital and management must contribute**. We are already contributing — by putting future investment, product allocation, and restructuring credibility on the line. But no, I will not accept the implication that the workforce can keep the current structure and the company can simply absorb the cost.

What I can consider is:
- temporary wage moderation,
- reduction of variable compensation,
- working-time adjustments,
- voluntary measures,
- and redeployment.

What I cannot accept is a package that preserves all present capacity, preserves all present cost structures, and calls that a solution.

### To the regional government
You are right to insist that this cannot be treated as a pure management diktat. But I need to be equally direct: **the state cannot veto competitiveness into existence**.

If your position is that closures are politically unacceptable under any circumstances, then you are effectively requiring the company to solve a structural overcapacity problem without the structural tools that problem may require. That is not a sustainable governance position.

I welcome your support for:
- negotiated restructuring,
- avoidance of unnecessary conflict,
- investment commitments,
- and socially managed adjustment.

But if the state position hardens into “no closures, ever,” then it becomes part of the blockage, not the solution.

### To the controlling family
You are right that uncontrolled conflict destroys value. That is exactly why I am not interested in symbolic confrontation for its own sake. But your warning cuts both ways: open-ended preservation of uncompetitive structures also destroys value.

I need the ownership side to be clear about what it supports:
- Is it prepared to back a deep structural cost reduction?
- Is it prepared to accept site decisions if they are necessary?
- Or is it only prepared to support a compromise that sounds acceptable but leaves the company unable to hit its margin targets?

Because if the latter is the answer, then we are not agreeing on a turnaround. We are delaying it.

### To institutional investors
You are right on one thing: the package must be durable, quantified, and credible to capital markets. I agree. But you cannot have credibility without structural change. A deal that avoids hard decisions entirely is not investable.

So yes:
- lower unit costs,
- better utilization,
- margin recovery,
- deal certainty.

But those outcomes will not emerge from goodwill alone. They require a package strong enough to change the economics. If the closure-free alternative can do that, I will consider it seriously. If it cannot, then it is not a viable substitute.

### The core point
Let me put this plainly:

**I am not asking for a “surrender package.” I am asking for a package that actually restores competitiveness.**

That means:
1. **A measurable and durable reduction in capacity**
2. **A genuine labour-cost contribution**
3. **A credible path to job reduction**
4. **Investment and product commitments to 2030**

If the employee side wants to avoid closures, then it needs to show how the same economics are achieved without them. Not in slogans — in numbers.

### What I need next
Stop repeating red lines and give me a working alternative:
- How much savings do you deliver?
- Which measures are permanent?
- What headcount reduction is achieved?
- What capacity comes out of the system?
- What is the timing?
- And who bears the cost?

If you bring me a package that truly closes the gap, I will engage.  
If not, then closures remain on the table, because I will not sign off on a restructuring that leaves the company structurally uncompetitive.
