The discussion is now sufficiently concrete to require a clear governance view.

## Where I agree

I agree with the CFO, management, and the capital markets on the central economic point: **the status quo is not sustainable**. The company needs a durable structural turnaround, not a cosmetic truce.

I also agree with the works council, union, and state on one important point: **a fake compromise that merely shifts pain around without fixing the cost base would destroy value**. That would be a bad outcome for everyone, including the family holding.

## Where I disagree

### To the works council and union
I do **not** accept the position that:
- no plant closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- full job guarantees through 2030,
- and only temporary concessions

can be treated as an adequate answer **without** proof that this delivers the required steady-state savings.

The CFO has made the test very clear: a closure-free package is only credible if it creates:
- permanent fixed-cost reduction,
- real capacity rationalisation,
- a meaningful labor-cost reset,
- and headcount reduction over time.

If that cannot be shown, then “no closure” is not a solution — it is delay.

So I am **not** prepared to endorse a blanket no-closure promise. I understand why you want it, but wanting it does not make it financially sufficient.

### To the regional government
I respect the state’s governance role and its legitimate concern for the industrial base. But I do **not** accept a pre-emptive political veto on closures or compulsory redundancies before the economics have been tested.

If closures turn out to be the least damaging way to restore competitiveness, then blocking them categorically would not be stewardship. It would be value destruction by inertia.

### To management
I agree with you that a cosmetic settlement would be worthless. But I also do **not** accept a posture that treats closures as justified simply because they are the cleanest management instrument.

You still have to prove, with numbers, that closure-free alternatives genuinely fall short. The burden of proof remains on the side that wants hard site decisions.

### To institutional investors
I agree with your insistence on durable, measurable savings and a credible margin path. But I do **not** accept the idea that closures are automatically the benchmark of seriousness. A strong closure-free package could be superior if it reaches the same steady-state outcome with lower execution and conflict risk.

## My governance position

The family holding’s position is this:

1. **We support a real restructuring.**
2. **We support site-preserving adjustment if it is economically sufficient.**
3. **We do not support permanent cost structures disguised as compromise.**
4. **We do not support open-ended conflict.**
5. **We do not support unnecessary destruction of productive capacity.**

That means I am open to a closure-free solution **only if** it is financially sufficient at steady state. I am not prepared to give a blank cheque to either side.

## My current line

I am **not** prepared to endorse:
- unconditional 2030 guarantees that freeze the cost base,
- permanent labor-cost weakness,
- a compromise that leaves overcapacity untouched,
- or a deal that fails to restore margins and investment capacity.

I am **also not** prepared to endorse closures as the starting point without a hard financial case.

That is the governance position. The numbers now have to decide whether the closure-free route is genuinely sufficient or whether harder decisions become unavoidable.
