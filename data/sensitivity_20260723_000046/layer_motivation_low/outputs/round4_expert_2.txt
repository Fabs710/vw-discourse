## 1) Core economic conclusion

A **closure-free package can be credible only if it is structurally deep**.  
If it relies mainly on temporary wage moderation and voluntary measures, it usually **does not** remove enough permanent fixed cost.

In plant-network terms:

- **Plant closures** are the cleanest way to remove **site fixed costs, indirect labor, and excess capacity** permanently.
- **Closure-free restructuring** can work only if it achieves:
  - major shift reductions,
  - line consolidation,
  - significant voluntary exits/attrition,
  - overhead simplification,
  - and durable labour-cost relief.

So the real question is not “closure or no closure,” but:

**Can the non-closure package permanently reduce the fixed-cost base enough to lift steady-state EBIT to target?**

## 3) What a closure-free package would need to include

For a closure-free route to be economically serious, it would need all of the following:

1. **Material capacity reduction**
   - fewer shifts
   - line consolidation
   - model rationalization
   - lower installed manufacturing capacity

2. **Permanent workforce reduction**
   - natural attrition alone is usually too slow
   - voluntary exit programs must be large enough
   - redeployment only works if there are real vacancies elsewhere

3. **Indirect cost reset**
   - plant overhead
   - engineering support
   - admin layers
   - internal logistics duplication

4. **Durable labour-cost relief**
   - not just one-year concessions
   - enough duration to bridge the turnaround
   - clear reset of bonus, allowance, and time-cost structures

5. **Productivity investment**
   - automation
   - digital process control
   - layout simplification
   - otherwise the company preserves an inefficient footprint

Without those five elements, “no closure” usually means **delay**, not restructuring.

## 4) Main trade-off

### Closure route
**Pros**
- Best at removing structural excess capacity
- Strongest effect on fixed-cost base
- Most convincing to capital markets if executed cleanly

**Cons**
- Highest one-time cost
- Highest industrial-relations risk
- Governance and veto complexity
- Potential disruption to output and supply chain

### Closure-free route
**Pros**
- Lower political and social friction
- Lower immediate disruption
- Better chance of negotiated implementation
- Preserves site optionality

**Cons**
- Often weaker at removing overhead and underutilized capacity
- More dependent on labor cooperation over time
- Risk of partial, temporary savings
- Can leave the company with too much fixed cost

## 5) Practical judgment

My professional assessment, based on the scenario as described:

- A **closure-free solution is possible in principle**
- But a **closure-free, no-compulsory-redundancy, full-site-preservation solution is unlikely to match the savings of closures**
- If management is serious about a closure-free option, it must show:
  - how much capacity comes out,
  - how many jobs disappear through voluntary means,
  - how much overhead is cut,
  - and what the steady-state EBIT bridge looks like

If it cannot show that, then the closure-free plan is **not economically equivalent**.
