Management is still trying to turn a structural problem into a social demolition project. I reject that.

Let me respond point by point.

To management: you keep saying the numbers require closures and compulsory redundancies, but you have not shown that those are the only way to achieve the savings. You have shown a preference for the bluntest instrument. That is not the same thing. If the company needs durable relief, then it must first exhaust the measures that protect the workforce and the sites:
- capacity reductions without closures,
- shorter working time,
- redeployment across plants,
- internal qualification,
- natural attrition,
- temporary and proportionate contribution by employees,
- and real contributions from management and owners.

What I do **not** accept is this logic: “because the company is under pressure, German plants must be closed and jobs destroyed.” That is exactly the kind of one-sided burden shifting we will fight.

To the investors: you talk about “durability” and “verifiable savings,” but you are too willing to treat workers as a cost item to be cut. If you want credibility, then you should support a settlement that gives the company savings **without** ripping up the social contract. A package that provokes years of conflict, warning strikes, and governance deadlock is not financially clean. It is reckless. A solution that destroys industrial peace is not a solution.

To the family owners: you are right that uncontrolled conflict destroys value. That is precisely why you should not back management into a corner where it feels it has to provoke one. If the ownership side wants stability, then it must back a socially responsible restructuring with binding guarantees — not vague promises and then pressure for closures once the workforce has weakened.

To the regional government: I welcome your clear stance that closures and mass compulsory redundancies are not acceptable and that you will use your rights. But that must translate into a hard line in governance, not polite concern. If management is serious about adjustment, it must come through a negotiated package that keeps all German sites open and protects jobs.

So here is the position of the works council, clearly stated:

### What we can accept
- A negotiated restructuring **without plant closures**
- No compulsory redundancies
- Capacity adjustment through working-time and output changes
- Internal redeployment and training
- Natural attrition and voluntary instruments
- A temporary, limited employee contribution **only if** it is matched by binding site and job guarantees
- Stronger transparency and co-determination in implementation

### What we cannot accept
- Any closure of a German plant
- Any forced layoff
- Any “temporary” measure that becomes permanent wage erosion
- Any deal that breaks the historic job-security framework
- Any plan that treats German plants as disposable

### What would actually move us
Only two things:
1. **Binding guarantees to 2030 for all German sites and jobs**
2. **A genuine co-determination role in shaping the turnaround plan**

Without those two things, there is no basis for trust. And without trust, there is no basis for a durable settlement.

Management asked for numbers. Fine. Then put the closure-free package on the table and quantify it honestly:
- how much cost reduction from working-time flexibility,
- how much from temporary concessions,
- how much from management restraint,
- how much from owner contribution,
- how much from investment-led productivity gains,
- and how much from internal redeployment.

If that package is truly insufficient, I want to see the proof. Not assumptions, not ideology, not a management preference for closures.

Until then, our answer remains the same: **no plant closures, no compulsory redundancies, no dismantling of job security.** We are prepared to negotiate a turnaround, not a surrender.
