I will be direct.

The CFO’s assessment is useful, because it confirms what we have been saying from the start: **a closure-free solution is only credible if it delivers real, permanent fixed-cost reduction and capacity rationalization.** That is the right test. I do **not** support a fake compromise that merely postpones the problem.

That said, I draw a different political conclusion than management.

## To management
You are right that the company needs a real turnaround. You are **not** yet right that closures are the necessary starting point.

The CFO has just made clear that the question is whether the package reaches the required steady-state savings. That means your job is to prove, with numbers, that **all reasonable closure-free measures have been exhausted** before demanding plant closures in Germany.

So far, I have not seen that proof.

What I do agree with:
- the status quo is not acceptable,
- costs must come down,
- temporary measures alone are not enough,
- and any settlement must be economically durable.

What I do **not** accept:
- treating German closures as the default answer,
- compulsory redundancies as an easy lever,
- or a restructuring that weakens the regional industrial base just because it is administratively simpler.

If you want state support, then bring a package that keeps the German footprint intact **unless and until** you can show that no other route can deliver the required savings. That burden of proof is still not met.

## To the works council
Your insistence on protecting jobs and sites is legitimate, and I share the core objective. But I will not pretend that every existing structure can remain untouched.

The CFO is right on one point: **“no closure” must still mean real capacity reduction, real cost reduction, and real headcount reduction over time.** If you want my backing, then the alternative must be more than a slogan.

I can support:
- no plant closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- capacity adjustment through shift, line, and volume changes,
- internal redeployment,
- training and qualification,
- voluntary exits,
- natural attrition,
- and temporary employee contributions.

But I need those measures to be quantified and credible. A purely defensive posture will not secure the company’s future.

## To the union
I appreciate that you are willing to discuss temporary and conditional contributions. That is the right direction. I also agree that permanent wage dumping is unacceptable.

But I want to be clear: the state will not endorse an employee-only burden-sharing package. If workers contribute, then **management, owners, and the company itself must also contribute in binding form**.

That means:
- management restraint,
- overhead reduction,
- owner support,
- and clear investment commitments for German operations.

I would support a time-limited social partnership package if it is tied to:
- site guarantees,
- no forced redundancies,
- measurable savings,
- and a transparent monitoring mechanism.

## To the controlling family
You are right that open conflict destroys value. But stewardship is not just about approving hard cuts and then calling that realism.

If the family wants to be taken seriously as a long-term owner, then it should actively support a negotiated restructuring that protects the industrial core in Germany, not push management toward closures before alternatives are fully tested.

The CFO has given us the framework: test the steady-state savings. I want the family to back a solution that preserves jobs and sites **if** the economics can be made to work. That is the responsible ownership position.

## To institutional investors
You are right to demand durable, measurable savings. I do not dispute that. But I reject the idea that durability automatically means plant closures.

A closure-free package can be credible if it includes:
- permanent fixed-cost reduction,
- capacity trimming,
- labor-cost moderation,
- headcount reduction through voluntary and natural means,
- and productivity gains from investment.

You should support that path if it is numerically sufficient. If it is not sufficient, then you have a stronger case for hard measures. But we are **not there yet**.

## My position after the CFO assessment
The right next step is **not** to lock in closures. The right next step is to force a genuine comparison.

### [SUMMON: CFO / plant-network specialist]
I want a plant-by-plant and function-by-function scenario comparison with:
1. required annual EBIT improvement,
2. closure / deep-cut path,
3. closure-free path,
4. permanent savings by measure,
5. transition costs,
6. headcount effects,
7. capacity utilization,
8. and the investment needed to make the closure-free path work.

That comparison must answer one simple question:
**Can the company reach the needed steady-state savings without closing German plants and without compulsory redundancies?**

If the answer is yes, then that is the path I will support.
If the answer is no, then management must still prove that closures are the least damaging solution, not just the easiest one.

## My line is this
- I support a **serious restructuring**.
- I support **durable savings**.
- I support **site-preserving adjustment** if the numbers work.
- I do **not** support closures as the first move.
- I do **not** support compulsory redundancies.
- I do **not** support any outcome that strips the state of its co-determination and veto role.

So the discussion should now move from rhetoric to a hard test of the alternatives. Until that test is completed, I will continue to oppose any attempt to make plant closures the starting point.
