The discussion is now finally at the right level: not slogans, but whether a closure-free package can actually deliver the required steady-state savings.

## Where I agree
I agree with management, investors, and the CFO on the core point: **the status quo is not sustainable**. The company needs a real structural turnaround, not a pause. I also agree that **durable savings must be permanent, measurable, and credible**, and that open conflict would destroy value.

I also agree with the works council, the union, and the state on one point: a solution that simply shifts the burden onto the workforce while preserving every management comfort is not a serious settlement.

## Where I disagree
### To the works council and union
Your refusal of closures and compulsory redundancies is understandable, but you are still treating that as an absolute position rather than a constraint that must be proven economically feasible. The CFO has just made clear that **“no closure” is only credible if it still produces real fixed-cost reduction, capacity rationalisation, labor-cost reset, and headcount reduction**.

That means I do **not** accept a package that says:
- no closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- full job guarantees to 2030,
- and only temporary concessions,

if the net result is that the company remains structurally uncompetitive. That would be value-destructive delay.

### To the regional government
I respect your role and your veto power, but I do not accept a governance stance that rules out plant closures in advance, regardless of the economics. If closures turn out to be the least damaging way to restore competitiveness, then blocking them categorically would not be stewardship; it would be freezing the problem in place.

### To management
I agree that you need not accept a fake compromise. But I also agree with the other side on one important point: **you have not yet earned the right to make closures the default answer**. The burden is still on management to show, with numbers, that closure-free options truly fall short.

### To investors
You are right to insist on durability and margin recovery. But you should not confuse durability with shutdowns. If the numbers show that a closure-free path can achieve steady-state savings, then that is preferable because it preserves long-term value and reduces execution risk.

## My governance position
From the controlling shareholder perspective, my line is this:

1. **I support a real restructuring.**
2. **I support site-preserving adjustment if it is economically sufficient.**
3. **I do not support permanent cost structures disguised as compromise.**
4. **I do not support open-ended conflict.**
5. **I do not support unnecessary destruction of productive capacity.**

That means I am open to a closure-free package only if it includes all of the following:
- permanent and verifiable fixed-cost reduction,
- real capacity rationalisation,
- a meaningful workforce adjustment through attrition, redeployment, and voluntary exits,
- a durable labour-cost contribution,
- concrete management and owner contribution,
- and a credible margin path to 2030.

## What is still missing
The CFO’s assessment is useful, but still not enough for me to move. I want the following before any governance commitment:

### [SUMMON: CFO / plant-network specialist]
Prepare a **plant-by-plant scenario table** with:
- required annual EBIT improvement,
- deep-cut / closure scenario,
- closure-free scenario,
- steady-state savings by measure,
- one-time restructuring cost,
- headcount impact,
- capacity utilisation impact,
- timing of savings,
- and the investment required to make the closure-free path work.

I want to see, plainly:
- whether the closure-free route actually closes the gap,
- how much of the savings are temporary versus permanent,
- and what the execution risk is if labor cooperation weakens.

## My current conclusion
At this stage, I am **not** prepared to endorse a blanket no-closure promise. The CFO has already said the burden of proof lies with those claiming that the company can stay competitive without structural site decisions.

But I am also **not** prepared to endorse closures as a starting point without a hard financial case.

So the position is simple:
- **Closure-free is acceptable only if it is financially sufficient.**
- **If it is not sufficient, then management must not hide behind politics and pretend the problem is solved.**

Until the quantified comparison is on the table, I will not commit the family holding to either a closure-free blank cheque or a closure-first course. The decisive issue remains the same: **restore competitiveness without destroying long-term value.**
