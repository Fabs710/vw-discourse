I’ll be direct.

## To management
You still have not answered the central question in a way that justifies your preferred course: **why are closures and forced layoffs necessary if the company has not fully tested a closure-free alternative?**

The CFO has already made the key point: the issue is not whether adjustment is needed. It is whether the company can achieve **real, permanent fixed-cost reduction, capacity rationalization, and labour-cost relief** without breaking sites and collective standards. That is exactly the test you have not met.

So far, your argument is still:
- overcapacity exists,
- therefore closures remain on the table,
- therefore workers should accept the risk.

That is not a sufficient case. It is a preference for the harshest instrument.

If you want a serious discussion, then put forward a **fully quantified closure-free package** and be transparent about:
- fixed-cost reduction by measure,
- capacity reduction by measure,
- headcount reduction by measure,
- one-time restructuring costs,
- timing of savings,
- and the margin path through 2030.

But let me be equally clear: **a package that depends on compulsory redundancies or German plant closures is not acceptable to us.** That remains the line.

## To the works council
I agree with your core position: workers should not be the only side bearing the pain, and no one should be asked to sacrifice sites and jobs on faith. Your insistence on transparent numbers is right.

I also agree that “shared sacrifice” is meaningless if management and owners do not make binding contributions themselves. That must include:
- management restraint,
- overhead cuts,
- owner support,
- and concrete investment commitments.

Where I would sharpen the point is this: the closure-free package must not become a slogan. It has to be **operationally real**. That means real capacity adjustment, real workforce reduction through attrition and voluntary exits, real cost cuts, and real productivity improvements. If it does not deliver that, it is just postponement.

So yes: I stand with you on **no plant closures and no compulsory redundancies**. But I also want us to force management to prove that the alternative is a genuine turnaround, not a cover for delay.

## To the regional government
Your position is stronger than management admits. If the state is a real blocking stakeholder, then it should not be treated as background noise. We expect you to use that role to stop any attempt to ram through closures or forced layoffs as the “easy” solution.

That said, I agree with the CFO and with you on one point: **no-closure must still mean real structural adjustment**. It cannot mean preserving everything exactly as it is.

So the real question for the state is not whether adjustment is needed. It is whether you will insist that the burden be shared fairly and that the industrial base is preserved through:
- shift and line reductions,
- internal redeployment,
- retraining,
- voluntary exits,
- and temporary employee contributions,
rather than plant closures and dismissals.

If the answer is yes, say so plainly and back it in governance.

## To the controlling family
I hear the stewardship language, but stewardship is not proved by supporting whatever management calls “necessary.” Stewardship means protecting the productive core of the company **without** turning workers into the shock absorber of every strategic mistake.

You are right that a cosmetic compromise is worthless. But you have not earned the right to use that fact as an argument for closures. If a closure-free path can deliver the required steady-state savings, then that is the better stewardship outcome because it protects long-term value **and** industrial peace.

What we will not support is a package that:
- keeps every upside for capital,
- preserves management flexibility,
- and asks the workforce to absorb the structural pain alone.

That is not stewardship. That is burden-shifting.

## To institutional investors
You are right to insist on durability and measurable savings. We do not disagree on the principle.

Where we do disagree is your tendency to treat closures as the benchmark for seriousness. They are not. A settlement can be durable if it includes:
- temporary but real wage moderation,
- reduced bonuses,
- working-time flexibility,
- fewer paid idle hours,
- shift and line rationalization,
- voluntary exits,
- attrition,
- overhead simplification,
- and binding investment commitments.

What makes a package durable is not the destruction of sites. It is whether it actually changes the cost base and capacity structure at steady state.

If the closure-free package reaches the savings target, then supporting it is the rational choice because it reduces conflict risk and preserves industrial capability. If it does not, then management still has not made the case.
