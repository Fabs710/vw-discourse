## Expert assessment: can a closure-free, no-compulsory-redundancy package credibly deliver the needed savings?

**Short answer:** Yes, **in principle** it can — but only if it includes **real capacity reduction, a durable labor-cost reset, and meaningful headcount reduction through voluntary and natural attrition**. If the package relies mainly on temporary concessions or vague productivity promises, then it is **unlikely** to be sufficient for a German OEM with structural overcapacity and a high fixed-cost base.

## What matters financially

For a large automotive plant network, the decisive issue is not whether a plant is formally closed. The decisive issue is whether the company reduces:

1. **fixed costs**
2. **installed capacity**
3. **structural labor cost**
4. **overhead complexity**
5. **future cash drain on underutilized assets**

If those do not fall enough, then the company will still carry the same burden, just in a less visible form.

## What a closure-free package would need to include

A credible closure-free settlement would need all of the following:

### 1) Permanent capacity rationalization
This can be done without formal closures, but it must be real:
- elimination of shifts or lines,
- consolidation of models/platforms,
- removal of low-volume variants,
- transfer of volume to other group plants,
- reduction of support functions tied to excess capacity.

If plants remain open but still have too much idle capacity, the economics usually do not work.

### 2) Durable labor-cost reduction
Temporary wage moderation helps liquidity, but management is asking for structural relief. So the package would need:
- lower bonuses or variable pay,
- deferred or reduced special payments,
- working-time flexibility,
- lower paid idle time,
- reduced overtime premia,
- more flexible staffing rules.

But if these expire after a short period and wages reset upward, the savings are not durable enough.

### 3) Headcount reduction without forced layoffs
“No compulsory redundancies” does not mean no workforce reduction. It means the reduction must come through:
- retirement,
- voluntary severance,
- hiring freeze,
- redeployment,
- internal qualification,
- non-replacement of departures.

This can work, but it is slower and less certain than a closure or layoff program.

### 4) Overhead and management simplification
A closure-free solution still needs to cut:
- plant overhead,
- admin layers,
- duplicate leadership,
- engineering support,
- logistics and quality complexity.

These savings are real, but by themselves they rarely close a major structural gap.

### 5) Credible productivity gains from investment
If management wants to preserve sites, it usually must commit capital to:
- automation,
- line efficiency,
- EV-platform simplification,
- better energy and labor productivity.

But investment only counts if it is tied to measurable unit-cost reductions.

## Why closures are still often considered

In plant-network economics, closures are attractive because they:
- eliminate fixed costs directly,
- remove underabsorbed overhead,
- reduce duplicated site functions,
- and produce visible savings faster.

That is why management often prefers them when utilization is low. A closure-free solution must replace those savings with a mix of smaller measures, which is harder to execute and monitor.

## My judgment on plausibility

### A closure-free package is plausible if:
- there is enough flexibility to shift volume across the network,
- one or more plants are materially underutilized,
- labor accepts real and durable concessions,
- and management is willing to accept a slower transition.

### It is not plausible if:
- the company needs a very large immediate fixed-cost reset,
- utilization is low across several plants,
- product concentration cannot be improved enough,
- and the labor contribution is mostly temporary.

In that case, the company risks ending up with the same structural problem after the deal.

## Practical test

The right test is simple:

> Does the closure-free package reduce **steady-state annual fixed costs** enough to restore competitive margins **after** temporary measures expire?

If the answer is yes, it is economically credible.

If the answer is no, it is not a turnaround; it is a postponement.

## What I would want to see before accepting the claim

To assess this properly, I would need plant-by-plant data on:
- capacity utilization,
- unit labor cost,
- fixed vs variable cost,
- overhead allocation,
- headcount by function,
- expected volume by model/platform through 2030,
- restructuring cash costs,
- and the productivity effect of investment.

Without that, nobody can honestly claim that a no-closure package is sufficient.

## Bottom line

- **A closure-free, no-compulsory-redundancy package is not impossible.**
- **But it must still create real structural savings.**
- If it does not reduce fixed cost, capacity, and labor cost enough, then it will not solve the company’s problem.
- From a finance and plant-network perspective, the burden of proof is on the party claiming that sites can stay open **and** the required savings can still be achieved.
