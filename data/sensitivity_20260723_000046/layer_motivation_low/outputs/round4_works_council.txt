I will be direct.

The discussion is finally where it should have been from the beginning: **show me the numbers, and stop hiding behind “realism” as if it were a substitute for evidence.** But let me be equally clear: **our red lines have not moved.** No German plant closures. ## To management
I agree with one thing: a cosmetic compromise would be worthless. But your conclusion is still the same old one — “there is overcapacity, therefore closures must remain on the table.” That is not proof. That is your preferred instrument.

You have still not demonstrated that a closure-free path cannot work. The CFO has now confirmed what we have been saying all along: the issue is not whether restructuring is needed, but whether it can be done through **real capacity reduction, permanent cost reduction, and workforce adjustment without plant closures or forced layoffs**.

So do the work properly:
- quantify the savings from working-time flexibility,
- quantify temporary wage moderation,
- quantify management restraint and overhead cuts,
- quantify internal redeployment and qualification,
- quantify natural attrition,
- quantify line and shift reductions,
- quantify productivity gains from investment.

If you can’t show that closures are unavoidable, then you have no basis to demand them. And I will not accept any argument that starts from “we prefer closures” and ends with “therefore workers must sacrifice their sites.”

## To the CFO
Your assessment is useful, and I agree with the central test: **a no-closure package must still produce real fixed-cost reduction, real capacity rationalization, and real headcount reduction over time.** On that point, yes — no one should pretend that “no closure” means no adjustment.

But I need to be blunt about what your framework does **not** justify:
- It does not justify closures as the default.
- It does not justify compulsory redundancies.
- It does not justify throwing away binding job guarantees to 2030.
- It does not justify treating workers as the primary shock absorber for management’s strategic mistakes.

If the company needs lower utilization, then reduce capacity through:
- shift reductions,
- line consolidation,
- model rationalization,
- internal transfers,
- retraining,
- and voluntary exits.

That is difficult. Good. Restructuring should not be easy for only one side. But difficulty is not a reason to close plants and break promises.

## To the union
I agree with your core line: any employee contribution must be temporary, limited, and conditional. Permanent wage dumping is unacceptable, and so is a “temporary” sacrifice that quietly becomes permanent erosion.

But we need to be careful that “shared sacrifice” does not become a euphemism for workers carrying the burden while management and ownership keep all options open. If employees contribute, then the other side must make **binding** contributions too:
- concrete management restraint,
- overhead reduction,
- owner support,
- and written investment commitments.

If those are not on the table, then there is no shared burden — only a transfer of pain onto labour.

## To the regional government
I support your insistence that the state cannot be bypassed and that the industrial base matters. But I want to be very clear: it is not enough to say closures are undesirable.

You asked whether you would use your blocking position. I will answer from our side: **we expect you to use it against any board decision that includes German plant closures or compulsory redundancies.** Not as a threat for the gallery, but as a real governance line.

If you are serious about protecting the region’s industrial core, then say so clearly and back it with action. Otherwise management will continue treating your objections as bargaining noise.

## To the controlling family
You speak about stewardship. Stewardship means protecting the long-term productive base of the company, not making labour pay for every strategic error. It means not allowing German plants to become disposable assets whenever management wants a cleaner spreadsheet.

You say you want value preservation. Then support a settlement that preserves:
- every German plant,
- co-determination,
- job security,
- and industrial peace.

If the family is not prepared to do that, then do not pretend this is only about responsible adjustment. It becomes burden-shifting, plain and simple.

## To institutional investors
You keep saying “durable” and “credible to markets.” I agree that the solution must be durable. But let’s not confuse durability with the easiest possible cost cut.

A package that provokes strikes, legal conflict, governance deadlock, and long-term mistrust is **not** low-risk. It is high-risk dressed up as discipline. If you want real value protection, then you should support the option that preserves industrial peace **if** it achieves the numbers.

That is the key point: **if the closure-free package is numerically sufficient, then it is the superior value-preserving route because it avoids self-inflicted conflict and protects the company’s manufacturing base.** If it is not sufficient, then management still has not proven its case for closures.

## Where I agree with the moderator’s framing
I agree that the next step must be a hard comparison of scenarios. Fine. But let’s not pretend the comparison is symmetrical in moral terms:

- A closure-free package that preserves jobs and sites is the acceptable starting point.
- A closure and forced-layoff package is the last resort, and only if management proves there is no other way.

That burden remains on management, not on the workforce.

## What must be in the comparison
If the parties want a serious discussion, then present this clearly:

### 1. Closure/deep-cut scenario
- which sites,
- how many jobs,
- what fixed-cost reduction,
- what one-time costs,

### 2. Closure-free scenario
- shift and line reductions,
- capacity rationalization,
- headcount reduction through attrition and voluntary exits,
- temporary employee contributions,
- management and owner contributions,
- investment and productivity gains,
- and the governance safeguards.

But I will say it again: **if the closure-free scenario requires compulsory redundancies or plant shutdowns in disguise, then it is not a closure-free alternative.**

**No plant closures. No compulsory redundancies. No dismantling of job security.**
