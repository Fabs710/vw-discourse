I’ll be equally direct.

## My overall position
The state remains **open to a serious restructuring**, but **not** to a package that starts from plant closures and compulsory redundancies as the default answer. That remains the wrong starting point for a company that is a regional industrial anchor and a major employer in Germany.

The CFO has helpfully clarified the real test: **can a closure-free package deliver permanent, measurable fixed-cost reduction, capacity rationalization, and labor-cost relief at steady state?** That is the right question. But I do **not** accept management’s leap from “overcapacity exists” to “therefore closures are needed.” That burden is still not met.

## To management
I agree with you on one point: a cosmetic compromise would be useless.

I disagree with you on the conclusion that closures and compulsory redundancies must stay on the table in order to force seriousness. They may be a theoretical option, but they are not a policy I will endorse absent proof that all less destructive routes fail.

What you still need to show, in numbers, is:
- how much savings come from working-time flexibility,
- how much from temporary wage moderation and bonus restraint,
- how much from management restraint and overhead cuts,
- how much from internal redeployment and qualification,
- how much from attrition and reduced hiring,
- how much from capacity rationalization **without** closures,
- and what the steady-state EBIT path looks like under that package.

If you cannot show that closure-free measures are insufficient, then closures are not justified — they are merely preferred.

## To the works council
I support your insistence that the workforce cannot be asked to carry the burden alone. That is legitimate, and the state will continue to defend that principle.

But I also need to be candid: saying “no closure” is not enough by itself. The CFO is right that a credible alternative must still achieve real structural adjustment:
- capacity reduction,
- cost reduction,
- and headcount reduction over time.

That can happen through shift cuts, line consolidation, voluntary exits, natural attrition, redeployment, and retraining. It does **not** have to mean shutting plants.

So yes, I support site preservation. But I will not support a frozen status quo disguised as protection. If you want my backing, bring a closure-free package that is operationally real.

## To the union
I agree that permanent wage dumping is unacceptable. I also agree that any employee contribution must be temporary, conditional, and matched by binding contributions from management and owners.

What I do **not** accept is a deal where labor is asked to give and the other side keeps all structural options open. If workers contribute, then management, the family holding, and the company must contribute in concrete form:
- management restraint,
- overhead simplification,
- owner support,
- and binding investment commitments for German operations.

A social partnership solution is possible. But only if it is genuinely shared.

## To the controlling family
I hear the stewardship language, and I take it seriously. Stewardship does mean protecting long-term value, not preserving every existing structure regardless of economics.

But stewardship also means not turning the German industrial base into collateral damage for a faster accounting fix. I agree that the company needs competitiveness. I do **not** agree that closures are the first or only responsible instrument.

If the numbers prove that a closure-free path works, then that is the better stewardship outcome: it preserves value, reduces conflict risk, and protects the regional footprint. If the numbers do **not** prove that, then we will have to talk harder about the least damaging fallback. But that proof has not yet been provided.

## To institutional investors
You are right to insist on durability, measurability, and a path back to margins. I do not dispute that.

What I dispute is the implied assumption that durability automatically means closures. It does not. A closure-free package can be credible if it delivers:
- permanent fixed-cost reduction,
- real capacity rationalization,
- meaningful workforce adjustment through voluntary means,
- durable labor-cost relief,
- and investment-linked productivity gains.

If that package reaches the steady-state target, then it is the better option because it reduces execution risk and preserves industrial capacity. If it does not, then the question of harder measures reopens. But again: the case has not yet been made.

## My line on governance
I want this stated clearly: **the state will not be sidelined**. We have a blocking-minority role under the special statute, and we will use it if necessary to prevent decisions that gut regional employment or bypass our co-determination role.

That is not obstruction for its own sake. It is part of the governance structure, and it reflects the public interest in preserving the company as a regional economic anchor.

## What would move us
The conditions that could move the state are still the same:
1. a restructuring that preserves sites and jobs to the greatest extent possible,
2. a negotiated social-partnership solution,
3. measurable, durable savings,
4. and binding commitments to future investment and regional industrial capacity.
