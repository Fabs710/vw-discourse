[Works Council (Betriebsrat) (Plant-level employee representation under co-determination) — Round 1]
We are entering this discussion with a very clear mandate from the workforce: **no plant closures in Germany, no compulsory redundancies, and no unilateral dismantling of the historic job-security framework**.

Management has chosen confrontation by putting closures and large-scale job cuts on the table. That is unacceptable. We will not accept a restructuring that shifts the full burden onto employees while destroying sites, livelihoods, and regional industrial value.

Our priorities are straightforward:

1. **Keep every German plant open.**
2. **Rule out forced layoffs.**
3. **Secure binding job and site guarantees through 2030.**
4. **Preserve co-determination and the workforce’s real say in any restructuring.**
5. **Use alternative measures instead of closure**: capacity adjustments, working-time solutions, internal redeployment, training, natural attrition, and a fair negotiated contribution from management and owners.

We are prepared to discuss a serious turnaround plan, but only on the basis that it is **socially responsible and reversible**. If the company needs cost relief, then management must first show that it is taking its share of the burden and that investment, product commitments, and future production in Germany are secured.

Let me be equally clear: if this discussion is being used to present a fait accompli — plant closures, mass redundancies, or the breaking of past guarantees — then we will resist it with all the means at our disposal, including mobilising the workforce and escalating industrial action.

So our starting point is not compromise on closures. Our starting point is **protecting jobs, protecting sites, and protecting the future of German manufacturing in this company**.

[Expert: CFO / plant-network planning specialist — Round 3]
Here is the CFO / plant-network assessment, focused on the question management posed:

## Bottom line

A **closure-free, no-compulsory-redundancy** package can be economically credible **only if** it delivers:

1. **permanent fixed-cost reduction**, not just deferred pain,
2. **capacity reduction in practice**, even if not via plant closure,
3. **a material labor-cost reset**,
4. **headcount reduction through attrition, voluntary exits, redeployment, and reduced hiring**, and
5. **enough margin uplift to fund EV/technology investment**.

If those elements are not large enough, then a closure-free package is usually **not sufficient** for a German OEM with a structurally high cost base and underutilized plants. In that case, keeping all sites open simply preserves overhead and depreciation absorption problems.

## What I would want to see in the numbers

Management asked for a quantified side-by-side. The right comparison is not “closure vs no closure” in the abstract; it is:

- **required annual EBIT improvement**
- versus
- **achievable run-rate savings**
- under each scenario.

The key cost buckets are:

### 1) Labor cost
For a large German carmaker, direct and indirect labor is usually one of the biggest controllable fixed costs.

A credible closure-free package could include:
- temporary wage moderation,
- reduced bonuses,
- lower or deferred special payments,
- working-time corridor changes,
- fewer paid idle hours,
- more flexible shift systems,
- lower external labor use,
- and slower progression of wage growth.

**But** if these are only temporary, the savings often fade in 2–3 years. Management is asking for **durable** relief, so temporary measures alone are not enough.

### 2) Headcount
No-compulsory-redundancy does **not** mean no headcount reduction. It means the reduction must come through:
- retirement attrition,
- voluntary severance,
- internal transfer,
- unfilled vacancies,
- and reduced hiring.

That can work, but it is slower and less certain.

Rule of thumb: if the company needs a meaningful structural reset, a closure-free path usually still requires a **large net workforce reduction** over time. If you cannot reduce the labor base enough, labor-cost concessions just mask the problem.

### 3) Capacity / overhead absorption
This is the most important issue.

If plants remain open but output is shifted around without enough throughput, you keep:
- the same building and utility costs,
- much of the same logistics cost,
- duplicated planning and support functions,
- and weak fixed-cost absorption.

A closure-free alternative can only work if it includes one or more of:
- line consolidation,
- platform harmonization,
- elimination of shifts or lines,
- removal of product variants,
- export of some volume to lower-cost plants in the group,
- and substantial simplification of plant support structures.

If not, “no closure” just means **spreading the same fixed cost over too few units**.

### 4) Non-labor overhead
There is usually meaningful savings in:
- engineering overhead,
- admin layers,
- duplicated plant leadership,
- procurement,
- quality systems,
- and discretionary spend.

But overhead savings alone rarely close a structural competitiveness gap for a full vehicle business.

## Short answer
A **closure-free, no-compulsory-redundancy package can sometimes deliver meaningful savings**, but it is **unlikely to match the full economic effect of a plant-closure package** unless it includes:

- substantial and permanent capacity reduction,
- very large voluntary headcount reduction,
- deep simplification of shift systems and indirect labor,
- durable labor-cost relief,
- and a clear follow-through on product and utilization alignment.

If the company’s German cost base is materially above target, then a closure-free path usually struggles with three structural limits:

1. **Fixed-cost absorption does not improve enough** if plant footprint stays too large.  
2. **Indirect labor and overhead are sticky**; voluntary exits remove direct labor faster than support cost.  
3. **Capacity mismatch remains** if sites, lines, and shifts are preserved beyond demand.

So economically, the key issue is not “closure vs no closure” in the abstract. It is whether the no-closure package can remove enough **permanent fixed cost and capacity** to raise steady-state EBIT to target.

---

## What needs to be compared

A proper comparison should separate three buckets:

### 1. Structural savings
These are durable and matter most:
- headcount permanently removed
- shift-line reductions
- plant-level overhead cuts
- management layers removed
- procurement and logistics simplification
- lower depreciation burden if assets are idled or written down

### 2. Temporary savings
These help cash flow but do not solve the structural issue:
- temporary wage moderation
- bonus restraint
- deferred compensation
- temporary working-time changes
- short-term hiring freeze

### 3. One-time costs
These often decide feasibility:
- severance and early-retirement costs
- dismantling and restart costs
- transfer and retraining costs
- asset write-downs
- relocation and logistics disruption
- strike and execution risk

---

## Economic logic of the two scenarios

### A. Deep-cut / closure scenario
**Strengths**
- Highest probability of removing fixed cost permanently
- Faster capacity reset
- Stronger signal to capital markets
- Better chance of aligning supply, utilization, and demand

**Weaknesses**
- High one-time cash and restructuring cost
- Labor conflict risk
- Governance difficulty
- Possible supply-chain disruption
- Loss of optionality if market recovers

**Typical economic profile**
- Larger long-term EBIT improvement
- Higher near-term cash burn
- Lower execution risk only if governance is clean; otherwise, high friction can erase part of the benefit

### B. Closure-free scenario
**Strengths**
- Lower political and labor conflict
- Lower immediate disruption
- Better chance of preserving social peace
- Can sometimes deliver most of the benefit if overcapacity is concentrated in shifts, lines, and indirect cost rather than entire sites

**Weaknesses**
- Usually weaker permanent savings
- Harder to remove overhead
- Risk of partial measures that fade out after temporary concessions expire
- Capacity can remain structurally too high
- May require repeated renegotiation

**Typical economic profile**
- Lower one-time cost
- Lower execution conflict
- Often insufficient unless accompanied by real capacity contraction and major labor-cost reset

---

## What would make a closure-free package credible

To be financially credible, a no-closure package would need to show, at minimum:

1. **Permanent headcount reduction**
   - mainly via voluntary exits and attrition
   - not just hiring freezes

2. **Real capacity reduction**
   - fewer shifts
   - line consolidation
   - model rationalization
   - elimination of underutilized production patterns

3. **Indirect cost reset**
   - overhead simplification
   - plant support functions reduced
   - admin and engineering duplication removed

4. **Labour-cost contribution with durability**
   - not just a one-year bridge
   - enough duration to carry the company through the investment cycle

5. **Investment tied to productivity**
   - automation, digitalization, and line modernization
   - otherwise the company preserves an inefficient footprint

If those elements are missing, the package may look cooperative but still fail the EBIT test.
