We agree with the broad diagnosis: **the status quo is not acceptable**, and the company needs a **real, durable restructuring**. We also agree that **open conflict, strikes, and governance deadlock destroy value**. From a capital-markets perspective, that part is settled.

Where we differ is on the **economic sufficiency** of the employee side’s preferred route.

## Our position
We will support **whatever credibly restores competitive margins and reduces the cost base on a durable basis**.

That means:
- **yes** to measurable, permanent cost reduction,
- **yes** to capacity rationalization,
- **yes** to real headcount reduction over time,
- **yes** to management, owner, and workforce contributions,
- **no** to a cosmetic settlement,
- **no** to a deal that preserves structurally uncompetitive costs,
- **no** to a compromise that looks peaceful but leaves margins impaired.

## Where we agree with the employee side
We agree with the works council, union, and state on one important point:  
**temporary concessions alone are not enough**.

If the package is only:
- short-term wage restraint,
- bonuses deferred,
- and promises of later productivity,

then that is not credible for capital markets. We need **steady-state savings**, not a pause.

We also agree that a closure-free package is **not ruled out in principle**. If it truly achieves the same economic result with lower execution risk, that is valuable.

## Where we disagree

### To the works council and union
Your red lines are understandable, but they are not enough to protect shareholder value unless they also deliver the required economics.

You say:
- no plant closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- binding job guarantees to 2030.

That is acceptable **only if** the package still produces:
- permanent fixed-cost reduction,
- real capacity reduction,
- material labor-cost reset,
- and a meaningful headcount reduction over time.

If it does not, then you are asking investors to finance a structurally weak business indefinitely.

We do **not** accept a settlement that simply preserves every site and freezes the cost structure.

### To management
You are right that a cosmetic compromise is worthless. But you have not yet fully proven that closures are unavoidable.

Your case still needs to be made with numbers:
- What is the **steady-state EBIT uplift** under the closure-free route?
- How much of the labor-cost relief is **permanent**, and how much expires?
- What is the **one-time restructuring cost** versus the ongoing savings?
- How much **capacity** and **headcount** actually come out?
- What is the **execution risk** if labour cooperation weakens?

Until that comparison is fully laid out, we are not prepared to endorse closures as the default answer. But equally, we will not endorse a closure-free package unless it closes the gap in a financially credible way.

### To the regional government
We respect your role and the public interest in the industrial base. But a veto is not a strategy.

If the no-closure route is financially sufficient, fine — support it.  
If it is not, then blocking necessary restructuring would simply postpone the pain and increase value destruction.

So our question is simple:  
**Will you support the economically sufficient solution, even if it is politically uncomfortable?**

### To the controlling family
We agree that stewardship is not about sacrificing productive capacity for a quick accounting fix. But stewardship also does not mean preserving every existing structure.

The right stewardship test is:
- does the package restore competitiveness,
- protect investment capacity,
- and reduce the risk of future underperformance?

If a closure-free package does that, support it.  
If not, then the company must not be forced into a compromise that leaves the underlying problem intact.

## What would move us
We would back a closure-free deal if it meets all of these conditions:
1. **permanent** fixed-cost reduction,
2. **material** capacity rationalization,
3. **real** headcount reduction,
4. a **durable** labor-cost reset,
5. clear **investment commitments** tied to productivity gains,
6. and a credible path back to target margins.

If those conditions are not met, then we will not support a settlement just because it avoids conflict.
