We agree with the central diagnosis: **the status quo is not acceptable**, and a real restructuring is necessary. We also agree that **open conflict, prolonged strikes, and governance deadlock destroy value**. On that level, the discussion has been useful.

But our support is conditional on one thing only: **a credible, quantified, durable path back to competitive margins**. Not a symbolic compromise. Not a delay tactic. Not a deal that preserves structurally uncompetitive costs and calls that stability.

## Where we agree
- The company needs **durable fixed-cost reduction**.
- Any package must be **measurable and enforceable**.
- Temporary pain without structural effect is **not enough**.
- Employee, management, owner, and state contributions should all be **on the table**.
- A closure-free route is acceptable **if and only if** it reaches the same economic result.

## Where we disagree

### To the works council and union
We understand your red lines, but we do **not** accept them as sufficient on their own.

- **No plant closures** is not a financially persuasive position unless you can show equivalent permanent savings.
- **No compulsory redundancies** is acceptable only if headcount still comes down materially through voluntary exits, attrition, redeployment, and hiring restraint.
- **Binding job guarantees to 2030** are not credible if they freeze an uncompetitive cost base and block structural adjustment.
- Temporary wage moderation, bonus cuts, and working-time measures are useful only if they are **large enough and durable enough** to matter.

Right now, you are asking investors to trust that a closure-free package can work. We are open to that — but trust is not enough. We need numbers.

### To management
You are right that a cosmetic restructuring is worthless. But you have not yet fully earned the right to insist that closures are necessary.

Your argument is still incomplete unless you show:
- a clear **side-by-side comparison** of closure/deep-cut versus closure-free options,
- the **steady-state savings** from each,
- the **transition costs**,
- the **execution risk**,
- and the **timing of margin recovery**.

If the closure-free package cannot close the gap, say so plainly. If it can, then it is the better value-preserving route because it may reduce conflict risk and preserve productive capacity. We will not back a closure-first approach without proof that it is genuinely necessary.

### To the regional government
Your role as a blocking stakeholder is real, but that does not solve the economics. A political veto is not a substitute for a viable operating model.

We support your insistence that the burden must not fall on workers alone. But we do **not** support a governance posture that rules out closures categorically before the financial case is tested. If the numbers show that closures are the least damaging path to restore competitiveness, then blocking them for political reasons would destroy value.

### To the controlling family
You are right that ownership must not underwrite permanent underperformance. But stewardship also means not forcing the company into unnecessary conflict or avoidable value destruction.

We need ownership to back a package that:
- restores margins,
- protects investment capacity,
- and avoids ideological rigidity on either side.

If a closure-free path can meet the economic test, support it. If not, don’t force the company to carry a disguised compromise.

## What would move us
We would support a settlement if it delivers all of the following:

1. **Quantified permanent cost reduction**
2. **Material capacity rationalization**
3. **Real headcount reduction**
4. **A durable labour-cost reset**
5. **Clear investment and product commitments**
6. **Deal certainty and low execution risk**

That could be through closures, but it could also be through a strong non-closure package. What matters is the economics, not the label.

## What would not move us
- A cosmetic truce
- Temporary concessions with no structural effect
- Full job guarantees that freeze the cost base
- A prolonged conflict that erodes cash flow and management focus
- Any outcome that leaves margins structurally impaired

## Our position
So we are **open** to a closure-free, no-compulsory-redundancy settlement — but only if it produces the same durable economic outcome as a deeper restructuring.

If the parties want capital markets on side, they must now do what the CFO asked for:
- quantify the savings,
- separate temporary from permanent effects,
- show headcount impact,
- show capacity impact,
- and show the margin path to 2030.

Until then, we are not endorsing any side’s preferred narrative.
