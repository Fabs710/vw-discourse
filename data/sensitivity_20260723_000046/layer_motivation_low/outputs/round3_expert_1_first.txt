Here is the CFO / plant-network assessment, focused on the question management posed:

## Bottom line

A **closure-free, no-compulsory-redundancy** package can be economically credible **only if** it delivers:

1. **permanent fixed-cost reduction**, not just deferred pain,
2. **capacity reduction in practice**, even if not via plant closure,
3. **a material labor-cost reset**,
4. **headcount reduction through attrition, voluntary exits, redeployment, and reduced hiring**, and
5. **enough margin uplift to fund EV/technology investment**.

If those elements are not large enough, then a closure-free package is usually **not sufficient** for a German OEM with a structurally high cost base and underutilized plants. In that case, keeping all sites open simply preserves overhead and depreciation absorption problems.

## What I would want to see in the numbers

Management asked for a quantified side-by-side. The right comparison is not “closure vs no closure” in the abstract; it is:

- **required annual EBIT improvement**
- versus
- **achievable run-rate savings**
- under each scenario.

The key cost buckets are:

### 1) Labor cost
For a large German carmaker, direct and indirect labor is usually one of the biggest controllable fixed costs.

A credible closure-free package could include:
- temporary wage moderation,
- reduced bonuses,
- lower or deferred special payments,
- working-time corridor changes,
- fewer paid idle hours,
- more flexible shift systems,
- lower external labor use,
- and slower progression of wage growth.

**But** if these are only temporary, the savings often fade in 2–3 years. Management is asking for **durable** relief, so temporary measures alone are not enough.

### 2) Headcount
No-compulsory-redundancy does **not** mean no headcount reduction. It means the reduction must come through:
- retirement attrition,
- voluntary severance,
- internal transfer,
- unfilled vacancies,
- and reduced hiring.

That can work, but it is slower and less certain.

Rule of thumb: if the company needs a meaningful structural reset, a closure-free path usually still requires a **large net workforce reduction** over time. If you cannot reduce the labor base enough, labor-cost concessions just mask the problem.

### 3) Capacity / overhead absorption
This is the most important issue.

If plants remain open but output is shifted around without enough throughput, you keep:
- the same building and utility costs,
- much of the same logistics cost,
- duplicated planning and support functions,
- and weak fixed-cost absorption.

A closure-free alternative can only work if it includes one or more of:
- line consolidation,
- platform harmonization,
- elimination of shifts or lines,
- removal of product variants,
- export of some volume to lower-cost plants in the group,
- and substantial simplification of plant support structures.

If not, “no closure” just means **spreading the same fixed cost over too few units**.

### 4) Non-labor overhead
There is usually meaningful savings in:
- engineering overhead,
- admin layers,
- duplicated plant leadership,
- procurement,
- quality systems,
- and discretionary spend.

But overhead savings alone rarely close a structural competitiveness gap for a full vehicle business.

## What a realistic closure-free package might look like

If I were building this as a finance case, I would expect something along these lines:

- **workforce reduction:** material, but phased over several years via attrition/voluntary measures
- **labor cost adjustment:** partial and time-limited, but with some structural elements
- **capacity reduction:** via shift elimination, product rationalization, and line consolidation
- **investment commitment:** only if it raises productivity and lowers unit labor content
- **governance:** strict monitoring and reopeners if savings underperform

In practical terms, a closure-free plan has to show:
- savings per year,
- savings at steady state,
- the transition cost to get there,
- and the break-even point versus closure.

## My judgment on economic plausibility

### Closure-free is plausible if:
- one or more plants are operating materially below efficient scale,
- there is enough product and platform flexibility to reallocate volume,
- labor can agree to a real cost reset,
- and management accepts a slower but still meaningful structural adjustment.

### Closure-free is not plausible if:
- the company has multiple plants with chronic underutilization,
- product assignments cannot be consolidated enough,
- German indirect costs remain high,
- and labor concessions are mainly temporary or symbolic.

In that case, the company will likely still carry too much fixed cost after the deal, and the savings will not be durable enough.

## What I would flag to management

Management should not use “closure-free” as a political label. It must be a **financially sufficient package**. The test is simple:

> Does the proposal reduce annual fixed costs enough to restore competitive returns **after** the temporary measures expire?

If the answer is no, then the package is not a turnaround; it is a postponement.

## What I would ask for next

To assess this properly, I would need:

1. current and target **unit labor cost** by site,
2. plant-level **capacity utilization**,
3. fixed vs variable cost breakdown,
4. expected **volume by platform and model** through 2030,
5. headcount by function and site,
6. overhead allocation by plant,
7. one-time restructuring charges,
8. cash impact by year,
9. and the investment required to achieve productivity gains.

Without that, no one can honestly say whether a no-closure package is sufficient.

## Final answer

My professional view is:

- **A closure-free package is not impossible.**
- **But it is only credible if it creates real, permanent fixed-cost reduction and capacity rationalization.**
- If it relies mainly on temporary wage restraint and promises of future improvement, it is **not enough**.
- If the company needs a deep structural reset and the closure-free plan cannot deliver the required steady-state savings, then management will still be facing the same economic problem after the deal.
