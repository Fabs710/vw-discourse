I’ll be equally direct.

We agree on the diagnosis: this company needs a durable restructuring, real competitiveness, and investment in future products. We also agree that a purely cosmetic deal would be worthless. On that, the works council, the state, and even the capital side are not wrong.

But I do **not** accept the leap from “the company needs change” to “therefore closures and compulsory redundancies must be on the table as normal tools.” That is exactly where the conflict begins.

## Where I agree
- **The cost problem is real.**
- **The company needs structural savings, not a short-lived patch.**
- **Investment in future products and technologies is essential.**
- **Burden-sharing is necessary.**

## Where I disagree
### 1) Plant closures are not the default answer
A closure is not just an efficiency measure. It is a permanent destruction of industrial capacity, skills, and trust. If alternatives can deliver the needed effect, then closures are not justified.

### 2) Forced redundancies are not acceptable
We will not sign off on a restructuring that makes employees pay with their livelihoods while management and owners limit themselves to demands. Natural attrition, redeployment, retirement pathways, and voluntary instruments are the only acceptable staffing routes.

### 3) The collective wage framework is not up for dismantling
Temporary and negotiated labor-cost contributions are discussable. A permanent erosion of collective standards under the label of restructuring is not.

## What a viable settlement would have to look like
If management wants our cooperation, the package must be concrete and balanced:

- **No plant closures**
- **No compulsory redundancies**
- **A binding employment framework**
- **Capacity reductions through non-destructive measures**
- **A negotiated employee contribution**  
  - e.g. moderation of variable pay elements, temporary sacrifice of bonuses, working-time flexibility
- **Management contribution**  
  - restraint at the top, no one-sided burden shifting
- **Owner contribution**  
  - credible commitment to long-term investment instead of pure pressure for short-term cuts
- **Enforceable investment and product commitments**
- **Real co-determination in the restructuring plan**

## On “equivalent savings”
Management keeps saying the alternative must be “economically equivalent.” Fine — then prove it.

But “equivalent” does not mean “we get to choose closures anyway if we don’t like the alternative.” It means the company must actually calculate and negotiate a package that achieves the needed savings **without** breaking the social contract.

That can include:
- working-time adjustments,
- productivity improvements agreed with the workforce,
- voluntary departure instruments,
- internal redeployment,
- targeted labor-cost contributions,
- capacity reductions that do not mean site destruction.

What it cannot include is a disguised ultimatum: “accept closures, or accept the same pain without guarantees.”

## To the works council
We are aligned on the red lines: no closures, no forced layoffs, no dismantling of co-determination. I support that completely.

Where I would sharpen the position is this: if there is any room for a durable settlement, it will come from a **shared-sacrifice package with binding guarantees**, not from endless repetition of management’s closure threat. We should keep the workforce mobilized, but we should also keep the door open to a serious alternative if management actually puts one on the table.

## To management
If your plan requires breaking the collective framework and threatening plant closures to work, then your plan is socially reckless and strategically weak. A company does not become competitive by provoking industrial conflict and destroying trust.

If you want a deal, bring:
- measurable savings,
- credible investment,
- burden-sharing above the shop floor,
- and a restructuring concept that protects jobs and sites.

Until then, we have nothing to negotiate except your ultimatums.

## To the state government
Your resistance to rash closures is justified. But your support has to mean more than “please negotiate harder.” If you mean social responsibility, then back a solution that keeps sites open and avoids forced redundancies. The region cannot afford a restructuring that exports the pain to workers and local communities.

## To the family holding and investors
You are right that delay and cosmetic compromise are value-destructive. But so is a one-sided cut that triggers conflict, weakens skills, and damages the workforce for years.

Long-term value depends on:
- stable labor relations,
- skilled retention,
- industrial capability in Germany,
- and a restructuring that people can actually stand behind.

## My minimum acceptable outcome
- No plant closures
- No compulsory redundancies
- Collective wage framework preserved
- Binding employment and site security
- Shared, temporary, negotiated contributions from labor
- Clear investment commitments

## My maximum concession
- Temporary moderation of pay elements
- Working-time flexibility
- Voluntary cost contributions
- Capacity reductions through redeployment and attrition
- Structured productivity measures

## Single most important condition
There must be **no forced layoffs and no closures**. If that condition is breached, there is no settlement from our side.

If management refuses that reality, then it should expect industrial resistance, not consent.
