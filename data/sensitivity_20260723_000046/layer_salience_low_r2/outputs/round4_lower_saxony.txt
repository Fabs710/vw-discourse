I will be equally direct.

## Where I agree

I agree with management, the family holding, investors, and the expert on one central point: **this company needs a real restructuring, not a cosmetic one**. The cost and capacity problem is real, and it cannot be wished away.

I also agree that:
- savings must be **durable**,
- the package must be **measurable and enforceable**,
- and the company needs room to invest in future products and technology.

And I accept the expert’s core industrial point: **labor concessions alone are not enough** if there is genuine overcapacity. A serious turnaround must include real capacity reduction and fixed-cost improvement.

## Where I disagree

I do **not** accept the leap from “real restructuring” to “therefore plant closures and compulsory redundancies must remain normal options.”

That is where I draw the line.

A closure is not just another lever. It is a permanent loss of jobs, skills, supplier confidence, and regional industrial capacity. For a company that is a regional anchor, that is not a routine management step. It is a serious economic and political rupture.

So my position remains unchanged:

- **No plant closures in Germany as the basis of the settlement**
- **No compulsory redundancies**
- **No weakening of co-determination**
- **No outcome that guts regional employment**

## To management

You keep saying that without closures you may not get the required savings. That may be true — but it is not yet proven.

What I need from you is not another assertion that closures are “on the table.” I need a **plant-by-plant comparison** showing whether the non-closure package can or cannot reach the expert’s threshold.

If you want me to take closure threats seriously, then show:
- which sites or lines lose shifts,
- how much capacity comes out,
- how many jobs are reduced through attrition, voluntary exits, redeployment, or other non-forced means,
- what the recurring annual savings are,
- and what investment is committed in return.

Without that, “closures are necessary” is still just a claim.

## To the works council and union

I support your insistence that the workforce should not be forced to pay for management’s failure through site destruction and forced layoffs.

That said, I need to be equally frank: if the answer is simply
- no closures,
- no layoffs,
- no real capacity adjustment,
- and no measurable burden-sharing,

then that is also not a solution.

If you want the state to stand behind a site-preserving route, then the package must be industrially real:
- shift elimination,
- product and volume consolidation,
- working-time changes,
- voluntary exit instruments,
- redeployment,
- management overhead cuts,
- and verified savings.

I am open to that. I am **not** open to a freeze that preserves every structure unchanged.

## To the controlling family and investors

I hear the stewardship argument. Long-term value matters. But long-term value is **not** protected by treating the workforce and the regional base as disposable.

A deal that breaks trust, triggers conflict, and weakens retention may look tidy on paper, but it can still be strategically destructive. That is especially true for a company whose strength depends on industrial capability and social partnership.

So yes, I take your value concerns seriously. But I reject the implication that the only serious path is one that keeps closures available as the first-line bargaining weapon.

I accept that standard.

- a **site-preserving restructuring**,
- **real capacity reduction**,
- **working-time flexibility**,
- **voluntary departure and redeployment**,
- **temporary negotiated labor contributions**,
- **management restraint and owner contribution**,

## What would move me

What would move me is not a vague promise of “no closures unless absolutely necessary.” What would move me is a **quantified, credible non-closure package** that:
- keeps the German plants open,
- removes real capacity,
- reduces the fixed-cost base materially,
- and preserves the regional economic anchor.

If that can be shown, I will engage constructively.

If it cannot, then I will say plainly: the proposed restructuring is not acceptable because it damages the regional base too severely.

## Bottom line

That means:
- yes to hard restructuring,
- yes to measurable savings,
- yes to burden-sharing,
