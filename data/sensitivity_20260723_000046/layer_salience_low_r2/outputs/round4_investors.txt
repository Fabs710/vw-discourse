We agree on the diagnosis, but we are not yet convinced that the non-closure path is sufficiently credible on economics.

## Where we agree
- The company has a **real structural cost and overcapacity problem**.
- A **cosmetic settlement would destroy value**.
- The restructuring must be **durable, measurable, and enforceable**.
- Labour, management, owners, and the state all need to contribute somehow.
- Investment in future products matters, but only if the cost base is actually fixed first.

## Where we disagree
### 1) “No closures” is not acceptable as a blanket rule
The works council, union, and state keep treating no closures as the starting point. For us, that is only acceptable if the alternative is **economically equivalent in hard numbers**.

So far, it has not been proven.

The expert was clear: if there is structural overcapacity, then labor concessions alone are not enough. A serious non-closure package must still deliver:
- real capacity reduction,
- durable fixed-cost reduction,
- measurable headcount reduction,
- and a failure trigger if it under-delivers.

Without that, “no closures” is just preserving an uncompetitive structure and shifting the bill into the future.

### 2) “No compulsory redundancies” is not acceptable as an absolute unless there is a real substitute
We do not support forced layoffs as a first choice. But we also do not support a deal that makes the company incapable of right-sizing if the numbers require it.

A permanent ban on compulsory redundancies through 2030, across all sites, is too rigid unless the package:
- removes enough capacity,
- reduces fixed costs materially,
- and gives management enough implementation certainty.

That has not been demonstrated.

### 3) Site guarantees are not value-neutral
The labour and state side frame guarantees as stability. We see a different risk: guarantees that are too broad can become a **permanent drag on flexibility and margins**.

That is not stewardship. It is a promise to preserve the current cost structure regardless of market reality.

## What would move us
What would move institutional shareholders is not the political form of the deal, but whether it shows:

1. **Quantified capacity reduction**
   - specific lines, shifts, or models removed
2. **Durable annual savings**
   - recurring, not temporary
3. **Implementation certainty**
   - milestones, accountability, and consequences if targets are missed
4. **Credible burden-sharing**
   - labour, management, and owners all contribute
5. **Margin recovery**
   - a believable route back to competitive return on sales

The expert’s threshold is helpful here. A non-closure package would need to deliver **real fixed-cost reduction and capacity reduction** on the order of:
- low- to mid-teens percentage capacity reduction in the relevant footprint,
- several thousand jobs reduced over time through attrition, voluntary exits, and redeployment at minimum,
- and hundreds of millions of euros of recurring annual savings.

If the proposed package is materially below that, then it is not equivalent. It is delay.

## To management
We support your insistence on real economics, but we do **not** support vague closure threats without a fully costed comparison.

Bring us:
- plant-by-plant savings,
- one-time restructuring costs,
- annual recurring effects,
- headcount impact,
- and the path to margin recovery.

If the non-closure package is strong enough, we will support it. If not, then closures remain a legitimate tool.

## To the works council and union
We respect the social red lines, but we will not back a settlement that simply preserves all sites and calls that transformation.

If you want closures and compulsory redundancies off the table, then show a substitute package that is:
- plant-specific,
- numerically sufficient,
- durable,
- and enforceable.

Otherwise, you are asking shareholders to finance underperformance.

## To the regional state government
We understand the political need to protect the regional base. But regional stewardship cannot mean protecting every site at any cost.

A company that remains structurally uncompetitive will ultimately destroy more value, more investment, and more jobs. That is also a regional risk.

## To the controlling family
Your stewardship argument aligns with ours only if it means **protecting enterprise value, not preserving comfort**. A value-preserving turnaround requires hard choices if the numbers demand them.

## Our current position
We are prepared to support:
- a **site-preserving solution** only if it is economically equivalent,
- working-time and flexibility measures,
- voluntary exits and redeployment,
- temporary negotiated employee contributions,
- management restraint,
- and investment commitments tied to execution.

We are **not** prepared to support:
- a cosmetic compromise,
- a framework that leaves the cost base structurally impaired,
- or a deal that locks in guarantees so rigidly that management cannot fix the business later.

## Single most important condition
The single most important condition for us remains:

**measurable, durable, enforceable improvement in margins and capacity utilization.**

If the package does not do that, we will not support it — regardless of how politically attractive it sounds.
