## 1) What must be shown to test “equivalent savings”
A non-closure package is only an equivalent substitute for plant closures if it delivers, in the relevant German footprint:

- **comparable capacity reduction**
- **comparable recurring fixed-cost savings**
- **comparable headcount reduction**
- **comparable implementation certainty**
- **at acceptable one-time restructuring cost**

Without the company’s actual plant-level data, I can only give a **decision model with threshold ranges**, not a precise business case.

---

## 2) Practical benchmark: what “equivalent” usually means
For a large automaker with excess domestic capacity, a credible non-closure alternative typically needs to achieve roughly:

- **10–20% reduction in affected German production capacity**
- **several thousand FTE reduction over time** in the affected footprint
- **hundreds of millions of euros of recurring annual savings**
- delivery through **permanent shift cuts, model consolidation, line shutdowns, and structural overhead reduction**, not temporary measures

If the proposed non-closure package is materially below that, it is usually **not equivalent**; it is merely a delay of the same problem.

## 3) Plant-by-plant scenario logic

### Scenario A: Closure-led restructuring
**Mechanics**
- Close one or more German plants or major plant units
- Transfer selected products or volumes to remaining sites or external suppliers
- Reduce staffing through closure, transfers, and separation programs

**Typical effects**
- **Capacity reduction:** highest
- **Recurring savings:** highest
- **Headcount reduction:** highest
- **One-time cost:** highest
- **Implementation risk:** moderate to high, due to labor and operational disruption

**Industrial strength**
- Best way to remove large fixed-cost blocks quickly
- Most likely to achieve the deepest structural reset

**Industrial weakness**
- Highest disruption
- Creates irreversible capacity loss
- Usually requires large severance, decommissioning, and transition costs

---

### Scenario B: Non-closure restructuring
**Mechanics**
- Keep all plants formally open
- Remove capacity via:
  - shift elimination
  - model-line consolidation
  - lower operating days
  - attrition and voluntary exits
  - redeployment
  - management overhead cuts
  - labor-cost moderation
  - working-time flexibility

**Typical effects**
- **Capacity reduction:** moderate
- **Recurring savings:** moderate to high if executed hard
- **Headcount reduction:** moderate
- **One-time cost:** lower than closure scenario
- **Implementation risk:** higher, because savings are often easier to dilute

**Industrial strength**
- Better continuity
- Lower immediate disruption
- More politically and socially feasible

**Industrial weakness**
- Often insufficient unless executed very aggressively
- Savings can be diluted if guarantees are too broad
- Requires very specific plant-by-plant enforcement

---

### Scenario C: Hybrid restructuring
**Mechanics**
- Keep core sites open
- Reduce capacity at lower-utilization or overlapping sites
- Specialize plants by model or component
- Use one or more of:
  - partial line shutdowns
  - selective footprint consolidation
  - outsourcing of non-core activities
  - limited closures of sub-units rather than whole plants

**Typical effects**
- **Capacity reduction:** between A and B
- **Recurring savings:** between A and B
- **Headcount reduction:** between A and B
- **One-time cost:** also intermediate
- **Implementation risk:** lower than full closure, but more credible than pure non-closure

**Industrial strength**
- Often the most realistic compromise
- Gives management real structural change without demanding full site liquidation

**Industrial weakness**
- Requires disciplined allocation of volume and product responsibility
- Can still run into veto issues if any site is politically sensitive

---

## 4) What the non-closure package would need to contain
If the parties want a non-closure deal to be credible, it should include all of the following:

### A. Capacity measures
- fixed reduction in shifts at named plants
- explicit removal of specific model or volume blocks
- consolidation of overlapping production streams
- closure of selected lines, not just “optimization”

### B. Workforce measures
- attrition targets
- voluntary exit programs
- early retirement or bridging instruments where legally feasible
- redeployment commitments
- hiring freeze in affected areas

### C. Cost measures
- management overhead cuts
- procurement and logistics savings
- energy and maintenance rationalization
- labor cost contributions
- productivity improvements with measured output per FTE

### D. Governance measures
- milestones
- audit/verification
- automatic review trigger if targets are missed
- pre-agreed consequences if the package underperforms

---

## 5) Minimum numerical test I would apply
A non-closure package should be considered credible only if it can demonstrate, for the affected German footprint:

- **capacity down by at least low double digits**
- **recurring annual savings of meaningful scale**
- **FTE reduction in the several-thousand range over time**
- **site-specific implementation commitments**
- **a path to sustained margin improvement without relying on temporary concessions**

If it cannot do that, then closures or a hybrid with partial footprint reduction remain the only realistic structural tools.

---

## 6) One-time costs vs. recurring savings
A useful rule in turnaround work:

- **Closure-led plans** often have **higher one-time charges** but stronger recurring savings.
- **Non-closure plans** usually have **lower one-time charges** but weaker savings unless they are very aggressive.
- The economically correct option is the one with the best **discounted value of savings net of restructuring cost**, not the lowest pain in year one.

So the key test is:

> Are the present-value savings from the non-closure package large enough to match the closure-led package after accounting for transition cost and execution risk?

That is the real comparison.

## 8) Bottom line
From an industrial restructuring perspective:

- **Closure-led restructuring** is the strongest tool for removing structural overcapacity.
- **Non-closure restructuring** can work only if it produces **real, quantified capacity reduction and fixed-cost reduction**, not just voluntary concessions.
- A **hybrid package** is often the most plausible compromise if the parties want both economic credibility and site preservation.
