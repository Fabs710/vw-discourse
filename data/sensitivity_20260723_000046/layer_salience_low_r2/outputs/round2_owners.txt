I agree with the central diagnosis from management, investors, and to a large extent the state: **the company needs a durable structural reset**. If the cost base and capacity remain where they are, we will continue to lose margin, flexibility, and ultimately value. That is not acceptable from a stewardship perspective.

But I also need to be equally clear: I do **not** support a settlement that produces a pyrrhic victory through prolonged conflict, reputational damage, and operational instability. The family holding’s interest is long-term value, not a headline restructuring that weakens the enterprise for years.

## Where I agree

### With management
You are right that:
- the current structure is not competitive enough,
- temporary measures alone will not solve the problem,
- and the package must be **durable, measurable, and enforceable**.

I also agree that any solution must create room for investment in future products and technology. Without that, we are just managing decline.

### With investors
You are right to insist on:
- measurable capacity reduction,
- durable labour-cost improvement,
- and implementation certainty.

A cosmetic compromise would destroy value. I would not support that either.

### With the state
You are right that a reckless, one-sided solution could damage the regional industrial base and destroy trust. That matters. A company like this needs social legitimacy to execute major change.

## Where I disagree

### With the works council and union
I respect the red lines, but I do **not** accept them as absolute conditions.

A blanket “no closures” and “no compulsory redundancies” stance only works if there is an alternative package that delivers the **same structural effect**. So far, I do not see that alternative clearly enough.

If the alternative is simply:
- voluntary measures,
- working-time flexibility,
- and temporary pay moderation,

then that may soften the pain, but it does not automatically fix the overcapacity and cost problem. The numbers have to work, not just the language.

Also, a **permanent employment guarantee through 2030 across all sites** would be too rigid if it prevents the company from reacting to market conditions. That would transfer the risk entirely onto the enterprise and its owners, which I cannot support.

### With the state’s “site-preserving first” framing
I understand the political imperative. But “preserve every site” cannot be the governing principle if it leaves the business structurally uncompetitive. Protecting the industrial footprint only has meaning if the company remains viable.

## What I think is necessary

The right question is not “closures or no closures” in the abstract. The question is:

**What package actually restores competitiveness, protects the enterprise’s future, and can survive in the real governance environment?**

That means we need a package with:
1. **real capacity reduction**,  
2. **real and lasting cost improvement**,  
3. **credible workforce contribution**,  
4. **investment commitments**, and  
5. **enforceability without permanent paralysis of management.**

## My position on the trade-offs

I am open to avoiding closures **if** the alternative is economically equivalent and durable.

But I am **not** open to:
- a deal that simply delays adjustment,
- a settlement that preserves all existing structures unchanged,
- or a framework that blocks future restructuring regardless of market developments.

Likewise, I do not believe that management should rely on closures as a bargaining threat if a serious negotiated alternative exists. The point is not to “win” against labour or the state; the point is to restore competitiveness.

## What would move me

What would genuinely move me is a package that shows:
- a quantified and credible path to margin recovery,
- capacity reduction with real operational impact,
- a meaningful labour-cost contribution,
- top-level restraint and visible burden-sharing from management and owners,
- and sufficient labour peace to allow execution.

If that can be achieved without closures, I would prefer that. If it cannot, then we should not pretend otherwise.

## My minimum acceptable outcome
- durable competitiveness improvement,
- no purely symbolic compromise,
- protection of long-term enterprise value,
- and a settlement that can actually be implemented.

## My maximum concession
- openness to non-closure solutions,
- acceptance of working-time and flexibility measures,
- willingness to support temporary and targeted employee contributions,
- and support for site-preserving restructuring **if** it is economically equivalent.

## Single most important condition
The single most important condition for the family holding is this:

**The restructuring must produce a durable improvement in competitiveness and margins without creating value-destroying instability.**

If a proposal fails that test, I will not support it — regardless of how much pressure is applied or how long the discussion continues.

### Bottom line
I am prepared to support a negotiated alternative to closures and forced redundancies **only if** it delivers the same structural result and is enforceable in practice. If not, then we are not talking about a solution; we are talking about delay.
