Yes. The right way to frame this is:

## Short answer
A credible **non-closure package** is possible only if it delivers, at plant level, a **durable fixed-cost reduction and capacity reduction** that is broadly equivalent to what closure/relocation would have achieved. In practice, for a large German OEM with persistent European overcapacity, that usually means:

- **low- to mid-teens percentage reduction in German assembly/system capacity** over the planning horizon, or
- an equivalent reduction in **annual fixed cost base** on the order of **hundreds of millions of euros**, not tens of millions,
- plus a **headcount reduction in the low-thousands to high-thousands range**, depending on current staffing density and product mix,
- with a **binding milestone structure** that allows the company to reopen the closure option if the package under-delivers.

If the package cannot show that scale, then it is not structurally equivalent; it is a delay mechanism.

---

## What “equivalent” means in industrial terms

For a German OEM in this situation, “equivalent savings” must cover four buckets:

1. **Plant fixed cost**
   - depreciation/carrying cost of underutilized assets
   - maintenance and utilities on idle capacity
   - plant overhead and indirect labor
   - logistics and internal transport inefficiency

2. **Direct labour cost**
   - base pay, supplements, overtime structure
   - paid time not matched by output
   - shift premiums, guarantees, and legacy protections

3. **Complexity cost**
   - too many derivatives, too many variants, too many internal interfaces
   - small-lot production and duplicated logistics

4. **Working capital / utilization effect**
   - lower throughput, higher unit cost, worse absorption of fixed cost

A non-closure package can offset part of this through labor concessions, but **not all of it**. If the business has genuine overcapacity, there must still be a **hard capacity cut** somewhere: fewer shifts, line consolidation, product-line removal, or transfer of a model to another plant or external site.

---

## Bounded estimate of the scale required

Without the company’s exact internal model, I would use the following **decision ranges**:

### Capacity
- **Minimum credible reduction:** about **10% of German passenger-car assembly / system output capacity**
- **More robust target:** **15–20%**
- Below **~10%**, the package usually does not change the economics enough if the cost base is structurally high.

### Headcount
- If the package is genuinely non-closure and no compulsory redundancies are allowed, the reduction must come mainly from:
  - attrition
  - retirement
  - voluntary exits
  - non-replacement
  - redeployment

A plausible range is:
- **low case:** ~**3–5%** of German workforce
- **base case:** ~**5–8%**
- **high case:** ~**8–12%**

For a very large domestic workforce, that is typically **several thousand to low double-digit thousand jobs** over the restructuring horizon.

### Annual savings
A meaningful non-closure package for a large German OEM typically needs:
- **~€300m–€700m per year** of durable recurring improvement to be taken seriously as a structural response,
- with a stronger case if the company is trying to close a material margin gap and fund EV investment simultaneously.

If the improvement is only **€100m–€200m**, it may be useful, but it is usually not enough to replace a closure option if the network is truly overbuilt.

## Plant-by-plant translation of a non-closure package

Because you asked for concrete terms, here is the operational logic plant by plant.

### 1) Final assembly plants
These are the main levers.

A non-closure settlement would require:
- removal of at least **one shift equivalent** at one or more plants, or
- transfer of a full model or derivative out of the plant, or
- consolidation of low-volume variants into fewer lines.

**Savings mechanism**
- lower direct labour hours
- less indirect support labour
- lower utilities and maintenance per unit
- better absorption of fixed cost

**Headcount effect**
- often **8–15% reduction** at the affected plant if one shift is removed or a model is removed
- with attrition/voluntary exits, not all of that needs to be forced, but the economic effect must be real

### 2) Body shop / paint / logistics-heavy sites
These are capital-intensive and sensitive to utilization.

A credible non-closure package could include:
- fewer operating hours
- consolidation of variants
- elimination of duplicate internal logistics flows
- reduced automation maintenance on underused lines

If these sites stay open but continue at low utilization, the economics remain weak. So the package must specify:
- **which line or module stops**
- **what throughput is removed**
- **what fixed-cost denominator falls**

### 3) Component and powertrain-adjacent sites
If these are still internal suppliers, the package may involve:
- consolidation of part families
- reduction in make-vs-buy scope
- redeployment into EV-related parts
- closure of some legacy machining or assembly loops within the site

Here the risk is “open but underloaded.” That is not enough. The plan needs:
- explicit volume transfer
- explicit SKU rationalization
- explicit workforce reduction

## What measures can actually replace closures

### Measures that can work
These can produce real savings if they are enforced:

- **shift elimination**
- **model consolidation**
- **product-line rationalization**
- **working-time reductions / flexible annual hours**
- **temporary pay moderation**
- **voluntary severance / retirement**
- **redeployment across plants**
- **management overhead cuts**
- **supplier insourcing/outso\-urcing rebalancing**
- **reduced complexity in trim/options/derivatives**

### Measures that usually do not fully substitute
These often help cash flow, but not enough for structural equivalence:

- one-time bonuses foregone
- temporary wage freezes without capacity cuts
- vague efficiency programs
- “digitalization” promises without line closure or shift removal
- board-level or executive pay cuts alone
- purely voluntary measures with no volume relocation

In other words: **cost concessions without capacity cuts** are not equivalent.

## A workable non-closure settlement architecture

If the parties want to avoid closures, the package needs to be written like this:

### Phase 1: Immediate 12–18 months
- no compulsory redundancies
- no plant closures
- temporary workforce contribution:
  - pay moderation
  - working-time flexibility
  - reduced premium load
- management contribution:
  - overhead reduction
  - executive restraint
  - capex prioritization
- immediate throughput and product simplification

### Phase 2: Structural implementation 18–36 months
- line or shift removals at named sites
- product consolidation between plants
- headcount reduction via attrition/voluntary exits/redeployment
- elimination of duplicate support functions
- verification of annual recurring savings

### Phase 3: Review gate
- independent audit against milestones
- if the savings/capacity targets are not met, the company regains the right to pursue closure/relocation options

This is the only way a no-closure package becomes credible: **it must still bite industrially**.

---

## Milestones and failure trigger

This is the critical piece.

### Milestones should include:
1. **Capacity**
   - specific line/shift reductions by date
   - target utilization for each site

2. **Headcount**
   - gross and net workforce reduction by site
   - split by retirement, voluntary exits, redeployment

3. **Cost**
   - recurring annual savings achieved
   - fixed-cost reduction, not just variable cost

4. **Complexity**
   - number of derivatives/SKUs removed
   - logistics simplification metrics

5. **Financial outcome**
   - improvement in plant-level contribution margin
   - brand return-on-sales trajectory

### Failure trigger
A reasonable trigger would be:
- **<80–85% of targeted recurring savings realized by the mid-point review**, or
- **capacity reductions delayed by more than 6–9 months**, or
- **headcount reductions materially below plan**, or
- **utilization remains below a threshold that still leaves the sites structurally oversized**

At that point, the non-closure package has failed as an industrial restructuring instrument.

---

## Practical conclusion

My assessment is:

- **A non-closure deal is possible only if it includes real capacity cuts.**
- Those cuts must be large enough to move the economics, not just moderate them.
- For a company of this type, that usually means **10–20% capacity reduction in the relevant German footprint**, **several thousand jobs reduced over time**, and **hundreds of millions of euros of recurring annual savings**.
- If the parties want to avoid closures, they must replace them with **named plant actions**, **dated milestones**, and a **hard failure trigger**.
