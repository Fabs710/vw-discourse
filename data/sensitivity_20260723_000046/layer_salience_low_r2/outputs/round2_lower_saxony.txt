I will be equally direct.

## Where I agree

I agree with management, the family holding, and investors on one point: **the company needs a durable restructuring, not a cosmetic patch**. If the cost base stays structurally uncompetitive, then the business, the brand, and ultimately the jobs are all at risk. I am not asking for delay dressed up as compromise.

I also agree that:
- investment in future products is essential,
- the solution must be economically credible,
- and burden-sharing has to involve more than just one side.

## Where I disagree

I do **not** accept that **plant closures in Germany** are the default or first-choice answer.

For me, closures are not a neutral instrument. They mean permanent damage to regional employment, local supply chains, and the company’s role as an industrial anchor in the state. Because of that, closures must be treated as a last resort, not as a bargaining baseline.

I also do **not** accept any logic that turns “structural change” into a justification for:
- compulsory redundancies as a normal tool,
- hollowing out sites by stealth,
- or weakening co-determination so that workforce and state influence become irrelevant.

That would be unacceptable politically, economically, and from a long-term stewardship perspective.

## What I think is necessary

If management wants the state to support a settlement, then the package must do three things at once:

1. **restore competitiveness**  
2. **preserve a viable German industrial footprint**  
3. **avoid unnecessary social destruction**

That means the discussion has to focus on **site-preserving restructuring**, not on closures as the presumed solution.

## My response to each side

### To management
You are right that the company needs a real cost and capacity adjustment. But you have not shown that closures are the only way to achieve it. Before talking about closing German plants, you must seriously test alternatives such as:
- capacity reductions through reorganization,
- working-time flexibility,
- voluntary departures,
- internal redeployment,
- retirement pathways,
- and negotiated temporary labor contributions.

If those alternatives cannot deliver enough, say so with numbers. But do not present closures as the only serious option without exhausting the others.

### To the works council and union
I support your insistence that the burden cannot fall one-sidedly on the workforce. Your red lines on compulsory redundancies and site destruction are politically understandable and, in my view, legitimate.

But I also need to be frank: a solution that simply freezes the current structure without measurable savings will not hold. If we want to protect jobs and sites in the long run, then the workforce side will need to contribute to a real restructuring package — though not by giving up the core principle that German sites should be preserved and forced layoffs avoided.

So I support:
- no forced redundancies,
- no rash plant closures,
- and a negotiated employee contribution,
but only in a package that genuinely changes the economics.

### To the family holding and investors
You are right to demand seriousness and durability. I share the concern about cosmetic compromise. But long-term value is not served by a restructuring that tears apart trust, triggers prolonged conflict, and undermines the company’s social legitimacy in Germany.

A company like this does not compete only on spreadsheets. It also competes on skills, industrial peace, and the ability to retain a committed workforce. If you destroy that foundation, the financial result may look cleaner for a moment and worse later.

## My line on guarantees

I am prepared to support **binding protection for sites and employment** if that is what makes a negotiated restructuring possible.

What I want is not a blank cheque forever, but a **credible medium-term framework** that:
- keeps German sites open,
- avoids compulsory redundancies,
- uses voluntary and flexible instruments first,
- and links workforce contributions to real investment and product commitments.

That is the kind of settlement that can preserve both competitiveness and legitimacy.

## On the state’s role

The state will not accept being reduced to a passive bystander while the company restructures at the expense of the region. Under the special statute, we have a legitimate co-responsibility. We will use it to defend:
- regional employment,
- industrial capability,
- and meaningful co-determination.

That does not mean blocking change for its own sake. It means insisting that change is done in a way that preserves the company’s role as a regional anchor rather than dismantling it.

## My minimum acceptable outcome

- no plant closures in Germany as part of the initial settlement,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- a real restructuring with measurable savings,
- binding site and employment protection for a meaningful period,
- and a negotiated package of flexibility and contribution from all sides.

## My maximum concession

I can accept:
- capacity reductions,
- working-time adjustments,
- voluntary departure instruments,
- targeted temporary labor-cost contributions,
- and phased implementation,

**if and only if** the result protects German sites and avoids forced layoffs.

## Single most important condition

The single most important condition for me is this:

**No outcome that permanently damages regional employment or sidelines the state’s veto and co-determination role.**

If the proposal crosses that line, I will oppose it.

## Bottom line

I am open to a hard restructuring. I am not open to a restructuring that treats closures and compulsory redundancies as normal, or that sacrifices the company’s role in the region.

If management can bring forward a serious, quantified, site-preserving package, I will engage constructively. If not, then we have not yet reached a basis for agreement.
