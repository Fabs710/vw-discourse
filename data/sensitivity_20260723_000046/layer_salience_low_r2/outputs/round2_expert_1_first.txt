Here is the restructuring assessment, strictly on capacity, cost, workforce, and scenario mechanics.

## 1) Core issue: what must a credible package achieve?

A viable turnaround package has to do three things at once:

1. **Reduce structural overcapacity**
2. **Lower the fixed-cost base durably**
3. **Right-size employment without creating a new cost problem through excess guarantees**

If those three are not achieved, the company may buy labour peace for a while but will not restore competitiveness.

---

## 2) Closure vs. non-closure: the industrial logic

### Plant closure
A closure is the strongest lever for **permanent capacity removal** and **fixed-cost elimination**. It usually gives the cleanest and most durable savings because it removes:
- plant overhead,
- maintenance and utilities,
- local management layers,
- some logistics duplication,
- and the direct labor tied to the site.

But closure also creates:
- high one-time restructuring costs,
- transition risk,
- volume transfer complexity,
- supplier disruption,
- and potential loss of production flexibility.

### Non-closure restructuring
If a plant stays open, the savings must come from:
- **lower hours per vehicle**
- **fewer shifts / lower utilization**
- **headcount reduction via attrition or voluntary exits**
- **work rule changes**
- **wage or benefit moderation**
- **increased productivity**
- **capacity consolidation within the network**

This can work, but only if the savings are large enough and durable enough. In practice, non-closure packages often deliver **partial** savings unless they include meaningful capacity removal, not just labor concessions.

---

## 3) What “equivalent savings” means in practice

For a non-closure option to be truly equivalent to a closure-led plan, it must match closure on three dimensions:

- **Magnitude:** same order of annual savings
- **Durability:** savings persist beyond 1–2 bargaining cycles
- **Enforceability:** management can actually implement it without returning immediately to the same crisis

In manufacturing turnarounds, “equivalent” rarely means identical in every line item. It means the package must produce a comparable reduction in **annual fixed cost per unit** and in **installed capacity relative to demand**.

If the company has genuine excess capacity, then labor-cost concessions alone typically do **not** solve the problem, because the main issue is not just cost per worker; it is cost per unit on underutilized assets.

---

## 4) Which measures can deliver savings without closures?

A non-closure settlement can be credible if it combines several levers:

### A. Capacity reduction without site closure
Examples:
- removing one shift,
- consolidating volumes to fewer lines,
- reducing model mix at a site,
- moving a site from volume production to parts or niche assembly,
- reallocating products across plants.

This can create real savings if utilization rises on the remaining footprint and the company removes duplicated overhead. But the savings are only strong if capacity is actually taken out, not just “reassigned.”

### B. Headcount reduction via attrition / voluntary exits
This is useful for fixed-cost reduction, but it is slower and less certain than closure. It works best where:
- workforce age profile supports retirement,
- turnover exists,
- voluntary packages are financially acceptable,
- and the site does not require a large immediate cut.

### C. Working-time and flexibility changes
These can improve capacity matching and reduce overtime/inefficiency. However, on their own they usually produce **modest** savings unless the company is significantly reducing hours across a large workforce.

### D. Wage moderation / temporary labor contribution
This helps cash cost and can be meaningful, but it is rarely enough by itself to fix a structural overcapacity problem. It is most effective as a bridge measure paired with capacity rationalization.

### E. Productivity improvements
These matter, but they should be treated cautiously. In a turnaround, projected productivity gains are often overstated unless they are backed by:
- line redesign,
- automation,
- process simplification,
- SKU reduction,
- and real management control.

---

## 5) Which measures cannot substitute for closures?

Non-closure tools generally cannot fully substitute for closures if the network has:

- persistent volume decline,
- too many plants for the demand base,
- structurally high German labor and overhead cost,
- duplicated platforms or logistics,
- and limited room to raise utilization elsewhere.

In that case, closures or equivalent hard capacity removals usually become the only way to remove enough fixed cost.

A key point: **labor cost reduction does not equal capacity reduction**. You can lower payroll and still be stuck with too much plant and too little volume. That produces a cheaper but still underutilized network.

---

## 6) Employment implications by scenario

Because exact workforce numbers were not provided, I’ll frame this directionally.

### Scenario 1: Closure-led restructuring
Typical effects:
- Largest direct job losses concentrated at the closed site(s)
- Additional indirect losses in support functions, logistics, and suppliers
- Faster reduction in total headcount
- Higher one-time severance/restructuring costs
- Better chance of achieving the full structural target

This is the fastest route to right-sizing, but also the most disruptive.

### Scenario 2: No closures, but major capacity reduction elsewhere
Typical effects:
- Lower direct job losses at named sites
- More job reductions spread across plants
- Slower implementation
- Greater dependence on voluntary exits and attrition
- Higher risk that the company fails to reach the full savings target

This is operationally feasible only if the company can truly remove capacity elsewhere.

### Scenario 3: No closures and no compulsory redundancies
Typical effects:
- Lowest immediate workforce disruption
- Highest risk of insufficient savings
- Slowest adjustment
- Largest chance the company returns to the same problem later

This is generally only workable if the required savings are moderate, not severe.

---

## 7) What makes a non-closure package credible?

A no-closure deal can be credible if it contains all of the following:

1. **Quantified capacity cuts**
   - specific lines, shifts, or volumes removed

2. **Large enough annual savings**
   - not just one-time concessions

3. **Defined headcount path**
   - expected reductions by function and site

4. **Implementation timetable**
   - 12–36 months, with milestones

5. **Exit mechanisms**
   - retirement, voluntary severance, redeployment, and if needed some compulsory measures

6. **Management cost discipline**
   - overhead reduction at headquarters and in plant support

7. **Investment linked to execution**
   - future product allocation tied to actual savings delivery

If any of those are missing, the package risks becoming a delay tactic rather than a turnaround.

---

## 8) The main trade-off

The central trade-off is:

- **Closures** = stronger, faster structural savings, higher social and transition cost
- **No closures** = lower immediate disruption, but only viable if other measures truly remove enough capacity and fixed cost

In a company with serious overcapacity and a weak margin position, the question is not whether non-closure measures are desirable. It is whether they are **numerically sufficient**.

## 10) Bottom-line expert view

From a turnaround perspective:

- **If the required savings are modest**, a negotiated non-closure package can work.
- **If the required savings are large and structural**, non-closure measures are usually only viable if they include genuine capacity removal, not just wage moderation or attrition.
- **If management’s diagnosis of overcapacity is correct**, then some combination of site rationalization, shift elimination, and headcount reduction is likely necessary.
- A promise of **no closures, no compulsory redundancies, and full guarantees through 2030** is usually not compatible with a deep structural reset unless the company can prove that alternative measures fully replace the lost fixed-cost savings.
