I will be direct.

## Where I agree
I agree with everyone on one point: **this company needs a real restructuring**, not a cosmetic one. The expert has confirmed what we already knew: if there is structural overcapacity, then there must be **real capacity reduction**, **real fixed-cost reduction**, and a package that is measurable and durable.

I also agree that the burden cannot fall only on one side. Management, owners, and capital must contribute as well. And I agree that a weak, vague compromise would only delay the next crisis.

## Where I disagree
I do **not** accept the conclusion that this means:
- plant closures in Germany,
- compulsory redundancies,
- or dismantling the job-security framework.

Those are not normal “tools.” They are the destruction of jobs, skills, and trust. Once a German plant is closed, that capacity is gone. Once forced layoffs start, the promise to the workforce is broken. That is not a side issue; it is exactly the line that cannot be crossed.

So let me repeat clearly:

- **No plant closures**
- **No compulsory redundancies**
- **No hollowing out of co-determination**
- **No settlement that turns job security into a dead letter**

## To management
You keep saying the numbers must work. Fine. Then show a package that works **without** closures and **without** forced layoffs.

The expert has already given the framework: the package must include:
- shift elimination,
- model and volume consolidation,
- working-time changes,
- voluntary exits,
- redeployment,
- management overhead cuts,
- and measurable fixed-cost savings.

That is the test. If your proposal cannot meet it, then your closure threat is not proof of necessity; it is leverage.

And I reject the attempt to make closures the default answer whenever negotiations become difficult.

## To the family holding and investors
You speak about value, stewardship, and long-term competitiveness. Then understand this: **value is not protected by breaking trust with the workforce**.

A company that tears up its social contract may improve a spreadsheet for one quarter, but it weakens itself in the real economy:
- lower trust,
- more conflict,
- weaker retention,
- damaged know-how,
- and a permanently more brittle industrial base.

If you want long-term value, then support a package that preserves the German production base and shares the burden fairly.

## To the regional state government
You say closures should not be the basis of the settlement. That is right. But that cannot remain just a political phrase.

If the state is serious, then it must support a **site-preserving restructuring** in practice:
- no closures in the initial settlement,
- no forced redundancies,
- and real backing for a negotiated industrial strategy that protects the regional base.

The state should not help turn a restructuring into a managed dismantling of German industry.

## To the union
I agree with you that labour should contribute, but not by accepting destruction of jobs and sites. Working-time flexibility, voluntary exit paths, redeployment, and temporary negotiated contributions are discussable.

But let me be equally clear: we are not discussing a blank cheque. We are not discussing permanent wage dumping. And we are not discussing a settlement where the workforce pays for management’s failure while the top stays protected.

## On the expert’s assessment
The expert’s numbers are useful because they show what a real alternative must look like. But they do **not** prove that closures are unavoidable. They prove something else:

A non-closure package must be **industrial, quantified, and real**.

That means:
- named plant actions,
- named shift removals,
- named volume transfers,
- named savings,
- named headcount reductions via attrition and voluntary measures,
- and a real failure trigger.

If you cannot show that, then you have not made the case for closures — you have only asserted them.

## What I am prepared to discuss
I am prepared to discuss:
- working-time flexibility,
- voluntary departure instruments,
- redeployment,
- temporary and negotiated employee contributions,
- product and volume consolidation,
- and productivity measures.

But only inside a **site-preserving package**.

## What I am not prepared to accept
I am not prepared to accept:
- closures dressed up as “rationalization,”
- relocation by stealth,
- forced redundancies,
- or a so-called conditional guarantee that is really just a trapdoor for later closures.

A promise that says “accept this now, and if management later decides the plan failed, we reserve the right to shut plants and force layoffs” is not security. It is extortion with a review clause.

## My minimum acceptable outcome
- every German plant remains open,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- binding employment security,
- genuine co-determination,
- and a negotiated restructuring package that shares burdens fairly.

## My maximum concession
- working-time flexibility,
- voluntary exit programmes,
- redeployment,
- temporary negotiated cost contributions,
- productivity measures,
- and capacity reduction through reorganization, **so long as no site is closed and no one is forced out**.

## My single most important condition
This remains unchanged:

**No closures and no forced layoffs.**

If that condition is breached, there is no agreement from the works council side.

## On the proposed “conditional protection” model
I reject any model that makes job security dependent on management later deciding whether the package is “enough.” That is not a guarantee; it is a threat with a timer.

If the company wants a real settlement, it must present a **non-closure plan that is already sufficient**. It does not get to keep the closure weapon in reserve as a way to force acceptance.

## What I want next
If management and capital claim there is no alternative, then they should bring the numbers in a plant-by-plant form, not keep speaking in abstractions.

[SUMMON: independent industrial restructuring advisor to provide a plant-by-plant comparison of a non-closure plan versus a closure plan, including capacity reduction, headcount reduction, annual savings, implementation timing, and whether the non-closure plan can meet the expert’s threshold without plant closures or compulsory redundancies.]

Until that is on the table, the claim that closures are unavoidable remains unproven.

## Bottom line
I am not moving from the core position because the facts presented do not justify moving:
- The company needs restructuring.
- The restructuring must be durable.
- But it does **not** follow that German plants must close or that workers must be forcibly laid off.

A real turnaround is possible only if it preserves the industrial base, respects co-determination, and protects jobs. If that cannot be done, then management is asking labour to pay for a failure we did not create. That is not acceptable.
