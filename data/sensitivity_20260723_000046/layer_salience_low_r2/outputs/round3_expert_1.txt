Yes. Based on the discussion so far, the key task is to convert the **non-closure option** into an industrially credible restructuring plan that can be compared against a **closure option** on the same metrics.

## 1) What a credible non-closure package must achieve
For a large German automotive footprint facing structural overcapacity, a non-closure package must deliver all of the following:

- **real capacity reduction**
- **durable fixed-cost reduction**
- **measurable headcount reduction**
- **lower complexity**
- **implementation certainty with a failure trigger**

If it does not reduce capacity, it is not structurally equivalent to closures. If it reduces cost only temporarily, it is not durable.

---

## 2) Minimum scale that is usually required
Without the company’s internal plant data, I can only give decision ranges.

### Capacity
A non-closure plan generally needs to remove about:

- **10% minimum credible capacity reduction**
- **15–20% more robust / safer target**

This should apply to the relevant German passenger-car manufacturing footprint, not the whole company globally.

### Headcount
If closures are avoided, workforce reduction must come mainly from attrition, voluntary exits, redeployment, and retirement. A plausible range is:

- **3–5%** of German workforce in a weak case
- **5–8%** in a base case
- **8–12%** if the overcapacity is severe and the package is to substitute for closures

For a very large domestic workforce, that typically means **several thousand to low double-digit thousand jobs** over the restructuring horizon.

### Recurring annual savings
A credible alternative to closures should typically generate:

- **~€300m to €700m per year** of recurring savings

If the number is materially below that, it is unlikely to substitute for a closure-based restructuring if the network is structurally oversized.

---

## 3) What measures can replace closures
The non-closure package has to create savings through **specific plant actions**, not general commitments.

### Measures that can work
- removal of one or more shifts
- model or derivative consolidation
- product-line simplification
- annual working-time flexibility / lower paid idle time
- voluntary severance and early retirement
- redeployment between plants
- management overhead cuts
- complexity reduction in variants, logistics, and support functions
- some negotiated labor-cost moderation

### Measures that do not fully substitute
- temporary wage freezes alone
- one-off bonuses forgone
- executive pay cuts alone
- vague efficiency promises
- “digitalization” without volume/line reduction
- voluntary exits without a binding capacity plan

Those can help cash flow, but they do not usually remove enough fixed cost or capacity.

## 4) Plant-by-plant logic
Since no plant-level operating data were supplied, I’ll describe the industrial logic by site type.

### A) Final assembly plants
These are the main levers.

A credible non-closure action would usually require:
- loss of **one shift equivalent** at one or more plants, or
- transfer of a model/derivative out of the plant, or
- consolidation of low-volume variants onto fewer lines

Typical effect:
- **8–15% plant headcount reduction**
- meaningful reduction in direct labor
- lower indirect labor
- better absorption of fixed cost

### B) Body shop / paint / logistics-heavy plants
These sites are sensitive to utilization. The package must specify:
- which line or module stops
- which throughput is removed
- how much support labor disappears
- what fixed cost comes out

If the plant stays open but underutilized, the economics remain weak.

### C) Component / powertrain-adjacent plants
If legacy ICE-related activity is being displaced, the package may involve:
- SKU/part-family consolidation
- insourcing/out-sourcing changes
- conversion to EV-related work
- closure of some internal machining or subassembly loops inside the site

Again, “open but underloaded” is not enough.

## 5) A workable non-closure structure
If the parties want to avoid closures, I would structure the deal in three phases:

### Phase 1: 12–18 months
- no compulsory redundancies
- no closures
- temporary workforce contribution
- working-time flexibility
- management overhead restraint
- immediate simplification of products/variants
- defined product or volume transfers

### Phase 2: 18–36 months
- named shift removals
- line consolidation
- attrition/voluntary exits/redeployment
- headcount reduction by site
- recurring savings verified

### Phase 3: Review gate
- independent audit of capacity, cost, and headcount targets
- if targets are missed, the closure option reopens under a predefined trigger

That failure trigger is critical. Without it, the package is just a delay mechanism.

---

## 6) Suggested failure trigger
A reasonable trigger would be one or more of the following:

- **less than 80–85% of targeted recurring savings achieved** by midpoint review
- capacity reductions delayed by more than **6–9 months**
- headcount reductions materially below plan
- utilization still too low to make the footprint economically sustainable
- plant-level contribution margins not improving as expected

If triggered, management regains the right to pursue closures or relocation.
