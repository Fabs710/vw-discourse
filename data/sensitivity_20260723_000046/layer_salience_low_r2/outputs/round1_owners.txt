I appreciate the clarity from all sides. From the perspective of the family holding, I will be equally direct.

We share the central diagnosis from management: the company cannot remain competitive with a structurally too-high cost base and persistent overcapacity. If we do nothing substantial, we will not protect the brand, the investment capacity, or the long-term value of the enterprise. That would be irresponsible stewardship.

At the same time, I do not view plant closures or compulsory redundancies as goals in themselves. They are tools, and only justifiable if they are truly necessary to restore competitiveness. The family holding’s concern is not to “win” against the workforce or regional stakeholders, but to avoid a slow erosion of the company’s value that ultimately harms everyone, including the employees.

Where I agree with the works council, the union, and the state:
- A purely destructive approach would be a mistake.
- Long-term competitiveness matters more than a short-lived headline solution.
- Investment in future products and technologies is essential.
- Industrial peace has real value and should not be sacrificed lightly.
- Burden-sharing should not fall only on the shop floor.

Where I disagree:
- A blanket rejection of plant closures or any compulsory redundancies is not a credible starting point if the economics require deeper change.
- Preserving every German site unchanged cannot be the only objective.
- Permanent guarantees that prevent necessary restructuring would be value-destructive and, in the end, not protect jobs but postpone a worse outcome.
- A settlement that only shifts costs temporarily, without durable capacity and productivity effects, would not be acceptable.

To management: I support the need for a serious restructuring, but I would expect discipline in how it is pursued. If the plan is framed only as a demand for labour concessions, it will likely fail and damage trust. You need a convincing industrial logic, clear priorities, and a structure that can survive beyond the next bargaining round.

To the workforce representatives: if you want to avoid closures and forced layoffs, then the alternative must achieve equivalent structural impact. That means real and measurable cost reduction, not just symbolic flexibility. It also means accepting that some combination of working-time adjustments, capacity reduction, redeployment, and employee contributions may be unavoidable.

To the regional state government: I understand the public-interest dimension and agree that this should not become a reckless exercise in cutting for its own sake. But the public interest also includes a competitive company that remains profitable, invests, and retains a viable industrial base. Protecting every existing structure at all costs is not the same as protecting the region in the long run.

So my position is this:
- We support a credible restructuring that restores margins and competitiveness.
- We prefer negotiated solutions over confrontation.
- We are open to alternatives to closures if they are economically equivalent and durable.
- But we will not support a settlement that leaves the company structurally uncompetitive or permanently blocks necessary governance and operational change.

If there is a serious package on the table that combines real labour-cost relief, capacity adjustment, and investment commitments, then it deserves support. If the result is only delay dressed up as compromise, then it is not a solution.
