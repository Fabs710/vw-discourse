We have now had enough repetition. The position of the workforce is clear, and I will not dilute it because management and shareholders keep calling it “realism.”

## Where I agree
I agree with everyone that this company needs a **durable restructuring** and that the current cost and capacity structure is under pressure. I also agree that the solution must be **credible, measurable, and long-term**. Cosmetic measures are useless.

I also acknowledge the expert’s point: if the company truly has structural overcapacity, then the package must contain **real capacity reduction** and not just slogans about flexibility.

## Where I disagree
I disagree with the conclusion that this automatically justifies **plant closures** and **compulsory redundancies**.

That step is not “just another instrument.” It is a permanent destruction of jobs, skills, trust, and industrial capacity. Once a German plant is closed, that capacity is gone. Once forced layoffs begin, the job-security framework is broken. And once co-determination is treated as an obstacle rather than a legal and practical necessity, the whole model that made this company strong is undermined.

So let me be explicit:

- **No plant closures in Germany**
- **No compulsory redundancies**
- **No dismantling of the historic job-security framework**
- **No settlement that sidelines co-determination**

That remains our line.

## On management’s argument
Management says: “If the numbers do not work without closures, then closures remain necessary.”

My answer is: then bring forward a package that actually works without destroying sites and livelihoods. The burden of proof is on those who want to break longstanding guarantees, not on the workforce to accept it as inevitable.

You keep speaking about “equivalent savings.” Fine. But that must be shown in concrete, plant-preserving terms. It is not enough to say:
- maybe closures,
- maybe layoffs,
- maybe some voluntary exits,
- maybe some flexibility.

That is not a solution. That is a threat.

## On the expert’s assessment
The expert is useful insofar as they confirm something we already know: labor concessions alone are not enough if the company is serious about restructuring. But the expert also shows that **non-closure restructuring can work** if it combines:
- capacity reduction through reorganization,
- shift elimination,
- product and volume consolidation,
- working-time changes,
- voluntary exits,
- management overhead cuts,
- and productivity measures.

That is exactly the path we are demanding.

What the expert does **not** show is that closures are unavoidable. It shows only that you need a real package. We agree on that. We do **not** agree that the package must include closures or forced layoffs.

## What we are prepared to accept
We are not pretending nothing can change. We are prepared to discuss:

- working-time flexibility,
- voluntary departure instruments,
- redeployment,
- temporary and negotiated labour-cost contributions,
- productivity measures,
- and capacity reduction through reorganization.

But these are acceptable only if they are part of a **site-preserving package**.

## What we are **not** prepared to accept
We are not prepared to accept:
- closures dressed up as “optimization,”
- relocation by stealth,
- forced redundancies,
- or a so-called turnaround that simply shifts the pain entirely onto the workforce.

If management wants a serious deal, then the first condition is simple:

**All German plants remain open, and no one is forced out.**

## To management
You say a full guarantee through 2030 is too rigid. I say your proposal is too rigid if it requires breaking trust and dismantling job security.

If you want workforce cooperation, you need to show that the burden is shared:
- at the top,
- across capital,
- and through genuine structural adjustments that do not destroy sites.

Restraint on executive pay is not enough, but neither is forcing workers to surrender the core protections that keep this company stable.

## To the family holding and investors
You talk about long-term value. Then stop treating the German workforce as a variable cost to be cut whenever market conditions tighten.

Long-term value depends on:
- skilled labor retention,
- stable industrial relations,
- production capability,
- and trust.

A company that breaks its employment commitments does not become stronger. It becomes more brittle. It may look leaner on a spreadsheet, but it is weaker in the real world.

## To the regional state government
You have said closures should be a last resort. Good. Then act like it. A “site-preserving first” line must mean something concrete:
- no closures in the initial settlement,
- no forced redundancies,
- and real political backing for a negotiated industrial strategy.

The state cannot simply ask the workforce to be patient while management keeps closure threats on the table.

## On the supposed compromise options
The moderator lists “conditional guarantees” and “hybrid packages.” I will be very clear: these are only acceptable if they are **genuine safeguards**, not a trapdoor.

A conditional guarantee that says, “accept our plan, and if it fails, we reserve the right to close plants and force layoffs later,” is not acceptable as a basis for trust.

A hybrid package that keeps every site open, avoids forced layoffs, and achieves savings through:
- shift elimination,
- capacity consolidation,
- attrition,
- voluntary exits,
- management cuts,
- and negotiated workforce contributions,
is a legitimate basis for discussion.

But the moment closures or compulsory redundancies are made part of the framework, we are no longer discussing a compromise. We are discussing how much damage the workforce is expected to tolerate.

## My minimum acceptable outcome
- every German plant stays open,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- binding employment security,
- genuine co-determination,
- and a negotiated restructuring package that shares burdens fairly.

## My maximum concession
- working-time flexibility,
- voluntary exit programmes,
- redeployment,
- temporary and negotiated cost contributions,
- and productivity improvements,
as long as they do not undermine site security or job security.

## Single most important condition
The single most important condition remains:

**No closures and no forced layoffs.**

If that condition is breached, there is no agreement from the works council side. Full stop.

## Bottom line
We are ready for a serious restructuring conversation. We are not ready to sign off on the destruction of German sites or the abandonment of the workforce’s protections.

If management wants a settlement, it must come back with a package that proves, with numbers and enforceable commitments, that the company can be turned around **without** closures and **without** compulsory redundancies.

Until then, our answer is no.
