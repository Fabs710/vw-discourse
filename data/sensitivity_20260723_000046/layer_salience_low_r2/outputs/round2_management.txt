I appreciate that everyone has now moved from abstract principles to concrete red lines. Let me respond just as concretely.

## Where I agree

I agree with all parties on one essential point: this company needs a **durable** solution, not a cosmetic one.

That means:
- the cost base has to come down structurally,
- capacity has to be reduced in a way that actually changes the economics,
- investment into future products and technologies has to be protected,
- and any settlement has to be enforceable beyond the next bargaining cycle.

On that, the union, works council, state, family, and investors are all describing parts of the same reality.

## Where I disagree

### 1) I do not accept “no closures” as a starting point
The works council and union keep repeating that closures are off the table. I understand why you say that. But from management’s point of view, that is not a solution to the overcapacity problem.

If all sites must remain open by definition, then you are asking us to preserve a cost structure that is already too high for a shrinking European market. That would be a defensive slogan, not a restructuring.

I am not saying closures are the only instrument. I am saying they remain a real option because the economics may require it. If an alternative package can deliver the same structural effect, I will consider it. But “no closures” without an economically equivalent alternative is not acceptable.

### 2) I do not accept “no compulsory redundancies” as an absolute if the numbers do not work
I also hear the demand for zero forced layoffs. Again, I understand the concern. But if the workforce insists on complete employment guarantees through 2030 while rejecting closures, then the burden of adjustment gets pushed entirely onto management and shareholders.

That is not a balanced restructuring. It is a request to freeze the current structure and hope the market improves. It will not.

I am prepared to prioritize voluntary instruments, retirement pathways, redeployment, and attrition. But I will not bind the company to a framework that prevents the workforce adjustment required to restore competitiveness.

### 3) “Equivalent savings” must be real, measurable, and durable
The union says: prove the equivalent savings. Fair enough. That is exactly the point.

But the proof has to be credible. I am not interested in vague promises of future productivity, soft flexibility, or temporary concessions that disappear in the next round. If the alternative to closures is to be accepted, it must produce:
- real capacity reduction,
- real labor-cost relief,
- and a permanent effect on the cost base.

If that cannot be shown, then the alternative is not equivalent.

## On the proposals from labour

### Working-time flexibility
Yes, that can be part of the package. It is not enough on its own, but it can contribute.

### Temporary pay moderation
That can be discussed, but only if it is substantial enough to matter and only if it is tied to measurable structural changes. Temporary sacrifice without structural change is exactly the sort of cosmetic deal I will reject.

### Voluntary departure and redeployment
These are useful tools. But they do not solve a large overcapacity problem on their own. They can support the transition; they cannot carry the entire burden.

### Binding employment and site guarantees through 2030
This is where I draw the line. A full guarantee through 2030 across all German sites would severely limit the company’s ability to react to market changes and would lock in the very cost problem we are trying to fix. I cannot support that.

## On the management contribution
To the union and works council: you are right to demand that management and owners contribute too. I accept that.

That means:
- top-level restraint,
- no pretending that labour alone can fix this,
- and a disciplined capital allocation approach.

But let me be equally clear: management restraint is not a substitute for structural change in the plants. Cutting executive costs alone will not close the gap.

## To the regional state government
I agree with you that reckless closures are not desirable and that the regional industrial base matters. But your position cannot become “preserve every site at any cost.”

If all sites are preserved unchanged, and if the company remains uncompetitive, then the result will be worse for the region in the medium term. The public interest is not served by a company that slowly loses money, investment capacity, and market position.

So yes, I am open to site-preserving solutions. But only if they deliver the necessary economic effect. If they don’t, we will be back here with a deeper crisis later.

## To the controlling family and investors
You are right to insist on value preservation and a credible path back to margins. I share that objective.

But let me also say this: value is not preserved by endlessly threatening conflict while refusing to design a workable alternative. We need a package that is implementable in the German governance environment. A plan that ignores the need for workforce consent will fail in practice, however attractive it looks on paper.

## What management needs in a deal

If we are to avoid closures, then I need to see a package with all of the following:

1. **A measurable and durable capacity reduction**
   - not just reshuffling volume,
   - not just shifting production around on paper.

2. **A real labour-cost contribution**
   - substantial enough to affect the cost base,
   - not symbolic.

3. **A credible structural mechanism**
   - voluntary departures, working-time redesign, redeployment, and productivity improvements,
   - but with enforceability.

4. **Investment and product commitments**
   - because restructuring without a future product plan is just shrinkage.

5. **No permanent blank cheque**
   - I will not accept a framework that makes later restructuring impossible if the market worsens.

## My minimum acceptable outcome

If we are being precise, management’s minimum acceptable outcome is this:

- durable reduction in overcapacity,
- measurable labour-cost improvement,
- no cosmetic compromise,
- and a framework that restores competitiveness through 2030.

That is the floor. Anything below that is not a deal.

## My maximum concession

I am open to:
- avoiding closures if the savings are truly equivalent,
- limiting compulsory redundancies if voluntary and structural measures are sufficient,
- site-specific solutions,
- phased implementation,
- and negotiated workforce contributions.

But I will not concede the core requirement: the restructuring must actually change the economics.

## Single most important condition

The single most important condition for management is this:

**The package must deliver a durable, enforceable reduction in the cost and capacity base.**

If the proposed “alternative” does not do that, then it is not a serious answer to the crisis.

## Bottom line

So let me be direct to the labour side:

- If you want to rule out closures and compulsory redundancies entirely, then you need to bring forward a package that achieves the same structural effect.
- If you cannot do that, then you are not protecting the company; you are protecting the status quo.
- And the status quo is exactly what this company can no longer afford.

I am prepared to negotiate hard on the instrument. I am **not** prepared to negotiate away the need for real structural change.
