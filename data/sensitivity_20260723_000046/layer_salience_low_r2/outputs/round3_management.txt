We have now heard a clear and repeated position from the labour side: **no closures, no compulsory redundancies, full site and job security**. I understand why you are drawing that line. I also understand the political and social logic behind it.

But I need to be equally clear: **that line does not solve the company’s structural problem unless you can replace closure-level savings with something truly equivalent.** So far, I have not heard a package that does that.

## Where I agree

I agree with the following:

- The crisis is real.
- A cosmetic deal would be worthless.
- The restructuring must be durable, not temporary.
- Investment in future products and technology is essential.
- Labour, management, owners, and the state all have to contribute in some form.
- A settlement that cannot be implemented in the German governance system will fail.

On that, we are not far apart.

## Where I disagree

### 1) “No closures” is not a solution by itself
You keep presenting site preservation as if it were an answer in itself. It is not.

If all German sites remain open, but the company still carries excess capacity, duplicated fixed costs, and an uncompetitive labour-cost base, then we have preserved structures we can no longer afford. That is not restructuring. That is postponement.

I am not saying closures are the only tool. I am saying they remain a legitimate and necessary option if the alternative does not deliver the same structural effect.

### 2) “No compulsory redundancies” is not credible as an absolute
You are asking the company to bind itself to zero forced separations while also rejecting the main mechanism that would remove capacity quickly. That would freeze adjustment risk almost entirely on management and shareholders.

I am willing to prioritize:
- attrition,
- retirement,
- voluntary exits,
- redeployment,
- and working-time redesign.

But I will not accept a framework that makes it impossible to right-size the business if the numbers require it.

### 3) Productivity and flexibility are not enough on their own
The union and works council keep listing:
- shift elimination,
- working-time flexibility,
- temporary pay moderation,
- redeployment,
- and productivity measures.

Those can help. They are not enough by themselves if the business has genuine overcapacity and structurally high fixed costs.

The expert was helpful here: **labor concessions do not automatically equal capacity reduction**. That is the central point. A cheaper but still oversized network is still a problem.

## To the works council

You say the burden of proof is on management. Fair enough. Here is the proof standard:

If you want to avoid closures, then you must bring forward a package that delivers:
- real capacity reduction,
- measurable annual savings,
- and durable improvement in the cost base.

Not vague promises. Not social symbolism. Not “we will work harder.”

Show me:
- which sites or lines will shrink,
- how much fixed cost comes out,
- how headcount falls,
- and how the savings persist beyond one bargaining cycle.

If that package is genuinely equivalent, I will consider it. If it is not, then “no closures” is just a slogan.

## To the union

You say labour can contribute but not by paying for management’s failure. I agree with the second part. Management must contribute. Owners must contribute. The top must show restraint.

But burden-sharing also means the workforce cannot insist on full protection while asking the company to absorb the entire adjustment elsewhere. That is not shared sacrifice.

A negotiated employee contribution is useful only if it is tied to hard structural measures. Without that, it is a temporary cash relief, not a turnaround.

## To the regional state government

You are right to stress the regional base and social legitimacy. I do not dismiss that. In fact, it is exactly why a weak compromise would be dangerous.

If we preserve every site unchanged while the business continues to underperform, we will end up with a weaker company, less investment, and eventually a deeper crisis for the region. That is not stewardship either.

So yes, I am open to site-preserving restructuring. But only if it produces the economics we need. The public interest is not served by locking in uncompetitive structures.

## To the family holding and investors

You are right to demand durable improvement and implementation certainty. I share that objective.

But I will also say this: a package that avoids hard decisions while claiming victory would damage value just as much as endless conflict. We need a result that the company can actually execute and that restores the margin trajectory through 2030.

That means:
- measurable capacity reduction,
- labor-cost improvement,
- capital discipline,
- and enough flexibility to adapt if the market worsens.

## What management is prepared to consider

I am prepared to consider **alternatives to closures** if, and only if, they meet the following conditions:

1. **Real capacity reduction**
   - not merely rearranging production on paper

2. **Durable labor-cost savings**
   - substantial enough to move the return-on-sales gap

3. **Enforceable implementation**
   - milestones, monitoring, and consequences if the package misses targets

4. **Meaningful burden-sharing**
   - from labor, management, and owners

5. **Investment commitments**
   - tied to the restructuring, not detached from it

6. **No permanent blank cheque**
   - the company must retain the ability to react later if the market changes further

## What I cannot accept

I cannot accept:
- a blanket “no closures ever” rule,
- a blanket “no compulsory redundancies ever” rule,
- full employment guarantees through 2030 regardless of market evolution,
- or a package that is economically soft but politically comfortable.

That would not restore competitiveness. It would merely delay the next crisis.

## My position in one sentence
I will support a non-closure solution **only if it is structurally equivalent, measurable, and durable**.

If it is not, then I will not pretend otherwise.

## Bottom line
I am not retreating from the need for deep restructuring. I am not retreating from the need to cut overcapacity and costs. And I am not retreating from the fact that closures remain on the table if they are required to achieve that.

If the labour side wants to rule them out, it must do more than repeat “no closures.” It must produce a package that **actually fixes the economics**.

Until then, my position stands.
