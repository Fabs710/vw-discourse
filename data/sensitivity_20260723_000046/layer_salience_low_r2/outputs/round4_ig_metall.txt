I will be equally direct.

## Where I agree
I agree with the works council, the state, and the expert on the essentials:

- this crisis is real,
- a cosmetic deal would fail,
- the restructuring must be durable,
- and there must be **real capacity reduction** and **real fixed-cost reduction**.

I also agree that burden-sharing cannot stop at the shop floor. Management, owners, and capital must contribute too. If they do not, then this is not restructuring — it is shifting the pain onto workers.

## Where I disagree
I do **not** accept the jump from “real restructuring is necessary” to “therefore closures and compulsory redundancies must remain normal options.”

That is the line I cannot cross.

A plant closure is not just another adjustment tool. It is permanent industrial destruction. Compulsory redundancies are not a neutral instrument. They break trust, weaken the collective wage framework, and make the workforce bear the adjustment risk alone.

So my position remains:

- **no plant closures**
- **no compulsory redundancies**
- **no dismantling of the collective bargaining framework**
- **no settlement that forces workers to pay for the crisis while management and owners keep full freedom of action**

## To management
You are right about one thing: the package must be economically credible. But credibility is not the same as having closures available as a threat.

If you want labour cooperation, then show a **plant-by-plant non-closure plan** that achieves the expert’s threshold through:
- shift elimination,
- product and volume consolidation,
- working-time changes,
- voluntary exits,
- redeployment,
- management overhead cuts,
- and negotiated employee contributions.

If you cannot present that, then your closure argument is not proof — it is leverage.

And let me be clear: we will not accept a “conditional” arrangement that simply keeps the closure weapon in reserve and later says the workforce failed the test. That is not a fair deal; it is a trapdoor.

## To the controlling family and investors
You keep speaking about value, competitiveness, and flexibility. Fine. But long-term value does not come from breaking the workforce’s trust and turning German industrial capacity into a disposable variable.

A company that destroys its own labour relations and industrial base may improve the short-term optics, but it becomes more fragile, not stronger.

If you want value preservation, then support a restructuring that is:
- site-preserving,
- measurable,
- burden-sharing,
- and enforceable without forced layoffs.

## To the regional state government
I appreciate that you reject closures as a starting point. But that has to become practical support for a site-preserving settlement, not just a political statement.

If the state is serious, then it should back a deal that:
- keeps German plants open,
- avoids compulsory redundancies,
- and commits to investment and regional industrial stability.

The state should not be a witness to managed dismantling.

## To the works council
We are aligned on the red lines. I also support your insistence that a deal has to be quantified plant by plant.

Where I would sharpen the point is this: we should not allow management to redefine “equivalent savings” in a way that quietly legitimizes closures by another name.

The benchmark must be transparent and objective:
- specific plant actions,
- specific savings,
- specific headcount effects,
- specific investment commitments,
- and a real failure trigger only if the agreed package itself is not implemented.

But that failure trigger cannot be a backdoor for immediate closure rights. If there is a review, it must first test whether the agreed non-closure package is being executed faithfully — not whether management has changed its mind and wants to reopen the same threat.

## On the expert’s assessment
The expert’s contribution is useful because it shows the scale of what a non-closure package must achieve:
- real capacity reduction,
- durable savings,
- and measurable headcount adjustment.

That does **not** prove closures are unavoidable. It proves that a serious alternative must be serious in industrial terms.

That is exactly what we are demanding.

## What I am prepared to support
I am prepared to support a package that includes:
- working-time flexibility,
- temporary negotiated labor-cost contributions,
- voluntary exit and retirement instruments,
- redeployment,
- capacity consolidation,
- product-line simplification,
- and verified management/owner contributions,

provided that:
- all German plants remain open,
- no one is forced out,
- the collective wage framework is preserved,
- and the package is backed by investment commitments.

That is a fair burden-sharing deal.

## What I will not support
I will not support:
- closures disguised as optimization,
- forced redundancies disguised as flexibility,
- or a “conditional” guarantee that gives management a ready-made escape hatch.

The workforce should not sign a blank check and hope for the best.

## My minimum acceptable outcome
- every German plant remains open,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- genuine co-determination,
- preserved collective bargaining standards,
- and a negotiated restructuring package that shares burdens fairly.

## My maximum concession
- working-time flexibility,
- voluntary departure paths,
- redeployment,
- temporary cost moderation,
- productivity measures,
- and capacity reduction through reorganization — **as long as jobs and sites are protected**.

## My single most important condition
It has not changed:

**No closures and no forced layoffs.**

If that condition is broken, there is no agreement from the union side.

## Bottom line
We are ready for a hard restructuring deal. We are not ready to endorse one that makes workers absorb the entire cost while management and owners retain all options.

If the other side wants a settlement, it must be a real one:
- site-preserving,
- quantified,
- fair,
- and enforceable without breaking the employment framework.

If not, then they should not expect consent — only resistance.
