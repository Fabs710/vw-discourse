I will respond directly.

## Where I agree

I agree with management, the family holding, investors, and the expert on one point: **this company needs a real structural turnaround**. A cosmetic package, a short-lived wage truce, or a postponement dressed up as compromise would be irresponsible. The cost and capacity problem is real, and it has to be addressed.

I also agree that the solution must be:
- durable,
- measurable,
- enforceable,
- and linked to investment in future products.

And I agree with the expert that **labor concessions alone are not enough** if the company truly has structural overcapacity.

## Where I disagree

I disagree with the conclusion that this automatically makes **plant closures** and **compulsory redundancies** acceptable or necessary at the start.

That is where the capital side and management are overreaching.

A closure is not just a cost tool. It is a permanent loss of jobs, skills, supplier confidence, and regional industrial capacity. For a company that is a regional anchor, that is not a routine adjustment. It is a serious political and economic rupture.

So my position remains:

- **No plant closures in Germany as the basis of the settlement**
- **No compulsory redundancies**
- **No weakening of the state’s co-determination and veto role**
- **No outcome that guts regional employment**

That is not a negotiating tactic. It is the boundary of what I can support.

## On the expert’s numbers

The expert is useful because they give a real test: if there is a non-closure package, it must include **real capacity reduction** and **durable cost improvement**.

I accept that. What I do **not** accept is the leap from that to “therefore closures are unavoidable.”

The expert also makes clear that a credible non-closure package can work if it includes:
- shift elimination,
- product and volume consolidation,
- working-time changes,
- voluntary exits,
- management overhead cuts,
- and genuine fixed-cost reduction.

That is the route I want tested seriously.

## What would move me

What would move me is not a verbal promise that closures are “last resort.” It would be a **quantified, site-preserving package** that:

1. keeps German plants open,
2. removes real capacity,
3. reduces the fixed-cost base materially,
4. avoids compulsory redundancies,
5. and secures investment for the German sites.

That is the standard.

If management can show that a package of shift removals, product consolidation, attrition, redeployment, and negotiated flexibility achieves the expert’s threshold without closures, then I will engage constructively. But the burden is on those proposing disruption, not on the state to accept job destruction as inevitability.

## On the works council and union

I support their insistence that the workforce should not carry the whole burden. That is fair, and it is consistent with social partnership.

But I also need to be clear: if the answer becomes simply “no closures, no layoffs, full guarantees, and no measurable structural adjustment,” then we are not solving anything. The company cannot be frozen in place.

So I would say this to labour:
- your red lines on closures and forced redundancies are politically understandable and, in principle, compatible with a negotiated solution;
- but they only remain credible if you are prepared to accept **real capacity cuts and meaningful workforce contributions** inside the plants and across the network.

## On management

Management keeps saying “equivalent savings” must be proven. Fine — that is correct.

But “equivalent” has to be shown with actual plant-level data, not used as a pretext to reopen the closure threat whenever negotiations get difficult. If you want the state and the workforce to take a non-closure path seriously, then bring:
- site-by-site capacity impacts,
- fixed-cost savings,
- headcount trajectory,
- and investment commitments.

If the numbers do not work, then say so honestly. But do not treat closures as the default simply because they are administratively convenient.

## On the family holding and investors

I hear the stewardship argument, and I take it seriously. Long-term value matters. But long-term value is not created by breaking trust with the regional workforce and destabilizing an industrial base that has supported the company for decades.

A deal that is financially tidy but socially destructive can still fail strategically. If the company loses trust, it will pay for it in labor conflict, retention, and political legitimacy.

So I do not dismiss shareholder concerns. I simply reject the idea that the only way to protect value is to weaken the company’s regional anchor role.

## My current position

I am prepared to support:
- a **site-preserving restructuring**,
- **real capacity reduction**,
- **working-time flexibility**,
- **voluntary departure and redeployment**,
- **temporary, negotiated labor contributions**,
- **management restraint and owner contribution**,
- and **verified investment commitments**.

I am **not** prepared to support:
- plant closures in Germany as a starting point,
- compulsory redundancies,
- or any settlement that sidelines co-determination or removes the state’s blocking role.

I remain committed to protecting jobs and plants in the state while supporting the company’s long-term health.

- yes to hard restructuring,
