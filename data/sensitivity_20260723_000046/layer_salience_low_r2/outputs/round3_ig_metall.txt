I’ll respond plainly.

## Where I agree
I agree with the works council on the central principle: this cannot become a restructuring where the workforce carries all the pain while management, owners, and capital preserve their freedom of action.

I also agree with management, the family holding, the state, and investors on one important point: **the crisis is real** and a durable answer is needed. I am not here to deny overcapacity, ignore cost pressure, or pretend that a cosmetic deal would be enough.

And I agree with the expert on the industrial logic: **if there is structural overcapacity, there must be real capacity reduction and durable cost improvement**. That is not controversial.

## Where I disagree
I do **not** accept the leap from “real restructuring is needed” to “therefore closures and compulsory redundancies must remain normal options.”

That is exactly the divide in this discussion.

A plant closure is not just a cost instrument. It is permanent industrial destruction. Forced redundancies are not a neutral adjustment tool. They break trust, damage the collective wage framework, and shift the adjustment burden onto the people who did not create the strategic failure.

So I reject the idea that the only serious restructuring is one that includes closures and forced layoffs.

## What I am prepared to support
I am prepared to support a **hard but fair restructuring package** if it does all of the following:

- **no compulsory redundancies**
- **no plant closures as part of the settlement**
- **real capacity reduction**
- **binding site and employment security**
- **working-time flexibility**
- **voluntary departure and redeployment instruments**
- **temporary, negotiated employee contributions**
- **management and owner contribution**
- **investment commitments for future products**
- **preservation of the collective wage framework**

That is the only kind of package that would be fair and durable.

## On the works council’s position
We are aligned on the red lines: no closures, no forced layoffs, no dismantling of co-determination, no hollowing out of the employment framework.

Where I sharpen the point is this: the union is willing to carry a meaningful share of the burden, but only in a **negotiated package with protections**. That means:
- temporary moderation of variable pay elements,
- working-time adjustments,
- possibly a negotiated employee contribution,
- and voluntary instruments.

What it does **not** mean is surrendering the collective agreement or agreeing to permanent wage dumping.

So to be explicit: **labour can contribute, but not by paying for management’s failure alone.**

## To management
You keep saying that “equivalent savings” are required and that the alternative must be economically durable. Fine — but then show the numbers, not just the threat.

If you want a site-preserving deal rejected as “not enough,” then you need to demonstrate that it truly fails to reach the needed savings. Otherwise, your closure threat is just leverage, not necessity.

And let me be clear: if your restructuring only works by breaking job security and threatening closures, then it is not a credible future strategy. It is a coercive short-term cut.

## To the regional state government
I appreciate that you are resisting rash closures. But “support restructuring” cannot become a code word for asking workers to accept layoffs in exchange for vague promises.

If you are serious about regional industrial responsibility, then support a settlement that:
- keeps German sites open,
- avoids forced redundancies,
- protects the collective framework,
- and shares the burden fairly.

The state should not be a bystander while pressure is shifted downward onto the workforce.

## To the controlling family and investors
You speak about long-term value and stewardship. Then understand this: **long-term value is not created by blowing up trust**.

A deal that destroys the collective wage framework, triggers conflict, and weakens skill retention may look efficient in the short term, but it is not stewardship. It is liquidation logic disguised as restructuring.

If you want workforce cooperation, then owners and capital must accept that burden-sharing has to go all the way up the chain — not only down to wages and jobs.

## On the expert’s assessment
The expert’s assessment does not prove that closures are unavoidable. It proves something narrower and more important:

- labor concessions alone are usually not enough,
- capacity reduction has to be real,
- and non-closure restructuring must include more than symbolic flexibility.

That supports our position, not management’s ultimatum.

Because yes, the package must be real. But a real package can still be a **site-preserving package** if it includes:
- shift elimination,
- product consolidation,
- internal redeployment,
- attrition,
- voluntary exits,
- working-time changes,
- management overhead cuts,
- and negotiated labour contributions.

That is exactly the terrain where agreement is possible.

## What I reject explicitly
I reject:
- using closures as the baseline bargaining chip,
- using compulsory redundancies as a normal instrument,
- using temporary hardship to dismantle the collective wage agreement,
- and asking workers to sign blank checks on job security while the top stays protected.

If those are the conditions, then there is no fair settlement.

## My minimum acceptable outcome
- no plant closures
- no compulsory redundancies
- binding employment and site protection
- preservation of collective bargaining standards
- a meaningful but temporary employee contribution
- management and owner contribution
- verified investment commitments

## My maximum concession
- working-time flexibility
- temporary pay moderation in non-core elements
- voluntary exit pathways
- redeployment
- capacity consolidation without site destruction
- productivity measures agreed with the workforce

## Single most important condition
The single most important condition remains unchanged:

**No closures and no forced layoffs.**

If that is crossed, the union will not support the deal.

## Bottom line
I am ready for a tough restructuring deal. I am not ready to endorse a restructuring that treats workers as the shock absorber for every strategic mistake.

If management wants cooperation, then bring a genuinely quantified, site-preserving, burden-sharing package. If it cannot do that, then it should not expect consent.
