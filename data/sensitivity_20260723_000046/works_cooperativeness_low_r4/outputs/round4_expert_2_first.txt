Below is a restructuring-finance comparison of the two paths, using only the facts and assumptions stated in the record. I am **not** opining on labor relations, legal issues, or politics.

## 1) Framing

The economic question is not “which option has the bigger gross savings?” but:

> **Which option has the higher risk-adjusted NPV after one-time charges, implementation timing, disruption/strike risk, and margin recovery?**

Because the record does not provide hard euro figures, the assessment must be **scenario-based** and **directional**.

---

## 2) Core finance logic

### Alternative A: Non-closure package
Typical components:
- temporary wage moderation / bonus restraint
- overtime reduction and shift flexibility
- voluntary exits / attrition / redeployment
- qualification / internal transfers
- management overhead cuts
- owner contribution / reduced distributions
- investment commitments

### Alternative B: Closure-based restructuring
Typical components:
- plant closure(s)
- capacity reduction
- compulsory redundancies and/or larger workforce reduction
- one-time closure, severance, logistics, and remediation charges
- longer implementation and higher conflict risk
- potentially larger structural savings, but with higher execution risk

---

## 3) Key economic differences

### A. One-time charges
**Closure-based restructuring is almost certainly more capital-intensive upfront.**
Likely one-time charges include:
- severance and separation costs
- asset write-downs / impairments
- site shutdown and remediation costs
- logistics, relocation, and supply-chain reconfiguration
- potential pension / contractual settlement costs

A non-closure package generally has:
- lower one-time cash charges
- more gradual, lower-severity implementation costs
- lower impairment risk

**Finance conclusion:** on upfront cash and reported charges, the non-closure path is usually superior.

---

### B. Disruption / strike risk
This is the main offset.

The record indicates:
- labor peace has ended
- strike action is possible
- closures are strongly opposed
- implementation conflict could be material

For a closure-based plan, the company faces:
- higher probability of warning strikes or prolonged conflict
- production losses during negotiation and execution
- supplier and launch disruption
- management distraction
- reputational and political friction

A non-closure deal is not disruption-free, but the strike risk is usually **lower** if it preserves sites and avoids compulsory layoffs.

**Finance conclusion:** closure-based savings must be discounted for a meaningful probability of industrial disruption. In many cases, that risk is large enough to erode a substantial part of headline savings.

---

### C. Timing of savings and margin recovery
This is where closure plans often look best on paper but can underperform in NPV terms.

#### Non-closure package
- savings usually start earlier
- implementation is more incremental
- margin recovery can begin sooner if working-time, overtime, and wage measures are executed quickly
- but total savings may be smaller and may taper if concessions are temporary

#### Closure-based restructuring
- headline savings may be larger
- but realization is slower because of:
  - consultation / board / political process
  - shutdown lead times
  - transfer of production
  - employee attrition timing
  - ramp-down and ramp-up issues at surviving sites

**Finance conclusion:** if closure savings arrive later by 12–24 months, the NPV penalty can be material even if gross annual savings are larger.

---

## 4) Scenario-based NPV assessment

Because we lack euros, the right way to compare is by **risk-adjusted relative value**.

### Scenario 1: Non-closure package succeeds materially
Assumptions:
- achieves a large share of the needed cost reduction
- no major strike
- savings begin within 0–12 months
- investment commitments support productivity and margin recovery
- employee contributions are time-limited but meaningful

**Likely result:**  
Non-closure package has the **higher NPV** if it closes most of the gap and avoids major disruption.

Why:
- lower one-time charges
- faster savings realization
- lower execution risk
- less destruction of operating continuity

---

### Scenario 2: Non-closure package only partially closes the gap
Assumptions:
- delivers perhaps a “substantial share” of closure-level savings, but not enough
- contributions are temporary or too shallow
- margin recovery stalls

**Likely result:**  
Non-closure may still beat closures **if** the closure option is heavily penalized by disruption and delayed realization.  
But if the residual gap is large and persistent, the company may remain structurally underperforming.

**Finance conclusion:** this is the break-point scenario. The economic decision turns on the size of the residual gap and how fast it must be closed.

---

### Scenario 3: Closure-based restructuring proceeds with significant conflict
Assumptions:
- closures trigger strikes / warning strikes
- output losses occur during transition
- implementation is delayed
- some projected savings are offset by conflict and restart costs

**Likely result:**  
The NPV advantage of closures can be sharply reduced or eliminated, especially if the conflict persists for multiple quarters.

**Finance conclusion:** headline savings are not enough; the “all-in” net present value can become inferior to a negotiated non-closure deal.

---

### Scenario 4: Closure-based restructuring proceeds cleanly
Assumptions:
- limited strike disruption
- rapid shutdown and transfer execution
- savings begin on schedule
- surviving sites absorb volume efficiently

**Likely result:**  
This is the only scenario where closures are likely to dominate on NPV if the structural savings are materially larger than the non-closure package.

**But** the record suggests this is not the base case because labor and governance resistance is strong.

---

## 5) Discount rate sensitivity

This matters a lot.

### Higher discount rate:
- favors earlier savings
- penalizes delayed restructuring benefits
- therefore can favor the non-closure package if it starts saving sooner

### Lower discount rate:
- makes larger long-term savings more valuable
- can favor closure-based restructuring if those savings are durable and eventually realized

**Practical implication:**  
If the firm is under near-term margin pressure, earlier non-closure savings may be more valuable than larger but delayed closure savings.

---

## 6) Relative ranking, based on the record

### Non-closure package
Pros:
- lower one-time charges
- lower risk of strike/disruption
- faster implementation
- better continuity of production and know-how
- better NPV if it closes most of the gap

Cons:
- may not fully eliminate structural overcapacity
- savings may be insufficient unless contributions are deep and durable
- risk of “temporary” measures not translating into permanent margin recovery

### Closure-based restructuring
Pros:
- larger gross structural savings potential
- clearer capacity reset
- stronger effect on cost base if executed without major disruption

Cons:
- high upfront charges
- substantial execution and strike risk
- slower realization
- potential for value destruction during conflict
- NPV highly sensitive to implementation delay and production losses

---

## 7) Expert assessment

### My judgment, within restructuring finance:
**A well-structured non-closure package is likely to have the higher near-term risk-adjusted NPV unless management can prove that the residual savings gap after non-closure measures is both large and unrecoverable otherwise.**

Put differently:
- **If non-closure measures can credibly cover most of the gap and start early, they are usually superior on NPV.**
- **If the residual gap remains large, closure-based restructuring can become superior, but only if strike/disruption risk is contained and implementation is timely.**

Given the stated governance structure and labor opposition, I would assign a **meaningful discount** to closure-based savings for:
- disruption risk
- timing delay
- implementation complexity

That discount may be large enough that a negotiated non-closure settlement wins on NPV even if its headline savings are smaller.
