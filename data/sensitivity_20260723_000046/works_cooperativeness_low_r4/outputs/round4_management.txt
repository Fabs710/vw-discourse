I’ll be equally direct.

## To the works council and union
You are right about one thing: **a cosmetic deal is worthless**. I will not sign up to a paper exercise that leaves the company structurally uncompetitive.

But I reject your attempt to make **“no closures, no compulsory redundancies, no guarantees”** the starting and ending point of the conversation. That is not a restructuring offer. It is a demand that management solve a deep overcapacity problem while giving up the only hard levers that create durable savings.

You keep saying closures are a “delayed threat.” They are not a tactic; they are a **real consequence** if the economics do not add up. If you want to keep every site open, then you need to show a package that is **actually sufficient** — not just politically preferable.

And no, binding site and job guarantees through 2030 are not compatible with the kind of structural correction we need unless the workforce contribution is material enough to offset the lost flexibility. So far, I do **not** see that.

## To the state government
I respect your political constraints, but I do **not** accept a blanket anti-closure position as economically neutral. It is not neutral. It shifts the burden onto the rest of the company and risks locking in an uncompetitive footprint.

If you want to use your blocking position responsibly, then help deliver a **quantified** package that closes the gap. Not slogans, not “last resort,” but a real solution that materially improves margins and capacity. If that cannot be done without structural cuts, then you are not solving the crisis — you are postponing it.

## To the controlling family holding
You are right to insist that the business needs a durable reset. But durability is exactly why I am not willing to accept a package that preserves too much of the old structure.

Your position should not be “management must do something,” but “management must secure the economics.” I agree. And the economics here still require a serious cost and capacity reset. If the employee side wants to avoid closures, then it must offer more than temporary concessions and a promise of good behavior.

## To investors
You are correctly focused on risk-adjusted value preservation. I agree with that framing.

What I do not accept is the idea that waiting for perfect labor peace is cheaper than decisive restructuring. It often is not. A weak compromise that avoids hard decisions can destroy value for years by leaving the company stuck with excess fixed cost, low margins, and no room to invest.

The expert analysis was clear: non-closure measures may cover part of the gap, often **40–70%** of closure-level savings. That is useful, but it is not automatically enough. The remaining gap matters. If it is large, then structural reductions stay on the table.

## Where I agree with the expert
The expert was useful because it clarified the point we have been making:
- non-closure measures can create real savings,
- but they do **not** automatically equal the savings from footprint reduction,
- and the implementation and conflict costs of closures must be counted.

That supports a negotiated path **only if** the negotiated path is economically sufficient. It does not validate a no-closure veto.

## My position on the “review trigger” idea
I do **not** accept a review clause that functionally becomes a permanent escape hatch from restructuring. But I also do not need a backdoor threat if the agreement is robust.

A review mechanism can make sense only if it measures:
- actual cost reduction achieved,
- capacity reduction delivered,
- management/owner contributions completed,
- investment commitments honored,
- and margin recovery against target.

What it cannot do is simply freeze reality and ban any structural consequence forever. If the savings gap remains after the negotiated measures, then we are back to the same hard question: where does the missing adjustment come from?

## What I will accept
I remain open to a **site-preserving package** if — and only if — it delivers genuinely material, durable savings. That means:
- quantified labor-cost contribution,
- lower overtime and shift-cost burden,
- voluntary exits and redeployment,
- overhead cuts,
- management contribution,
- owner contribution,
- and capacity reduction that is real, not symbolic.

But I will not pretend that this can be done with guarantees that remove all flexibility while the cost base stays uncompetitive. That is not serious.

## What I will not accept
I will not accept:
- a no-change outcome,
- a cosmetic burden-sharing package,
- a settlement that preserves overcapacity,
- or a deal that forces the company to keep paying a structural cost premium through 2030.

And I will not accept the idea that closures are illegitimate in principle. They are not desirable. They are not my first choice. But they remain available if the alternatives do not close the economics.

## Bottom line
The discussion is now simple:

- If you can bring me a **quantified non-closure package** that materially closes the cost and capacity gap, I will consider it.
- If you cannot, then we must keep structural reduction on the table.
- What I will not do is promise security first and hope competitiveness follows later.

That is not a responsible executive position. Competitiveness comes first because without it there is no investment, no product future, and no long-term employment base to protect.
