I will be direct.

## To management
You are still treating **closures and forced layoffs** as the benchmark against which everything else is judged. I reject that framing.

The expert analysis did **not** prove that closures are necessary. It showed only that closures can produce larger headline savings, while also carrying much higher conflict, execution, and transition risk. That is not a blank cheque for plant closure. It is a warning that **all costs must be counted**.

If you want a serious settlement, then stop asking the workforce to pre-accept its own destruction as “economic realism.”  
We have already put a constructive alternative on the table:
- temporary wage moderation,
- bonus restraint,
- reduced overtime,
- shift flexibility,
- voluntary exits,
- redeployment,
- qualification,
- internal transfers,
- and capacity reduction without plant shutdowns.

What you have **not** done is prove, measure by measure, that this cannot close the gap.

### Our response to your “review trigger later” idea
No. A review trigger that leaves closures in reserve is **not** acceptable.  
That is not a settlement; it is a delayed threat. It would let management squeeze concessions now and reopen closure demands later. We will not sign up to that.

If there is to be a review, it can only assess:
- whether the negotiated measures were implemented,
- whether management and owners delivered their contributions,
- whether investment commitments were honored.

It must **not** be a backdoor to plant closures or compulsory redundancies.

## To the works council
We are aligned on the essentials:
- no plant closures in Germany,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- no dismantling of the collective wage framework,
- no permanent wage dumping.

I support your insistence on binding protection. That is the only way to create trust after months of threats.

Where I would sharpen the position is this: we should not let anyone turn “review” into a weapon. If the agreement is conditional in a way that reintroduces closure pressure later, then the workforce is not protected. So yes to transparent monitoring; no to reopening the closure question.

## To the regional state government
You say you reject closures and forced layoffs. Good. Then use your blocking position accordingly.

I do **not** want political statements about “last resort.” I want you to help secure:
- binding site protection,
- binding job security through 2030,
- meaningful owner and management contributions,
- and a negotiated restructuring path that keeps German plants open.

If the state shareholder wants to be credible, it must do more than express concern. It must help prevent management from using closures as leverage.

## To the controlling family holding
You are right about one thing: we cannot preserve the old structure unchanged and hope for the best.  
But you are wrong if you think the only “serious” path is to normalize plant closures.

If the family truly wants long-term value, it should stop legitimizing a management strategy that undermines trust and industrial peace. The company’s value depends on:
- skilled workers,
- stable production,
- social partnership,
- and predictable wage relations.

Closing plants and threatening forced redundancies are not signs of discipline. They are signs that management wants the workforce to absorb all the pain while capital keeps its options open.

Use your influence to support a site-preserving deal.

## To investors
You keep saying “risk-adjusted value preservation.” Then apply that logic honestly.

A closure-based strategy is not automatically superior if it triggers:
- warning strikes or longer conflict,
- production disruption,
- loss of know-how,
- damaged supplier relations,
- political backlash,
- and years of industrial mistrust.

That is not a theoretical risk. It is exactly what this confrontation is already moving toward.

If you want a truly investable solution, then a **non-closure package with real burden sharing** is the better path:
- it is faster,
- less disruptive,
- more reversible,
- and more likely to preserve brand and production stability.

But it must be fair. And fairness means:
- management contributes,
- owners contribute,
- and the workforce’s contribution is **time-limited and matched** by security.

## Our line on burden sharing
We are prepared to contribute. We have said that repeatedly. But we will not carry the whole burden.

That means we are open to:
- temporary wage moderation,
- bonus restraint,
- reduced overtime premiums,
- flexible working-time arrangements,
- voluntary exit schemes,
- redeployment,
- training and qualification,
- and targeted capacity adjustments.

We are **not** open to:
- plant closures,
- compulsory redundancies,
- permanent wage dumping,
- or dismantling job and site guarantees.

If management wants savings, it must also accept:
- overhead cuts at top level,
- reduced distributions,
- and visible owner contribution.

## On the numbers
The expert confirmed what we have been saying:
- non-closure measures can deliver real savings,
- often a substantial share of closure-level savings,
- and the comparison must include conflict and execution costs.

That means the burden of proof is still on management. If they believe the residual gap is too large, then they must show:
1. exactly what the gap is,
2. exactly what each non-closure measure contributes,
3. exactly what management/owner contributions are included,
4. and exactly why that still is not enough.

Until that is shown transparently, we will not accept closures as “necessary.”

## What we can support now
A real settlement could include:
- no closures in Germany,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- binding job and site guarantees through 2030,
- a time-limited employee contribution,
- management overhead reductions,
- owner support through reinvestment,
- investment commitments for German plants,
- and a jointly monitored savings bridge.

That is a serious restructuring.  
What we will not accept is a settlement that uses “review” as a trapdoor back to closures.

## Final position
Let me be absolutely clear:

- We support restructuring.
- We support negotiated savings.
- We support fair burden sharing.
- We support binding investment commitments.
- We support capacity reduction through non-destructive means.

But we do **not** support:
- plant closures,
- compulsory redundancies,
- or any agreement that leaves those as an open threat over the workforce.

If management and investors refuse that, then there is no genuine compromise on offer — only a demand that workers pay for the crisis with their jobs and sites. We will oppose that with all available collective-bargaining means.
