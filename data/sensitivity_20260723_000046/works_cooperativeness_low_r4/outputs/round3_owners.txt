I will be direct.

The expert comparison is useful, but it does **not** move me to the employee side’s position. It confirms that a non-closure package can deliver meaningful savings — but also that it usually falls short of the full structural correction if the overcapacity is deep. That is exactly why I cannot endorse a blanket no-closure stance as the final answer.

## Where I agree
I agree with all sides on the need for:
- a **durable** restructuring, not a symbolic one,
- **quantified** savings,
- **investment commitments**,
- and avoidance of a prolonged conflict that would destroy value.

I also agree that voluntary exits, redeployment, qualification, working-time flexibility, and internal transfers should be used first wherever they genuinely produce savings.

## Where I disagree

### To the Works Council and the union
Your red lines are understandable, but they are not enough by themselves.

You keep saying “no closures, no compulsory redundancies” and asking management to prove otherwise. The expert has now shown the problem: non-closure packages can often cover **part** of the gap, not always all of it. So a no-closure position can only be credible if it comes with **specific, large, durable, enforceable savings**.

I do **not** see that yet.

I also reject the framing that closures are somehow illegitimate by definition. They are not desirable, but if the economics truly require a permanent footprint reduction, then refusing to consider them is not protecting jobs — it is risking a larger crisis later.

What I will not support is:
- a deal that preserves every site and every guarantee,
- while producing only marginal or temporary savings,
- and leaving the company structurally weak.

### To management
I agree with you that the current footprint cannot be preserved unchanged. But I am **not** giving you a blank cheque for closures either.

You still need to show, measure by measure:
- the savings from non-closure tools,
- the savings gap that remains,
- the timing of those savings,
- the one-time costs of each option,
- and the impact on margins and competitiveness.

If you cannot show that the non-closure path falls materially short, then closure is not justified. If you can show it, then the restructuring must remain strong enough to actually restore competitiveness.

That means I am not aligned with a closure-first narrative, but I am equally not aligned with a no-change outcome.

### To the regional state government
I understand your political and regional constraints. But I cannot support a position that turns the state’s blocking power into an unconditional shield against structural adjustment.

If you want to block closures, then you must help produce a package that is **economically sufficient** without them. “Closures only as a last resort” is not a complete answer unless the last resort threshold is clearly tied to numbers.

### To investors
You are right to focus on capital value and return on sales. But you are too quick if you treat headline closure savings as automatically superior once transition costs are ignored.

The real question is net economics:
- savings,
- disruption,
- execution risk,
- strike risk,
- political backlash,
- and long-term brand damage.

A closure-based plan may win on paper and lose in execution. But that does not make the no-closure plan acceptable by default either. It has to be big enough.

## My position
I will support a negotiated settlement only if it contains all of the following:
- **material, quantified, durable savings**
- **real capacity reduction**
- **a substantial employee contribution**
- **visible management and owner contribution**
- **binding investment commitments**
- **a credible path back to target margins**
- **preference for voluntary and negotiated measures first**

I am **not** prepared to endorse:
- a cosmetic compromise,
- a package that leaves overcapacity largely intact,
- or an outcome that permanently weakens the enterprise’s competitiveness.

## What would move me
What could move me is not rhetoric, but a serious, side-by-side package with numbers:
1. a full non-closure scenario,
2. a residual gap after all non-destructive measures,
3. and a clear explanation of why that gap cannot be closed without structural reduction.

If the employee side can truly close that gap with a stable, enforceable agreement, I will support it.  
If not, then the company must retain the ability to make structural cuts.

So my answer is: **I back a negotiated solution, but not a no-closure veto.**
