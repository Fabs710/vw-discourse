The expert analysis confirms what management has been saying all along: **a non-closure package can help, but it is not automatically equivalent to the structural reset we need**.

## Where I agree
I agree with the works council and union on one point:  
**we need durable, quantified restructuring, not a cosmetic deal.**  
I also agree that **voluntary departures, redeployment, qualification, working-time adjustments, and internal transfers** are part of the solution. And yes, any package must include **investment commitments** and a clear route back to sustainable margins.

## Where I disagree
What I do **not** accept is the attempt to turn “no closures, no compulsory redundancies” into a universal veto over the company’s future.

The expert said it plainly:
- non-closure restructuring typically achieves **only part** of the savings of a closure-based package,
- often **40–70%** of closure-level savings,
- while closure-based restructuring delivers **larger and more durable cost and capacity reductions**.

That is the central issue.  
We are not debating slogans. We are debating whether the company can actually restore competitiveness at the scale required.

### To the works council
You keep saying “show us the numbers.” Fair enough. The numbers are now in front of us, and they cut both ways.

Yes, non-closure measures can produce meaningful savings.  
No, that does **not** prove they close the gap fully.

If you want to preserve every German plant, then you must accept one of two things:
1. materially deeper employee contributions than you have yet put forward, or  
2. a lower level of structural savings than the company needs.

I cannot accept either outcome if it leaves us uncompetitive through 2030.

Your proposal still relies heavily on:
- temporary wage moderation,
- bonus restraint,
- overtime reduction,
- voluntary exits,
- redeployment,
- and training.

Those are useful tools, but they are **not enough by themselves** if the footprint remains too large for the market. They do not fully remove fixed cost. They do not fully correct overcapacity. And they do not guarantee that the cost base will be competitive in a shrinking European market.

### To the union
You argue that closure-based restructuring is more destructive and therefore not justified. I understand the concern, but you are still missing the commercial reality: **a business can be destroyed by preserving too much of the old structure as surely as by closing a plant.**

I also reject the implication that management is somehow refusing to count conflict costs. Of course we count them. A prolonged strike would hurt output, reputation, and margins. That is exactly why I am pressing for a serious settlement now.

But conflict cost does **not** make an insufficient restructuring acceptable.

If your answer is “avoid closures at any price,” then you are asking management to sacrifice long-term competitiveness to preserve a footprint that the market may no longer support. That is not responsible bargaining.

### To the state government
You say closures should not be the default. I can accept that.  
But I cannot accept a position that turns your blocking power into a blanket shield against necessary industrial adjustment.

If the economics require structural cuts, then the state cannot simply say no and expect the rest of us to solve the problem with accounting tricks.

What I need from you is not a veto posture, but a willingness to support a **serious negotiated package** that includes:
- real capacity reduction,
- a genuine labour-cost contribution,
- investment commitments,
- and, if necessary, site-specific adjustment.

If you rule out closures categorically, you are risking a compromise that looks socially nicer but leaves the company structurally weaker.

### To the family holding
You are right that endless conflict would destroy value. But the same is true of pretending the current footprint can be sustained without hard adjustment.

I agree that we should not start with a closure-first narrative.  
But I will not support a settlement that leaves the company trapped in a high-cost structure just because closures are politically difficult.

### To investors
You are also right to focus on capital value and return-on-sales. But I will not accept the framing that the only credible path is the harshest one.

What the expert has shown is that the question is not “closures or nothing.”  
The real question is whether a non-closure package can deliver **enough** savings, **durably**, with **enforceable commitments**.

So here is my position:
- If the non-closure package can deliver the required structural savings, I am open to it.
- If it cannot, then structural reductions including closures remain on the table.

I am not going to pretend otherwise.

## What would move me
I would be prepared to shift only if the employee side brought a package that is not just principled, but **economically sufficient**. That would need to include:
- quantified labor-cost reductions at a material scale,
- durable capacity reduction, not just deferrals,
- binding flexibility on shifts and utilization,
- measurable management and owner contributions,
- and a credible investment path tied to the new cost base.

If those conditions are met, then I will back a negotiated solution.  
If they are not, then I will not endorse a deal that protects every current structure while leaving the business uncompetitive.

## Bottom line
The expert analysis strengthens the case for negotiation — but it does **not** validate a blanket no-closure position.

My position remains:
- **We need deep, durable cost and capacity reduction**
- **We prefer negotiated instruments first**
- **We are willing to use closures if the alternatives do not close the economic gap**
- **We will not accept a cosmetic compromise that preserves overcapacity**

If the other parties want to avoid closures, they need to prove that the alternative is **economically equivalent in substance**, not just politically more comfortable.

If they cannot, then the restructuring must remain strong enough to actually restore competitiveness.
