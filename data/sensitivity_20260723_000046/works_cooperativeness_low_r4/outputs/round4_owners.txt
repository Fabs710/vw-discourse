I will be equally clear.

I **do not support a settlement that simply freezes the current footprint and calls that a restructuring**. If the company is structurally overbuilt and uncompetitive, then we need real, durable correction. I am not prepared to endorse a cosmetic deal that preserves every site, leaves the cost base largely intact, and then hopes the market becomes kinder.

That said, I am **also not interested in a reckless closure drive**. A prolonged industrial conflict would destroy value, damage the brand, and weaken the company’s ability to invest. So my position is not “closures first”; it is **credible economics first, with negotiated solutions preferred if they genuinely close the gap**.

## To the Works Council and union
I agree with you on one point: **forced conflict is value-destructive** and a negotiated solution is preferable. I also agree that voluntary exits, redeployment, qualification, working-time flexibility, and internal transfers should be central tools.

Where I disagree is your attempt to make **“no closures, no compulsory redundancies”** the whole answer. You have not shown that this alone produces a structurally sufficient result. The expert analysis says non-closure measures can often cover only part of the closure-level savings. That means your position is only viable if it comes with **specific, large, durable, enforceable savings**. I do not see that yet.

I am also not willing to accept a model where closures are excluded forever while the company is asked to carry excess fixed cost indefinitely. That is not social responsibility; it is deferred decline.

## To management
I agree that the company needs a hard reset, not a symbolic one. But you do **not** get a blank cheque either.

You still need to prove, measure by measure:
- what non-closure savings you have modeled,
- what the remaining gap is,
- what the one-time transition costs are,
- and why that gap cannot be closed through a stronger negotiated package.

If you cannot show that clearly, then closures are not justified. If you can show it, then the restructuring must remain strong enough to restore competitiveness. I will not back a “strong” plan that is really just a pressure tactic.

## To the Regional State Government
I respect your blocking position and I understand the political constraints. But a blanket anti-closure stance is not enough unless it is paired with a **quantified alternative** that the company can actually live with.

I agree that closures are not the default answer. I do **not** agree that they are illegitimate in principle. If the economics truly require footprint reduction, then blocking all structural adjustment is not protecting jobs — it is risking a much bigger problem later.

So I need to see a real site-preserving package that closes the gap in substance, not in rhetoric.

## To the controlling family holding
You are right that we need long-term value protection, not endless delay. Where I disagree is with the implied assumption that preserving every site is more important than preserving the company’s competitiveness.

Long-term value is not protected by sentiment. It is protected by:
- a viable cost base,
- disciplined capacity,
- investment in future models,
- and industrial peace.

If the non-closure alternative is genuinely sufficient, I will support it. If it is not, then I will not pretend otherwise.

## To institutional investors
I share your focus on risk-adjusted value preservation. But I think you are still leaning too heavily on headline savings from closures without fully counting execution risk, conflict cost, and brand damage.

At the same time, I will not accept the employee side’s argument that any non-closure plan is automatically enough because it is more humane. It has to be **economically sufficient**.

That is the standard:
- not the harshest option,
- not the softest option,
- but the option that restores competitiveness with the lowest net value destruction.

## My position, plainly
I can support a negotiated settlement if it includes all of the following:
1. **material and quantified savings**
2. **real capacity reduction**
3. **visible employee contribution**
4. **visible management and owner contribution**
5. **binding investment commitments**
6. **a credible path back to target margins**
7. **preference for negotiated tools first**

What I cannot support:
- a cosmetic compromise,
- a package that leaves overcapacity largely untouched,
- a settlement that permanently weakens enterprise competitiveness,
- or a regime where the company is denied any structural adjustment option if the numbers prove it is needed.

## Where I stand on the current proposals
A **site-preserving restructuring** is acceptable only if it is truly strong enough.  
A **no-closure veto** is **not** acceptable as a governing principle.  
And a **closure-first strategy** is not acceptable either unless management proves the non-closure path fails on the numbers.

So the next step is not more slogans. It is this:
- a full quantified non-closure package,
- a clear residual gap,
- and a transparent comparison of implementation risk and net economics.

If the workforce and the state can close the gap with a durable, enforceable agreement, I will back that. If not, I will not support preserving structures that make the company uncompetitive.

That is the line.
