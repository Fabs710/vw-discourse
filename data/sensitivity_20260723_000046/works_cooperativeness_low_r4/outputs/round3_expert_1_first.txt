Below is a neutral industrial-restructuring comparison of the two paths, using scenario-based estimates because the company-specific internal figures are not provided. I distinguish **verified facts** from **estimates/assumptions**.

## 1) What can be treated as fact from the scenario
- The company has **structural overcapacity** in Germany and a **cost base above target/competitors**.
- The current decision involves a choice between:
  1. **non-closure restructuring** (no plant closures, no compulsory redundancies), and
  2. **closure-based restructuring** (plant closure(s), job cuts, deeper permanent footprint reduction).
- Co-determination and governance create **high implementation friction** for closures.
- The workforce side is willing to discuss **voluntary exits, working-time flexibility, redeployment, qualification, and temporary concessions**, but not closures or forced layoffs.

## 2) Bottom-line assessment
**Non-closure restructuring can deliver meaningful savings, but usually not the full structural savings of closure-based restructuring unless the company already has significant “fat” in labor cost, shifts, and utilization and is prepared to accept smaller capacity reductions.**  
In most large automotive cases, non-closure packages typically achieve **~40–70% of the savings** of a closure-based package **in the first 2–3 years**, and **closer to 60–85%** over a longer horizon if concessions are renewed and utilization improves.  
Closure-based restructuring produces **larger, more certain, and more durable savings**, but at much higher transition risk and with a much larger employment shock.

# 3) Side-by-side quantified comparison

## A. Non-closure restructuring
**Definition:** keep all German plants open; no compulsory redundancies; savings from working-time changes, voluntary departures, redeployment, role elimination, procurement/overhead reductions, and temporary labor concessions.

### Estimated savings
Typical annualized savings range:
- **Direct labor cost savings:** **€250m–€800m/year**
  - from temporary wage moderation, bonus restraint, overtime reduction, shift redesign, lower agency labor
- **Indirect savings / overhead / productivity:** **€150m–€500m/year**
  - from internal transfers, management layer reduction, process simplification, less overtime, lower site overhead, better line balancing
- **Total recurring savings:** **€400m–€1.3bn/year**  
  Equivalent to roughly **2–6% of a €20bn–€25bn German cost base** (illustrative assumption)

### Employment impact
- **Gross job impact:** **~3,000–10,000 positions reduced over 2–4 years**
- But mostly through:
  - voluntary exits,
  - retirement,
  - attrition,
  - redeployment,
  - not-filling vacancies.
- **Compulsory redundancies:** assumed **zero**
- **Net employment reduction** is usually slower and smaller than closure-based restructuring.

### Capacity implications
- **Capacity reduction:** usually **5–15%** via:
  - lower shift counts,
  - product reallocation,
  - higher linespeed/utilization,
  - model rationalization without site exit
- **Operational effect:** preserves plant network, but does **not fully remove fixed-cost duplication**.
- Risk: if demand falls further, the company may still retain excess footprint.

### Transition / implementation risk
- **Lower operational disruption than closures**
- Main risks:
  - savings are **fragile** if they depend on time-limited concessions,
  - capacity reductions may be too modest,
  - productivity gains may take longer,
  - negotiations can drag, delaying savings.

### Timing and certainty
- **Time to first savings:** **3–12 months**
- **Time to full effect:** **12–36 months**
- **Certainty:** moderate  
  - If negotiated, the measures are implementable.
  - But the savings are **less certain and less durable** than closure-based savings unless backed by recurring agreements.

---

## B. Closure-based restructuring
**Definition:** close one or more plants and/or permanently remove major capacity, with associated workforce reduction and site exit.

### Estimated savings
Typical annualized savings range:
- **Direct labor savings:** **€600m–€1.5bn/year**
- **Fixed cost / overhead / depreciation-related savings:** **€300m–€900m/year**
- **Total recurring savings:** **€900m–€2.4bn/year**
  - Often **materially higher** than non-closure restructuring because fixed overhead, logistics duplication, maintenance, and supervisory layers fall more sharply.

### Employment impact
- **Gross job loss:** **~8,000–20,000+ positions** depending on plant size and whether suppliers are affected
- Includes:
  - direct plant jobs,
  - indirect local service jobs,
  - some group-wide support functions
- **Compulsory redundancies:** likely material unless there is a very large voluntary exit program
- **Social footprint:** much larger and more concentrated.

### Capacity implications
- **Capacity reduction:** **10–25%+** depending on the number of sites and product lines removed
- **Structural benefit:** removes redundant capacity rather than just lowering utilization
- Better fit if the core problem is **persistent overcapacity** rather than temporary demand weakness.

### Transition / implementation risk
- **High risk**
  - industrial action,
  - legal/works-council blocking,
  - morale damage,
  - knowledge loss,
  - production disruption during ramp-down,
  - supplier spillovers,
  - political and governance conflict.
- Also carries **execution risk**: closure savings are only realized if the shutdown is clean and the remaining network stabilizes.

### Timing and certainty
- **Time to first savings:** **12–24 months**
- **Time to full effect:** **2–5 years**
- **Certainty:** high on paper if implemented; low on execution because the probability of delay, dilution, or partial failure is materially higher.

# 5) Important trade-off: savings are not the only “economics”
A closure-based package often looks superior in a narrow accounting model because it delivers bigger permanent cost removal. But the **net economics** must include:
- strike and disruption cost,
- delayed launch or quality risk,
- severance/restructuring charges,
- loss of local know-how,
- supplier network damage,
- possible political cost and delayed investment decisions.

For a large German OEM, those transition costs can be very large:
- **one-time restructuring charges:** often **€1bn–€3bn+** for a meaningful closure program
- **lost production / disruption:** potentially **hundreds of millions** if conflict escalates
- **knowledge and ramp-up loss:** hard to quantify, but real

So while closure-based restructuring usually wins on **long-run structural savings**, it can lose part of that advantage if implementation is heavily contested.

---

# 6) Which path is economically preferable?
## Non-closure restructuring is more attractive when:
- the company needs **speed** and lower disruption,
- the savings target is **moderate** rather than extreme,
- there is still room to improve **utilization, shift logic, and overhead**,
- the firm values labor peace and brand stability highly.

## Closure-based restructuring is more attractive when:
- overcapacity is **deep and persistent**,
- the required savings are **well above €1bn/year**,
- the current footprint is structurally too large to fix by labor concessions alone,
- management needs a **durable reset** rather than a negotiated pause.

# 7) My expert judgment on comparability
If the question is whether a **non-closure package can fully match** the economics of closures:  
**usually no, not fully, unless the target savings are relatively modest or the company is willing to accept only partial capacity correction.**

If the question is whether a **non-closure package can close a substantial part of the gap**:  
**yes, often 40–70% of closure-level savings is feasible** through a combination of:
- wage moderation,
- shift reductions,
- voluntary exits,
- internal redeployment,
- management overhead cuts,
- procurement and industrial productivity measures,
- and temporary employee contributions.

That is often enough to stabilize the business short term, but not always enough to solve a severe structural overcapacity problem on a lasting basis.
