We are not persuaded by rhetoric on either side; we are persuaded by **credible economics, implementation certainty, and margin recovery**.

## Our position
We support a **durable restructuring that restores competitiveness**.  
We do **not** support a cosmetic compromise that preserves an uncompetitive cost base.  
We also do **not** support a restructuring that is so conflict-heavy it destroys value through strikes, delay, and execution failure.

## Where we agree
### With management
You are right that the company has a structural problem, not just a temporary one. The German footprint and cost base must come down. We also agree that voluntary exits, redeployment, qualification, working-time flexibility, and internal transfers should be used first wherever they produce real savings.

### With the expert
The comparison was useful and, frankly, sobering:
- non-closure measures can create real savings,
- but they often only deliver a portion of the savings of structural footprint reduction,
- and closure-based plans carry high execution and conflict risk.

That is exactly the trade-off we need to see quantified.

### With the state government
We agree that prolonged industrial conflict would destroy value. We also agree that social partnership and German industrial capability matter. But that cannot be used to justify freezing the company in an uncompetitive configuration.

## Where we disagree

### To the Works Council and union
Your red line against closures and compulsory redundancies is understandable, but it is **not investable by itself**.

What you still have not shown is a **quantified, durable savings bridge** that credibly restores competitive margins without structural footprint reduction. Saying “no closures” is not a financial plan. It is a constraint.

We also reject the idea that review mechanisms are automatically a “delayed threat.” If the package is truly durable, then performance review should simply confirm whether the agreed measures are delivering. What we cannot accept is a deal that locks in job/site protections while leaving the economics unresolved.

### To management
You are right that closures cannot be ruled out in principle, but you have not yet justified them with enough precision. The expert did not prove closures are necessary; it showed they can be more powerful on paper. That is not the same thing.

If you want investor support for structural reductions, then show:
- the exact residual savings gap after all non-closure measures,
- the one-time restructuring charges,
- the disruption and strike cost assumptions,
- the timing to margin recovery,
- and the net effect on risk-adjusted value.

Without that, closures remain a possibility, not a justified conclusion.

### To the family holding
We do not accept either a closure fetish or a freeze-the-footprint approach. The question is not ideological; it is whether the package **materially improves the return on sales and free cash flow** with acceptable execution risk.

## What would move us
We could support a non-closure path **only if** it is genuinely close to economically equivalent in substance. That means:
- quantified labor-cost moderation,
- meaningful overtime and shift-cost reduction,
- voluntary exit volumes large enough to matter,
- overhead and management cuts,
- visible owner contribution,
- binding investment commitments,
- and a credible path to target margins.

If that package only closes 40–70% of the gap and leaves the remainder structurally unresolved, then it is not enough.

## Our bottom line
We are open to either path **only on one condition**: it must restore competitiveness with a credible, durable margin outcome.

So our answer is:

- **Yes** to serious restructuring.
- **Yes** to negotiated measures first.
- **Yes** to quantified burden sharing.
- **No** to a cosmetic no-closure deal that leaves the cost base uncompetitive.
- **No** to closure-driven restructuring unless management proves, with numbers, that the residual gap cannot be closed otherwise and that the net value impact remains superior after disruption costs.

If the parties want our support, bring us a **side-by-side financial case** showing:
1. the non-closure package,
2. the residual gap,
3. the closure-based alternative,
4. and the net risk-adjusted value of each.

Without that, we will continue to judge this on one criterion only: **which option credibly restores competitive margins and protects capital value.**
