## Independent industrial economics assessment

I’ll stay within the cost, restructuring, and execution domain.

### Short answer
A **non-closure restructuring can be economically comparable** to a closure-led plan **only if** it delivers most of the same structural savings on:
- **headcount**
- **fixed cost**
- **capacity/utilization**
- **labor cost per vehicle**
- **and implementation certainty**

If it does **not** achieve that scale, it is usually **not comparable**, even if it avoids immediate plant closures.

---

## 1) What a closure-led plan typically buys
A plant closure or major site exit usually delivers savings through:
- removal of direct labor,
- elimination of site overhead,
- lower maintenance and energy burden,
- reduced logistics complexity,
- and less duplicated capacity.

But the headline savings are often overstated because closure plans also bring:
- severance and restructuring charges,
- pension and transfer liabilities,
- write-offs of specialized assets,
- supplier and logistics reconfiguration costs,
- transition costs for transferred production,
- and strike / delay risk.

So a closure’s **nominal savings** and **realized NPV** can diverge materially.

---

## 2) What a non-closure plan must contain to be comparable
If no plant closes, then the same economics have to come from other levers. For a large German OEM, I would treat the package as potentially comparable only if it includes most of the following:

### A. Headcount reduction
A credible scale is usually:
- **10–15% reduction in the affected footprint** as a minimum starting benchmark,
- more if current utilization is very poor or the cost gap is large.

If the plan only offers attrition and minor voluntary exits, it is generally too small.

### B. Capacity reduction
There must be a real cut in:
- shifts,
- lines,
- variants,
- or output volumes.

If capacity is left broadly intact, the company keeps too much fixed cost.

A serious non-closure plan typically needs:
- **meaningful shift eliminations**
- and/or **line consolidation**
- and/or **platform / model simplification**

### C. Fixed-cost removal
Savings have to come from:
- plant overhead,
- engineering support,
- admin layers,
- maintenance scope,
- indirect labor,
- and internal logistics complexity.

If the only savings are wage moderation, the package is not structurally sufficient.

### D. Labor-cost moderation
This can help, but in isolation it is rarely enough. It can contribute through:
- temporary pay moderation,
- removal or suspension of allowances,
- lower bonus accruals,
- longer working time for the same headcount,
- reduced premium payments,
- flexibility on shift patterns.

But labor-cost moderation is usually a **supporting lever**, not the main lever.

---

## 3) Thresholds: when non-closure starts to look comparable

### A practical benchmark
For a large German passenger-car operation under pressure, I would generally want to see something like:

- **annual run-rate savings: ~€700m or more**
- **headcount reduction: ~10–15% in the affected footprint**
- **visible capacity reduction**
- **cash benefit within 12–24 months**
- **low risk of collapse from labor conflict**

If the required restructuring gap is very large, the comparable threshold may be closer to **€1bn+** annual run-rate savings.

### Why this level?
Because the all-in economics of a closure-led plan are not just labor savings. They include:
- site overhead,
- depopulation of support functions,
- logistics simplification,
- and sometimes platform rationalization.

A non-closure plan must replicate enough of those effects to matter.

---

## 4) Hidden costs that often make “soft” alternatives less attractive
Non-closure packages often look cheaper but can hide costs in:

- prolonged wage agreements with sub-target savings,
- retaining excess capacity longer,
- paying for redeployment and qualification,
- higher internal transfer friction,
- duplicated support structures,
- deferred site rationalization,
- and the risk that savings evaporate if volumes fall again.

So if the company keeps all sites open but leaves utilization weak, the apparent compromise can become a **permanent cost drag**.

---

## 5) Hidden costs that often make closures less attractive than claimed
The opposite is also true. Closure-led plans often understate:
- severance and transfer payments,
- legal and works-council delay,
- union strike risk,
- political intervention,
- supplier disruption,
- loss of tacit knowledge,
- and transitional inefficiencies.

In a German co-determined environment, these costs can be large enough that a closure plan’s theoretical advantage shrinks materially.

---

## 6) Timing matters as much as headline savings
A non-closure plan can outperform a closure plan on timing if it is:
- negotiated faster,
- easier to implement,
- and less likely to be blocked.

That matters because savings realized in year 1–2 are worth more than savings deferred by litigation or industrial action.

So I would not assess the two routes only by gross savings. I would compare:
1. **gross savings**
2. **restructuring cost**
3. **probability of delivery**
4. **speed to cash**
5. **NPV after execution risk**

---

## 7) My stress test of the scenario
Based on the information given, the employee-side package can be economically comparable **only if** it is not merely a labor-friendly alternative but a **hard restructuring by other means**.

That means:
- substantial voluntary exits,
- strong internal redeployment,
- real shift and line reduction,
- multi-year wage moderation or equivalent labor contribution,
- overhead cuts in management and support functions,
- and enforceable capacity rationalization.

If any of those are missing, the non-closure alternative is likely **inferior in economic effect**, even if preferable in labor-relations terms.

---

## 8) Bottom-line judgment
### I would conclude the non-closure alternative is economically comparable only if:
- the affected footprint loses **roughly 10–15% of headcount or more**,
- annualized savings are **at least in the high hundreds of millions of euros**,
- capacity is visibly reduced,
- and the package is implementable without major delay.

### I would conclude it is **not** comparable if:
- savings are mostly temporary,
- capacity stays largely unchanged,
- headcount reduction is minor,
- or delivery depends on optimistic assumptions about labor peace and utilization recovery.
