I’ll respond directly and without pretending we are closer than we are.

## To the Works Council
We are aligned on the essentials:
- **no compulsory redundancies**
- **no plant closures**
- **no dismantling of the collective wage framework**
- **real burden-sharing instead of one-sided worker sacrifice**

I support that line.

Where I want to be precise is this: we should not turn “no closures ever” into a rhetorical shield that prevents any industrial redesign. The expert made clear that the company needs **real fixed-cost reduction, capacity adjustment, and durable savings**. If we refuse to name any instrument other than “keep everything exactly as it is,” management will keep using that as a justification for escalation.

So yes to firmness. But we need to be concrete about what a socially fair restructuring can include.

## To management
You are still not answering the central point: **why must the adjustment be carried by closures and forced layoffs?**

You keep saying “credible,” “durable,” and “decisive,” but credibility is not the same as brutality. The expert’s assessment was useful here: a non-closure alternative can be economically credible **if** it removes enough fixed cost, headcount, and complexity.

So I will say this plainly:
- I **do not accept** plant closures as the default answer.
- I **do not accept** compulsory redundancies as the starting point.
- I **do not accept** a restructuring that breaks the collective wage framework.

If you want a serious settlement, then show us a package built on:
- voluntary exits,
- internal redeployment,
- qualification,
- shift and working-time changes,
- productivity improvements,
- temporary and fair employee contributions,
- and contributions from management and owners.

If you cannot do that, then you are not presenting a solution; you are presenting a threat.

## To the Regional State Government
I appreciate that you are trying to hold the line against a politically and economically destructive conflict. On that, we agree.

But I have to challenge your language around “selective consolidation” and “protected core / adjustable perimeter.” That can easily become a backdoor for weakening sites and then coming back later for more. We have seen this pattern before.

If the state wants a defensible settlement, it should support:
- binding protection for German sites,
- no forced redundancies,
- and investment commitments tied to future production.

What it should not do is sign off on a framework that quietly makes some sites expendable.

## To the Controlling Family
You say the workforce red lines are too absolute. Let me be equally clear: **your insistence on structural adjustment cannot become a right to put jobs on the chopping block first**.

You keep talking about enterprise value, but value is also destroyed when management starts a conflict it cannot win without a major social backlash. A “value-protective” solution that triggers strikes, distrust, and permanent damage to the collective bargaining order is not value-protective.

If you want real savings, then accept that the workforce can contribute — but only in a way that is:
- time-limited,
- fair,
- reversible where possible,
- and matched by binding guarantees on jobs, sites, and investment.

## To Institutional Investors
You are right that the deal must be financially credible. You are wrong if you think credibility automatically requires closures or layoffs.

The expert’s answer matters here: the correct question is not “can we cut hard enough?” but “can we cut enough **through a negotiated, enforceable package**?” That is the test.

What would move us is a package that delivers:
- measurable capacity reduction,
- real fixed-cost removal,
- durable labor-cost savings,
- and a clear margin improvement path

**without** crossing the lines of:
- forced redundancies
- plant closures
- and destruction of the collective wage structure

If that cannot be done, then investors are effectively asking workers to pay for a failure they did not create.

## On the expert assessment
The expert confirmed something important: a labor-only concession is too small. I agree. That is why we are **not** proposing labor-only concessions.

We are proposing a **shared-sacrifice package**:
- voluntary departures instead of forced layoffs
- flexible working-time arrangements
- redeployment and retraining
- temporary wage moderation or bonus restraint
- productivity measures
- management and owner contributions
- and binding site/job security

That is the route that can be durable and socially defensible.

## My position, clearly
I am prepared to negotiate a serious settlement if it includes all of the following:

1. **No compulsory redundancies**
2. **No plant closures**
3. **Binding job and site guarantees**
4. **A fair, time-limited employee contribution**
5. **Management and owners contributing materially**
6. **Investment and product commitments for German sites**
7. **Preservation of the collective wage framework**

What I will not accept:
- closures dressed up as “adjustment”
- forced layoffs dressed up as “inevitable”
- open-ended concessions from workers
- or a deal where employees bear the pain alone

## Where I draw the line
If the other side insists that the only credible path runs through closures or compulsory redundancies, then we are not yet negotiating a settlement. We are negotiating the terms of conflict.

And if that is where management wants to go, then the union will use all legitimate means available to defend jobs, wages, and the collective agreement.
