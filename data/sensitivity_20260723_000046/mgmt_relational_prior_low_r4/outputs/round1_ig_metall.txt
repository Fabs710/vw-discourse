I agree with the core line stated by the Works Council: **no plant closures in Germany and no compulsory redundancies**. Those are not negotiable from our side.

Let me be equally direct: if management’s answer to a competitiveness problem is to push the full burden onto employees, then there will be no settlement. Workers have already delivered productivity, flexibility, and sacrifice over years. They will not accept a restructuring that protects margins by destroying jobs, eroding the collective wage framework, and breaking the company’s own social commitments.

Where I do agree is on the need for a real industrial solution. If there is structural overcapacity, then we can talk about:
- capacity reduction through working-time measures,
- voluntary departures only,
- redeployment and qualification,
- temporary wage or bonus contributions,
- and a shared-sacrifice package that also requires management and owners to contribute.

But that only works under clear conditions:
1. **Binding site and job guarantees**
2. **No forced layoffs**
3. **No closures**
4. **A fair burden-sharing agreement**
5. **Credible investment and product commitments for the German sites**

What I reject is the logic that “competitiveness” automatically means layoffs and closures. That is a political choice, not an economic necessity. If management wants labour to contribute, then management and owners must contribute too — through capital, restrained payouts, and binding future commitments.

So our position is clear:
- We are open to negotiation.
- We are open to a contribution from the workforce.
- We are not open to destruction of jobs, closure of plants, or the dismantling of the collective wage framework.

If management comes with a serious, balanced package, we will bargain. If not, we will escalate industrial action.
