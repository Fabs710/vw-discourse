Here is the economically relevant answer.

## Bottom line
A **credible non-closure restructuring** can work only if it achieves a **large, durable cost base reduction**. In a large German automotive group, that usually means:

- **headcount reduction:** roughly **10–20% of the domestic workforce** at the affected brand/footprint, mostly via voluntary exits, retirement, redeployment, and attrition rather than forced layoffs;
- **fixed-cost removal:** about **€500m to €1.5bn per year** depending on scale, plant mix, and overhead reduction;
- **capacity reduction:** typically **10–25% of domestic output capacity** or equivalent shift/line consolidation;
- **labor-cost contribution:** often **mid-single-digit to low-double-digit percent** of annual labor cost in the short run, but not enough by itself unless combined with structural changes.

A **closure-led plan** can deliver larger and faster structural savings, but only if it is **actually executable**. In this governance setting, execution risk is very high: co-determination, political stakes, strikes, and shareholder blocking positions can delay or dilute it materially. That means the nominal savings from closures are often discounted heavily in present-value terms.

## What “economically comparable” means
For a non-closure alternative to be economically comparable to a closure-led plan, it must usually satisfy all three of these:

1. **Near-term cash relief within 12–24 months**
   - at least **€200m–€500m** annualized cash improvement, depending on the company’s burn rate and market conditions;
   - achieved through payroll moderation, voluntary exits, reduced overtime/shift premia, and lower overhead.

2. **Structural annual run-rate savings within 2–4 years**
   - ideally **€700m+** at the brand/cluster level if the company is genuinely overcapacity;
   - otherwise the plan is likely a delay mechanism rather than a fix.

3. **Capacity utilization improvement**
   - enough to raise plant loading materially, typically by **5–15 percentage points** at the retained sites, or to move production into fewer lines/shifts so that fixed cost per vehicle falls decisively.

If those targets are not met, then a non-closure deal is economically weaker than it looks.

## Why labor concessions alone are insufficient
If the employee-side package is mainly:
- temporary pay moderation,
- reduced bonuses,
- working-time flexibility,
- and voluntary exits,

then the savings are real but usually **not large enough** to solve a serious structural overcapacity problem on their own.

Typical magnitude:
- **pay/benefit moderation:** often only **1–3% of total labor cost** if time-limited;
- **bonus restraint and minor flexibility measures:** cash benefit is helpful but modest;
- **voluntary exits:** useful, but the savings arrive gradually and require upfront severance/bridging costs.

So labor concessions can bridge part of the gap, but they rarely replace the need for **capacity rationalization** and **overhead reduction**.

## Closure-led plan: economic advantages and risks
### Advantages
- larger potential **run-rate savings**;
- clearer elimination of redundant fixed cost;
- stronger signal to capital markets that management is serious.

### Risks
- **high execution delay**: works council, union action, supervisory-board blocking, regional political intervention;
- **strike risk**: even short industrial action can destroy a meaningful share of annual savings;
- **transition costs**: severance, decommissioning, site remediation, supply-chain requalification, logistics reconfiguration;
- **lost output during transition**.

In practice, a closure plan may look stronger on paper than in realized NPV if it triggers multi-quarter disruption.

## Non-closure alternative: where it can outperform
A non-closure plan can outperform a closure-led plan when:
- the company can consolidate production into fewer shifts or fewer lines without losing too much efficiency;
- there is substantial excess overhead that can be cut centrally;
- voluntary exits are abundant and relatively cheap;
- and industrial peace is preserved.

In that case, the company may get:
- **faster realized cash savings**,
- lower legal/industrial conflict costs,
- and less operational disruption.

This is especially relevant if the objective is to **stabilize margins in the next 12–18 months** rather than redesign the footprint permanently.

## Non-closure alternative: where it fails
It fails if:
- the plant network remains structurally too broad;
- utilization stays low;
- too much saving depends on temporary concessions;
- or management cannot enforce future discipline once the immediate crisis passes.

Then the result is often:
- deferred pain,
- partially realized savings,
- and a later, more expensive restructuring.

## My estimate of the minimum package
If you ask what I would require to judge a non-closure package economically comparable, I would look for something like:

- **headcount reduction:** at least **10%**, preferably **15%+** in the affected German footprint;
- **capacity reduction/shift consolidation:** enough to remove at least **one meaningful layer of fixed cost**, often equivalent to **10–20% capacity**;
- **annualized cost savings:** at least **€500m**, and for a large core brand under real pressure, closer to **€1bn** if the footprint is large;
- **implementation horizon:** savings visible within **12–24 months**, not just promised for year 4 or 5;
- **transition cost ceiling:** severance and restructuring cash outlay must not swallow so much of the present value that the plan becomes a postponement.

## Sensitivity
The answer changes with three assumptions:

- **If European demand weakens further**: the threshold for “enough” savings rises.
- **If EV ramp costs remain high**: more fixed-cost removal is needed.
- **If strikes or political blocking are likely**: closure-led nominal savings become much less attractive in present value terms.

## Final assessment
From a purely industrial-restructuring perspective:

- A **closure-led plan** is more radical and may offer the largest theoretical savings.
- But in this governance environment, its **execution risk is unusually high**.
- A **non-closure package** is economically credible only if it includes **real capacity reduction, substantial headcount reduction, and durable fixed-cost cuts**.
- If it does not, it is probably just **delay**, not restructuring.
