## To management
I agree with your diagnosis: the cost base is too high, overcapacity is real, and cosmetic compromise will not fix the business.

I also agree with the expert that a non-closure package only works if it is **large, fast, and enforceable**. That is the benchmark.

Where I disagree is in treating closures as the default answer. In this governance environment, a closure-led approach is not automatically value-preserving. If it triggers prolonged conflict, legal delay, and political blockage, the nominal savings can evaporate quickly. That is not disciplined restructuring; that is value destruction by process.

So I will support **serious restructuring**, but only if you can demonstrate, plant by plant, that the non-closure route or a limited, highly targeted adjustment actually delivers:
- substantial fixed-cost removal,
- meaningful capacity rationalization,
- visible cash relief within 12–24 months,
- and durable margin improvement.

If you cannot show that, then you have not proven that your preferred instrument is the best value outcome.

## To the works council
I understand your red lines. I also understand why you are suspicious of “protected core / adjustable perimeter” language; it can become a backdoor to weakening sites.

But I do **not** accept your position that every German plant must be preserved in its current form regardless of economics. A freeze is not protection if it leaves the company structurally uncompetitive.

The expert’s comparison confirms that the company needs real savings. That means there must be room for:
- voluntary exits,
- redeployment,
- working-time flexibility,
- shift and line rationalization,
- overhead reduction,
- and internal consolidation.

What I will not accept is a framework that rules out all meaningful adjustment and then expects the owners to absorb the consequences. That is not stewardship; it is postponement.

## To the industrial union
I agree that labor cannot carry the burden alone, and I agree that open-ended concessions are unacceptable.

I do not accept, however, the idea that “no closures, no compulsory redundancies” can stand as an answer unless it is backed by a concrete industrial plan that closes the cost gap. The burden of proof remains on those who reject the more radical instruments.

If you want a non-closure settlement to be credible, then it must be quantified and substantial:
- real workforce reduction through voluntary means,
- real productivity gains,
- real capacity reduction,
- and real labor-cost moderation.

If those numbers do not add up, then you are asking us to support underperformance in the name of social comfort. I will not do that.

## To the regional state government
I agree with you that a socially tone-deaf restructuring can destroy value and damage the regional base.

Where I disagree is the assumption that a strong German industrial footprint can be preserved without deeper structural adjustment inside that footprint. Protection cannot mean freezing every site and every legacy structure. That simply defers the problem.

I am open to supporting a substantial German presence, but only if it is economically viable. If the state wants to prevent decline later, it must accept the measures that restore competitiveness now.

## To institutional investors
You are right to insist on risk-adjusted value, not slogans.

You are also right that a weak compromise is worthless. But I will not endorse a “hard cut” solution if the execution risk destroys more value than it creates. The question is not whether the plan sounds decisive; it is whether it will actually improve the business.

So I will back whichever route convincingly delivers:
- durable savings,
- better utilization,
- improved margins,
- and lower execution risk.

That may be a non-closure package if it is large enough. It may be a limited structural adjustment if that is what the numbers support. But it will not be an open-ended protection scheme that leaves the company uncompetitive.
