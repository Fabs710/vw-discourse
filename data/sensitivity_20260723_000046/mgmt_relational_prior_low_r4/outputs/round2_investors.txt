From the perspective of dispersed capital-market shareholders, the discussion is now clearer — and the key financial test remains unchanged:

**Does this package credibly restore competitive margins, or does it merely postpone the correction?**

## Where I agree
I agree with management, the family, and partly the state on one central point: **the current cost base is not sustainable**. If German operations remain structurally overcosted and overcapacity persists, then the equity story stays broken. The market will not reward sentiment, history, or symbolic social peace if margins continue to lag.

I also agree with the union and works council on one narrower point: **a prolonged industrial conflict is value-destructive**. Strikes, operational disruption, and political stalemate hurt investors as well. A deal that takes too long or becomes a public confrontation will destroy value even if the final savings are theoretically meaningful.

## Where I disagree
I do **not** accept the employee-side position that:
- no plant closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- and binding guarantees through 2030

can be treated as absolute starting conditions **if** they leave the cost base structurally uncompetitive.

That is not a viable financial outcome. If those red lines prevent meaningful footprint adjustment and durable labor-cost reduction, then they are incompatible with restoring returns.

At the same time, I also do **not** accept the idea that closures and layoffs are automatically the best answer. From an investor standpoint, **severity is not the same as value creation**. A closure-heavy approach that triggers prolonged strikes, political vetoes, legal delay, or reputational damage may destroy as much value as it creates.

So my position is not “closures at any cost.”  
My position is: **real savings, credible execution, and durable margin improvement**.

## To management
Your diagnosis is broadly credible. Your execution case is not yet complete.

You are right that a cosmetic compromise is unacceptable. But if you push too hard on closures and compulsory redundancies without securing implementability, you may end up with:
- labor resistance,
- political blockage,
- delayed savings,
- and weaker near-term margins.

That would be a poor capital outcome.

What I need from management is not rhetoric about toughness. I need:
- quantified savings,
- a timeline,
- implementation certainty,
- and a clear route back to return-on-sales target.

If you can deliver that through negotiated capacity reduction, working-time measures, voluntary exits, and targeted site rationalisation, that is preferable to a confrontation-driven solution.

## To the Works Council and the Union
You are right to reject a cosmetic deal. But your current framing still risks that very outcome by making the hardest adjustments too difficult in advance.

I disagree with the idea that “no closures” and “no compulsory redundancies” can remain absolute regardless of the economics. If the company’s German footprint cannot be adjusted enough to restore competitiveness, then shareholders will continue to bear the cost through weak margins and valuation pressure.

What would move us is not a promise to protect everything. It is:
- meaningful capacity reduction,
- durable labor-cost savings,
- flexibility on hours and working patterns,
- voluntary departures at scale,
- and a binding, measurable improvement in margins.

If those outcomes are achieved without closures, fine. I do not need closures as a principle. But I do need the economics fixed.

## To the Regional State Government
I understand the political need to preserve industrial presence in the region. But the state cannot support a structure that protects employment today while allowing the company to underperform indefinitely. That is simply deferred value destruction.

A “substantial industrial footprint” is only defensible if it comes with:
- genuine competitiveness,
- operating discipline,
- and a realistic cost base.

If you want investor support, the outcome must be more than regional preservation. It must be financially sustainable.

## To the Controlling Family
You are closer to the right place than some others because you recognise that the status quo is not sustainable.

But I need more concreteness from you as well. “Material change” and “durable savings” are not enough on their own. Investors will look for:
- how much cost comes out,
- how quickly,
- through what mechanisms,
- and whether the result is actually enforceable.

I am prepared to support a solution that avoids unnecessary conflict **if** it is sufficiently strong. I am not prepared to support a weak compromise simply because it is quieter.

## My bottom line
Dispersed shareholders will support **whatever credibly restores competitiveness**. That means:

- **Yes** to a serious restructuring with real, durable savings.
- **Yes** to capacity reduction, labor-cost discipline, and a credible route to margin recovery.
- **No** to a cosmetic settlement that preserves excess cost and leaves profitability impaired.
- **No** to a prolonged conflict that destroys value without fixing the problem.

So my message is simple:

If you can produce a package that is **specific, measurable, and implementable**, we can back it.  
If not, then this remains a value problem, not a turnaround.

3. Durable labor-cost savings
4. Clear evidence that margins will recover
5. Low execution risk relative to a closure-heavy conflict
