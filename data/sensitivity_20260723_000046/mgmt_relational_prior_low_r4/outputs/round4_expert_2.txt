The expert’s assessment is broadly consistent with standard restructuring economics in large auto groups.

## Bottom-line answer
A **non-closure alternative** is economically comparable to a closure-led plan only if it achieves **most of the same structural effects** on:

- **headcount**
- **fixed cost**
- **capacity / utilization**
- **labor cost per vehicle**
- **implementation certainty**

If it does not, it is usually **not** comparable, even if it avoids plant closures.

---

## Practical threshold
For a large German OEM footprint under pressure, I would use this as a working benchmark:

- **Headcount reduction:** about **10–15%** in the affected footprint as a minimum
- **Annual run-rate savings:** roughly **€700m+**, and in a deeper case closer to **€1bn**
- **Capacity reduction:** real shift / line / volume reduction, not just a promise
- **Cash impact:** visible within **12–24 months**
- **Execution risk:** low enough that savings are likely to materialize, not just be announced

If the package falls materially short of that, it is generally not an economic substitute for plant closure or equivalent footprint exit.

---

## Why that bar is so high
A closure-led plan typically saves on:
- direct labor
- site overhead
- maintenance and utilities
- logistics complexity
- duplicated support functions

But closure also brings large offsetting costs:
- severance and restructuring charges
- transfer and relocation costs
- asset write-offs
- supplier disruption
- political / legal delay
- strike risk

So the **headline savings** of closure often overstate the **realized NPV**.

A non-closure plan can compete if it can still remove enough:
- labor cost
- indirect cost
- and capacity

to close the gap.

---

## When non-closure works best
It is most plausible when the company can do all of the following:
- eliminate one or more shifts
- consolidate lines or variants
- reduce indirect and overhead layers
- secure substantial voluntary exits
- use redeployment and attrition aggressively
- obtain temporary labor-cost moderation
- keep industrial peace so savings arrive on time

In that case, a non-closure package can sometimes match or beat a closure plan on **risk-adjusted value**.

---

## When closure is economically stronger
A closure-led plan tends to dominate when:
- the footprint is structurally underutilized
- excess capacity is severe
- the gap is too large for labor measures alone
- productivity improvements are unlikely to stick
- and the non-closure package would leave too much fixed cost in place

In those cases, a non-closure deal may be socially easier but economically incomplete.

---

## My judgment on the scenario
Given the governance and labor constraints described, a **non-closure path is only credible if it is a hard restructuring by other means**, not a soft compromise.

That means:
- substantial headcount reduction
- meaningful capacity rationalization
- major fixed-cost removal

- and early cash realization

Without that, it is not economically equivalent to closures; it is just a slower and usually weaker adjustment.
