From the perspective of dispersed capital-market shareholders, the round-3 discussion has now clarified the real issue:

**the company needs a restructuring that is economically large enough, fast enough, and credible enough to restore margins — not a symbolic settlement that merely postpones the correction.**

## Where I agree

I agree with management, the family, and the expert on the core diagnosis:
- the German cost base is too high,
- structural overcapacity is real,
- and the current margin profile is not acceptable.

I also agree with the works council, union, and state on one important point:
- a prolonged industrial conflict would destroy value.

So I am not asking for conflict for its own sake. I want the **highest risk-adjusted value outcome**. If a negotiated route can deliver that, fine. If not, I will not pretend a softer deal is acceptable just because it sounds calmer.

## Where I disagree

I do **not** accept the employee-side line that:
- no plant closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- binding guarantees through 2030,
can remain absolute if they leave the company structurally uncompetitive.

That is the central problem.

A package that protects every site and every legacy expectation, while only delivering modest concessions, is not a turnaround. It is a delay mechanism. The market will see through that immediately, and so will I.

At the same time, I also do **not** accept the view that closures are automatically the best answer. If a closure-led plan triggers:
- strikes,
- political vetoes,
- legal delay,
- reputational damage,
- and lost production,

then the NPV can collapse very quickly. So I am not endorsing “hard cuts at any cost.” I am endorsing **durable, implementable cost reduction**.

## To management
You are right that the company needs real structural savings, not cosmetics. But you still need to prove that your preferred instruments are actually the best value outcome after execution risk.

The expert made the key point: a non-closure package can be economically credible **only if** it delivers substantial headcount reduction, fixed-cost removal, and capacity reduction quickly enough.

So I need more than assertions that closures are “credible” or “necessary.” I need:
- quantified savings by year,
- implementation risk assessment,
- and a comparison against a negotiated non-closure alternative.

If you cannot show that closure-heavy restructuring still wins on a risk-adjusted basis, then I would not support forcing that route.

## To the works council and union
I respect that you want to defend jobs, sites, and co-determination. But your current red lines are too absolute to be a credible answer to the economics.

The expert’s numbers are sobering:
- labor concessions alone are not enough,
- meaningful capacity adjustment is required,
- and the non-closure route only works if it is **large**.

That means you cannot simply say “no closures, no forced layoffs” and expect capital to absorb the rest. If the alternative does not remove enough fixed cost and capacity, then you are asking shareholders to finance structural underperformance.

That said, I do **not** need closures as a principle. If you can produce:
- substantial voluntary exits,
- genuine shift and capacity reductions,
- material fixed-cost cuts,
- and durable productivity gains,

then I would prefer that to a conflict-heavy shutdown plan.

But those savings must be real, not rhetorical.

## To the regional state government
You are right that a socially tone-deaf restructuring can destroy value. I agree with that.

Where I disagree is the implication that a “protected industrial footprint” can be broad enough to keep everything essentially intact while still restoring competitiveness. That only works if the state is prepared to accept real adjustment inside the footprint:
- consolidation,
- shift reduction,
- redeployment,
- and materially lower fixed cost.

If “protection” means freezing the structure, then it is not protection — it is deferred decline.

## To the controlling family
You are closer to the necessary position than some others because you are at least willing to say the status quo is not sustainable.

But I need to be equally direct: “material change” is not enough as a phrase. I need a package that shows:
- how much cost comes out,
- how quickly,
- through what instruments,
- and whether the company remains investable after the restructuring.

I will not support a settlement that preserves governance control while leaving enterprise value structurally impaired. Ownership control matters, yes — but it is only worth preserving if the business is made healthier, not just quieter.

## On the expert’s assessment
The expert effectively set the threshold for me.

A non-closure package is only credible if it achieves roughly:
- **10–20% workforce reduction** in the affected footprint,
- **major fixed-cost removal**,
- **meaningful capacity reduction or shift consolidation**,
- and savings visible within **12–24 months**.

That is the benchmark.

If the proposed package cannot get close to that, then it is not a turnaround. It is just a delay of the same margin problem.

## What would move me
I could support a negotiated solution if it delivers all of the following:

1. **Durable structural savings**
2. **Real capacity reduction**
3. **A credible path back to return-on-sales target**
4. **Implementation certainty**
5. **Low execution risk relative to a closure-led conflict**
6. **A settlement that is not cosmetic**

That means I am open to:
- voluntary exits,
- redeployment,
- retraining,
- working-time flexibility,
- temporary labour-cost moderation,
- targeted consolidation,
- and a protected core of strategically important operations,

**if and only if** those measures are large enough to restore competitiveness.

## What would not move me
I will not support:
- a package that merely freezes the footprint,
- open-ended concessions without hard savings,
- a symbolic “shared burden” arrangement that leaves the cost base uncompetitive,
- or a closure-heavy strategy that destroys value through execution failure.

## My position, plainly
I remain neutral on the instrument and hard on the outcome.

**I will back whatever credibly restores margins and competitiveness.**  
If that is a negotiated non-closure package, fine — but it must be big, durable, and enforceable.  
If not, then the company should not expect capital markets to support a weak compromise or a drawn-out conflict.
