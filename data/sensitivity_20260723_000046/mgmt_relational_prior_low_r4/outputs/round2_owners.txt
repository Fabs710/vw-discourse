I will respond plainly, because the discussion has reached the point where vagueness is no longer helpful.

## Where I agree

I agree with management, the state, and the investors on the core diagnosis:

- the current German cost base is too high,
- overcapacity is real,
- and delaying structural change will continue to erode margins and investment capacity.

I also agree with the union and works council on one important point:

- a prolonged industrial conflict would be damaging and value-destructive.

So yes, we need a settlement that is not cosmetic. We need something durable.

## Where I disagree

I do **not** accept the employee side’s position that:

- **no plant closures**
- **no compulsory redundancies**
- **binding guarantees through 2030**
- and only voluntary or temporary measures

can be treated as absolute starting conditions.

That is not a credible restructuring framework for a company with a structurally uncompetitive base. It may be politically understandable, but it does not solve the economics.

I also do **not** accept the state’s implication that a “substantial industrial footprint” can be preserved in its current form regardless of economics. That would simply shift the problem into the future.

And I am not prepared to support a settlement that preserves every legacy expectation while leaving the company unable to restore margins.

## To management

You are right to say that a cosmetic compromise is unacceptable.  
You are also right that capacity reduction and labour-cost savings must be real.

But I want to be equally direct: if management leads with plant closures and compulsory redundancies as the default answer, you will provoke exactly the kind of prolonged conflict that destroys value and weakens execution.

So I support your objective, but not an unnecessarily blunt process.

What I need from management is a package that proves:

- how much cost comes out,
- by when,
- through which instruments,
- and with what certainty.

If you cannot show that, then you have not yet presented a board-worthy restructuring.

## To the Works Council and the Union

I respect your mandate. But your red lines are too absolute.

You say:
- no closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- binding guarantees through 2030.

I understand the logic, but I will not endorse a framework that effectively rules out any serious footprint adjustment before the numbers are even tested.

That said, I do **not** require closures as a matter of principle. What I require is a **credible, quantified, durable savings package**.

So if you want to avoid closures and forced layoffs, then the alternative must be substantial enough to work. That means concrete commitments on:

- working-time flexibility,
- voluntary exit volumes,
- redeployment,
- qualification,
- productivity improvements,
- and a meaningful employee contribution.

Not symbolic concessions. Real ones.

### What would move me
I would be prepared to support a negotiated package if it delivers all of the following:

1. **A measurable reduction in capacity**
2. **A material reduction in labour cost**
3. **A credible path to restored margins**
4. **Implementation certainty**
5. **Protection of the long-term enterprise, not just short-term calm**

That is the standard.

### What would not move me
I will not support:
- a deal that simply freezes the footprint,
- a deal that preserves every site regardless of economics,
- or a deal that postpones hard decisions while calling that “social responsibility.”

## To the Regional State Government

I agree that the company has a strategic role in the region. I do **not** agree that this can be used to block all structural change.

The state is right to insist on social responsibility. It is wrong if that becomes a veto against necessary adaptation.

A workable solution must preserve a meaningful German industrial presence, yes. But it must also restore competitiveness. If those two objectives are not balanced, then the long-term damage will be worse.

## To the institutional investors

You are right about the financial test:

- no cosmetic compromise,
- no endless delay,
- no value destruction through conflict.

But I would caution you as well: “decisive” does not automatically mean “closure-heavy.” Decisive means credible and executable.

If a negotiated package can deliver:
- capacity reduction,
- labour-cost savings,
- and a visible margin path,

then it deserves support. If not, it does not.

## My position

I will support a settlement only if it satisfies these conditions:

- **durable competitiveness restoration**
- **real structural savings**
- **no value-destructive escalation**
- **no permanent impairment of enterprise value**
- **no weakening of ownership governance control**

That last point matters. I will not back any path that undermines the family holding’s governance position or leaves the company structurally weaker for the long term.

## What I am prepared to accept

I am prepared to accept:
- voluntary exits,
- redeployment,
- qualification,
- working-time flexibility,
- temporary employee contributions,
- targeted capacity reductions,
- and a protected core of strategically important operations.

I am not prepared to accept:
- a blanket guarantee that nothing can close,
- a permanent ban on compulsory redundancies,
- or a restructuring that is too weak to restore margins.

## Bottom line

The company needs real restructuring, not slogans.  
The workforce cannot be asked to carry everything alone.  
But the owners will not support a settlement that leaves the enterprise structurally impaired.

If there is a package that genuinely closes the competitiveness gap through negotiated, measurable, and durable measures, I am open to it.

If the answer is simply “no closures, no layoffs, and hope for the best,” then I do not support that.
