I’ll be blunt: the works council is right on the core red lines, and management is still trying to turn a structural problem into a workforce bill.

## Where I agree

I agree with the works council, the state government, and even parts of what management and investors are saying:

- The company does need a real industrial plan.
- Capacity has to be reduced in some form.
- Future investment in products and technology is essential.
- Open-ended conflict would destroy value for everyone.

And yes, the workforce is prepared to contribute — **but only in a package that is fair, shared, and durable**.

## Where I disagree

### 1. Plant closures are not an acceptable “default”
Management keeps saying closures must remain on the table. I understand that as a negotiating statement, but I do **not** accept it as the starting point for a settlement.

Why? Because once you make closures the opening threat, everything else becomes coercive bargaining. That is not a serious foundation for trust, and it pushes the burden onto employees before alternatives are even properly tested.

If closures are ever to be considered, management would have to prove, transparently and quantitatively, that every site-preserving alternative has failed. We are nowhere near that standard yet.

### 2. Compulsory redundancies are not acceptable
I want to be equally clear here: **no compulsory redundancies**.

Not because restructuring is easy, but because forced layoffs are the wrong answer to a competitiveness crisis. They destroy skills, morale, and the company’s capacity to recover. They also amount to asking workers to pay for strategic failures they did not cause.

If management wants labor flexibility, then the tools are:
- voluntary exits,
- natural attrition,
- working-time adjustments,
- redeployment,
- qualification and training,
- and phased capacity reduction.

Those are legitimate negotiation topics. Forced layoffs are not.

### 3. “The workforce must contribute materially” is true — but not alone
I agree with the investor and owner side on one thing: symbolism will not close the gap. There must be real savings.

But I reject the idea that “real savings” automatically means deeper cuts in jobs and sites. There are other ways to generate durable relief:
- temporary or partial wage concessions,
- forgoing bonuses,
- working-time flexibility,
- deferred compensation elements,
- productivity agreements,
- and a restructuring contribution from the workforce **if** management, owners, and the company itself also contribute.

That is the only fair formula. Workers cannot be the sole shock absorber.

### 4. A rigid job guarantee is not “freezing the problem”
To the shareholder side: no, a job guarantee is not just a sentimental demand. It is the condition that makes workforce participation possible.

If employees are asked to accept a contribution to competitiveness, they need:
- binding site security,
- binding job security through 2030,
- and a credible investment commitment for the German plants.

Without that, “cooperation” just means workers surrender bargaining power while management keeps discretion.

### 5. Co-determination is not decorative
To management: co-determination is not a box to tick after the plan is built. If employee representatives are supposed to help secure a durable solution, they must be involved from the start, and the outcome must be negotiable, not pre-decided.

## What would move the union

I have already signaled that we are open to a **shared-sacrifice package**. That remains true. But it has to meet these conditions:

- no compulsory redundancies,
- no plant closures as part of the starting package,
- preservation of the collective wage framework,
- binding site and job guarantees,
- real investment commitments,
- and a fair burden-sharing contribution from management and owners.

If those conditions are met, then we can discuss:
- labour-cost relief,
- capacity reductions,
- working-time instruments,
- voluntary exit programs,
- productivity measures,
- and temporary concessions.

## What I want answered now

Management needs to stop talking in abstractions and put numbers on the table:

1. What is the exact cost gap?
2. What savings come from each proposed measure?
3. How much capacity reduction is really needed?
4. What savings are expected from management, owners, and capital-side contributions?
5. What is the concrete investment plan for German sites?

If the company claims there is no alternative to closures, then it should show the math. Until then, I treat that claim as pressure, not proof.

## Bottom line

The union will not accept a restructuring in which:
- workers carry all the pain,
- closures are used as leverage from the outset,
- forced layoffs are imposed,
- and the collective framework is weakened.

But I am not closing the door on an agreement. I am saying the agreement must be **fair, negotiated, and secure**.

If management wants industrial peace, it needs to earn it with real burden-sharing and real guarantees. If not, then it should expect resistance — including warning strikes and escalation.
