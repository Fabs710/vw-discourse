From a plant-level industrial and cost-modelling perspective, the works council’s core question is:

**Can a non-closure restructuring package, without forced redundancies, plausibly close the competitiveness gap while preserving required investment?**

## Short answer
**Yes, in principle — but only if the gap is moderate and the package is deep, durable, and quantitatively disciplined.**  
If the structural gap is large, then **non-closure measures alone are often not enough** unless they are combined with:
- substantial working-time reduction or flexibility,
- meaningful labor-cost relief,
- very large voluntary exit volumes,
- major productivity gains,
- and/or cross-site capacity reallocation.

What I cannot say from the information provided is whether the gap here is small enough for that. That depends on the missing numbers.

## Industrial logic
To assess this properly, you need to compare the expected annual savings from the non-closure package against the annual savings implied by closure/redundancy options.

The relevant cost buckets are:

1. **Fixed plant costs**
   - depreciation
   - maintenance
   - site overhead
   - utilities base load
   - logistics infrastructure
   - plant management
   - selected maintenance/IT/security costs

2. **Labor costs**
   - direct wages
   - indirect labor
   - shift premiums
   - bonuses
   - social costs
   - temporary labor
   - overtime

3. **Capacity utilization effects**
   - underutilized plants carry high unit cost
   - closing a plant removes a large fixed-cost burden
   - shifting volume to other plants can reduce unit cost if those sites have spare capacity

4. **One-time restructuring costs**
   - severance
   - transfer and retraining
   - tooling relocation
   - supply-chain changes
   - ramp-up losses at receiving plants
   - write-downs of stranded assets

A non-closure package has to offset not only the recurring operating gap, but also the company’s need to fund future products and capex. That is why “saving jobs” by itself is not the metric. The real test is **annual recurring cash cost per vehicle, per site, and group-wide**.

## What a credible non-closure package would have to include
If forced redundancies and closures are excluded, the package usually needs most of the following:

### 1) Capacity reduction without formal closure
- one or more shifts removed
- line consolidation
- product assignment changes
- volume transfer to higher-efficiency sites
- slower output at structurally weak plants

This can save cost, but only if the receiving sites have enough slack and logistics cost does not explode.

### 2) Labor-cost relief
Examples:
- temporary wage restraint
- bonus deferral or reduction
- reduction in supplements
- working-time accounts
- lower paid hours
- longer weekly hours for the same base pay in some cases
- suspension or modification of legacy guarantees

This is often the biggest lever short of closure, but it must be durable to matter.

### 3) Voluntary attrition and exit programs
- retirement bridges
- voluntary severance
- internal transfer
- qualification-based redeployment

This can reduce headcount without forced redundancies, but the pace is limited. It rarely delivers enough fast enough if the overcapacity is very large.

### 4) Productivity and organization measures
- line balancing
- automation
- indirect labor reduction
- supplier footprint changes
- maintenance rationalization
- logistics simplification

These help, but they usually take time and capex.

### 5) Investment commitments tied to site specialization
A plant can be saved if it is assigned a clearer product role and gets the investment needed to be competitive.  
But this only works if the resulting volume is sufficient to absorb fixed costs.

## When non-closure is economically plausible
A non-closure solution becomes plausible if all of the following are true:

- the company’s structural gap is **not too large**,
- at least one or two plants have **credible specialization cases**,
- there is enough **spare capacity elsewhere** to absorb volume,
- labor can deliver a **durable** contribution,
- and the company is prepared to accept a **multi-year transition** rather than immediate full savings.

In automotive, that usually means a package that reduces cost enough to bring the core business back toward target margin **after** some short-term inefficiency from transition.

## When non-closure is usually not enough
It is usually not enough if:

- several German plants are materially underutilized,
- fixed-cost absorption is structurally poor,
- the product mix is weak or declining,
- labor cost per vehicle is far above peer sites,
- and management needs rapid, large-scale annual savings.

In that case, “no closures, no forced redundancies” can be economically inconsistent unless the workforce contribution is exceptionally large and the company is willing to accept lower near-term returns.

## What I would ask for before judging viability
To answer this rigorously, I would need:

1. **Site-by-site P&Ls**
   - annual output
   - direct labor hours per vehicle
   - overhead allocation
   - energy and logistics cost
   - capex needs

2. **Capacity utilization by plant**
   - current utilization
   - feasible post-restructuring utilization
   - receivable volume transfer options

3. **Savings map by measure**
   - labor-cost measures
   - capacity reduction measures
   - voluntary exit volumes
   - productivity measures
   - investment costs

4. **One-time restructuring cost**
   - severance and transfer
   - relocation
   - write-downs
   - ramp-up losses

5. **Target gap**
   - how many euros per year must be saved
   - or how many margin points must be recovered

Without that, any claim that closures are “necessary” or that they are “avoidable” is not yet proven.

## My judgment
Based on industrial logic alone:

- **A non-closure settlement is technically possible.**
- **It is not automatically economically sufficient.**
- Whether it works depends on the size of the savings gap, the level of overcapacity, and the depth of labor-cost and capacity measures available.
- If the required recurring savings are very large, then **excluding closures and forced redundancies may force the company into a weaker cost position**, unless the workforce contribution and investment-led specialization are unusually strong.

## Bottom line
The works council is right on one narrow point:  
**A credible alternatives test is necessary before any closure conclusion is accepted.**

But from a restructuring-cost perspective, the test must be numerical, not rhetorical. The company should present:
- required savings,
- site economics,
- recurring savings by measure,
- one-time costs,
- and the effect on investment capacity.
