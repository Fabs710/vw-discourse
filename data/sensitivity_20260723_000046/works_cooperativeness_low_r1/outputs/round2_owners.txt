I will respond directly.

## To the works council and the union
I understand why you are drawing hard lines on closures, compulsory redundancies, and job security. A drawn-out conflict would damage the company, and nobody should pretend otherwise. On that point, we agree.

But I do **not** agree that the answer is to rule out structural change in advance.

### Where I agree
- The company needs a negotiated solution, not chaos.
- Industrial peace has value.
- Capacity has to be reduced somehow.
- Investment in future products and technology is essential.
- Co-determination must be respected.

### Where I disagree
- **“No plant closures” as an absolute is not acceptable** if the numbers do not work otherwise.
- **“No compulsory redundancies” as an absolute is not acceptable** if the cost gap cannot be closed any other way.
- A **binding job guarantee through 2030** is not credible if it freezes a structure that remains uncompetitive.

You keep asking for proof that alternatives fail. That is a fair request, but I do not accept the implication that closure is merely a bluff. It is a real option if the economically sufficient package cannot be delivered otherwise.

## To the state government
I appreciate your insistence on regional stability and your resistance to rash closures. That is understandable and, in principle, reasonable.

But I do **not** accept a position that turns “last resort” into “practically never.” This company cannot be treated as if every site has an equal right to survive regardless of competitiveness.

### Where I agree
- The burden cannot fall on labour alone.
- Management must show the numbers.
- Site-preserving alternatives must be examined seriously.
- Industrial conflict would destroy value.

### Where I disagree
- A closure must remain available if it is genuinely necessary.
- The state cannot have an effective veto over economically necessary restructuring through informal pressure.
- Political discomfort is not a substitute for a viable business case.

## To management
I am aligned with your diagnosis: the current cost base and overcapacity are not sustainable. But I want to be equally clear that **threatening closures is not the same as presenting a credible restructuring plan**.

If you want support from the controlling shareholder, the supervisory board, and the other governance stakeholders, you need to put forward a **quantified package**. Not slogans, not generalized pressure.

I would expect:
1. the exact cost gap to be closed,
2. the savings contribution of each measure,
3. the employment and capacity effects by site,
4. the timing to restore margins,
5. and the contingency if the non-closure measures underdeliver.

Without that, the argument remains incomplete.

## To the institutional investors
You are right to insist on numbers and to reject a cosmetic solution. That part I support.

But I also want to caution against an investor view that treats closures as valuable in themselves. They are not. The only test that matters is whether the package restores competitiveness and durable returns.

I will not support:
- a symbolic settlement,
- open-ended conflict,
- or a package that preserves the current cost base under another label.

## My position as controlling shareholder
Let me be explicit.

I will support a serious restructuring **only if** it delivers:
- durable labour-cost reduction,
- meaningful and measurable capacity reduction,
- a credible path back to target margins,
- investment commitments tied to the restructuring,
- and enough stability to implement the plan.

I am open to alternatives to closures if they are **economically equivalent**. That could include:
- working-time changes,
- voluntary exits,
- redeployment,
- site specialization,
- productivity agreements,
- temporary concessions,
- and phased capacity rationalization.

But I will **not** support a settlement that:
- rules out closures regardless of economics,
- rules out compulsory redundancies regardless of the savings gap,
- and leaves management unable to restore competitiveness.

## What I need next
Before anyone asks me to choose between “closures” and “trust,” I want a proper scenario comparison:
- cost gap by measure,
- savings by site and by instrument,
- what labour contributes,
- what management and owners contribute,
- and where the break point is if the negotiated route fails.

If the employee side can come back with a package that truly closes the gap without closures, I will consider it seriously. If not, then we should stop pretending that an absolute no-closure line is a viable answer.

## Bottom line
I am not here to preserve every legacy structure at any cost, and I am not here to trigger value-destroying instability either.

My standard is simple:
- **real restructuring,**
- **real competitiveness,**
- **real burden sharing,**
- **real stability.**

If a negotiated plan can achieve that without closures, fine. If it cannot, then closures and deeper measures remain on the table. That is the reality.
