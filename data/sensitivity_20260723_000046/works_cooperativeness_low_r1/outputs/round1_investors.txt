From the perspective of dispersed institutional investors, the key issue is not who “wins” this confrontation; it is whether the company restores a credible path to competitive margins and an investable return profile. Right now, the market will punish delay, cosmetic fixes, and open-ended conflict.

## Where I agree

I agree with management, the controlling family, and the state on one central point: **the current cost base is too high and the company needs durable structural improvement**. The brand cannot keep missing its return-on-sales target while carrying excess capacity and an uncompetitive German footprint.

I also agree with the works council and the union that:
- prolonged industrial conflict destroys value,
- a negotiated solution is preferable to a drawn-out strike,
- and future investment in products and technology matters.

But agreement on the diagnosis does not mean agreement on all the red lines being drawn.

## Where I disagree with the employee side

The works council’s and union’s “no closures, no compulsory redundancies, no weakening of protections” position is understandable politically, but from an investor perspective it is **not sufficient unless it can be shown to deliver equivalent economic effect**.

If the outcome preserves an uncompetitive cost base, then it is not a solution — it is a postponement. And postponement has real costs:
- weaker margins,
- delayed investment,
- more pressure on share price,
- and a higher probability of even harsher action later.

So I would be blunt: **I will not support a settlement that protects every site and every legacy guarantee if that leaves the company structurally impaired**.

At the same time, I do not think management should mistake investor support for a blank cheque for closures. Closures may be necessary, but they are not valuable in themselves. What matters is whether the package actually closes the gap on a durable basis.

## Where I disagree with management

Management is right to insist that the restructuring has to be real. But I would caution against a posture that relies too heavily on closure threats without showing the numbers behind the alternative paths.

If the company cannot demonstrate:
- what savings come from each measure,
- how much capacity is being removed,
- what labour contribution is expected,
- how quickly margins improve,
- and what the endpoint looks like,

then the market will conclude that this is a negotiation tactic, not a strategy.

That is damaging because uncertainty itself destroys value. Investors can tolerate decisive restructuring; they cannot tolerate strategic ambiguity.

So management needs to do more than say “all options are on the table.” It needs to show:
- the target cost base,
- the required reduction in capacity,
- the financial effect of each scenario,
- and the implementation timeline.

Without that, neither the capital markets nor the employee side can assess whether the proposal is serious.

## Where I disagree with the state and the family

I understand the state’s political constraints and the family’s long-term stewardship argument. But neither can be a reason to water down restructuring until it becomes ineffective.

A statement that closures are a “last resort” is fine. Turning that into a de facto veto over meaningful structural change is not. The same applies to long job guarantees that lock in the present structure regardless of market reality.

The company cannot be asked to carry politically preferred outcomes if those outcomes keep margins structurally below target. That would be a transfer of risk from labour and regional actors onto shareholders without an adequate return.

## What would move us

We would support a package that is clearly tied to value creation, with:
- **durable labour-cost reduction**,
- **real and measurable capacity rationalization**,
- **a credible timeline back to target margins**,
- **investment commitments focused on profitable future products**,
- and **enough certainty to prevent a prolonged strike or recurring conflict**.

We are open on the form that takes. It could include:
- working-time changes,
- voluntary exits,
- site specialization,
- productivity measures,
- and selective closures if necessary.

But it must add up financially. The test is simple: does it restore competitiveness and protect enterprise value?

## What is unacceptable

What we will not support:
- a cosmetic settlement with no material savings,
- a prolonged dispute that burns value without improving competitiveness,
- or a package that keeps the company trapped in structural underperformance.

We are patient capital, but not indifferent capital. If the company cannot show a credible route to competitive margins, we will treat this as a deterioration in investment case, not a restructuring success.

## Bottom line

Our position is conditional but firm:

- **Yes** to a serious restructuring that materially improves margins and competitiveness.
- **No** to solutions that preserve the existing cost structure under a different label.
- **No** to open-ended conflict that destroys value.
- **No** to “social peace” if it comes at the expense of durable economic viability.

If management can bring forward a quantified, credible, and decisive package, we can support it. If not, then the market will rightly conclude that the company is still avoiding the real adjustment.
