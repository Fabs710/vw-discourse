We are aligned on the diagnosis, but not yet on the instrument mix.

## Where I agree
- The company has a real structural competitiveness problem.
- The current cost base is too high for the market environment.
- Capacity must come down in a durable way.
- Open-ended industrial conflict would destroy value.
- Management should not be forced to improvise a rescue without quantified scenarios.

## Where I disagree

### 1) “No closures” as an absolute is not acceptable
To the works council, union, and state: I understand why you are resisting closures, but an absolute prohibition is not investable if it leaves the company structurally impaired.

If the numbers show that some sites are uncompetitive and that no alternative can close the gap, then closures must remain available. Not as a threat for its own sake, but as a real option. Capital markets will not support a package that preserves all sites if the result is still subscale margins and chronic underperformance.

### 2) “No compulsory redundancies” is not a valid blanket rule unless the savings are proven
I am open to avoiding forced layoffs if management and labour can deliver enough through:
- voluntary exits,
- redeployment,
- working-time changes,
- productivity gains,
- site specialization,
- and durable labour-cost relief.

But if those measures do not close the gap, then ruling out redundancies in advance simply shifts the burden onto shareholders through lower returns and delayed recovery. That is not acceptable.

### 3) Job and site guarantees cannot be allowed to freeze the structure
I do not support long binding guarantees through 2030 unless they are paired with a package that actually restores competitiveness. A guarantee is not value creation by itself. If it prevents necessary adjustment, it is a liability, not reassurance.

### 4) Management still needs to quantify the case
To management: “all options on the table” is not enough. Investors need a proper bridge from today’s cost base to target margins:
- exact cost gap,
- savings by instrument,
- impact by site,
- timeline to return-on-sales target,
- and what happens if the negotiated route underdelivers.

Without that, the market will assume the plan is still incomplete.

## What would move us
We would support a package that is clearly:
- **durable**: not temporary cosmetics,
- **quantified**: with real savings and a clear endpoint,
- **competitive**: restoring margins to target,
- **stable**: not inviting recurring conflict,
- **balanced**: with meaningful contributions from labour, management, and owners.

That package could include:
- capacity rationalization,
- working-time flexibility,
- voluntary exit programmes,
- productivity agreements,
- selective plant exits if economically necessary,
- and investment commitments tied to the restructuring.

What we will not support is a cosmetic settlement that protects every legacy structure and simply defers the reckoning.

## To the works council and union
You are right that the workforce should not bear the whole burden. I agree. But the answer cannot be to insist on full site and job immutability while demanding investors keep funding the shortfall. That would be a transfer of value from shareholders to labour without a credible return.

## To the state and family
Your insistence on social stability is understandable, but it cannot become a de facto veto over economically necessary restructuring. “Last resort” is reasonable; “effectively never” is not.

## Bottom line
Our support is conditional, not automatic:

- **Yes** to serious restructuring that materially improves competitiveness.
- **Yes** to negotiated solutions if they close the cost gap in a durable way.
- **No** to packages that preserve an uncompetitive structure under a softer label.
- **No** to prolonged conflict that destroys value.
- **No** to guarantees or red lines that prevent the company from restoring margins.

If management brings a quantified package showing how the company gets back to competitive returns, we can support it. If not, then the market will treat this as delay, not restructuring.
