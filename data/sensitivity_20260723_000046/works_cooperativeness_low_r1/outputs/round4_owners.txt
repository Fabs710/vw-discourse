I will be direct.

## Where I agree
There is now no dispute that this company needs a **real restructuring**, not a cosmetic one. I agree with:
- the diagnosis of structural overcapacity,
- the need for durable cost reductions,
- the need for future investment,
- and the need to avoid open-ended industrial conflict.

I also agree with the expert’s narrow but important point: **without plant-level numbers, nobody can honestly prove that non-closure measures are sufficient or insufficient**. That is exactly why the process must become quantitative, not rhetorical.

## Where I disagree
I do **not** accept the employee side’s attempt to turn “no closures” and “no compulsory redundancies” into absolute starting constraints.

That may be understandable from a workforce and political perspective, but it is not yet compatible with restoring competitiveness if the savings gap is large. A company with a structural cost problem cannot be told in advance that the hard instruments are off the table regardless of the numbers.

### To the works council
You keep saying closures are a historic breach and that co-determination is not decorative. On the second point, I agree completely: co-determination must be respected.

On the first, I disagree. The fact that closures are painful does not make them illegitimate if the economics ultimately require them. You say management has not proven necessity. Fine — then prove it. But do not assume that proof will automatically point to a site-preserving solution.

What I cannot accept is a restructuring framework that:
- rules out closures in advance,
- rules out compulsory redundancies in advance,
- and then expects the company to absorb a major competitiveness gap through goodwill alone.

That is not a solution. It is a constraint that may preserve comfort while eroding value.

### To the union
I agree that the workforce should not carry the whole burden. I also agree that voluntary exits, working-time flexibility, redeployment, and temporary labor-cost relief are legitimate tools.

But I do **not** accept binding job and site guarantees through 2030 as a blanket precondition. If such guarantees freeze an uncompetitive structure, they may protect today’s anxiety while damaging the company’s long-term viability. That would hurt owners, workers, and the regional base alike.

Your contribution must be real, but it must also be matched by a restructuring that actually closes the gap. Shared sacrifice is necessary; burden transfer is not.

### To the regional state government
I understand your responsibility for regional stability, and I respect the role of the state under the special company statute.

But I do **not** accept a posture that effectively turns “site-preserving first” into a veto over economically necessary action. A review process is fine. A process that permanently insulates German sites from closure risk regardless of performance is not.

You are right that a proper audit is needed. But the audit must be used to test viability, not to pre-decide that closures are politically forbidden.

### To the controlling family
I agree with the family-side insistence on long-term value preservation. That is my core concern as well. But preserving value does **not** mean preserving every legacy structure.

A settlement that avoids hard measures today but leaves the company structurally weaker tomorrow is not stewardship. Nor is a settlement that creates prolonged conflict and reputational damage. That is why I support a disciplined, evidence-based restructuring — but not one that self-handicaps by excluding necessary tools.

### To institutional investors
You are right to insist on durability and investability. I agree that a cosmetic deal would destroy value.

But I also disagree with any posture that treats closures and deeper headcount cuts as merely normal financial instruments with no strategic downside. They have major downside, and that is why they should only be used if the numbers show that less disruptive options cannot close the gap.

Your demand for quantified proof is legitimate. Your impatience with indefinite delay is also legitimate. But the answer is not a symbolic settlement. The answer is a hard, numerical test.

## On the expert assessment
The expert has helped clarify the central issue: a non-closure solution is **technically possible in principle**, but only if the gap is not too large and the package is deep enough.

That does **not** justify ruling out closures. It just confirms what I have been saying from the outset: we need the numbers.

So yes, I support an independent audit. But the audit must be designed to answer a real question: **can the company restore competitiveness without closures and without forced redundancies, or not?**

[SUMMON: independent industrial restructuring audit team with site-by-site P&L access, capacity-utilization data, recurring savings by measure, one-time restructuring costs, and investment requirements]

The audit must produce:
- the exact annual savings gap,
- savings by site and by instrument,
- one-time costs by scenario,
- investment requirements,
- and a clear break point at which non-closure measures fail.

## On the proposed compromise models

### 1) Time-limited no-closure / no-forced-redundancy phase
This is the closest thing to a workable process, but only if it is genuinely time-limited, measured, and tied to objective outcomes.

I can support a defined first phase if it includes:
- transparent numbers,
- real savings targets,
- workforce contribution,
- management and owner contribution,
- and a hard review gate.

What I cannot accept is an open-ended no-closure pledge that simply defers the problem.

### 2) Binding guarantees only for selected core sites
I do **not** support a two-tier settlement that protects a few plants and leaves others exposed. That may be administratively convenient, but it creates a permanent hierarchy of insecurity and invites fragmentation.

### 3) “No compulsory redundancies first”
I can support prioritizing voluntary tools. But only if there is a credible second step if the package underdelivers. If the second step is removed in advance, the first step becomes a lock-in to underperformance.

## My position, unchanged
Let me state it plainly:

- I support a serious restructuring.
- I support negotiated solutions if they restore competitiveness.
- I support burden-sharing from all sides.
- I support an independent, site-by-site alternatives test.
- I do **not** support absolute no-closure or no-redundancy rules in advance.
- I will not back any package that leaves the company structurally uncompetitive.

If the employee side can produce an economically equivalent package that closes the gap without closures, I will consider it seriously. If it cannot, then deeper measures must remain available.

That is the responsible position for a long-term owner.
