I will be equally direct.

The state government agrees that the company needs a serious restructuring. We do **not** agree that the only credible route is to threaten closures and force the workforce into accepting a one-sided adjustment.

## Where I agree
I agree with management, the family, and investors on one point: the status quo is not sustainable. The company needs:
- a lower cost base,
- a more efficient capacity structure,
- and investment in future products.

I also agree with the works council and the union that:
- this cannot be imposed unilaterally,
- industrial conflict would damage everyone,
- and the company’s future must be negotiated, not dictated.

## Where I disagree with management
Management keeps saying that if the employee side does not accept a very deep package, closures remain necessary. I do **not** accept that as a proper starting point.

You have still not shown that you have exhausted the site-preserving alternatives. Before speaking of closures, I want to see a **credible quantified comparison** of:
1. capacity reductions without plant exits,
2. shift and working-time adjustments,
3. internal redeployment and qualification,
4. voluntary programmes and natural attrition,
5. productivity and process improvements,
6. and contributions from management and owners.

If those do not close the gap, then we can discuss what remains. But today, the closure threat is still being used as a pressure tactic, not as a proven last resort.

## Where I disagree with the controlling family and investors
You both speak the language of value preservation, which I understand. But value is not preserved by destroying regional industrial capacity and social trust.

An absolute or quasi-absolute stance that says “closures must remain on the table from the outset” is not compatible with the special responsibility that comes with this company’s footprint and governance structure. We are not discussing a normal portfolio adjustment. We are discussing a company that is a regional anchor employer with deep public consequences.

And to be clear: I do **not** accept a restructuring logic that treats the workforce as the primary shock absorber while management, ownership, and the capital side remain protected.

## Where I disagree with the employee side
I support the core demand for:
- no compulsory redundancies as a starting point,
- binding site security,
- real investment commitments,
- and strong co-determination.

But I also need to say this: if the employee side insists that **every German site must remain untouched regardless of economics**, that will not be enough either. The company does need real structural adjustment. The question is **how** we achieve it without gutting the regional base.

So I am not endorsing denial. I am endorsing a **site-preserving restructuring test**: every alternative to closure must be genuinely examined, quantified, and negotiated first.

## What I can support
The state can support a package if it contains all of the following:
- **no plant closures as long as site-preserving alternatives can achieve the required savings**,  
- **no compulsory redundancies**,  
- **material contributions from management and owners**,  
- **capacity reductions through reversible and negotiated measures first**,  
- **binding investment commitments for German plants**,  
- **and a serious role for co-determination in shaping the implementation**.

That is not sentimental. It is the minimum basis for an economically credible and politically durable settlement.

## What I cannot support
I cannot support:
- closures as the opening move,
- a package that shifts the bulk of adjustment onto workers,
- a restructuring that weakens the state’s veto role or sidelines co-determination,
- or a plan that sacrifices regional employment without proving necessity.

## To management: what I need next
If you want the state to engage constructively, then bring a **scenario-based package** with numbers:
- cost gap by measure,
- capacity reduction by site,
- employment impact,
- investment commitments,
- and the point at which a non-closure package actually fails.

Without that, the discussion is still abstract, and closures remain an unproven threat.

## Bottom line
We are prepared to back a tough but fair restructuring. We are **not** prepared to back a plan that makes closures the default answer or turns the state’s co-determination role into a formality.

If there is a negotiated, site-preserving path that restores competitiveness, we will support it. If there is not, then the burden is on management to justify why not.
