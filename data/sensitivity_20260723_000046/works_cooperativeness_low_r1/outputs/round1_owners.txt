I appreciate that all sides are finally speaking in terms of a real industrial solution, not slogans. But I need to be equally clear: from the perspective of the controlling shareholder, **value preservation and competitiveness are non-negotiable**. If the company does not restore margins and structural resilience, there will be no durable jobs, no future investment capacity, and no real ownership value to protect.

## Where I agree

I agree with management, the works council, the union, and the state on one central point: **this cannot be solved by denial or delay**. The current cost base and overcapacity are not sustainable.

I also agree that:
- a negotiated solution is preferable to a destructive escalation,
- the company needs a credible future investment path,
- and co-determination is a structural reality that has to be respected.

On that basis, I am not dismissing social stability as a side issue. It matters. Prolonged conflict would destroy value.

## Where I disagree

### 1. “No plant closures” is not a credible absolute
I understand why employees and the state are drawing a red line. But from an ownership and governance perspective, **an absolute ban on closures is not acceptable if the economics do not work otherwise**.

If a site is structurally uncompetitive and the alternatives do not close the gap, then pretending that capacity can be preserved at any cost is not stewardship — it is value destruction. The controlling family cannot support a formula that merely protects every legacy structure while the business erodes.

That said, I am **not** saying closures are the preferred answer. I am saying they must remain available if they are genuinely necessary and if a negotiated alternative cannot deliver equivalent savings.

### 2. A job guarantee through 2030 is too rigid if it freezes the problem
I hear the request for certainty. But a long binding guarantee, without a credible and enforceable restructuring package, would simply lock in today’s imbalance and push the burden into the future. That would not be responsible ownership.

### 3. The burden cannot fall only on labour — but labour cannot be exempt
I agree with the state that management and owners must share responsibility. But I do not accept a framing in which the workforce contributes only if everything else is already protected. The company needs a meaningful contribution from labour if it is to restore competitiveness.

That contribution does not have to mean mass layoffs. But it does have to mean **material, durable cost relief**, not symbolic measures.

## What would move us

A settlement becomes credible only if it contains all of the following:

- **substantial and durable labour-cost reduction**,  
- **real capacity reduction** that is economically meaningful,  
- **credible productivity and flexibility gains**,  
- **a path to restore returns to target**,  
- **investment commitments tied to the restructuring**,  
- and **labour peace for a defined period**, so the company can execute.

That is the standard by which I would judge any proposal.

## On alternatives to closures

I am open to alternatives if they are real, measurable, and sufficient. That could include:
- reduced shifts or lines,
- working-time adjustments,
- redeployment,
- voluntary exit programs,
- site specialization,
- and phased capacity rationalization.

But I want to be plain: **“we will find the savings elsewhere” is not enough** unless someone shows how the numbers actually close. The controlling shareholder will not endorse cosmetic savings packaged as transformation.

## On co-determination and governance
No one is being asked to bypass the supervisory board or the workforce representation. But co-determination is not a veto over economic reality. If the proposal does not restore competitiveness, it is not a viable governance outcome.

At the same time, any attempt to force through an approach that permanently damages the enterprise would also be unacceptable. The family holding is a long-term steward, not a trader looking for a quick exit. We want a viable company years from now, not just a headline today.

## Bottom line

I will support a **serious restructuring package** only if it is:
- economically sufficient,
- socially manageable,
- and stable enough to survive implementation.

I will **not** support:
- a purely defensive package that preserves all current structures,
- a guarantee that makes real adjustment impossible,
- or a settlement that leaves management unable to restore margins.

So my message to all parties is this: bring forward a plan that closes the cost and capacity gap in a durable way. If there is a credible, negotiated route that does that without closures, I will look at it seriously. If there is not, then we should not pretend that status quo preservation is a responsible option.
