I will be equally clear.

## My position
I support a serious restructuring. I do **not** support a restructuring that normalizes plant closures in Germany, compulsory redundancies, or the weakening of the state’s co-determination and veto role.

That is the line.

We are not here to preserve every legacy cost structure at any price. But we are also not here to let the regional industrial base be dismantled under the label of “competitiveness.”

---

## To management
I agree with you on one point: the company needs durable savings, not cosmetics.

I also agree that:
- capacity has to come down,
- future investment is necessary,
- and a negotiated solution is preferable to open conflict.

Where I disagree is your repeated insistence that closures and deeper headcount cuts must remain freely available from the outset.

That may be an acceptable shareholder instinct in a normal business. It is **not** acceptable here if it means gutting German employment and sidelining the governance rights of an anchor shareholder with a statutory blocking minority.

You have still not shown, in a transparent and auditable way, that:
- non-closure measures fail site by site,
- voluntary exits and redeployment cannot close the gap,
- or that the only path is to threaten closures first and ask for concessions later.

So no: I do not accept closure-first bargaining.

---

## To the works council
I support your insistence that workers should not be the sole shock absorber.

I agree with you that:
- the company needs restructuring,
- future investment matters,
- and a cosmetic deal would be worthless.

Where I am more cautious is your absolute framing of “no closures, no compulsory redundancies, no exceptions.” I understand why you say it, but I do not think the conversation can stop there if the numbers genuinely show a serious structural gap.

That said, I am **not** moving to accept closures by default. Quite the opposite: any serious process must begin with a proof-first, site-by-site test of alternatives. Management has not earned a blank cheque.

So I support your demand for transparency and a real audit. I do **not** support a process that uses your workforce contribution while keeping the closure threat as a negotiating club.

---

## To the industrial union
I agree with your principle that a workforce contribution must be matched by management and owner contributions.

That is fair.

I also agree that:
- voluntary exits,
- working-time flexibility,
- redeployment,
- productivity measures,
- and temporary labor-cost relief

are legitimate tools.

Where I disagree is if “no closures as the starting point” becomes too weak a constraint to protect jobs in practice. For this company, in this state, the issue is not just financial efficiency. It is industrial stability, skills retention, regional economic anchoring, and social peace.

So if labour is asked to contribute, it must be within a framework that protects the sites and preserves meaningful security. I will not support a deal that gives workers less security while asking them to pay more.

---

## To the controlling family holding
I recognize your concern for value preservation. But I disagree with the idea that value can be protected by keeping closure risk permanently on the table as leverage.

A settlement that destabilizes the workforce, damages trust, and threatens the company’s regional base is not responsible ownership. It may improve short-term bargaining power; it does not necessarily improve long-term value.

I also do not accept the implication that the state should simply step aside because the family or management believes deeper cuts are always available. This company is not just a financial asset. It is a major industrial anchor with public responsibilities and a special governance structure.

If you want a viable package, then you should be prepared to support one that preserves sites and jobs as far as economically possible, not one that treats closures as the convenient fallback from the start.

---

## To institutional investors
You are right to want credibility, durability, and a path back to margin.

I agree that a fake settlement would be worse than no settlement. I also agree that the company must remain investable.

Where I disagree is the assumption that closures and forced headcount reduction are just neutral instruments. They are not neutral here. They carry industrial, political, and social costs that can easily exceed the short-term accounting benefit if handled badly.

I would accept a tough restructuring if it is:
- numerically sound,
- durable,
- and balanced across all stakeholders.

I will not accept a deal that treats the state, sites, and workforce as disposable to satisfy an abstract margin target.

## On the proposed compromise models

### 1) Time-limited no-closure / no-forced-redundancy phase
This is the only framework that is even remotely serious at this stage.

I could support a defined phase if it includes:
- no German plant closures during the phase,
- no compulsory redundancies during the phase,
- capacity reduction through shifts, redeployment, voluntary exits, and working-time instruments,
- binding investment commitments,
- and an independent audit with real data access.

But I will be very clear: this cannot be a disguised path to reopen closures later as soon as the workforce has conceded enough. If there is a review, it must be a review of how to deepen a site-preserving package, not a pretext for shutdowns.

### 2) Binding guarantees only for “core sites”
I do **not** support a two-tier settlement that protects a few sites and leaves others exposed.

That would divide the workforce, weaken trust, and create a permanent hierarchy of insecurity. It may look administratively neat; it is politically and socially corrosive.

The state’s position is broader:
- preserve the company as a regional anchor,
- protect employment in the state as far as possible,
- and avoid a restructuring that picks winners and abandons the rest without exhausting alternatives.

### 3) “No compulsory redundancies first”
I can support prioritizing voluntary instruments. But only if the process does **not** quietly preserve closures as the default fallback. The sequencing has to be real, not tactical.

---

## What I am prepared to support
I am prepared to support:
- working-time flexibility,
- voluntary exit programmes,
- redeployment,
- qualification and retraining,
- productivity measures,
- temporary labor-cost relief,
- and investment commitments tied to restructuring.

I am **not** prepared to support:
- plant closures as an opening assumption,
- forced layoffs as a standard tool,
- or any outcome that strips the state of its effective co-determination role.

That last point is not negotiable for me.

---

## What has to happen next
Before anyone asks for final commitments, we need:
1. the independent site-by-site audit,
2. a quantified comparison of closure-led, non-closure, and hybrid packages,
3. a real burden-sharing proposal from management and owners,
4. and a clear statement of what can be achieved without rupturing the regional industrial base.

If management’s numbers show that a non-closure package cannot work, then I will scrutinize those numbers very hard — because the consequences for this state are too serious to accept claims on faith.

But if the numbers show that a viable path exists without closures and without compulsory redundancies, then that is the path I will back.

---

## Bottom line
I am prepared to back a tough restructuring if it:
- protects core sites and employment in the state as far as economically possible,
- preserves the company as a regional anchor,
- respects co-determination,
- and delivers real competitiveness.

I am **not** prepared to support a package that uses the crisis to normalize closures, weaken the state’s role, or make labour carry the entire burden.

Bring the audit, bring the numbers, and bring a real burden-sharing proposal. Then we can talk.
