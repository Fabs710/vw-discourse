I will respond plainly.

## Where I agree with the works council
I agree that the company needs restructuring. I agree that capacity has to come down. I agree that future investment matters. And I agree that a cosmetic deal would be useless.

I also agree with the works council on the core principle: **the workforce cannot be asked to carry the entire burden while management, owners, and capital preserve all their options.**

That part is right.

## Where I disagree with the works council
I do **not** agree that an acceptable settlement can be reduced to a blanket, unconditional “no closures, no redundancies, no exceptions” line without any further economic test.

Not because I want closures. I do not.  
But because if we are going to defend jobs and the collective agreement in the long run, we need a package that is not only morally right but also **durable and financially credible**.

So my position is:

- **No plant closures as the starting point.**
- **No compulsory redundancies as the negotiating basis.**
- **But yes to a strict, independent alternatives test and a real burden-sharing package.**

That is not retreat. That is disciplined bargaining.

## To management
You are still speaking as if closures must remain available simply because you say the structure is too expensive. That is not enough.

I agree with you on one point: the company needs durable savings, not symbolic gestures. I also agree that the current cost base must come down.

Where I disagree is this:
- You have not yet proven that non-closure measures fail.
- You have not yet quantified the gap transparently enough.
- And you are still trying to make workers accept pain before management and owners have put real skin in the game.

That is not an industrial solution. That is pressure.

If you want the union to move, then show:
1. the exact annual savings gap,
2. the site-by-site economics,
3. the savings from each measure,
4. the one-time costs,
5. and the investment needed to keep the sites viable.

Until then, I am not accepting “closures remain on the table” as a substitute for proof.

## To the controlling family holding
You talk about value preservation. I agree that the company must remain competitive and viable. But I disagree with the logic that the easiest way to protect value is to keep closure threats alive from the start.

If the family wants a durable settlement, it must help fund it. That means:
- real owner-side contribution,
- not just governance pressure,
- and not just expecting labor to absorb the cost.

I can support a restructuring if owners also contribute through:
- capital support,
- restraint on distributions,
- and support for investment and site specialization.

What I will not support is a settlement where the family expects the workforce to make the big sacrifice while retaining a free hand for closures later.

## To the regional state government
I agree with your insistence on a real process and real data. I also agree that the state should not be forced into a passive role.

But I disagree if “site-preserving review” becomes a way to postpone decisions indefinitely while workers are left in uncertainty. A review only helps if it is tied to:
- a genuine no-closure first phase,
- no compulsory redundancies during that phase,
- and access to the full plant-level financial data.

So yes, I support the review — but only if it is a **closure-avoidance test**, not a closure-justification exercise.

## To institutional investors
I understand your demand for a credible margin path. I also understand that you do not want a settlement that just freezes problems in place.

But I disagree with the way you present closures and forced headcount reductions as if they were neutral instruments. They are not neutral. They have major industrial and social consequences.

You want durability? Then durability includes:
- stable employment,
- stable skills,
- stable industrial relations,
- and no permanent destruction of the wage framework.

A deal that drives conflict and weakens the workforce may look efficient on paper, but it is not necessarily durable in reality.

## To the expert / audit issue
The expert’s key point still stands: **without site-by-site numbers, nobody can honestly say whether non-closure measures are enough or not.**

That is why I support an independent audit — but with a very specific mandate.

[SUMMON: independent industrial restructuring audit team with access to plant-level P&Ls, capacity utilization, savings-by-measure modelling, and investment needs]

The audit must answer:
- What is the real annual savings gap?
- What does each non-closure measure save?
- What are the one-time restructuring costs?
- What investment is required to keep each site competitive?
- Can the gap be closed **without plant closures and without forced redundancies**?

That is the only serious basis for further bargaining.

## On the proposed compromise models
### 1) Time-limited no-closure / no-forced-redundancy phase
This is the only route I can treat as realistic right now.

I would support:
- no plant closures during a defined first phase,
- no compulsory redundancies during that phase,
- capacity reduction through working-time changes, redeployment, voluntary exits, productivity measures, and internal transfer,
- plus a binding workforce contribution.

But only if:
- management and owners also contribute,
- the deal includes binding investment,
- and the process is reviewed independently with real data.

What I will not accept is a hidden Phase 2 that simply reopens the closure threat after workers have conceded. That would be bait-and-switch bargaining.

### 2) Binding guarantees only for selected core sites
I understand why this is being floated, but I do not support dividing the workforce into protected and expendable plants.

That kind of two-tier approach would poison trust and invite fragmentation. The union’s role is to defend the collective framework, not to manage a hierarchy of survival.

### 3) “No compulsory redundancies first”
That is too weak on its own if closures remain the fallback from day one.  
I want the sequence reversed:
- first prove the need,
- first exhaust non-closure measures,
- first secure burden-sharing,
- and only then, if the numbers truly force it, revisit the question.

## What the union is prepared to put on the table
We are prepared to discuss:
- working-time flexibility,
- bonus deferrals or temporary reductions,
- voluntary exit programs,
- redeployment,
- qualification and retraining,
- productivity agreements,
- and moderated labor-cost relief.

We are **not** prepared to discuss:
- compulsory redundancies,
- plant closures as a bargaining starting point,
- or a restructuring that shifts all the downside onto workers.

That remains our line.

## My bottom line
The works council is right that workers should not be the shock absorber.  
Management is right that the company needs a real restructuring.  
The missing piece is **proof, burden-sharing, and a credible no-closure first phase**.

So here is my position, clearly:

- I support a serious restructuring.
- I support an independent, site-by-site alternatives test.
- I support temporary and bounded workforce contributions.
- I support binding owner and management contributions.
- I support a time-limited no-closure / no-forced-redundancy phase while the numbers are tested.

I do **not** support:
- plant closures as an opening assumption,
- forced layoffs,
- or a settlement in which labor pays first and everyone else keeps their escape hatch.

If management wants cooperation, it should bring a real package, not just a threat wrapped in arithmetic.
