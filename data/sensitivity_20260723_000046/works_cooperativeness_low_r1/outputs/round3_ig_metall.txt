I’ll respond directly and without softening the core issue.

## First: what the expert confirms — and what it does not
The expert says a non-closure solution is **possible in principle** if the gap is moderate and if the package is deep enough. That is important, because it directly undermines the management narrative that closures are automatically the only credible route.

But the expert also says something management and capital are trying to blur: **without the actual site-by-site numbers, nobody can honestly claim that closures are necessary.**  
So at this stage, the burden is still on management to prove necessity, not on the workforce to surrender first.

That said, I also hear the expert’s warning: if the savings gap is truly large, then a weak package of voluntary exits and symbolic concessions will not be enough. On that point, I agree. We are not offering a decorative solution.

---

## To management: your “closures remain necessary” line is still not proven
You keep saying the structural gap is large enough that closures must remain available. That may be true — but it is still an assertion, not a demonstrated conclusion.

What I disagree with is your attempt to make closures the default negotiating reality before the alternatives test is complete.

### Where I agree with you
- The company needs durable savings, not temporary optics.
- Capacity must come down.
- Labor must contribute.
- The restructuring has to be economically meaningful.

### Where I disagree
- You have not shown that site-preserving measures fail.
- You have not quantified the effect of each measure sufficiently.
- You keep treating closures and redundancies as if they are simply normal instruments, when in fact they are social and industrial rupture points.

So no: I do **not** accept your position that the proof is already in the numbers. Show the numbers by site, measure, and time horizon.

---

## To the controlling family and investors: you are still trying to shift the burden onto labour first
You both talk about value preservation and competitiveness. Fine. But the practical logic of your position is still: workers should accept insecurity now, and the company may or may not protect them later.

That is exactly what we reject.

### Where I agree
- The company must be viable.
- A cosmetic settlement would be worse than no settlement.
- Durability matters.
- Management cannot be left without tools.

### Where I disagree
- You cannot insist on closures being on the table from the outset and then call that neutral.
- You cannot demand labor-cost relief while refusing to give job and site security.
- You cannot treat co-determination as acceptable only when it endorses your preferred result.

If you want a serious workforce contribution, you need to put something durable on the other side of the scale:
- binding site guarantees,
- binding job security,
- real investment commitments,
- and owner-side financial participation.

---

## To the regional state government: your instinct is right, but I need more than a “test”
You are right to insist that the company should not rush to closures. You are right that the burden must not fall mainly on workers. And you are right that alternatives must be tested.

But I need to be equally clear: I do **not** want an “independent test” to become a procedural trap that legitimizes closures later while giving workers nothing now.

So yes, an alternatives test can help — **if it is framed correctly**.

It must mean:
- no closures during the test period,
- no compulsory redundancies during the test period,
- binding site and job security during the test period,
- and genuine access to the relevant plant-level financial data.

Otherwise it is not a test. It is a holding pattern for pressure.

---

## To the expert: your contribution is useful, but incomplete without the actual figures
Your industrial logic is sound. I agree with you on the key point: the issue is numerical, not rhetorical.

But your conclusion also confirms what we have been saying all along:
- the company needs to show the site P&Ls,
- the capacity utilization,
- the savings map,
- the one-time costs,
- and the target gap.

Until then, no one gets to say with confidence that closures are required.

So yes, the expert makes the right case for **independent numerical verification**. That is exactly why I will support a structured alternatives review — but only if it cannot be used to smuggle in predetermined closure decisions.

## What the union is prepared to discuss
Let me be precise, because I do not want this discussion blurred into fake compromise.

We are prepared to discuss:
- working-time flexibility,
- temporary labor-cost relief,
- bonus deferral or suspension,
- productivity agreements,
- voluntary exits,
- redeployment,
- qualification and retraining,
- internal transfers,
- and capacity reduction through negotiated, reversible means.

We are also prepared to discuss **a fair employee contribution**. That was already on the table in our counter-proposal. But it has to be:
- bounded,
- shared,
- and linked to real security.

### What we are not prepared to accept
- compulsory redundancies,
- plant closures in Germany,
- or a settlement in which workers take the hit while management and owners preserve flexibility and return.

That remains my line. Nothing in this round has given a compelling reason to abandon it.

## On the “two-stage restructuring” idea
This is the only pathway that could still be serious — but only if it is genuinely protective, not a trap.

### I would consider a two-stage process if:
1. **Phase 1 contains a real no-closure, no-forced-layoff commitment**, not a rhetorical one.
2. **The workforce’s contribution is matched by management and ownership contributions**.
3. **Investment commitments are real and enforceable**.
4. **The alternatives review is independent, transparent, and site-specific**.
5. **Any trigger for Phase 2 is very strict and objectively verified**.

But let me be plain:  
If Phase 2 is designed to reintroduce closures as an easy fallback, then this is not a compromise. It is an extended threat.

So I will not sign up to a “review” that quietly preserves management’s right to come back later and dismantle jobs after workers have already conceded.

## My position on the expert’s conditional conclusion
The expert says a non-closure settlement is possible if the gap is moderate, but maybe not enough if the gap is large.

That is not an argument **for** closures. It is an argument for:
- full disclosure,
- real bargaining,
- and a serious burden-sharing package.

If the gap is truly large, then management and owners must come forward with something substantial of their own.  
Not more pressure on labor. Not more talk about inevitability. Something real.

That could include:
- owner-side capital support,
- foregone distributions,
- management compensation restraint,
- investment prioritization,
- and deeper internal efficiency measures above the shop floor.

If they want workers to contribute materially, then the contribution must be **shared**, not extracted.
