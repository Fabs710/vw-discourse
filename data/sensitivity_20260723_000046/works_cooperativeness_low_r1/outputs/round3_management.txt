I’ll respond directly.

## First, what I agree with

I agree with everyone on one point: **this company needs a real, durable restructuring**.  
Not a symbolic package, not a postponement, and not a deal that leaves the cost base structurally uncompetitive.

I also agree that:
- capacity must come down,
- future investment is essential,
- industrial conflict would damage everyone,
- and management must put numbers on the table.

The expert’s contribution is useful insofar as it confirms the obvious: **a non-closure solution is only plausible if the savings gap is not too large and the package is genuinely deep**. That is exactly why I have been pressing for structural change, not cosmetic measures.

## Where I disagree — and why

### 1) The employee side is still trying to turn a hard restructuring into a protection exercise
The works council and union keep saying:
- no closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- binding job guarantees,
- binding site guarantees,
- and then, somehow, a sufficient competitiveness reset.

That is not yet a credible industrial answer.

I do **not** dispute that job security matters. I dispute the claim that it can be treated as an absolute while the company is required to absorb a structural cost gap, overcapacity, and declining European demand. If the package does not materially cut recurring costs, then it is not a restructuring.

### 2) “Show the numbers first” is fair — but it does not change the underlying diagnosis
The expert is right that site-by-site numbers are needed. We will provide them in the proper process.

But I disagree with the implication that, until every table is published, closures are somehow illegitimate to discuss. We already know enough to say the current structure is too expensive. That is not conjecture; it is the business reality.

The point is not to “threaten” for effect. The point is that **if non-closure measures do not close the gap, site exits remain necessary**. I will not pretend otherwise to make the discussion more comfortable.

### 3) The state’s “site-preserving first” line is understandable, but it cannot become an economic veto
I understand the political responsibility. I understand regional stability concerns. I do not dismiss them.

But I disagree with the idea that the state can insist on a no-closure presumption so strong that economically necessary restructuring is effectively blocked. That would not be stewardship. It would be delay dressed up as responsibility.

### 4) The family and investors are right about value preservation, but they are still not getting to the heart of execution
I agree with the shareholder side on the need for:
- durable labor-cost relief,
- measurable capacity reduction,
- and a path back to target margins.

But I disagree with any suggestion that the company can simply declare a preference for competitiveness and then wait for a labor-only solution to materialize. If the savings gap is significant, then the package must have real structural force. Otherwise we are just bargaining over timing.

## On the expert’s assessment

The expert says a non-closure package is possible in principle.

Fine. That is **not** the same as saying it is sufficient here.

The expert also says that if the gap is large, voluntary-only and reversible measures are often too slow and too small. That supports my position, not the contrary.

So let me be precise:
- I am open to non-closure measures **if they are economically equivalent**.
- I am open to no forced redundancies **if the savings are still sufficient**.
- I am not open to a package that protects every site, rules out compulsory job reductions, and then expects the company to magically regain competitiveness.

That would be a surrender of industrial reality.

## What I think is actually required

To restore competitiveness, we need a package that does all of the following:

1. **Cuts recurring fixed costs**
   - not just one-off savings,
   - but durable annual cost relief.

2. **Reduces overcapacity materially**
   - whether through closures, specialization, shift removal, or volume transfer.

3. **Delivers a real labor-cost contribution**
   - not a cosmetic gesture,
   - but meaningful, measurable relief.

4. **Restores margin trajectory by 2030**
   - because that is what funds future product and technology investment.

5. **Preserves implementation credibility**
   - because a deal that cannot be executed is worthless.

## What I am prepared to discuss

I am prepared to discuss:
- working-time changes,
- voluntary exits,
- redeployment,
- site specialization,
- productivity agreements,
- temporary concessions,
- and investment commitments tied to restructuring.

I am **not** prepared to accept:
- an absolute no-closure rule,
- an absolute no-redundancy rule,
- or binding guarantees that freeze the structure regardless of economics.

That remains my position.

## To the works council

You say closures are a historic breach. I say the greater breach would be letting this company drift into permanent underperformance and declining industrial relevance.

You say we have not proven necessity. I say the current cost base already proves that change is necessary. The only open question is whether the change can be delivered through alternatives that are **truly equivalent** in economic effect.

If you want to avoid closures, then present a package that:
- closes the gap in hard numbers,
- does not rely on temporary optics,
- and produces a durable 2030 cost structure.

Until then, closure must remain on the table.

## To the union

You are right that the workforce should not carry the whole burden. I agree.

But I disagree with your insistence that any labour contribution must be paired with binding job and site guarantees through 2030 as a precondition. That is too rigid if it prevents the restructuring from being economically effective.

A labour contribution is necessary because the cost base is too high. The scale of that contribution must be determined by the savings required, not by what is politically easiest to defend.

## To the regional state government

Your call for a site-preserving review is acceptable as a process, but not as a way to rule out closure in advance.

If the review shows that alternatives are insufficient, then the discussion must move to deeper measures. I will not allow the process to become a mechanism for indefinite deferral.

## To the controlling family and investors

I agree with your insistence that value preservation requires more than rhetoric. The company needs a package that actually restores competitiveness.

But I will also say this plainly: investors and owners cannot demand a clean margin recovery while rejecting the instruments that may be required to achieve it. If closures or deeper capacity cuts are necessary, they must remain available.

## On the independent expert and audit request

The call for an independent audit is sensible **if** it is designed to verify economics rather than create a procedural shield against restructuring.

So yes:

[SUMMON: independent industrial restructuring audit team with access to site-by-site P&Ls, capacity utilization, and scenario modeling]

I want an independent, numerically grounded assessment that compares:
- closure-led restructuring,
- no-closure / no-forced-redundancy restructuring,
- and hybrid packages.

But let me be very clear: I am not summoning the expert to validate a preordained no-closure outcome. I am summoning them to test whether alternatives are actually sufficient. If they are not, then we must not pretend otherwise.

## Bottom line

My position remains unchanged:

- We need **deep, durable structural savings**.
- We need **real capacity reduction**.
- We need **meaningful labor-cost relief**.
- We need a path that restores **competitiveness and margin** by 2030.
- And we must not exclude closures or deeper measures **in advance** if the numbers require them.

If the employee side can produce an economically equivalent package without closures, I will consider it seriously.  
If not, then the responsible course is to keep closures and compulsory-redundancy tools available as part of the restructuring set.

That is the reality, and I will not soften it.
