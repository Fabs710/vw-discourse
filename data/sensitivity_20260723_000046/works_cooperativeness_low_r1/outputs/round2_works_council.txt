Management, the controlling shareholder, and the capital markets keep repeating the same message in different words: “accept closures or accept decline.” I reject that framing.

## Where I agree
I agree with everyone on one point: the company needs a serious industrial plan, not symbolic declarations. We also agree that capacity has to be addressed and that future products and investment matter. On that, there is no dispute.

But agreement ends there.

## Where I disagree — clearly

### 1. Plant closures are not an acceptable bargaining chip
Let me be explicit: **closing German plants is not a neutral option**. It is a historic breach, a social rupture, and a strategic mistake unless every other path has been genuinely exhausted. So far, I have not seen that demonstrated.

Management says “all options remain on the table.” That sounds managerial, but in practice it means workers are told to negotiate under threat. That is not a serious basis for trust.

### 2. Compulsory redundancies are not acceptable
The workforce did not cause the strategic missteps alone, and it will not accept being sacrificed to fix them. Forced layoffs would destroy skills, morale, and long-term operational capability. They also violate the core job-security framework that has underpinned this company’s industrial peace.

If you want restructuring, use:
- internal redeployment,
- qualification,
- working-time adjustments,
- voluntary exits,
- natural attrition,
- and temporary capacity reductions.

Those are difficult too — but they are reversible and socially responsible. Forced redundancies are not.

### 3. A rigid job guarantee is not “freezing the problem” — it is the condition for cooperation
The state and the union are right to insist on security. Workers are being asked to contribute to a transformation they did not design. If management wants real cooperation, it must offer **binding site guarantees and binding job guarantees through 2030**. Anything less is just asking the workforce to surrender protections first and trust later.

Trust is exactly what management has damaged by bringing closures into the discussion in the first place.

### 4. Co-determination is not decorative
Management keeps implying that co-determination is somehow compatible with predetermined restructuring. It is not. If the company wants a durable solution, employee representatives must shape it from the start — not rubber-stamp decisions after the fact.

The works council has a legal and moral mandate to defend jobs and sites. We will use that mandate.

## To the controlling family and investors
You talk about value preservation and competitive margins. Fine. But value does not exist in a vacuum. You cannot protect enterprise value by destroying the industrial base that creates it.

If you insist on treating every plant as a balance-sheet line, then you will get conflict, strikes, legal resistance, and a damaged company. That is not responsible stewardship.

The controlling family says closures must remain available if needed. I say: **show the evidence first**. Quantify the gap. Prove that site-preserving alternatives fail. Until then, closure threats are a pressure tactic, not a solution.

To investors: if you want competitiveness, support a package that actually works. A rushed downsizing that tears out competence and triggers social conflict is not “discipline”; it is self-harm dressed up as financial logic.

## What I am prepared to discuss
We are not refusing change. We are refusing destructive change.

I am prepared to discuss:
- capacity reductions through shift and working-time changes,
- internal transfers across sites,
- training and requalification,
- voluntary programmes,
- productivity improvements agreed with the workforce,
- and fair contributions from management and owners.

But these discussions only make sense if they are anchored by:
- **no plant closures in Germany,**
- **no compulsory redundancies,**
- **binding job security through 2030,**
- **binding site guarantees for all German plants,**
- **real investment commitments for future products,**
- and **full involvement of the works council in shaping the restructuring**.

## What I want answered in this round
Before anyone repeats “the numbers don’t work,” I want management to state plainly:

1. What is the exact cost gap to be closed?
2. How much would each measure contribute?
3. Which alternatives to closure have actually been tested?
4. Why are job-secure, site-preserving measures insufficient?
5. What concrete investment commitments are tied to the restructuring?

If management cannot answer that, then its closure agenda is not a plan — it is a threat.

## Bottom line
We will not be pressured into accepting plant closures or forced layoffs as the price of management’s restructuring failure. If the company wants a future, it must be built **with** the workforce, not against it.

And let me say this plainly: if closures or compulsory redundancies are pushed through, expect resistance — industrial, political, and through every co-determination instrument available to us.
