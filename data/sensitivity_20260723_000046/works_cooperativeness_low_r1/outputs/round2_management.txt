I’ll respond directly.

## To the works council and union: I hear the concern, but your red lines do not close the cost gap

You both keep saying “no closures, no compulsory redundancies, no weakening of protections.” I understand why you are drawing that line. But I need to be equally clear: **that line, by itself, does not solve the structural problem**.

The company is not facing a normal cyclical downturn. We are dealing with:
- a shrinking European market,
- intense low-cost competition,
- EV transition pressure,
- and a German cost base that is materially above target.

If we preserve the current cost structure and only redistribute pain through reversible measures, we will still not be competitive enough by 2030. That is not a strategy; it is delay.

### Where I agree
- We do need a negotiated settlement.
- We do need investment in future products and technology.
- We do need to avoid industrial conflict if possible.
- We do need meaningful workforce involvement.

### Where I disagree
- **No plant closures in Germany** cannot be an absolute. If a site is structurally uncompetitive, it has to be possible to close or repurpose it.
- **No compulsory redundancies** is not something I can accept as a blanket rule if the required savings cannot otherwise be achieved.
- **Binding job guarantees through 2030** are not credible unless they are tied to a restructuring package that actually restores margins.
- **“We need proof first”** is fair in principle, but the proof is already in the numbers: the current structure does not meet the required return profile.

You ask for exact figures. Fine. Then let’s be precise about the logic: if the combination of capacity reductions, working-time changes, productivity measures, voluntary exits, and wage relief does not deliver enough durable savings, then **a site exit remains on the table**. I will not pretend otherwise.

## To the works council specifically
You say plant closures are a historic breach. What I say is: **letting the company drift into permanent underperformance would be a bigger breach of responsibility**.

A company like this cannot be treated as a protected museum of every legacy site. Industrial reality changes. We have to adapt the footprint to demand, technology, and competitiveness.

If you want to avoid closures, then bring forward a package that is genuinely equivalent in economic effect. Not symbolic. Not temporary. Equivalent.

That means:
- substantial and durable labour-cost reduction,
- real and measurable capacity reduction,
- site specialization where appropriate,
- working-time flexibility,
- and enforceable productivity gains.

Natural attrition and training alone are not enough.

## To the union
You are right that the workforce should not bear the burden alone. I agree with that. But you are still not acknowledging the scale of the adjustment required.

A package built mainly around:
- voluntary exits,
- bonus deferrals,
- and partial wage concessions

will not be sufficient unless it is deep enough to reset the cost base. And if it is deep enough, then it is already a serious workforce contribution. So let’s stop pretending there is a low-pain route to the same outcome.

You also say closures are a coercive opening threat. I say they are not a tactic; they are a consequence if the numbers demand it. Management cannot be asked to rule out the only credible lever that may restore competitiveness.

## To the state government
I appreciate your insistence on regional stability, and I agree that closures should not be the first move. But “last resort” cannot become “never.” If you turn that into an effective veto, then you are not supporting restructuring — you are blocking it.

You asked for alternatives. We are evaluating them. But they must be real, quantified, and sufficient. Until someone shows that they are, closures remain a necessary part of the option set.

## To the controlling family
You are right on one point: value preservation is non-negotiable. But value is destroyed just as surely by indecision as by overreach.

I agree that we need:
- durable labour-cost reduction,
- real capacity reduction,
- a path back to target returns,
- and investment commitments tied to restructuring.

Where I diverge is that I will not let “social manageability” become a code word for avoiding the hard decisions. The package has to be economically sufficient first. If a site cannot be made competitive, it cannot be exempt forever.

## To the institutional investors
You are right to demand quantified scenarios and a credible timeline. Management will provide that. But let me be equally direct: investors cannot demand a clean margin recovery while simultaneously opposing the tools needed to achieve it.

If we need a combination of:
- capacity rationalization,
- labour-cost relief,
- and selected closures,

then that is what the restructuring must deliver. Otherwise the market will be right to discount our strategy.

## On what I am prepared to discuss
I am not closing the door to alternatives. I am saying they must be sufficient.

I am prepared to discuss:
- working-time adjustments,
- voluntary exit programmes,
- redeployment,
- site specialization,
- phased capacity reductions,
- productivity agreements,
- temporary concessions tied to measurable savings,
- and investment commitments for future products.

But I am **not** prepared to accept a package that:
- rules out closures in advance,
- rules out compulsory redundancies in advance,
- preserves the current cost base,
- and expects management to call that a solution.

## What I need from the employee side
If you want to avoid closures, then come back with a package that answers these points in hard numbers:
1. How much cost reduction is delivered?
2. Which sites lose capacity, and by how much?
3. What labour-cost contribution is durable through 2030?
4. How quickly does the package restore the return-on-sales target?
5. What is the contingency if the savings fall short?

Without that, we are not negotiating a restructuring. We are negotiating the timing of a crisis.

## Bottom line
I am prepared to negotiate seriously, but not to sign up to a defensive arrangement that freezes today’s uncompetitive structure.

The company needs:
- a durable reduction in cost and capacity,
- a genuine labour-cost contribution,
- and enough flexibility to restore competitiveness.

If that can be achieved without closures, I will consider it. If it cannot, then closures and deeper measures remain necessary.

That is the reality, and I will not soften it to make the discussion more comfortable.
