I will be very direct.

The regional state government is not prepared to endorse a restructuring that **starts from plant closures in Germany** or from a package that pushes the main burden onto the workforce. That would be economically short-sighted and politically unacceptable for the region we are elected to protect.

## Where I agree

I agree with management on one point: **the company needs a credible competitiveness plan**. A mere postponement of the problem is not a solution. If costs are structurally too high and capacity is mismatched to demand, then we do need a negotiated restructuring.

I also agree with the works council and the union that:
- this cannot be imposed unilaterally,
- the workforce cannot carry the whole burden,
- and the company must show a real future for its German plants, not just demand sacrifices.

## Where I disagree with management

I disagree fundamentally with the framing that **closures are the default discipline mechanism**.

For a company of this size, with this industrial footprint and this governance structure, closures are not “just another option” to be casually threatened until others give in. They are a last resort, and if they are being put forward as the opening move, then management is escalating far too early.

I also do not accept the implication that a meaningful labour contribution must come only after closures are made credible. That is not a fair bargaining posture. If you want the workforce and public stakeholders to engage constructively, then the company must first demonstrate that it is serious about:
- preserving sites where possible,
- using internal restructuring tools before irreversible cuts,
- and sharing the adjustment burden across management, owners, and labour.

## Where I disagree with the idea of “no change unless closures are possible”

I understand the argument that competitiveness has to be restored. But I am not convinced that the only credible path is a large-scale cut package with plant exits. There are other levers that have not been exhausted:
- capacity reductions without closures,
- shift and working-time adjustments,
- productivity measures agreed with labour,
- internal redeployment and qualification,
- staged savings with clear review points,
- and a fairer contribution from the capital side and senior management.

If management cannot show that these options have been genuinely explored, then pushing closures is not responsible restructuring — it is the easiest way to shift risk onto workers and the region.

## On the workers’ conditions

The works council and union are right to insist on:
- no compulsory redundancies,
- binding site security,
- investment commitments,
- and a genuine co-determination role.

Those are not decorative demands. They are the minimum basis for a socially sustainable agreement in a company that is a central economic anchor for several regions.

At the same time, I would encourage the employee side not to reduce the discussion to a pure defensive posture. If there is a negotiated package that preserves sites and avoids forced layoffs, then the state can support a reasonable transition contribution from the workforce. But that requires trust, and trust requires management to stop using closure threats as the opening bargaining position.

## What the state government expects

If there is to be an agreement, we need a package with these characteristics:

- **No German plant closure unless every lesser alternative has been exhausted and publicly justified.**
- **No compulsory redundancies as the starting point.**
- **A balanced burden-sharing package**, including contributions from management and owners, not just labour.
- **Protective mechanisms for sites and employment**, especially in the state.
- **A credible industrial future**, with investment in products and technology.
- **Respect for co-determination and the state’s veto rights**, not attempts to sideline them.

## Bottom line

I am open to a serious restructuring, but not to an approach that sacrifices jobs and regional stability as the price of management convenience.

If the company comes forward with a **site-preserving, socially negotiated, economically credible plan**, the state government can engage constructively. If the answer remains “accept closures or accept decline,” then you should not expect us to facilitate that path. We have both the responsibility and the means to block it.
