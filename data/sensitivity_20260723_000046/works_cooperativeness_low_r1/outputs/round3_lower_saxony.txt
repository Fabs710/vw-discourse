The discussion has clarified the problem, but it has **not** changed my core position.

## Where I agree

I agree with everyone on the basic diagnosis:
- the company has a serious structural competitiveness problem,
- capacity has to be reduced somehow,
- future investment is necessary,
- and a negotiated outcome is far better than a destructive conflict.

I also agree with the expert on one important point: **without site-by-site numbers, nobody can honestly claim that closures are either necessary or avoidable**. That is exactly why I have been pushing for a quantified process rather than accepting pressure arguments.

## Where I disagree

### To management
You still speak as if closures and compulsory redundancies are the only credible instruments if the numbers do not work. I do **not** accept that as the starting premise.

You have not yet shown, with transparent plant-level figures, that:
- non-closure measures are insufficient,
- voluntary exits and redeployment cannot close the gap,
- or that the state’s regional and co-determination role must give way to your preferred solution.

Right now, your position is still: “trust the diagnosis, accept the pressure, and we’ll reveal the numbers later.” That is not good enough for a company of this importance.

### To the controlling family and investors
I understand your insistence on competitiveness and value preservation. But I disagree with the idea that value can only be protected through closure risk and workforce insecurity.

A package that destroys regional trust, triggers strikes, and weakens the company’s social base is not “discipline.” It is a high-risk strategy with long-term costs.

You are treating closure as a normal governance tool. For this company, in this state, that is not acceptable as an opening assumption.

### To the works council and union
I support your insistence that workers should not be made the sole shock absorber. I also support the demand for real burden-sharing and binding security.

But I need to be equally clear: if the employee side insists that every German site must remain untouched regardless of economics, that also will not resolve the problem.

So I am **not** endorsing denial. I am endorsing a serious site-preserving restructuring test.

## What I can support

I can support a package that includes:
- no plant closures **during a defined first phase**,
- no compulsory redundancies **during that phase**,
- capacity reductions through shift changes, working-time adjustments, redeployment, and specialization,
- voluntary exit programmes,
- productivity improvements,
- and binding investment commitments.

But only if this is backed by:
- transparent plant-level economics,
- management and owner contributions,
- and a real decision gate if the package underdelivers.

## What I cannot support

I cannot support:
- closures as the opening move,
- forced redundancies as a default instrument,
- a package that shifts most of the pain onto labour,
- or a restructuring that sidelines co-determination and the state’s blocking role.

Those are not minor objections; they go to the core of what this company is and how it must be governed.

## On the expert’s assessment

The expert has confirmed that a non-closure solution is **possible in principle** if the gap is not too large and the package is deep enough.

That means two things:
1. management must prove the gap, not just assert it;
2. employee-side alternatives must be tested seriously, not dismissed rhetorically.

That is exactly why I support an independent audit — but only if it is genuinely independent and site-specific.

[SUMMON: independent plant-level financial audit team with access to site P&Ls, capacity utilization, and scenario modelling]

The audit must answer, site by site:
- what the real cost gap is,
- what each measure saves,
- what the one-time restructuring costs are,
- what the investment requirements are,
- and whether a non-closure package can close the gap without forced redundancies.

## My political and shareholder line

Let me state this plainly:

- I will **not** accept a restructuring that guts regional employment.
- I will **not** accept a plan that removes the state’s co-determination and veto role in practice.
- I will **not** accept plant closures as a default bargaining tool.
- I will **not** accept compulsory redundancies as the first answer.

At the same time, I am not blocking change. I am saying change must be:
- economically sound,
- socially responsible,
- and politically durable.

That means the company must be restructured **with** the workforce and regional stakeholders, not against them.

## What I want next

Before anyone asks for final commitments, I want three things:
1. the independent site-by-site audit,
2. a quantified comparison of closure-led, non-closure, and hybrid packages,
3. a credible burden-sharing proposal from management and owners.

Until then, there is no basis for demanding that the state surrender its position or that workers give up their protections.

## Bottom line

I am prepared to back a tough restructuring if it:
- protects core sites and employment in the state as far as economically possible,
- preserves the company’s role as a regional anchor,
- respects co-determination,
- and delivers real competitiveness.

I am **not** prepared to support a package that uses the crisis to normalize closures, weaken the state’s role, or make labour carry the entire burden.

That is my position.
