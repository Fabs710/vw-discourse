I’ll be equally direct.

## Where I agree
I agree with a few things that have been said:

1. **A cosmetic deal is worthless.**  
   If this just postpones the structural problem, we will be back here in a year with the same cost gap and worse credibility.

2. **Prolonged conflict would be damaging.**  
   A strike cycle would hurt output, customers, suppliers, and the brand. That is not in anyone’s interest.

3. **The burden cannot fall only on employees.**  
   Management, owners, and the wider governance structure all have to contribute.

4. **Numbers matter.**  
   I accept the expert’s point that this has to be judged by fixed cost, utilization, and durable unit cost — not by slogans.

So yes: we need a durable restructuring, not theatre.

## Where I disagree — and why
### 1) “No German plant closures” cannot be the starting line
The Works Council and union keep repeating that every German plant must remain open. I understand the political logic. I do **not** accept it as an economic premise.

If the footprint is structurally oversized, then refusing closures in advance is not a solution. It is a veto on the adjustment the business may require. I am not prepared to sign up to a framework that protects every site regardless of whether the business case supports it.

If a serious non-closure package can deliver the same structural effect, I will examine it. But I will not rule out closures before seeing hard evidence that the alternative works.

### 2) “No compulsory redundancies” is not automatically compatible with restructuring reality
I do not prefer forced layoffs. But I also cannot accept a position that makes headcount adjustment impossible no matter what the economics are.

If the employee side wants to exclude compulsory redundancies, then it must show a concrete alternative that:
- permanently lowers capacity,
- materially reduces labour cost,
- and gets the company back to target margins.

So far I have heard red lines, not a fully credible replacement for the scale of change required.

### 3) Binding guarantees through 2030 are not credible without a real reset
You want binding employment and site security through 2030. I understand why. But a blanket guarantee is not credible if the cost and capacity problem remains unresolved.

We cannot lock in an uncompetitive structure and call that responsibility. That would simply postpone the crisis and weaken the funds available for future products and technology.

## On the expert assessment
The expert made the decisive point: **temporary concessions are not enough if overcapacity is real**. I agree.

That means the question is not “closure or no closure” as a slogan. The question is whether the proposed package permanently removes enough:
- fixed cost,
- excess capacity,
- and unit labour cost
to restore competitiveness.

If it does not, then it is not a solution.

## What a credible non-closure package would have to prove
If the Works Council, union, or state want me to consider a non-closure path as economically equivalent, it must show:

1. **exact capacity reduction by site**
2. **permanent fixed-cost savings**
3. **durable labour-cost reduction**
4. **product and investment allocation that sustains the footprint**
5. **a margin path back to target**
6. **a clear backstop if the savings do not materialize**

Without those numbers, “no closures” is not an answer. It is a preference.

## On the compromise proposals
### Proposal 1: “Non-closure first, closure backstop”
This is not a real compromise from my side. It can only work if the non-closure phase is genuinely rigorous and time-bound, with meaningful savings targets and a credible enforcement mechanism. I am open to that **as a test**, not as a permanent shelter from hard decisions.

### Proposal 2: “Time-limited guarantees in exchange for measured structural concessions”
This is closer to reality, but only if the concessions are actually structural, not just temporary pain.

A few years of wage moderation or bonus waivers will not solve an ongoing competitiveness gap by themselves. If the workforce wants guarantees, the company needs durable savings. Those two have to match.

### Proposal 3: “Hybrid footprint adjustment with protected core sites”
This is the most plausible direction so far, but only if “protected core” is not just a slogan and “adjustment” means real capacity removal, not cosmetic shift reshuffling.

## What I reject
I reject:
- a blanket no-closure line with no credible alternative,
- a blanket no-layoff line with no equivalent savings,
- guarantees that preserve uncompetitiveness,
- a settlement that leaves us with the same cost base and less room to invest,
- and any deal that is mainly about buying peace, not restoring margins.

## To the Works Council
You say a closure backstop destroys trust. But trust cannot be one-sided either.

If you want me to stop treating closures as a tool, then show me the operational package that makes them unnecessary. Right now, I do not have that evidence. I have principles. Principles do not balance the P&L.

Also, if your position is that **every German plant must remain open regardless of economics**, then that is not negotiation. That is a demand to freeze the current footprint and hope the market improves. It won’t.

## To the union
I respect your rejection of forced redundancies. But again: if compulsory layoffs are off the table, then the alternative must be materially equivalent.

Voluntary exits, retirement corridors, internal redeployment, working-time adjustments, and productivity measures can help. But they are not automatically enough if the structural overcapacity remains.

If you want to avoid harsher measures, you need to help produce a package that actually closes the gap.

## To the state government
I respect your concern for regional stability and industrial capability. But regional importance cannot become a veto over necessary adjustment.

A weak company protects nobody long term. If the business remains structurally uncompetitive, the region loses anyway — only later, and under worse conditions.

## To the family holding
You are right that a cosmetic compromise would be destructive. On that point we agree.

Where I push back is your tendency to leave closures and headcount cuts as open-ended tools without first exhausting and proving alternatives. I am not interested in closure for its own sake. I am interested in the least value-destructive credible path that actually fixes the economics.

## To investors
You are right to demand a durable reset. But closures are not a strategy by themselves; they are an instrument.

If a non-closure package can truly deliver durable savings, higher utilization, and restored margins, then it is the better value-preserving route. If it cannot, then we need to be honest about that too.

## My position, plainly
I will support a settlement only if it contains all of the following:
1. **durable and measurable cost reduction**
2. **real capacity reduction**
3. **a meaningful employee contribution**
4. **management and owner contributions**
5. **investment commitments for future products and technology**
6. **a credible path back to return-on-sales target**
7. **labour peace for a meaningful period**

That is the minimum basis for a deal.

## What I will not accept
I will not accept:
- a symbolic no-closure promise with no economic substance,
- guarantees that lock in uncompetitiveness,
- a settlement that protects every site but leaves the company structurally weak,
- or a deal that shifts the adjustment burden entirely onto capital without workforce contribution.

## Bottom line
We are not here to preserve the status quo. We are here to restore competitiveness.

If the employee side wants to avoid closures and forced redundancies, then bring a package that is economically equivalent in effect — not just rhetorically equivalent. If you can do that, I’ll engage seriously. If you cannot, then closures and headcount reductions remain available because the business problem is real and urgent.
