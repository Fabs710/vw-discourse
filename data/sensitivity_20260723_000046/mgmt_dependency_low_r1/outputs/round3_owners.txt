I will be direct.

## To management
I agree with your core diagnosis: the company needs a **durable competitiveness reset**, not a cosmetic deal. Structural overcapacity, a cost base above target, and weak margins cannot be ignored. If the business remains uncompetitive, that destroys long-term value, and the family holding will not support that.

Where I disagree is the implication that **closures and headcount cuts are the only credible route**. They may be necessary, but they are not automatically the best answer. If you want our support for any deep restructuring, you need to show, in hard numbers, why a **non-closure package is or is not economically sufficient**. Right now, I hear a diagnosis; I do not yet hear a fully tested comparison of options.

What I will not support is a settlement that:
- preserves uncompetitiveness,
- merely postpones the problem,
- or triggers prolonged conflict that destroys value through strikes and governance paralysis.

So: **yes to structural repair, no to a symbolic compromise, no to value-destructive escalation**.

## To the Works Council
I understand your red lines on trust, co-determination, and the fear that closures become a bargaining weapon. I also agree that the workforce should not carry the entire burden.

But I have to be equally clear: **an absolute no-closures, no-forced-redundancies position is not enough by itself**. It is understandable politically, but from an ownership perspective it only works if you can demonstrate a package that genuinely delivers equivalent structural savings and capacity reduction.

You keep saying “keep every German plant open.” I am open to that **if** it is economically real. But if the non-closure path does not materially solve the cost and capacity problem, then insisting on it as a principle does not protect the company — it weakens it.

I also do not accept the suggestion that management is simply not doing its homework if it asks for structural adjustment. It may well be that alternatives exist, but they still need to be proven, quantified, and durable.

## To the union
I agree with you on one important point: **forced layoffs are highly undesirable** and should be avoided if at all possible. Industrial peace is valuable, and a strike cycle would be bad for everyone.

Where I disagree is your suggestion that time-limited employee contributions plus guarantees can simply substitute for a structural reset without hard economic proof. Temporary waivers, bonus moderation, or flexible hours may help, but they do not automatically fix the underlying margin gap.

If the workforce wants no compulsory redundancies, then I need to see:
- how capacity comes down,
- how labour cost comes down durably,
- and how the company gets back to target margins.

A fair contribution from employees is possible. But it must be **material enough to matter**, and it must not just buy time.

## To the regional state government
I share your concern about regional employment, industrial capability, and the company’s role as an anchor employer. A disorderly plant closure would be damaging, and I do not dismiss that.

But I cannot accept the idea that regional importance should function as a veto against necessary restructuring. If a site is structurally uneconomic, then the real question is whether there is a credible alternative that preserves value. If there is, we should pursue it. If not, postponing the issue only increases the eventual damage.

So I agree with you on avoiding closure-first reflexes. I do **not** agree that preserving every site is automatically the right answer regardless of economics.

## To institutional investors
I agree with your insistence on margin restoration, durable cost reduction, and avoiding a deal that simply defers the problem. You are right that a symbolic compromise would be value-destructive.

Where I would caution you is this: **closures are not a strategy by themselves**. They are an instrument. If they are needed, they must be justified against the alternatives and their full second-order costs — labour conflict, execution risk, reputational damage, and loss of know-how. Good governance means choosing the least value-destructive credible path, not just the hardest-sounding one.

## What would move the family holding
We would support a package if it contains all of the following:
1. a **durable, measurable cost reduction**
2. a **real capacity reduction**
3. a **credible employee contribution**
4. a **management and owner contribution**
5. **investment commitments for future products and technology**
6. **labor peace for a meaningful period**
7. a solution that does **not permanently impair enterprise value or governance control**

That last point matters. We will not back arrangements that weaken the owners’ ability to steward the company over the long term.

## My position, plainly
- **No cosmetic compromise**
- **No permanent erosion of competitiveness**
- **No prolonged conflict that destroys value**
- **No blank-cheque guarantees if the structural problem remains unsolved**
- **Yes to a negotiated restructuring if it genuinely fixes the economics**

If the employee side wants to avoid closures and forced redundancies, then it must bring forward a package that is economically equivalent in effect — not rhetorically equivalent. Until then, I will keep closures and headcount reduction as possible tools, because the company’s competitiveness and long-term value are at stake.
