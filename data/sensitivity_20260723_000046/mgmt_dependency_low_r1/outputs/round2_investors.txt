From the perspective of dispersed capital-market shareholders, the central issue remains unchanged: **we need a credible, durable path back to competitive margins**. Not a symbolic settlement, not a delay, and not a conflict that destroys value.

## Where we agree
I agree with all sides on one important point: **the company cannot continue with a structurally uncompetitive cost base**. The current German footprint, as management describes it, is clearly under pressure, and a mere “business as usual” outcome would be value-destructive.

I also agree that **prolonged industrial conflict would be costly**. Strikes, uncertainty, and governance paralysis would hit margins, execution, and investor confidence. That risk matters to us.

And I agree that **the workforce should not bear the burden alone**. If there is a restructuring, it should not be a one-sided transfer of pain onto employees while other stakeholders preserve everything else.

## Where I disagree
### To the Works Council and union
Your repeated red lines — **no closures, no compulsory redundancies, no framework changes** — are understandable politically, but they are only acceptable to us if the alternative is economically equivalent.

So far, I have not heard a package that proves that point.

- If every plant must stay open,
- and no forced redundancies are allowed,
- and job-security guarantees run through 2030,
then the burden of proof is very high.

That is not impossible in principle, but it must come with **hard, enforceable numbers**:
- how much capacity is removed,
- how much structural cost is cut,
- how labour costs are reduced durably,
- and how margins return to target.

Without that, the position looks less like a solution and more like a demand to preserve an uncompetitive structure.

I also do **not** see temporary waivers, bonus sacrifices, or reversible flexibility as sufficient on their own. Those may help in the short run, but they do not fix a structural margin problem.

### To management
I agree with your diagnosis that the current cost base is too high. But I want to be equally direct: **“closures remain on the table” is not, by itself, a strategy**.

If management pushes closure threats without being able to demonstrate the full economics, it risks escalating conflict and destroying value faster than it saves it. We will not applaud toughness for its own sake.

If closures are truly necessary, show the numbers. If they are not, then do not use them as leverage to extract concessions that are not durable.

### To the state government
I agree that a closure-first approach is socially and politically dangerous, and I agree that regional industrial capacity matters. But I also need to say this plainly: **regional importance cannot be used to justify permanent underperformance**.

If a package protects sites but leaves margins structurally impaired, it may preserve jobs for a time, but it will ultimately weaken the enterprise and threaten value more broadly. That is not a sustainable way to protect the region or shareholders.

### To the controlling family
I agree with your point that cosmetic compromise is unacceptable. But I also caution against treating “no closures” as something that can only be rejected in principle. If the employee side can produce a genuinely equivalent solution, it should be assessed on economics, not ideology.

At the same time, “shared sacrifice” must be **real and durable**. We do not support a settlement built mostly on temporary gestures that leave the underlying competitiveness gap intact.

## What would move us
We could support either of the following:
1. **A deep restructuring with closures and headcount reduction**, if that is genuinely required to restore competitiveness; or
2. **A negotiated non-closure package**, but only if it delivers broadly the same economic outcome in a durable way.

For a non-closure alternative to be acceptable, it would need to include:
- measurable and durable cost reduction,
- real capacity rationalisation,
- a structural labour contribution,
- clear productivity improvements,
- and enough certainty to restore margin credibility.

## What we will not support
We will not support:
- a cosmetic compromise,
- a delay tactic,
- a deal that preserves every site while leaving the company structurally uncompetitive,
- or a package that buys short-term peace at the cost of long-term value destruction.

## Bottom line
Our position is not “closures at any price.”  
It is also not “no closures, no matter the economics.”

Our position is:

- **restore competitive margins**
- **reduce structural cost durably**
- **accept the least value-destructive credible path**
- **avoid prolonged conflict if, and only if, the deal is economically real**

If the other stakeholders want investor support, they need to bring forward a package that is not just socially defensible, but financially convincing. Without that, we will regard the outcome as insufficient.
