I will be equally direct.

I hear the Works Council and the union insisting on **no plant closures, no compulsory redundancies, and no dismantling of the job-security framework**. I understand why you are drawing those lines. But I need to say plainly: **if those lines also block a durable reduction in cost and capacity, then they do not solve the problem — they preserve it**.

## Where I agree
I agree on three points:

1. **A cosmetic compromise would be useless.**  
   If we only postpone the structural gap, we will be back here in a worse position.

2. **A disorderly conflict would damage everyone.**  
   Prolonged strikes, reputational harm, and uncertainty would be costly for the company and the workforce.

3. **The burden cannot fall only on employees.**  
   Management, owners, and the wider governance structure must also contribute to a credible package.

## Where I disagree
### 1) “No closures” cannot be a blank cheque
You both keep repeating “no plant closures” as an absolute. I cannot accept that as a starting point if the footprint remains structurally oversized.

If a site is uneconomic, then pretending otherwise is not responsible stewardship — it is delay. And delay is exactly what we cannot afford given the cost position, the shrinking European market, and the pressure on the brand’s returns.

So I am not prepared to rule out closures in advance. If there is a credible alternative that achieves the same structural effect, I will consider it. But **the outcome must fix the economics, not just the optics**.

### 2) “No compulsory redundancies” is not automatically compatible with reality
I want to be careful here: forced layoffs are not my preferred route. But I also will not accept a framework in which headcount and capacity are untouchable regardless of business needs.

If the workforce insists that no compulsory redundancies are possible under any circumstances, then it must provide a concrete alternative that delivers equivalent savings and capacity reduction. So far I hear principles; I do not yet hear a package that closes the gap at the required scale.

### 3) Time-limited or reversible measures alone are not enough
Working-time changes, bonus waivers, internal redeployment, and voluntary programs can help. But they do **not** by themselves deliver a durable reset of the cost base if the structural overcapacity remains.

I am not interested in a settlement that looks cooperative but leaves us unable to meet the return-on-sales target or finance investment to 2030.

## What would actually move us
A serious non-closure alternative would need to show, in hard terms, how it delivers:

- a **material reduction in structural labour costs**
- a **real reduction in capacity**
- a **durable improvement in margins**
- enough headroom to fund future products and technology

That means numbers, not slogans.

## On guarantees
You are asking for binding site and employment guarantees through 2030. I have said before, and I repeat it now: **I cannot sign up to blanket guarantees unless the cost and capacity problem is genuinely solved**.

Guarantees without a credible economic basis would just lock in uncompetitiveness. That is unacceptable to management, owners, and investors — and it would be irresponsible toward the company’s long-term future.

## On “shared sacrifice”
I do agree that shared sacrifice is the right principle. But shared sacrifice has to be **material**. It cannot mean:
- short-term concessions only,
- while the underlying structural gap remains,
- and the company continues to underperform.

That is not shared sacrifice. That is deferral.

## What I am prepared to consider
I remain open to a negotiated package, but only if it contains:

- a **durable and measurable cost reduction**
- a **real capacity adjustment**
- a **meaningful employee contribution**
- productivity and flexibility measures that are structurally effective
- investment commitments that support future competitiveness
- and labour peace for a meaningful period

If that can be achieved **without** closures, then that is preferable. But I am not accepting “no closures” as a prior condition that overrides the economics.

## Bottom line
So my position is unchanged:

- **No cosmetic deal**
- **No protection of every site at any cost**
- **No agreement that leaves the company structurally uncompetitive**
- **Yes to a negotiated solution if it genuinely resets cost and capacity**

If the employee side wants to avoid closures and layoffs, then it must bring forward a package that is economically equivalent in effect. Until then, closures remain on the table, because the company’s competitiveness is on the line.
