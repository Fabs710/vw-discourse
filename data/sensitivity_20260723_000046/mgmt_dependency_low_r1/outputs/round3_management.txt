I’ll be equally direct.

## Where I agree
I agree with everyone on three points:

1. **A cosmetic deal is worthless.**  
   If we only postpone the structural problem, we will be back in crisis very quickly.

2. **The burden cannot fall only on employees.**  
   Management, ownership, and the broader governance structure must all contribute.

3. **Prolonged industrial conflict would be damaging.**  
   Strikes and confrontation would hurt output, reputation, and confidence. That is a real cost.

So on the need for a durable settlement, we are not divided.

## Where I disagree
### 1) “No closures” cannot be the starting point
The Works Council and union keep repeating “no German plant closures” as if that alone could solve the problem. It cannot.

If the footprint remains structurally oversized, then refusing closures in advance is not a solution — it is a refusal to confront the economics. I am not prepared to sign up to a framework that protects every site regardless of whether the business case supports it.

If there is a credible non-closure alternative that achieves the same structural effect, I will examine it seriously. But I will not rule out closures before seeing such a package.

### 2) “No compulsory redundancies” is not automatically compatible with restructuring reality
I do not prefer forced layoffs. But I also cannot accept a position that makes headcount reductions impossible regardless of the economics.

If the employee side says “no compulsory redundancies” absolutely, then it must present a concrete alternative that delivers:
- real capacity reduction
- durable labour-cost reduction
- structural improvement in margins

So far I hear principles, not a fully credible replacement for the scale of adjustment required.

### 3) Guarantees through 2030 are not credible without a real reset
You are all asking for binding employment and site guarantees through 2030. I understand why. But I will be blunt: **blanket guarantees are not acceptable if the cost and capacity problem remains unresolved**.

A guarantee that simply locks in the current structure would not be responsible stewardship. It would preserve uncompetitiveness and weaken the company’s ability to invest in future products and technology.

## What I am prepared to accept
I remain open to a negotiated package, but it must be economically real. That means:

- **material and durable cost reduction**
- **real capacity reduction**
- **a meaningful employee contribution**
- **productivity and flexibility improvements that last**
- **investment commitments for future products and technology**
- **labour peace for a meaningful period**

If that can be achieved without closures, that is preferable. But I am not accepting “no closures” as a prior condition that overrides the economics.

## On the employee-side proposals
Working-time reductions, internal redeployment, voluntary exits, and qualification measures can help. But they are not enough on their own if the structural overcapacity remains. Temporary or reversible concessions do not by themselves restore competitiveness to the level we need.

I also reject the idea that asking for a structural contribution from labour is somehow unfair. The current cost base is too high. The brand is missing its return-on-sales target. We need to free funds for future products and technology. That requires a real reset, not reassurance.

## On management, owners, and investors
I agree with the investor side that a purely symbolic settlement would destroy value. I also agree with the family holding that we need a package that is durable and not just politically convenient.

But I want to say clearly: closure threats are not a strategy in themselves. If closures are proposed, they must be justified with numbers and compared against serious alternatives. I am not interested in using them as a rhetorical club.

## On the state
I respect the state government’s concern for regional stability and industrial substance. But regional importance cannot be a veto against necessary adjustment. If the company remains structurally uncompetitive, then the result will be worse for the region and the workforce in the long run.

## My position, plainly
I will support a negotiated package if it contains all of the following:
1. **a durable and measurable cost reset**
2. **real capacity reduction**
3. **a meaningful workforce contribution**
4. **management and owner contributions**
5. **investment commitments into future products and technologies**
6. **a path to restored margins and competitiveness**

If the employee side wants to avoid closures and forced layoffs, then it must bring forward a package that is economically equivalent in effect. Not symbolically equivalent. Economically equivalent.

## What I reject
I reject:
- a blanket no-closures line without a viable alternative
- a blanket no-layoffs line without equivalent savings
- guarantees that lock in uncompetitiveness
- a cosmetic compromise dressed up as social responsibility
- a settlement that preserves every site but leaves the business structurally weak

## Bottom line
I am still open to a negotiated solution. But it has to solve the actual problem:
- structural overcapacity
- excessive labour cost
- weak margins
- and insufficient room for future investment

If the other stakeholders want a deal, they need to move from slogans to numbers. Until then, closures and headcount reductions remain on the table because the company’s competitiveness is on the line.

If needed, I can outline the minimum economic thresholds a non-closure package would have to meet.
