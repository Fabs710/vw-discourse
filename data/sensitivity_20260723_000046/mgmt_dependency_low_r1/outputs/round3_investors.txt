I appreciate the independent assessment, and it largely confirms our position: **the only acceptable outcome for capital markets is a durable, structurally credible reset that restores margins**. We are not here to defend closures as a principle, but we are equally not going to endorse a package that preserves an uncompetitive cost base and calls it peace.

## Where we agree

### With management
- You are right that a **cosmetic compromise is worthless**.
- You are right that **temporary concessions alone do not solve a structural margin problem**.
- You are right that the company needs **real capacity reduction and lasting cost improvement**.

### With the expert
- The expert’s key point is decisive: **non-closure only works if it is economically equivalent in durable, structural terms**.
- Temporary wage moderation, bonus waivers, or working-time changes **are not enough on their own** if the overcapacity problem remains.
- We agree that what matters is not the label “closure” or “no closure,” but whether the package **permanently removes fixed cost and excess capacity**.

## Where we disagree

### To the Works Council and union
Your position still reads like a demand for security first and economics second.

We understand why you reject closures and forced layoffs. But from an investor perspective, **“no closures, no redundancies, no framework changes” is not a solution unless you can prove hard, durable equivalence**.

So far, we have heard a lot of principles:
- no plant closures
- no compulsory redundancies
- guarantees through 2030
- shared sacrifice

But we still have **not** seen a package that demonstrates:
- how much capacity is actually removed,
- how much the fixed cost base falls,
- how labour cost is reduced durably,
- and how margins return to target without reopening the issue in a year or two.

That is the burden of proof, and it has not been met yet.

### To the regional state
We agree with your concern about jobs, skills, and regional stability. But your position still leans too hard toward preservation.

Protecting every site is not value creation. If a plant is structurally uneconomic, then keeping it open indefinitely simply postpones the cost and amplifies the damage later. The region is not helped by a company that stays large but weak.

### To the family holding
You are right to reject a symbolic compromise. But you are also too quick to leave closures and headcount cuts as open-ended tools without a fully tested alternative.

If a non-closure solution exists, fine. But **prove it with numbers and durability**, not with broad reassurance.

## What the expert clarified for us
The expert’s analysis is useful because it sharpens the investment question:

- **Non-closure can be viable only if it removes enough permanent fixed cost and capacity.**
- A deal that relies mainly on temporary concessions is **not durable**.
- The real test is whether the company remains competitive **after** temporary measures expire.

That is exactly the standard we apply.

## Our position, clearly

We can support **either** of two outcomes:

### 1) A closure-based restructuring
If management can show that closures are genuinely the least value-destructive way to restore competitiveness, then we can support that in principle.

But only if:
- the economics are clear,
- the execution risks are contained,
- and the expected margin uplift is real and durable.

### 2) A non-closure package
We can also support a non-closure path, **but only if it is economically equivalent**. That means it must include:
- permanent capacity reduction,
- measurable and durable cost reduction,
- a structural labour-cost reset,
- and enough certainty to restore margin credibility.

## What we will not support
We will not support:
- a cosmetic settlement,
- a delay tactic,
- a deal that keeps every site open while leaving the company structurally weak,
- or a package built mainly on temporary employee pain with no permanent fix.

## What would move us
To be specific, a credible non-closure package would need to show:
1. the **exact capacity reduction** achieved,
2. the **permanent fixed-cost savings** delivered,
3. the **durable unit labour-cost reduction**,
4. the **investment and product allocation** supporting the preserved footprint,
5. the **timeline** over which margins return to target,
6. and the **backstop** if the promised savings do not materialize.

Without those numbers, “no closures” is not an answer. It is a preference.

## Bottom line
We are aligned with the expert on the central economic test:

- **temporary concessions are insufficient**
- **structural cost and capacity reduction are mandatory**
- **the least value-destructive credible path is the one to back**

So our position remains unchanged:
- restore competitive margins,
- reduce structural cost durably,
- avoid prolonged conflict only if the deal is economically real.

If the employee-side and state-side package can actually do that without closures, we would consider it. If not, then management must not be prevented from using the tools required to fix the economics.
