I’ll be direct.

## Where I agree
I agree with the Works Council, the state, and the expert on the essentials:

- The company needs a **durable restructuring**, not a cosmetic pause.
- **Employees cannot carry the whole burden**.
- A deal that only postpones the problem would be irresponsible.
- If the company is truly structurally uncompetitive, then the economic numbers must be changed, not just the rhetoric.

I also agree that management should bring **plant-by-plant and year-by-year numbers**. That is exactly what is needed now.

## Where I disagree
### 1) I do not accept closures as the default or as leverage
To management, the family holding, and investors: saying “closures remain on the table” is not neutral. It is pressure. It makes every other offer look conditional and every commitment reversible.

I do **not** accept a closure-first logic. And I do not accept using closures as a bargaining club to force the workforce into concessions.

If there is a serious alternative, then show it. But I will not endorse a framework that keeps German plant closures as an open threat while demanding trust from the workforce.

### 2) Forced redundancies remain unacceptable
On this point I am completely clear: **no compulsory redundancies**.

Not as a first step.  
Not as a fallback.  
Not as a hidden reserve.

If capacity must come down, then the instruments are:
- voluntary exits
- retirement and bridge models
- internal redeployment
- qualification and transfer
- working-time adjustments
- productivity and organization measures

Forced layoffs would shatter trust and almost certainly destroy more value than they create.

### 3) Temporary concessions are not enough by themselves
I agree with the expert and with investors on one thing: a few temporary concessions do not solve a structural problem.

But that does **not** mean the answer must be closures or forced layoffs. It means the package has to be **structural on the cost side** while still respecting job security.

That is the point management is still avoiding: you need permanent competitiveness gains without making the workforce absorb all the pain through job cuts.

---

## On the expert’s assessment
The expert was useful in one very important respect: they made the real issue clear.

A non-closure package is only viable if it delivers:
- **real fixed-cost reduction**
- **durable capacity reduction**
- **lower unit labour cost**
- **simplification of complexity**
- **investment-linked future volume**

I accept that test.

What I do **not** accept is the conclusion that the only credible way to achieve that must be closures or compulsory redundancies. That conclusion has not been proven.

## What would make a non-closure path credible
A non-closure package can be discussed only if it contains all of the following:

1. **No German plant closures**
2. **No compulsory redundancies**
3. **Binding employment security through 2030**
4. **Binding site security through 2030**
5. **Real capacity reduction without shutdowns**
6. **A meaningful, time-limited workforce contribution**
7. **Management and owner contributions**
8. **Investment commitments for German future products and technologies**
9. **A fully costed plan proving the savings are durable**

That is the minimum basis. Without that, there is nothing to negotiate.

## On the moderator’s compromise ideas
### Proposal 1: “Non-closure first, closure backstop”
I reject this.

A closure backstop is not a compromise. It keeps the threat alive and turns every commitment into a conditional one. That is not a basis for trust or industrial peace.

### Proposal 2: “Time-limited guarantees in exchange for measured structural concessions”
This is only discussable if the guarantees are **real, binding, and unconditional on plant survival**.

If “guarantee” means “until the next review,” then it is not a guarantee. It is a pause button.

### Proposal 3: “Hybrid footprint adjustment with protected core sites”
I reject any model that pre-selects expendable sites. We are not negotiating a protected core built on sacrificing other German plants.

Every German plant must remain open. If management cannot make the economics work under that condition, then management must show its work in numbers — not in threats.

## To management
You keep asking for an economically equivalent alternative.

Fine. Then stop speaking in abstractions and put the numbers on the table:
- fixed costs by site
- utilization by site
- product allocation by site
- one-off restructuring costs
- logistics and transfer costs
- margin impact over 3–5 years

If you want the workforce to consider contributions, show us the non-closure path that actually works. Until then, you are asking employees to pay first and trust later.

And let me be blunt: if you insist that closures remain an open tool, then the workforce will treat your “offer” as a threat, not a compromise.

## To the family holding
You say you want durable value protection. Good. Then stop acting as if preserving value automatically means keeping closure pressure alive.

There is also value in:
- preserving skills,
- keeping industrial know-how,
- avoiding conflict,
- protecting labor peace,
- and maintaining trust in co-determination.

A deal that cuts costs but damages the social settlement that made this company strong is not stewardship. It is short-termism.

## To investors
You are right that a symbolic compromise is worthless. I agree.

But your definition of “equivalence” must include the full economics, not just a narrow margin view:
- one-off closure costs
- execution risk
- launch disruption
- supplier disruption
- strike risk
- reputational damage
- loss of know-how

If you ignore those, then your economic logic is incomplete.

## To the state government
I appreciate your support against forced redundancies and closure-first logic. But support should become leverage.

The state should not stand by while management uses regional dependence as a pressure tool. If the company wants the legitimacy of a regional anchor, then it must respect the workforce and co-determination.

---

## What I can support
I can support a negotiated package if it includes all of the following:

- **no compulsory redundancies**
- **no German plant closures**
- **binding job and site security through 2030**
- **real co-determination in the restructuring**
- **meaningful management and owner contributions**
- **investment commitments for future products and technologies**
- **a time-limited workforce contribution**
- **a fully costed non-closure restructuring**

That is the minimum serious basis.

## What I reject
I reject:
- closure-based restructuring as the default
- closure threats as leverage
- forced layoffs
- permanent erosion of the collective wage framework
- one-sided sacrifice from labour
- “guarantees” that can be broken at the next review

---

## Bottom line
The other sides keep trying to turn our red lines into variables. They are not variables.

- **No closures**
- **No compulsory redundancies**
- **No plant shutdowns as a bargaining instrument**
- **No agreement without binding security through 2030**
- **No agreement without a real, costed non-closure package**

If management wants a settlement, bring the numbers and a real alternative. If not, then this is not a negotiation — it is an attempt to impose restructuring on the workforce. And in that case, we will resist it with all legitimate means available to us.
