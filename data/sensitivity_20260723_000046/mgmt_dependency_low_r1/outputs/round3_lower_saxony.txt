As the regional state and anchor shareholder, I’ll speak plainly.

## Where I agree
I agree with all sides on one fundamental point: **the company needs a durable restructuring, not a cosmetic pause**. The current cost and capacity problem is real. I also agree that **the burden cannot fall on employees alone**, and I agree that **a prolonged industrial conflict would damage the company, the region, and investor confidence**.

I also agree that **numbers matter**. If management says a non-closure path cannot work, then it must show that convincingly. And if the employee side says closures are unnecessary, then it must also show a credible operational alternative, not just a political line.

## Where I disagree
### 1) I do not accept closure-first logic
Management, the family holding, and investors keep repeating that plant closures remain on the table. I understand the economic argument, but I do **not** accept closures as the default answer or as routine leverage.

A German plant closure is not just a line item. It means:
- lasting damage to regional employment,
- loss of industrial capability,
- political destabilization,
- and a weakened role for the company as an anchor of the region.

So my position is clear: **closures are not the starting point**. If they are even to be considered, management must first prove that serious alternatives have been exhausted.

### 2) I do not accept compulsory redundancies as a normal restructuring tool
I align with the workforce and union on this: **forced layoffs are not the right way to secure a durable settlement**.

If capacity has to come down, then the first tools must be:
- working-time adjustments,
- internal redeployment,
- qualification and transfer,
- voluntary exits,
- retirement and bridge models,
- productivity and organizational measures.

Compulsory redundancies would deepen conflict and likely destroy morale, skills, and trust — exactly the things the company needs to recover.

### 3) I do not accept “shared sacrifice” that is one-sided
Management and investors keep asking for a “material and structural” workforce contribution. I understand that demand. But a contribution is only legitimate if it is **balanced by real security and a credible future**.

I will not support a model where:
- employees give up pay and guarantees,
- while sites are left in uncertainty,
- and capital retains all strategic flexibility.

That is not social partnership. That is risk transfer downward.

## What I can support
I am prepared to support a package if it contains all of the following:

1. **No plant closures as the starting point**
2. **No compulsory redundancies**
3. **Real capacity reduction through non-closure instruments**
4. **A fair, negotiated employee contribution**
5. **Management and owner contributions as well**
6. **Binding investment commitments for future products and technologies**
7. **A credible employment and site framework through 2030**
8. **Real co-determination involvement in the restructuring**

That is the minimum basis for a socially and economically credible deal.

## To management
You are right that a symbolic compromise will not solve the problem. But you are wrong if you think the only credible solution is to keep closures and layoffs on the table until the workforce gives in.

If you want state support, then bring a **fully costed non-closure package** showing:
- the cost reduction target,
- the capacity reduction target,
- the measures to reach it,
- and the impact on margins and future investment.

If that package is not credible, then I will say so. But I will not pre-accept closures just because management says they are possible.

## To the Works Council and union
I support your refusal of forced redundancies and closure-first logic. That said, I also need you to engage seriously with the economic side.

A sustainable non-closure solution must be more than a list of red lines. It must show:
- how the company gets to a lower cost base,
- how capacity is reduced without site shutdowns,
- and what time-limited contribution the workforce can make in exchange for binding guarantees.

If that can be done, I will defend it. If it cannot, then we need to be honest about the limits. But I will not support a deal that guts jobs or weakens the state’s co-determination role.

## To the family holding
You are right that a cosmetic compromise is unacceptable. I agree. But I also reject the idea that “economic realism” automatically means closures.

Realism also includes:
- the value of industrial stability,
- the value of retained skills,
- the cost of labor conflict,
- the importance of the company’s regional legitimacy.

A solution that technically cuts costs but destroys trust and industrial substance is not a good long-term solution.

## To investors
You are right to insist on a durable improvement in margins. But I would caution against treating closure as the only serious instrument.

If the alternative protects the company’s industrial base, avoids conflict, and still achieves the required economic effect, that is not softness — that is better governance.

## Bottom line
My line remains:

- **no closure-first approach**
- **no forced layoffs**
- **no agreement that guts regional employment**
- **yes to a negotiated restructuring if it preserves sites, protects jobs, and restores competitiveness**
- **yes to a solution that respects co-determination and the company’s role as a regional anchor**

If management wants my support, it must put forward a genuine non-closure package with real numbers. Until then, I will not move from my core position.
