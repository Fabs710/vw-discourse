I’ll be equally direct in round 3.

## Where I agree
I agree with the core premise that a cosmetic deal is worthless. The company needs a **durable restructuring**, and nobody benefits from drifting until the next crisis. I also agree that the burden cannot fall only on employees, and I agree that prolonged industrial conflict would damage the company, the workforce, and the region.

I also agree with the Works Council and the state on one important point: **co-determination and social partnership are real assets, not obstacles**. If management wants a sustainable solution, it cannot simply push a textbook downsizing plan and expect the workforce to applaud.

## Where I disagree
### 1) “No closures” as an absolute is still not enough by itself
I understand why the Works Council is drawing a hard line against closures. But I want to be precise: **I can support no closures only if the non-closure alternative is economically credible and structurally meaningful**.

So far, management has not produced a convincing fully costed non-closure plan. And management, owners, and investors should be careful not to turn “closures remain on the table” into a negotiating tactic that pre-decides the outcome. That said, I will not pretend that a non-closure path is acceptable merely because it is morally preferable. It has to work in numbers, not just in principle.

### 2) Forced layoffs remain a red line
On this point I am firm: **no compulsory redundancies**.

If the company needs lower capacity or a smaller workforce, then the instruments must be:
- voluntary exits
- retirement and bridge models
- internal redeployment
- qualification and transfer
- working-time reductions
- productivity and organization measures

Forced layoffs would shatter trust, trigger escalations, and likely destroy more value than they create. They are not a serious answer unless every other avenue has genuinely been exhausted — and I do not see that case made.

### 3) Employee contributions must not become permanent one-way pain
I hear management, the family holding, and investors insisting on “structural” contributions. Fine — but **structural cannot mean permanent sacrifice by labour alone**.

If employees contribute, then that contribution must be:
- fair
- time-limited where possible
- tied to binding guarantees
- matched by contributions from management and owners

If management wants durable wage moderation, then it has to give something durable in return: security, investment, workload protection, and a credible future for the sites and jobs.

## What I can support
I can support a negotiated package if it contains all of the following:

1. **No compulsory redundancies**
2. **No German plant closures**
3. **Binding employment and site security through 2030**
4. **A measurable workforce contribution**
   - bonus waivers
   - temporary wage moderation
   - working-time flexibility
   - productivity measures
5. **A management and owner contribution**
   - overhead restraint
   - investment commitments
   - no one-sided extraction from labour
6. **A fully costed non-closure restructuring**
   - with real capacity reduction
   - internal flexibility
   - voluntary exit paths
   - and clear savings targets

That is the minimum basis for a settlement.

## What I reject
I reject:
- closure-first thinking
- forced layoffs
- using job insecurity as leverage
- permanent weakening of the collective wage framework
- “shared sacrifice” that is actually one-sided pain
- deals that protect financial optics while weakening industrial substance

## On the issue of economic equivalence
To management, family owners, and investors: you keep saying the employee side must present an “economically equivalent” alternative.

That is fair as a demand, but then you must also be honest about what equivalence includes:
- not only short-term cost cuts,
- but also retained skills,
- labour peace,
- production stability,
- and the value of avoiding a destructive conflict.

If you measure only immediate margin effects, you are missing part of the picture. If you want an economically serious alternative, then the company itself must put a proper package on the table — not just threats.

## On the state and the works council
I appreciate the state’s support for avoiding forced redundancies and closure-first logic. But support has to become leverage. The state and co-determination stakeholders should not allow management to use regional dependency as a pressure tool.

I also support the works council’s insistence on binding guarantees. Without them, any workforce contribution is just a handover of risk downward.

## What would move us
We remain open to a settlement if management brings forward:
- a **fully costed non-closure plan**
- a **real capacity reduction path without forced layoffs**
- **binding job and site guarantees through 2030**
- **a fair and time-limited employee contribution**
- **a meaningful management/owner contribution**
- **clear investment commitments for future products and technologies**

If management cannot do that, then it is not asking for a compromise — it is asking employees to finance the restructuring alone.

## Bottom line
My position remains unchanged where it matters:
- **No compulsory redundancies**
- **No plant closures**
- **No dismantling of the collective wage framework**
- **Yes to a fair contribution if and only if jobs, sites, and the future are secured**

If the other side refuses that basis, then I am prepared to support further industrial action. If they want peace, they need to earn it with a real package, not with pressure.
