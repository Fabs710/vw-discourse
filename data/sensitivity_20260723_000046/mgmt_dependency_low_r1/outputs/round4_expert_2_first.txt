Yes. Based on the scenario as framed, the key question is:

**Can a non-closure or hybrid package remove enough fixed cost and capacity, on a durable basis, to be economically equivalent to closure-based restructuring over 3–5 years?**

My expert assessment is: **possibly, but only under a fairly strict set of conditions; otherwise closure-based restructuring usually delivers the stronger and more durable cost reset.**

## 1) What usually drives the answer
In automotive manufacturing, the gap between closure and non-closure options is usually not about headline labour concessions alone. It is driven by:

- **fixed cost removal**: plant overhead, maintenance, utilities, indirect labour, local administration
- **capacity utilization**: lowering the denominator matters as much as lowering cost
- **product allocation**: whether a site keeps enough volume and mix to be efficient
- **one-off restructuring charges**: severance, asset write-downs, logistics reconfiguration, remediation
- **execution risk**: industrial conflict, launch disruption, supplier disruption
- **durability**: whether savings survive after temporary concessions expire

A deal that mainly consists of **temporary pay restraint, working-time changes, and voluntary exits** can help cash flow, but it often does **not** fully solve a structural overcapacity problem if the plant network remains too broad.

## 2) Closure-based restructuring: economics
Closure is usually the most direct way to remove:
- a large block of fixed cost
- excess capacity
- underutilized overhead
- duplicated complexity

### Typical strengths
- Highest certainty of **permanent fixed-cost elimination**
- Best chance to restore **utilization at remaining plants**
- Often the clearest route to margin repair if there is true excess capacity

### Typical weaknesses
- Very high **one-time charges**
- Higher **industrial-relations and strike risk**
- Potential **launch / logistics / supplier disruption**
- Potential loss of **skills and flexibility**
- Political and governance friction can delay implementation

In 3–5 year terms, closure usually has the strongest operating-cost effect, but the net value depends heavily on:
- how large the closure charge is
- how much of the lost volume can be absorbed elsewhere
- whether remaining plants can run at materially better utilization

## 3) Non-closure restructuring: economics
A non-closure package can be economically credible if it achieves a real structural reset through:
- shift reductions
- product concentration
- line consolidation
- internal redeployment
- voluntary exits / retirement corridors
- permanent process simplification
- lower indirect overhead
- supplier and logistics simplification

### The important distinction
- **Temporary labour concessions**: helpful, but usually not enough alone
- **Structural capacity reduction without closure**: can work if it is deep enough
- **Hybrid network rationalization**: often the most realistic compromise

A non-closure path tends to underperform closure when:
- capacity reduction is too shallow
- sites remain materially underutilized
- overhead cannot be removed quickly
- product portfolio fragmentation remains
- labour-cost concessions expire before the business is fixed

## 4) My judgement on the three scenario types

### A. Closure-based restructuring
**Most likely to deliver the largest durable cost reduction.**  
Best when:
- overcapacity is clear
- the company can reallocate volume efficiently
- the closure site is genuinely uncompetitive
- governance can actually execute it

### B. Non-closure restructuring
**Can work, but only if it is more than a labour deal.**  
It needs:
- permanent capacity removal
- permanent overhead reduction
- durable productivity gains
- enough product concentration to lift utilization

If it relies mainly on wage moderation, bonus reductions, or temporary concessions, it is usually **not equivalent** to closure.

### C. Hybrid restructuring
**Often the best trade-off in politically constrained automotive systems.**  
This is usually the most feasible route when:
- closures are blocked or too costly
- but the business still needs real capacity reduction
- some plants can be partially downsized while a core footprint is protected

A hybrid can be economically close to closure if it includes:
- one or more major site reductions
- permanent headcount removal
- shift elimination
- product concentration
- and enforceable follow-through

## 5) What I would consider the minimum threshold for a viable non-closure package
A non-closure package must show, by site and by year:

1. **Permanent capacity reduction**
2. **Permanent fixed-cost reduction**
3. **Sufficient utilization improvement at retained plants**
4. **Labour-cost savings that remain after temporary measures expire**
5. **A credible 3–5 year margin path**
6. **One-off costs that do not overwhelm the present value of savings**
7. **No hidden reversion of costs into the back half of the period**

If those are not demonstrated, then the non-closure package is likely just a delay mechanism.

## 6) Practical conclusion
### If I were comparing the three options purely on manufacturing economics:
- **Closure-based restructuring** is usually the strongest for durable cost reset.
- **Hybrid restructuring** is often the best compromise if closures are politically constrained.
- **Non-closure-only restructuring** is viable only if it removes enough fixed cost and capacity to match the economics, which is hard but not impossible.

## 7) Bottom line
My expert view is:

- **Temporary employee concessions alone are not enough** if the company truly has structural overcapacity.
- A **non-closure package can be credible only if it achieves permanent capacity and fixed-cost removal comparable to closure**, not merely a temporary labour-cost pause.
- In most automotive cases, the **least value-destructive credible package** is often a **hybrid**: partial footprint adjustment, real capacity cuts, voluntary exits, product concentration, and a limited number of hard site actions if required.
