## What the expert has made clear
The key test is simple:

- If the proposed package **permanently removes enough fixed cost and capacity**, then capital can support it.
- If it mainly relies on **temporary concessions**, then it is not enough.
- If it is a **hybrid** that really reduces footprint, utilization bottlenecks, and ongoing overhead, then it may be the least value-destructive credible route.

## Our position, plainly

We can support **either** of these outcomes:

### 1) Closure-based restructuring
If management can show that closures are the least value-destructive way to restore competitiveness, then we can support that in principle.

### 2) Non-closure or hybrid restructuring
We can also support a non-closure or hybrid path, but only if it is **economically equivalent** in durable terms. That means:
- real capacity reduction,
- permanent fixed-cost removal,
- measurable labour-cost reset,
- and a credible margin recovery path over 3–5 years.

## What we will not support
We will not support:
- a cosmetic settlement,
- a delay tactic,
- a package that keeps every site open while preserving an uncompetitive cost base,
- or a deal built mainly on temporary employee pain without permanent structural improvement.

## What would move us
A credible package would need to show, by site and by year:
1. the **exact capacity reduction** achieved,
2. the **permanent fixed-cost savings** delivered,
3. the **durable unit labour-cost reduction**,
4. the **product and investment allocation** supporting the retained footprint,
5. the **3–5 year margin path**,
6. and a **backstop** if the promised savings fail to materialize.

## Bottom line
We are not here to defend closures as a principle. We are here to defend **risk-adjusted value**.

So our position is unchanged:
- restore competitive margins,
- reduce structural cost durably,
- avoid prolonged conflict only if the deal is economically real.

If the employee-side and state-side package can actually do that without closures, we will consider it seriously. If not, then management must retain the tools needed to fix the economics.
