I’ll be direct.

## Where I agree
I agree with management, the expert, and the investors on the central economic point: **a cosmetic settlement would be value-destructive**. If this only buys a few quarters and leaves the company structurally uncompetitive, it would damage the enterprise and the family holding’s long-term value.

I also agree that:
- the burden cannot fall only on employees,
- temporary concessions alone do not fix a structural cost gap,
- and prolonged industrial conflict would be harmful.

On those points, there is no real dispute.

## Where I disagree
### 1) I do not accept “no closures” as an absolute starting line
To the Works Council, union, and state: I understand why you are drawing hard lines. But from a stewardship and ownership perspective, I cannot endorse a framework that rules out closures before the economics are proven.

If a site is structurally surplus, then refusing to consider closures in advance is not protection — it is delay. And delay in a business with margin pressure is expensive.

That said, I am not saying closures should be the default. I am saying they remain a legitimate tool **unless and until** a non-closure package is shown to be economically equivalent in durable terms.

### 2) I do not accept “no compulsory redundancies” as a blanket rule without a credible alternative
Forced layoffs are obviously undesirable. I would prefer to avoid them. But I cannot support a categorical ban if the company cannot otherwise achieve the required capacity and cost reset.

If the employee side wants to exclude compulsory redundancies, then it must produce a package that:
- permanently reduces capacity,
- durably lowers unit labour cost,
- and restores margins without simply postponing the issue.

So far I hear repeated red lines, but not yet a fully convincing mechanism that matches the scale of the problem.

### 3) I do not accept guarantees through 2030 unless the restructuring is genuinely structural
Binding employment and site guarantees are not credible if the company remains overcapacity and uncompetitive. A guarantee that locks in the wrong structure would protect today’s optics and weaken tomorrow’s company.

The right sequence is not “guarantees first, economics later.” It is:  
**credible restructuring first, then durable security.**

## On the expert assessment
The expert’s contribution was useful because it clarified the real test:

- Temporary measures are not enough.
- A non-closure package only works if it permanently removes enough fixed cost and capacity.
- The question is not what sounds fair, but what survives after the temporary concessions expire.

I accept that standard. That is exactly the lens I want used.

## What I need to see
If management wants support for a hard restructuring without closures, or the workforce wants to avoid closures and forced redundancies, then I need a **fully costed comparison** showing:

1. plant-by-plant cost base
2. utilization by site
3. exact capacity reduction
4. permanent fixed-cost savings
5. durable labour-cost reduction
6. investment allocation for future products
7. 3–5 year margin path
8. execution and strike-risk assumptions

Without that, nobody can honestly claim that a non-closure route is economically equivalent.

## My view on the compromise proposals
### “Non-closure first, closure backstop”
This is not a compromise I would endorse lightly, but it is a rational governance device if it is tightly defined. I would only consider it if:
- the non-closure phase has clear, measurable targets,
- the review is objective,
- and the backstop is real, not symbolic.

I understand why labour dislikes it. But from an ownership perspective, a time-bound test is better than open-ended drift.

### “Time-limited guarantees in exchange for measured structural concessions”
This is the most plausible route so far, but only if the concessions are actually structural and durable enough to matter. Temporary wage moderation by itself is not enough. It has to come with real capacity and cost measures.

### “Hybrid footprint adjustment with protected core sites”
This is likely the most practical concept, provided it is not just rhetoric. If it means real capacity reduction without immediately resorting to full closures, then it deserves serious work.

## To the Works Council and union
You are right to insist that the workforce should not finance the restructuring alone. I agree.

But I do not accept the notion that your red lines are sufficient in themselves. Saying “no closures, no redundancies, no concessions without full security” is understandable politically, but economically it only works if you can prove the same structural effect another way.

If you can show that, I am prepared to back it. If not, then you are asking owners and management to accept a weaker company in the name of stability.

## To the state government
I respect the regional concerns. I do not dismiss the importance of industrial jobs and skills.

But I also have to be clear: regional importance cannot become a veto against necessary adjustment. If the company is structurally uncompetitive, preserving every site at all costs may simply spread the damage over a longer period.

We should try hard to avoid closures. But we should not pretend that preserving every footprint is automatically the best long-term outcome.

## To management
You are right not to accept a symbolic compromise. But closure threats are not a strategy by themselves. They are a tool, not a plan.

If you want governance support, bring the hard comparison:
- closure-based restructuring,
- non-closure restructuring,
- hybrid adjustment.

Show which is least value-destructive over 3–5 years, including conflict risk, one-off costs, and launch disruption. Until then, I will not endorse any blanket commitment to closures either.

## What would move me
I would support a settlement if it contains all of the following:
1. a **durable and measurable cost reset**
2. a **real capacity reduction**
3. a **credible workforce contribution**
4. a **management and owner contribution**
5. **investment commitments for future products and technology**
6. a **credible path back to margins**
7. a solution that does **not permanently impair enterprise value or governance control**

That is the standard.

I am not here to preserve the status quo. I am here to protect the long-term value of the core asset.

So my position is:
- **no cosmetic compromise**
- **no permanent erosion of competitiveness**
- **no prolonged conflict that destroys value**
- **no blank-cheque guarantees without structural repair**
- **yes to a negotiated restructuring if it genuinely fixes the economics**
