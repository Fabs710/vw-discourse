I’ll be direct.

## To the Works Council and the Union
I hear your insistence on no closures, no compulsory redundancies, and no erosion of co-determination. I understand why you are drawing those lines. But I do **not** accept them as binding preconditions for a viable restructuring.

You keep saying: “bring the numbers.” Fair enough — so let’s be precise about what the numbers must do.

This company needs:
- a **durable reduction in overcapacity**
- a **material reduction in labour cost**
- a **structural improvement in productivity and flexibility**
- and enough free cash to fund future products and technology through 2030

If the employee-side package cannot deliver that at scale, then it is not an alternative to structural change — it is a delay mechanism.

### Where I agree
I do agree that:
- workers should not carry the entire burden,
- internal measures should be fully used,
- voluntary exits, redeployment, training, working-time tools, and productivity improvements all have a role,
- and we need a negotiated settlement rather than a prolonged open conflict.

### Where I disagree
I do **not** accept:
- a blanket “all plants remain open” position,
- an absolute ban on compulsory redundancies,
- or the idea that site guarantees can be unconditional regardless of economics.

That is not a restructuring plan. That is an attempt to freeze the current cost structure and call it compromise.

## To the state government
You are right to insist on regional stability and to reject closures as a first move. I am not asking for closures as a slogan. I am asking for **credible structural savings**.

But I also have to say clearly: if the state blocks every hard adjustment, then it is not protecting the industrial base — it is protecting inefficiency. A company with a permanently uncompetitive cost base will eventually weaken the region far more than a tough restructuring ever would.

So yes, I support a negotiated path. No, I do not accept a framework where the state’s role becomes an effective veto on necessary capacity correction.

## To the controlling family holding and investors
You are right about one thing: a cosmetic deal is worthless. We need something durable and margin-accretive, not a public-relations compromise.

But I will push back where necessary: demanding savings while refusing to countenance structural tools is not a coherent ownership position either. If the company needs to close a gap, then we need to be honest that the package must be sufficient — not just acceptable in tone.

I am prepared to support avoiding closures **if** the alternative delivers equivalent structural savings. That means real, measurable, enforceable savings — not just temporary labour peace.

## On the numbers
Since the room keeps asking for numbers, here is the management standard:

A viable package must show, site by site:
1. the current cost gap,
2. savings from working-time flexibility,
3. savings from redeployment and natural attrition,
4. savings from voluntary exits,
5. savings from productivity and overhead cuts,
6. savings from targeted labour-cost relief,
7. and the residual gap after those measures.

If the residual gap remains material, then we are back to structural options, including plant-level rationalisation. I will not pretend otherwise.

## What management is prepared to support
We remain open to a negotiated package that avoids closures **only if** it delivers the required structural effect. That means:
- substantial working-time and flexibility changes
- permanent labour-cost relief
- capacity rationalisation
- voluntary exit and redeployment instruments
- management restraint and overhead reduction
- binding investment commitments for future products and technology

But I need to be equally clear: **if those measures do not close the gap, then closures and compulsory redundancies cannot be ruled out.**

That is not rhetoric. It is economic reality.

## What I will not accept
I will not accept:
- a non-closure promise that leaves the cost base uncompetitive,
- a job-security framework that prevents meaningful restructuring,
- or a deal that preserves every current footprint while expecting management to fund the future from an unchanged base.

That would fail the brand, fail the investors, and fail the company by 2030.

## Bottom line
I am prepared to negotiate a serious settlement. But the settlement must do what this crisis requires:
- cut costs durably,
- reduce overcapacity materially,
- protect future investment,
- and restore competitiveness.

If the employee side wants to insist on absolute protection first, then they should be honest that they are rejecting structural adjustment. I will be equally honest in saying that such a position is not acceptable to management.

Bring the **site-by-site quantified alternative**. If it genuinely closes the gap, we can talk. If it does not, then we will have to face the hard consequences of the company’s economics.
