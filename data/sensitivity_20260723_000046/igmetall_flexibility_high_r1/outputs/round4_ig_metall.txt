I will respond plainly.

## Where I agree
I agree with nearly everyone on one point: **this company needs a real restructuring**, not a cosmetic peace pact.  
I also agree that:
- the cost base is too high,
- productivity must improve,
- future investment must be protected,
- and burden-sharing must be real.

So no one should misread the union’s position as denial of the crisis.

## Where I disagree sharply

### 1) Closures are not the benchmark
Management keeps talking as if plant closures are the natural reference case and everything else must prove itself against that threat.

I reject that logic.

The burden is on management to show, with site-by-site figures, that the non-closure instruments do not work. Until then, closures are a **choice**, not a necessity. And if management wants trust, it should stop using irreversible damage as the starting condition for bargaining.

### 2) Compulsory redundancies are not an acceptable instrument
We do not accept forced layoffs as a legitimate restructuring tool.  
Not as a “last resort,” not as an automatic fallback, not as a bargaining threat.

If the company has options available in a co-determined system, then it must use them:
- working-time flexibility,
- internal redeployment,
- training and reskilling,
- voluntary exits,
- natural attrition,
- productivity gains,
- management restraint,
- and capacity adjustment through shifts and planning, not dismissals.

If those are not enough, management must show that fact concretely, not assert it.

### 3) An absolute guarantee is not a fantasy; it is the basis for contributions
The works council is right on the core issue: workers will not contribute fairly if the company keeps the sword of closure and forced redundancies over their heads.

If you want labour to concede on wages, time, or bonuses, then you must first give a binding framework:
- no German plant closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- protected co-determination,
- and real site security through 2030.

That is not radical. That is the minimum for a serious settlement.

## To management
You say you need:
- durable cost reduction,
- material capacity reduction,
- flexibility,
- and cash for future products.

Fine. Show us the numbers without starting from closures.

What I want is a **site-by-site non-closure package** with:
1. current cost gap,
2. savings from working-time changes,
3. savings from voluntary exits,
4. savings from redeployment and training,
5. savings from productivity and overhead cuts,
6. management contribution,
7. owner contribution,
8. remaining gap.

If you can demonstrate that the gap can be closed without closures and without forced layoffs, then we have a basis for negotiation. If you cannot, then the problem is not labour’s unwillingness; it is management’s preferred instrument.

## To the works council
We are aligned on the essentials:
- no closures,
- no forced redundancies,
- no dismantling of job security,
- no weakening of co-determination.

I support your refusal to let management normalise closure politics.  
I would only sharpen one point: temporary employee contributions must never become permanent cuts in the collective framework. Any concession must be:
- limited in time,
- matched by management and owner sacrifice,
- and tied to binding investment and site protection.

## To the regional state government
Your stance matters. But a “test period” with closures as a fallback is too weak.

A fallback is not a safeguard. It is a deferred threat. It keeps the workforce bargaining under pressure and invites management to wait out resistance.

If the state wants to defend the industrial base, it should back a package that begins with:
- no closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- and binding protection through 2030.

Not a probation regime for plant shutdowns.

## To the controlling family holding and investors
You are right about one thing: a cosmetic compromise would be worthless.

But you are wrong if you think value discipline requires keeping closures and forced layoffs on the table from the outset. That approach may look tough, but it is often the fastest way to destroy trust, provoke industrial conflict, and weaken execution.

A company’s value is not just a margin line. It is also skills, quality, continuity, and labour peace. If you damage those, you may gain a short-term accounting effect and lose the industrial base.

## To the expert
The expert’s point about site-level data is exactly right. We need a proper comparison.

But I do not accept the hidden conclusion that closures are therefore “probably” necessary. “Typical” restructuring logic is not proof for this company. Management must prove necessity, not presume it.

## What the union is prepared to discuss
We are prepared to negotiate a serious package that includes:
- temporary and fair labour-cost moderation,
- bonus restraint,
- working-time flexibility,
- internal redeployment,
- training and reskilling,
- voluntary exits,
- productivity measures,
- and capacity adjustment **without closures**.

We are not prepared to negotiate:
- plant closures,
- compulsory redundancies,
- or the erosion of the collective wage framework.

## My bottom line
The union’s position is clear:

- **No German plant closures**
- **No compulsory redundancies**
- **No weakening of the collective framework**
- **No settlement that makes workers the shock absorber for all adjustment**
- **Yes to shared sacrifice, but only if management and owners also contribute materially**

If management wants a settlement, bring the **numbers-based non-closure plan**.  
If it cannot, then say plainly that it prefers closure threats over genuine restructuring. We will answer that accordingly.
