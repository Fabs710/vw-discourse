As the regional state government and anchor shareholder, I need to be very clear: **I do not accept plant closures in Germany as the starting point, and I do not accept compulsory redundancies as the default instrument.**

I do understand the management case: the cost base is too high, capacity is too loose, and the company needs durable savings to fund future products and remain competitive. I am not denying the arithmetic. But I am rejecting the conclusion that the only serious answer is to threaten German sites and jobs first, and then ask everyone else to “find alternatives” under that shadow.

## Where I agree
I agree with management, the family holding, and investors on one point: **this has to be a real restructuring, not a cosmetic one**.  
That means:
- measurable savings,
- genuine productivity gains,
- a lower cost base,
- and a credible investment path for future technologies.

I also agree with the works council and union that the workforce cannot be treated as the sole adjustment variable. That would be unfair, politically reckless, and strategically short-sighted.

## Where I disagree
I disagree with the idea that plant closures and forced layoffs are just “last resorts” that can remain fully on the table as a pressure instrument while trust is supposedly being built.

That logic is exactly what destroys the chance of a durable settlement.

For the state, the red line is clear:
- **no German plant closures as the first instrument**
- **no compulsory redundancies before all internal and negotiated alternatives are exhausted**
- **no weakening of co-determination or the existing job-security framework**
- **no package that hollows out the company’s regional anchor role**

If those fundamentals are compromised, then the company may save a line item in the short term but it will pay for it in long-term legitimacy, skills, supplier networks, and social stability.

## On the numbers
Management keeps asking for a package that “closes the gap.” Fair enough. But then put the gap and the remedy on the table in a way that is **site-specific, quantified, and transparent**.

What I would expect in the next step is not more general declarations, but a genuine comparison of options:
- how much savings come from working-time changes,
- how much from redeployment and attrition,
- how much from productivity measures,
- how much from management restraint and overhead reduction,
- how much from labor-cost contribution,
- and what remains unresolved after those measures.

If, after exhausting those instruments, there is still a residual gap, then we can discuss whether there are truly no alternatives at all. But that is not where we are yet.

## To management
You say closures cannot be ruled out if alternatives do not deliver enough savings. I hear that. But I do **not** accept closures as an opening position or as a bargaining club.

If you want the state’s support, then you need to come back with:
1. a **quantified non-closure scenario**
2. a **credible burden-sharing package**
3. **investment commitments tied to German sites**
4. and a structure that preserves the company as a regional economic anchor

If you can show that the competitiveness gap can be closed without shutting plants, then that is the path I will support. If you cannot, then you need to explain why your own instruments have not been exhausted before you ask the workforce and the region to absorb irreversible damage.

## To the works council and union
I support your insistence that the workforce cannot carry this alone, and I support your call for binding protection of sites and jobs. That said, I also need to be frank: a posture that simply says “all German plants remain open, no matter what” risks becoming a dead end if it is not paired with an actual restructuring mechanism.

So I am not asking you to surrender your red lines. I am asking you to engage on a **real negotiated package** that secures:
- sites,
- jobs as far as possible,
- investment,
- and the company’s future viability.

That may include temporary, fair, reversible employee contributions and internal flexibility. It does **not** mean accepting plant closures or compulsory redundancies as a bargaining starting point.

## To the family holding and investors
You are right to demand a durable improvement in competitiveness. But I want to caution against treating social and regional stability as a secondary issue.

A deal that looks good in a spreadsheet but detonates trust in the company’s home region will eventually cost value as well. For that reason, I will not support a package that makes the state’s co-determination role irrelevant or uses the governance structure to push through irreversible industrial damage.

## My bottom line
The state is prepared to support a tough restructuring **if and only if** it:
- protects German sites as far as possible,
- avoids compulsory redundancies,
- preserves co-determination and the special governance rights,
- shares the burden fairly,
- and links any employee contribution to concrete investment and future-product commitments.

What I will **not** support is:
- a package that starts from closures,
- a strategy of fear and ultimatum,
- or an outcome that weakens the company’s regional and social foundation.

If management wants my support, bring a **numbers-based, non-closure restructuring plan** that proves the gap can be closed with internal measures first. If that cannot be done, then we will have to discuss very seriously why the company is asking the region to accept a course that may be economically and socially reckless.

If needed, I can also help broker a structured package once the figures are on the table. But I will not sign off on plant closures and forced layoffs as a first answer.
