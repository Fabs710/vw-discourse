I appreciate the clarity from management, the works council, the union, and the state government. Let me be equally clear from the position of the controlling family owners.

We are not here to preserve the status quo at any price. We are also not here to authorize a value-destructive confrontation that damages the company’s long-term earning power, its reputation, or its governance stability.

## Where I agree

I agree on three core points:

1. **The company needs a durable competitiveness fix.**  
   If German operations remain structurally too expensive and underutilised, the enterprise will keep bleeding margin and investment capacity. That is unacceptable for a long-term owner.

2. **The solution must be negotiated, not theatrical.**  
   A public escalation cycle, followed by a forced deal, would destroy trust and may worsen value more than it improves cost. We need a serious settlement process.

3. **Burden-sharing is necessary.**  
   Employees cannot be the only ones asked to contribute. Management, owners, and the company itself must all show that they are part of the adjustment.

## Where I disagree

I do **not** accept the idea that the only credible restructuring is one that begins with plant closures and compulsory redundancies.

That may be one possible instrument, but as a first principle it is too blunt and too destabilising given:
- the special governance structure,
- co-determination realities,
- the blocking position of the state,
- and the long-term damage that a prolonged conflict could inflict.

At the same time, I also do **not** accept a position that effectively freezes the entire industrial footprint and rules out structural change in advance. A blanket “no closures, no layoffs, no real capacity adjustment” stance is not a business plan; it is a postponement strategy.

That is where I differ from both sides:
- I am **not** prepared to support cosmetic restructuring.
- I am **not** prepared to support value-destructive conflict.
- I am **not** prepared to support an outcome that permanently impairs competitiveness.

## What would move us

For the family holding, the essential test is simple: **does the package restore margins and competitiveness in a durable way while preserving the long-term franchise?**

That means a viable solution must include:

- **a measurable and lasting cost reduction**
- **a real reduction in overcapacity**
- **a credible workforce contribution**
- **investment commitments for future products and technologies**
- **industrial peace for a meaningful period**
- **no governance settlement that weakens shareholder control or creates open-ended strategic drift**

## On plant closures

I am not announcing support for closures as a default. But I will say this plainly: **no option can be ruled out if it is the only way to secure the company’s future**.

That said, closures are not automatically the best answer. If comparable savings can be achieved through:
- capacity reductions,
- working-time flexibility,
- redeployment,
- voluntary exits,
- targeted cost adjustments,
- and productivity improvements,

then that is preferable to a major closure shock.

But those alternatives must be real and quantifiable. “Alternatives” cannot mean preserving every structure while hoping the market improves.

## On compulsory redundancies

I would strongly prefer to avoid compulsory redundancies if there is a serious negotiated path that reaches the required savings. Forced layoffs should be a last resort, not the starting point.

However, I cannot endorse a settlement that makes job guarantees absolute while also demanding that shareholders accept the full cost of structural adjustment. If the savings do not materialise otherwise, then every option has to remain on the table.

## To management

Management needs to be precise and credible.

Do not present this as an ultimatum if you have not exhausted negotiated alternatives. But also do not come back with a package of vague efficiency slogans and hope that the owners, state, and workforce will sign off on a half-measure.

We need:
- numbers,
- timing,
- site-by-site impact,
- and proof that the proposal closes the gap.

## To the works council and union

Your insistence on social stability is understandable, and I do not dismiss the importance of the industrial base. But I need to be frank: **binding guarantees cannot be unconditional if they prevent the company from adapting**.

I am open to:
- site protection where it is economically defensible,
- no forced redundancies where voluntary and internal instruments work,
- negotiated labour-cost relief,
- and investment commitments tied to productivity gains.

I am **not** open to:
- permanent immunity from structural change,
- blocking all capacity rationalisation,
- or allowing the company to drift into weaker competitiveness in the name of peace.

## To the state government

I understand your political and regional responsibilities. But I want to caution against turning the shareholder role into a veto against economically necessary change.

If the state blocks every painful measure, it may protect short-term employment optics but weaken the company and the region over time. That would not be stewardship; it would be delay with a high future bill.

## Bottom line

The family holding will support a restructuring that is:
- economically credible,
- durably margin-accretive,
- socially negotiated,
- and compatible with long-term industrial strength.

We will **not** support:
- a purely defensive settlement,
- a prolonged conflict that erodes value,
- or a package that preserves every site and every guarantee while failing to restore competitiveness.

If the parties can bring forward a serious alternative that achieves comparable savings without destroying the enterprise’s industrial foundation, we are prepared to consider it. But it must be real restructuring, not symbolic compromise.
