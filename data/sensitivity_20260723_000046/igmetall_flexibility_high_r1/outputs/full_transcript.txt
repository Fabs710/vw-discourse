[Works Council (Betriebsrat) (Plant-level employee representation under co-determination) — Round 1]
As the Works Council, our position is clear:

We will not accept plant closures in Germany, and we will not accept compulsory redundancies.

The company’s crisis cannot be solved by making tens of thousands of workers pay for management’s strategic mistakes. The workforce has already delivered flexibility, productivity, and sacrifice over many years. What is now being demanded goes beyond restructuring; it risks destroying the industrial base, trust, and long-term capacity of the company.

Our priorities are:

- Binding guarantees for every German plant
- No forced layoffs
- Preservation of co-determination and the existing job-security framework
- A credible, negotiated restructuring that protects sites and jobs through 2030
- Investment in future products and technologies at all German locations

We are prepared to discuss alternatives that secure competitiveness without sacrificing people and sites: capacity adjustments, working-time solutions, internal redeployment, training, efficiency measures, and a fair negotiated contribution — but only if they are accompanied by enforceable guarantees.

What we reject is any fait accompli, any unilateral closure announcement, and any attempt to break the historic employment promise. If management wants a solution, it must be a solution with the workforce, not against it.

[Expert: Industrial restructuring finance/operati — Round 3]
Below is a restructuring-operations assessment of the decision logic, with a focus on what can and cannot be inferred **without site-level data**.

## 1) The core quantitative question
The room is really asking one thing:

**Can a non-closure package generate savings and capacity relief comparable to a closure-inclusive package, on a durable basis, without leaving the company structurally uncompetitive?**

From an industrial finance perspective, the answer depends on five items that are not yet disclosed here:
1. **Site-level fixed cost by plant**  
   - direct labor
   - maintenance/utilities
   - plant overhead
   - logistics
   - depreciation and lease burden
2. **Utilization rates and installed capacity by site**
3. **Model allocation by site**
4. **Marginal contribution per vehicle / per line**
5. **The actual size of the earnings gap** that management wants to close

Without those inputs, no one can honestly prove that closures are or are not necessary.

## 2) What a closure typically saves
A plant closure usually captures savings in four buckets:

### A. Fixed operating cost elimination
- plant labor
- plant overhead
- security, maintenance, utilities
- local logistics and indirect functions
- some depreciation may remain if assets are impaired but not disposed

### B. Capacity rationalization
- removes a whole cost block, not just headcount
- increases utilization at surviving plants
- can improve unit fixed-cost absorption if volume is shifted efficiently

### C. Labor cost reset
- avoids the need to match legacy labor terms across a redundant footprint
- may enable a lower cost structure at remaining sites if bargaining power improves

### D. Strategic simplification
- fewer SKUs/sites/complexity points
- lower coordination cost
- potentially better capex concentration

**Important:** closure savings are usually large and durable, but not equal to “all plant cost.” Some costs are stranded or transitionary:
- severance
- relocation
- dual-running during ramp-down/ramp-up
- write-downs
- supplier and logistics restructuring
- possible lost scale if volume is not fully transferred

So closure savings are real, but the net benefit depends on how much volume can be reassigned and at what transfer cost.

## 3) What a non-closure package can realistically save
A non-closure package usually operates through **three levers**, not one:

### A. Labor-cost moderation
- wage freeze or restraint
- bonus reduction
- reduction of special payments
- temporary or permanent working-time changes
- productivity-linked concessions

### B. Workforce reduction without compulsory layoffs
- natural attrition
- voluntary exits
- early retirement / bridging programs
- internal redeployment
- training and reskilling

### C. Capacity reduction without closure
- fewer shifts
- line consolidation
- model mix simplification
- temporary or permanent downtime
- outsourcing of selected volumes

These measures can produce meaningful savings, but typically **less than a full closure** unless:
- labor cost concessions are deep,
- headcount reduction is material,
- and idle capacity is actually taken out of the system.

### Key limitation
A non-closure package often saves **cash** faster than it saves **structural cost**.  
For example:
- working-time changes can reduce annual payroll
- voluntary exits reduce payroll
- but if the site remains open with the same equipment and overhead base, a large share of fixed cost remains

So non-closure measures can close part of the gap, but they rarely match the full fixed-cost elimination of a plant shutdown unless they are unusually aggressive.

## 4) The right comparison is not “closure vs no closure”
The right comparison is:

### Scenario 1: Non-closure package
Savings from:
- labor concessions
- voluntary exits
- redeployment
- productivity
- overhead reduction
- shift / line consolidation

### Scenario 2: Closure-inclusive package
Savings from all of the above **plus**
- full site fixed-cost elimination
- capacity removal
- potential transfer of volume to surviving plants

That is why management keeps arguing from a structural standpoint: if the cost gap is driven by excess footprint, only footprint reduction fully removes the fixed burden.

## 5) Site economics: what matters by plant
A plant-by-plant assessment should include:

### For each site
- annual output
- designed capacity
- utilization %
- direct labor cost per unit
- indirect labor per unit
- maintenance/utilities per unit
- overhead per unit
- logistics distance / transport cost impact
- model complexity / changeover burden
- capex requirement to stay competitive
- ability to absorb transferred volume

### Why this matters
A plant with:
- low utilization,
- high fixed overhead,
- limited future model allocation,
- and high capex needs

is the strongest closure candidate in purely economic terms.

A plant with:
- high utilization,
- strategic model allocation,
- strong logistics position,
- and low incremental capex need

is harder to justify closing, because the displaced volume may cost more to relocate than it saves.

## 6) Margin effect mechanics
Margin impact should be modeled in three layers:

### A. Gross margin
- affected by labor cost per vehicle
- supplier cost
- yield/scrap
- logistics

### B. EBITDA margin
- adds fixed plant overhead and SG&A
- captures the main restructuring benefit from footprint changes

### C. Free cash flow
- closure can improve future FCF
- but near-term FCF may worsen because of severance, write-offs, and transfer costs

So a closure may be:
- **negative in year 1**
- **positive from year 2/3 onward**
depending on transition scale.

A non-closure package may have:
- less negative near-term cash impact
- but also a lower long-run EBITDA uplift

## 7) What “equivalent savings” would require
If the employee side wants to prove that closures are unnecessary, the non-closure package would need to show a savings stack approximately like this:

- **workforce cost relief:** meaningful and durable
- **headcount reduction:** sizable and mostly permanent
- **capacity reduction inside footprint:** real and enforceable
- **productivity gains:** not just aspiration, but measured output per labor hour improvement
- **management/overhead cuts:** visible and quantified
- **investment commitment:** enough to prevent the site from becoming a stranded-cost burden

In practice, “equivalent” means:
- same annualized EBITDA improvement
- same cash-cost reduction
- same capacity correction
- similar durability through 2030

That is a very high bar.

## 8) Likely qualitative conclusion from restructuring economics
Absent evidence to the contrary, the typical pattern is:

- **Non-closure measures can close part of the cost gap**
- **Closures capture the full structural footprint savings**
- **The best package may be a hybrid**:
  - one or more closures or partial site exits
  - plus labor concessions at remaining sites
  - plus voluntary exits and productivity measures
  - plus investment commitments to the retained footprint

That is usually the economically strongest compromise if the overcapacity is real and significant.

## 9) Decision logic by scenario

### If the cost gap is modest
Then a non-closure package may be sufficient if it includes:
- real wage moderation
- strong voluntary exit volumes
- shift reduction / line consolidation
- management cuts
- moderate capex discipline

### If the cost gap is large and structural
Then a non-closure package is unlikely to fully compensate unless it becomes so aggressive that it resembles closure economics in another form.

### If demand decline is permanent
Then the case for actual footprint reduction strengthens, because temporary labor measures do not remove permanent excess capacity.

## 10) What should be requested next
To make this decision evidence-based, the parties should produce a table for every German site with:

1. current output and capacity
2. utilization rate
3. annual fixed cost
4. variable cost per vehicle
5. model allocation through 2030
6. capex required to keep site competitive
7. savings from:
   - wage moderation
   - voluntary exits
   - redeployment
   - productivity
   - overhead reduction
   - shift reduction
8. closure cost:
   - severance
   - write-downs
   - transfer cost
   - ramp-up cost at receiving plants
9. net annual savings and NPV under each scenario

## Bottom line
From an industrial restructuring perspective:

- **A non-closure package can be credible only if it delivers a quantified, durable savings stack close to the structural gap.**
- **If the company’s problem is true excess footprint and high fixed cost, closures will usually deliver more durable savings than labor-only measures.**
- **The right answer is almost certainly site-specific, not ideological.**

[Expert: Industrial restructuring finance/operati — Round 4]
Below is a practical **site-by-site scenario template** you can use to compare **non-closure** versus **closure-inclusive** restructuring on a common economic basis.

## 1) What the model must answer
For each German site, the decision is driven by four numbers:

1. **Current annual EBITDA contribution**
2. **Annualized savings under the non-closure package**
3. **Annualized savings under closure / footprint reduction**
4. **Cash cost to get there**  
   - severance
   - wind-down / remediation
   - retooling
   - transition costs
   - working-capital release or drag

The core test is:

> **Does the non-closure package close enough of the EBITDA gap, at acceptable cash cost, to justify preserving the site?**

If yes, closure is not financially required.  
If no, closure remains the stronger economic lever.

---

## 2) Simple scenario structure
Use the same line items for each site in both scenarios.

### A. Baseline by site
- Current annual vehicle output
- Capacity utilization %
- Headcount
- Direct labour cost
- Fixed overhead
- Energy / utilities
- Logistics / inbound / outbound
- Maintenance / property / depreciation
- EBITDA contribution
- Capital intensity
- Working capital tied up

### B. Non-closure package
Typical measures:
- working-time reduction / short-time equivalent adjustment
- voluntary exits / attrition
- redeployment to other plants
- wage moderation / temporary concessions
- productivity gains
- management overhead cuts
- shift pattern changes
- SKU / complexity reduction
- insourcing / outsourcing changes

### C. Closure / consolidation package
Typical measures:
- transfer volume to other plants
- eliminate site fixed costs
- severance / redundancy costs
- asset write-downs
- remediation / decommissioning
- logistics cost increase from reallocation
- one-time transition losses
- possible capacity constraints at receiving plants

## 4) Economics logic by scenario

### Scenario 1: Non-closure package
This is usually strongest when:
- the site has **strategic product relevance**
- utilization can be improved materially
- fixed overhead is high but not irreducible
- labour flexibility can be bought at moderate cost
- voluntary exits are enough to right-size headcount
- logistics savings from closure would be modest

Typical annualized benefit range in a mature auto plant:
- **Labour-cost / flexibility measures:** meaningful, but often limited if the plant is already lean
- **Productivity and overhead measures:** modest to moderate
- **Total annualized savings:** often enough to bridge a **partial** gap, not always a full structural overcapacity problem

Key risk:
- if the site remains underutilized, non-closure savings may not offset the fixed-cost penalty.

### Scenario 2: Closure-inclusive package
This is usually strongest when:
- a site is structurally underutilized
- product allocation can be moved without large quality or supply risk
- the receiving plants have spare capacity
- the site’s fixed costs are high relative to output
- the required savings exceed what labour flexibility can realistically deliver

Typical economics:
- higher annual fixed-cost elimination
- but significant one-time cash costs
- plus some offset from higher logistics and transition cost

Key advantage:
- closure gives **durable fixed-cost relief**, not just recurring labour concessions.

---

## 5) The key financial test
For each site, calculate:

### Non-closure net benefit
**Annual EBITDA improvement**
minus
**annualized implementation cost**

### Closure net benefit
**Annual EBITDA improvement**
minus
**annualized transition cost**
minus
**any ongoing logistics cost increase**

Then compare:

> **NPV(non-closure)** vs **NPV(closure)** over 5–7 years

Also compare:
- payback period
- cash required in years 1–2
- margin impact by 2030
- sensitivity to volumes / EV mix / utilization

## 6) Practical rule of thumb
A non-closure deal is economically credible only if it can do most of the following:

- close a large share of the gap on a **durable** basis
- improve utilization materially
- reduce fixed cost per unit enough to restore target margins
- require limited recurring labour concessions
- avoid a large permanent overcapacity overhang

If the gap is mostly fixed-cost driven, then **capacity reduction usually outperforms pure labour measures**.

That is the central operational point.
