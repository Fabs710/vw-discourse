I will respond plainly.

## Where I agree
I agree with management, the state, the union, and the works council on one fundamental point: **the company needs a real restructuring, not a cosmetic arrangement**.

I also agree that:
- the cost base is too high,
- overcapacity is a real problem,
- future investment must be protected,
- and the workforce should not carry the entire burden.

From an ownership perspective, we want long-term value, not a temporary peace that simply postpones the crisis.

## Where I disagree

### 1) I do not accept an unconditional “all German plants stay open” line
The works council and union are drawing a hard red line on closures, and I understand the motivation. But I do **not** accept that as a binding starting point for governance.

If the numbers show that a non-closure package can fully close the gap, then fine — that is obviously preferable.

But if it cannot, then insisting on permanent site protection regardless of economics would be value-destructive. It would lock in weak margins, reduce strategic flexibility, and ultimately harm the enterprise we are all supposed to steward.

So I am not prepared to pre-commit to “no closures ever” or to an unconditional site guarantee through 2030.

### 2) I do not accept the idea that compulsory redundancies must be ruled out in advance
I would prefer to avoid forced layoffs. Nobody benefits from prolonged conflict or avoidable social damage.

But I will not exclude them categorically if they are necessary to restore competitiveness. A company with structural overcapacity needs credible instruments, not just voluntary ones.

That said, compulsory redundancies should only be a **last resort**, after:
- working-time measures,
- internal redeployment,
- training and reskilling,
- voluntary exits,
- productivity improvements,
- and management restraint
have been fully quantified and applied.

### 3) I do not accept that guarantees can be unconditional
The request for binding job and site guarantees is understandable, but unconditional guarantees are not credible if they override economic reality.

Any guarantee must be:
- tied to measurable savings,
- linked to productivity and performance,
- and consistent with the company’s ability to fund future products and technology.

A guarantee that prevents necessary restructuring is not stability; it is deferred decline.

## On the numbers
The expert was right: this debate cannot be settled by slogans.

We need a **site-by-site comparison** showing:
1. current cost gap,
2. savings from working-time changes,
3. savings from voluntary exits,
4. savings from redeployment and training,
5. savings from productivity and overhead cuts,
6. management and owner contribution,
7. residual gap under a non-closure package,
8. and the corresponding closure-inclusive scenario.

That is the only serious basis for deciding whether non-closure measures are sufficient.

## To management
You are right to insist that the restructuring must be durable and margin-accretive. A cosmetic compromise would be worthless.

But I do not want management to use closures as a reflex or a negotiation shortcut. That would harden resistance and increase execution risk.

So my message is:
- show the numbers,
- quantify the alternatives,
- state what management itself is contributing,
- and only then argue whether structural footprint changes are genuinely required.

If the non-closure package closes the gap, I would support it over closures. If it does not, then we must keep harder instruments available.

## To the works council
I agree with you that workers should not be the shock absorber for all of this.

I also agree that co-determination matters and that a deal has to be stable if it is to be credible.

Where I disagree is your absolute refusal to contemplate any structural change. That position may feel protective, but if it blocks the company from regaining competitiveness, it will eventually damage the workforce as well.

If you want a non-closure settlement, then help produce one that actually closes the gap in numbers, not just in principle.

## To the union
You are right that the burden must be shared and that temporary concessions must not become permanent erosion of the collective framework.

But I do not share your view that closures and compulsory redundancies should be excluded categorically. They should remain available if all other instruments fail to restore competitiveness.

## To the regional state government
I appreciate your concern for the industrial base and regional stability.

But I cannot support a framework in which the state effectively vetoes all meaningful structural adjustment before the figures are even tested. That would protect the status quo, not the future.

## Bottom line
The family holding will support a settlement only if it is:
- economically credible,
- durable,
- margin-accretive,
- and capable of restoring competitiveness without permanently impairing enterprise value.

So our position is:

- **Yes** to a negotiated solution.
- **Yes** to shared burden-sharing.
- **Yes** to a serious non-closure alternative if it is truly sufficient.
- **No** to unconditional site/job guarantees.
- **No** to an absolute ban on closures or compulsory redundancies.
- **No** to a cosmetic compromise that leaves the company structurally weak.

### What I want next
Bring the **site-by-site quantified package**:
- savings by instrument,
- management contribution,
- owner contribution,
- residual gap,
- and timing through 2030.

If the non-closure route is genuinely enough, it will show up there. If it is not enough, then we should stop pretending otherwise.
