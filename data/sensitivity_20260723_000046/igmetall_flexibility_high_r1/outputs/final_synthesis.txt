## 1. Decision context
The company faces a structural competitiveness crisis: excess German capacity, high fixed and labour costs, weak European demand, and intense low-cost competition. Governance is unusually constrained: co-determination gives labour an effective veto on plant closures, the controlling family and the regional state each hold strong blocking influence, and ordinary investors have limited leverage. The decision is therefore not simply whether to cut costs, but whether to do so through closures and compulsory redundancies or through a negotiated non-closure restructuring that can still deliver durable savings and investment capacity.

## 2. Stakeholder assembly
- **Group & Brand Management**: drives the restructuring and seeks durable cost and capacity reduction.
- **Works Council**: plant-level workforce representation with co-determination power; prioritizes job and site protection.
- **Industrial Union**: collective-bargaining counterpart, able to strike; seeks shared burden-sharing and no forced layoffs.
- **Regional State Government**: anchor shareholder and political actor; seeks industrial base protection and regional stability.
- **Controlling Family Holding**: controlling owner with governance influence; prioritizes long-term value and competitiveness.
- **Institutional Investors**: dispersed capital-market shareholders; prioritize EBITDA, cash flow, and execution certainty.
- **Industrial restructuring expert**: non-decision stakeholder; clarifies financial comparability of scenarios.

## 3. Final positions
### Group & Brand Management
Management’s position is that non-closure measures should be tested, but closures and compulsory redundancies must remain available if the savings gap persists. It accepts burden-sharing and internal flexibility, but rejects unconditional site guarantees and absolute bans on structural action.

### Works Council
The works council’s final position is categorical: no German plant closures, no compulsory redundancies, no weakening of co-determination, and job security through 2030. It is willing to discuss internal flexibility, productivity, redeployment, and voluntary exits only within a binding non-closure framework.

### Industrial Union
The union aligns closely with the works council on rejecting closures and forced layoffs as legitimate restructuring instruments. It is prepared to negotiate temporary labour-cost moderation, voluntary exits, working-time flexibility, redeployment, and productivity measures, but only if the settlement is paired with binding site and job protection.

### Regional State Government
The state supports a serious restructuring and burden-sharing, but does not accept plant closures or compulsory redundancies as the starting point. It wants a quantified non-closure plan first and is willing to back flexible, investment-linked adjustment only if the regional industrial base is not hollowed out.

### Controlling Family Holding
The family holding wants a durable, value-preserving restructuring and agrees the company cannot remain structurally uncompetitive. It will not accept unconditional site guarantees or an absolute ban on closures and redundancies, but says harder measures should be used only if a quantified non-closure package fails.

### Institutional Investors
Investors support a deep restructuring that restores margins and cash flow. They are open to non-closure measures only if these credibly and durably close the economic gap; otherwise they want management to retain closure and workforce-reduction options.

### Industrial restructuring expert
The expert’s position is that the issue must be tested site by site, with the same economic variables across scenarios. Non-closure packages can work where the gap is modest and utilisation can be improved, but if the problem is mostly fixed-cost and underutilisation driven, closures or consolidation will usually dominate on long-run EBITDA and cash flow.

## 4. Genuine conflicts
1. **Absolute site protection vs restructuring flexibility**  
   The works council, union, and state demand no closures; management, the family, and investors insist closures must remain available if required.

2. **No compulsory redundancies vs last-resort workforce reduction**  
   Labour rejects forced layoffs categorically; management, the family, and investors see them as an acceptable last resort.

3. **Unconditional guarantees vs conditional economic commitments**  
   Labour and the state want binding job/site guarantees through 2030; management, owners, and investors want guarantees tied to performance and economics.

4. **Non-closure-first principle vs closure-as-benchmark logic**  
   Labour wants the company to prove non-closure insufficiency before invoking closure; management and capital sides want structural options preserved from the outset.

## 5. Areas of convergence
- All parties accept that the company faces a real structural crisis.
- All agree a negotiated solution is preferable to prolonged confrontation.
- All accept that burden-sharing must not fall solely on labour.
- All want future investment in products and technology preserved.
- All now agree that the decision must be informed by site-by-site, quantified comparisons rather than slogans.
- All reject a purely cosmetic compromise that leaves the economic problem unresolved.

## 6. Key trade-offs for the decision-maker
- **Job and site security vs credible competitiveness gains**
- **Immediate industrial peace vs preserving hard restructuring tools**
- **Binding guarantees vs conditional flexibility**
- **Short-term cash costs of voluntary/internal measures vs long-term savings from footprint reduction**
- **Regional stability and political legitimacy vs financial discipline**
- **Execution risk from confrontation vs execution risk from under-restructuring**

## 7. Integrative pathways
1. **Sequenced non-closure test**
   Implement a time-bound, site-by-site non-closure package first, with agreed savings targets, management/owner contributions, and a review date.

2. **Conditional, site-specific guarantees**
   Protect core sites with binding commitments, while allowing differentiated treatment for sites that underperform against agreed metrics.

3. **Partial footprint adjustment**
   Preserve strategically essential plants while permitting limited consolidation elsewhere, combined with investment and retraining commitments.

4. **Trigger-based fallback**
   If the non-closure package misses quantified targets, predefined second-stage negotiations open on specific sites rather than the entire network.

## 8. Open questions
- What is the exact savings gap by site?
- How much does each non-closure instrument contribute?
- Which sites are structurally viable under a non-closure package?
- What management and owner contribution is concrete and immediate?
- What guarantee language can be made credible without freezing necessary adjustment?
- What threshold proves that non-closure measures are insufficient?
- What investment commitments are truly enforceable over time?

## 9. Recommended next step
Commission and circulate a **site-by-site scenario model** using common assumptions for:
- current output and utilisation,
- current EBITDA,
- annual savings from each non-closure measure,
- annual savings from closure/consolidation,
- one-time cash costs,
- 2030 EBITDA and cash-flow impact,
- capacity and supply-chain effects.

Then convene a second-stage bargaining round focused only on the quantified residual gap and the minimum set of guarantees required to secure labour’s participation.

## 10. Emergent insights that only appeared through multi-turn interaction
- The debate shifted from abstract principles to a **numbers-based legitimacy test**: management no longer can rely on generic claims of “structural” need without site-level proof.
- Labour’s position hardened into a **non-closure absolute**, revealing that “conditional guarantees” are not accepted as meaningful protection once closures remain a fallback.
- Capital and ownership converged on a practical insight: **non-closure measures are acceptable only if they are economically equivalent, not merely politically preferable**.
- The expert clarified that the real dividing line is not “closure vs no closure” in the abstract, but whether the problem is mainly **recurring fixed-cost overhang** or **recoverable inefficiency**.
- The state’s role became clearer as a **political legitimacy broker**: it will support adjustment, but only if the regional industrial base is not visibly sacrificed.
- A previously missing compromise architecture emerged: **sequenced testing with trigger-based fallback**, which could let each side claim it preserved its core principle while moving the process forward.
