I’ll respond clearly and without pretending the core conflict has disappeared.

## Where I agree with others

I agree with management, the family holding, investors, the works council, and the state on one fundamental point: **the company needs a real restructuring, not a cosmetic one**.

I also agree that:
- the cost base has to come down,
- productivity has to improve,
- future investment must be protected,
- and the workforce cannot be the only party making sacrifices.

So yes: there must be burden-sharing. That part is not in dispute.

## Where I disagree

### 1) I do not accept closures and compulsory redundancies as the default “solution”
Management keeps saying closures and forced layoffs cannot be ruled out. I reject that as the basis for negotiations.

That is not a neutral starting point. It is a threat that shifts the burden onto workers before the alternatives have been properly exhausted.

The union’s position is clear:
- **no German plant closures**
- **no compulsory redundancies**
- **no one-sided dismantling of the collective framework**

If management wants a settlement, it must show that it is prepared to solve the problem **without** making jobs and sites the first sacrifice.

### 2) I do not accept the argument that “economic necessity” has already proved the case for closures
What we have heard so far is still a general claim:
- overcapacity exists,
- the margin is weak,
- the cost base is high.

I do not deny that.

But that is not yet a proof that closures or forced redundancies are necessary. It is only a statement that management prefers those instruments if they can get them.

If management says non-closure measures are insufficient, then bring the numbers:
- savings by working-time changes,
- savings by redeployment and voluntary exits,
- savings by productivity and overhead cuts,
- savings by management restraint,
- savings by owner contribution,
- and only then what remains.

Until that is laid out, I do not accept closure and redundancy as “necessary.”

### 3) I do not accept workers paying for this alone
This is the point where the union will draw the hard line.

Employees can and will contribute. We already signaled that. But the contribution must be:
- fair,
- temporary,
- reversible where possible,
- and matched by real concessions from management and owners.

What we will not accept is a package where:
- wages are cut,
- working conditions are worsened,
- jobs are lost,
- and ownership and management preserve their freedom of action and escape responsibility.

That would not be restructuring. That would be social transfer from labour to capital.

## What the union is prepared to discuss

We are still prepared to negotiate a serious package, including:
- temporary or targeted labor-cost moderation,
- bonus restraint,
- working-time flexibility,
- internal redeployment,
- training and reskilling,
- voluntary exits,
- productivity measures,
- and capacity adjustment **without closures**.

That is not a refusal to change. It is a refusal to accept that change must mean irreversible damage to the workforce.

## To the works council

We are aligned on the essentials:
- no closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- no dismantling of job security,
- no weakening of co-determination in practice.

Where I would sharpen the point is this: the union must not be drawn into a false debate where closures are treated as an acceptable benchmark and everything else is measured against that threat. That only legitimises pressure bargaining.

The right question is not: “How close can workers get to a closure scenario without accepting closures?”

The right question is: **What restructuring is sufficient to restore competitiveness while protecting jobs, sites, and the collective framework?**

## To management

You ask for a serious counterproposal. You have one: a **non-closure restructuring package** with real concessions from labour, management, and owners.

But you must stop treating plant closures and compulsory redundancies as the reference point and the workforce as the adjustment variable.

If you really want a settlement, then put forward:
1. the exact savings target,
2. the savings from each internal instrument,
3. the management and ownership contribution,
4. the investment commitments for German sites,
5. and the binding protection of jobs and collective standards.

If you cannot close the gap without closures, then say so plainly and explain why the company has not exhausted the instruments already available in a co-determined system.

## To the state government

We appreciate your resistance to making plant closures the starting point. That matters.

But I want to be very clear: the state should not accept the idea that closures are somehow the “responsible” fallback if workers refuse to surrender enough.

If the state wants to defend the industrial base, then it should insist on:
- a negotiated package,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- no first-choice closures,
- and binding site commitments tied to future investment.

The state’s role should not be to bless ultimatum politics in a softer tone.

## To the family holding and investors

You are right that the package must be credible and margin-accretive. I agree with that.

Where I disagree is the implication that the only credible route is to reserve the right to close plants and force people out.

That may look like financial discipline from a distance, but it ignores the long-term cost of destroying trust, skills, and industrial capability. Once those are gone, they do not come back on a balance sheet.

If you want a durable company, then support a settlement that keeps the workforce engaged rather than demoralised and defensive.

## Bottom line

The union is ready for a serious deal, but not for a deal that:
- sacrifices jobs first,
- weakens the collective agreement,
- and asks workers to absorb the full crisis.

What we can support is:
- shared sacrifice,
- fair burden-sharing,
- temporary labour-cost contribution,
- internal flexibility,
- training and redeployment,
- voluntary exits,
- and binding investment and site protections.

What we cannot support is:
- plant closures,
- compulsory redundancies,
- or a restructuring built on worker insecurity.

If management wants to test a real compromise, bring the **numbers-based non-closure package**. If not, then the conflict remains where it is, and the workforce knows how to defend itself.
