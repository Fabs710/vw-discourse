From the perspective of dispersed capital-market shareholders, the key test is not ideology, regional politics, or bargaining comfort. It is whether the company can restore **competitive margins, cash generation, and long-term equity value** with a package that is **credible, durable, and executable**.

### Where we agree
We agree with management, and to a degree with the family owners, on the core diagnosis:

- the German cost base is too high,
- overcapacity is real,
- margins are structurally too weak,
- and a cosmetic deal will not fix the problem.

We also agree that the workforce should not be treated as the sole adjustment variable. A credible restructuring must involve burden-sharing by all sides, including management.

### Where we disagree
We do **not** support an outcome that leaves the business with:
- an uncompetitive cost base,
- only temporary savings,
- or a settlement that preserves all the structural inefficiencies and simply postpones the pain.

That would be value-destructive. It would likely mean continued margin erosion, weaker investment capacity, and a lower valuation over time.

At the same time, we are also not comfortable with a restructuring strategy that relies primarily on **prolonged confrontation**, plant-closure threats, and uncertainty as a substitute for a clean industrial plan. Strikes, legal deadlock, and governance warfare destroy value too. They increase execution risk and delay the very savings management says it needs.

### On plant closures
We are not attached to closures as a principle. If comparable savings can be delivered through:
- permanent capacity reduction,
- working-time flexibility,
- voluntary exits,
- redeployment,
- and durable labour-cost reduction,

then that is preferable to a closure-heavy solution that creates major disruption.

But alternatives must be **quantified and credible**. We would not support a package that merely re-labels delay as compromise. If the numbers do not add up, then management cannot pretend the competitiveness gap has been solved.

### On compulsory redundancies
We would prefer to avoid compulsory redundancies if a serious deal can achieve the savings otherwise. Forced layoffs are costly, politically explosive, and disruptive. But they also cannot be ruled out absolutely if the business case requires real structural change and if no equivalent alternative is feasible.

### On guarantees
We are very sceptical of broad, unconditional guarantees that lock in today’s cost structure while asking shareholders to fund the downside. Site guarantees, job guarantees, and investment commitments only make sense if they are paired with:
- measurable savings,
- durable productivity gains,
- and a clear timeline to margin improvement.

A guarantee that protects employment but leaves profitability impaired is not a positive outcome for shareholders.

### On management
Management needs to come back with hard numbers:
- how much cost is being removed,
- over what time frame,
- from which locations,
- and with what impact on returns.

Vague statements about competitiveness are not enough. We need a package that can credibly restore return on sales and support the investment case for the equity.

### Bottom line
We would support a negotiated restructuring **if and only if** it delivers:
1. a durable reduction in the cost base,
2. a meaningful reduction in overcapacity,
3. credible labour-cost relief,
4. execution certainty,
5. and a clear path back to competitive margins.

We will not support a cosmetic settlement that preserves structural weakness. And we will not support prolonged conflict that destroys value while pretending to defend it.

If management, labour, and the anchor shareholders can produce a serious deal with real, lasting economics, we are prepared to back it. Otherwise, capital will continue to reprice the business accordingly.
