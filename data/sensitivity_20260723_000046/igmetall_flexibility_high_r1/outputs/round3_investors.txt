We agree with the expert and management on the central point: **this has to be a real restructuring, not a cosmetic peace deal**. From a capital-markets perspective, the non-negotiables are durable margin restoration, lower fixed costs, and execution certainty.

## Where we agree
- The company’s **cost base is structurally too high**.
- **Overcapacity is real** and cannot be hand-waved away.
- The workforce should **not carry the whole burden**.
- A prolonged strike or open-ended conflict would **destroy value**.
- Management must present a **site-by-site, numbers-based case**, not slogans.

## Where we disagree
### 1) An absolute “no closures, no compulsory redundancies” line is not acceptable
That position may be politically understandable, but financially it is not sufficient. If it becomes a hard precondition, it risks locking in:
- excess fixed cost,
- weak utilization,
- continued margin erosion,
- and lower valuation for shareholders.

We cannot support a settlement that leaves the company structurally uncompetitive just to preserve every current footprint.

### 2) But management still has not proven that closures are necessary
That matters. “Closures may be needed” is not yet the same as “closures are required.”  
Before any irreversible move, we want the full comparison the expert described:
- savings from working-time changes,
- voluntary exits,
- redeployment,
- productivity gains,
- overhead cuts,
- management restraint,
- and any residual gap versus a closure scenario.

If a non-closure package can genuinely close the gap on a durable basis, we would prefer that to a destructive shutdown fight.

### 3) Binding guarantees must be conditional, not absolute
We do not support unconditional site/job guarantees through 2030 if they freeze the economics. Guarantees should be tied to:
- quantified savings,
- productivity milestones,
- investment commitments,
- and a review mechanism if targets are missed.

Blanket guarantees that prevent structural correction would be value-destructive.

## What would move us
We would back a deal if it clearly delivers:
1. **durable fixed-cost reduction**
2. **real capacity rationalization**
3. **credible labor-cost relief**
4. **management and owner contribution**
5. **execution certainty**
6. **a clear path back to competitive margins by 2030**

That can include avoiding closures — but only if the alternative is numerically convincing and enforceable.

## Our bottom line
- **No** to a cosmetic settlement that preserves current inefficiency.
- **No** to unconditional site guarantees that block restructuring.
- **No** to prolonged conflict that burns cash and reputation.
- **Yes** to a serious, quantified package that restores competitiveness.

So our position is straightforward:  
bring the site-by-site numbers, show us the residual gap under each scenario, and then choose the least value-destructive path that still restores margins. If the non-closure route is truly sufficient, we can support it. If it is not, then the company must have the freedom to act.
