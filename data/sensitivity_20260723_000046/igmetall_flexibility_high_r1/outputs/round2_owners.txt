We appreciate the positions laid out by management, the works council, the union, and the state government. We are not here to repeat slogans, and we are not going to support a package that is either cosmetic or value-destructive.

Let me be equally direct from the controlling family’s perspective.

## Where we agree

I agree with three points that have been made repeatedly:

1. **The competitiveness problem is real.**  
   The German cost base is too high, overcapacity exists, and the brand must restore margin if it is to fund future investment.

2. **A negotiated solution is preferable.**  
   A prolonged confrontation would destroy value, damage the brand, and weaken the company’s ability to invest.

3. **Burden-sharing is necessary.**  
   It cannot be employees alone. Management, shareholders, and the company itself must contribute too.

On that basis, I also agree that we should not rush into an unnecessarily dramatic closure program if comparable savings can be achieved otherwise.

## Where I disagree

I do **not** support the works council’s and union’s attempt to make “no closures, no compulsory redundancies” absolute red lines in advance.

That position may be understandable politically, but it is not a credible way to close a structural competitiveness gap. If every site is guaranteed and every structural instrument is off-limits, then the company is being asked to restore margins without changing the economics that caused the problem.

I also do **not** support management’s tendency to treat closures and forced layoffs as the default benchmark before the alternatives are fully tested. That is too blunt and too destabilising. It increases resistance, raises execution risk, and can destroy more value than it creates.

So I am pushing back on both extremes:
- no blanket immunity for the current structure,
- but also no default rush to irreversible closures.

## What we require

The test for the family holding is simple: **does the package restore competitiveness in a durable way without permanently impairing the enterprise’s value or governance stability?**

That means we need:
- a **quantified savings package**
- a **real reduction in overcapacity**
- **durable labour-cost relief**
- **meaningful productivity gains**
- **investment commitments in future products and technology**
- and **industrial peace for a meaningful period**

If those elements are not present, then the proposal is not a restructuring; it is postponement.

## On plant closures

I am **not** endorsing closures as the first answer. But I will also not endorse a package that rules them out regardless of economics.

If management can demonstrate that comparable savings and capacity reduction can be achieved through:
- working-time flexibility,
- internal redeployment,
- voluntary exits,
- natural attrition,
- targeted cost adjustments,
- and productivity improvements,

then that route is preferable.

But that alternative has to be **real, measurable, and sufficient**. A non-closure plan that only shuffles costs around or delays the adjustment is not acceptable.

## On compulsory redundancies

I strongly prefer to avoid compulsory redundancies. They are disruptive, expensive, and corrosive to trust.

But again, they cannot be ruled out absolutely if no negotiated path reaches the required savings. I will not accept a settlement that makes employment guarantees unconditional while the owners are asked to absorb the entire burden of adjustment.

## On guarantees

I agree with the employee side and the state on one point: guarantees matter.

But they must be **conditional and credible**, not open-ended. Guarantees that freeze the footprint irrespective of economics would lock in weakness and create future damage.

A sensible framework would have to tie any site or employment commitments to:
- measurable savings,
- investment commitments,
- productivity milestones,
- and a clear review mechanism.

That is stewardship. Blank checks are not.

## On management

Management needs to stop speaking in abstractions and bring numbers.

We need a serious, site-aware package:
- what savings are required,
- which measures produce them,
- what the timeline is,
- how much comes from labor, management, and structural change,
- and what the effect is on margins and investment capacity.

If the plan cannot show that it closes the gap, then it will not command support from ownership.

## On the employee side

The workforce is right that it should not be the sole shock absorber. But the solution cannot be built on the assumption that all pain can be avoided.

If the employee side is prepared to discuss:
- temporary or targeted labor-cost moderation,
- working-time changes,
- redeployment,
- voluntary exits,
- capacity reductions without closures where feasible,
- and real productivity improvements,

then there is a basis for a deal.

If the position remains “all plants open, no compulsory redundancies, no structural change,” then I do not see how the company reaches a viable result.

## On the state government

We respect the state’s role and the importance of the regional industrial base. But I have to say this plainly: a state shareholder cannot block every painful adjustment and still claim to be protecting the company’s future.

Protecting the regional anchor means preserving the company’s earning power, not just preserving the appearance of stability. If the economics do not improve, the long-term risk to the region becomes greater, not smaller.

## Bottom line

The family holding will support a restructuring only if it is:
- economically credible,
- durably margin-accretive,
- socially negotiated,
- and consistent with long-term industrial strength.

We will **not** support:
- a purely defensive package,
- a cosmetic non-closure promise with no numbers,
- or a prolonged conflict that erodes value.

At the same time, we will **not** support a rush into closures or forced redundancies unless management can prove there is no other route to equivalent savings.

So the next step is clear: bring forward a **numbers-based, side-by-side comparison** of:
1. the closure scenario,
2. the non-closure scenario,
3. the cost gap remaining in each,
4. and the binding commitments attached to either path.

Without that, the discussion stays at the level of positions. And positions alone do not restore competitiveness.
