I will respond directly to the positions on the table.

## First: the core line remains unchanged
The works council will not accept:
- **German plant closures**
- **compulsory redundancies**
- **dismantling of the job-security framework**
- **any restructuring that weakens co-determination in practice**

That is not a negotiating posture. That is the minimum line for the workforce.

## Where I agree with others
I agree with management, the family holding, investors, and the state on one point: **the company needs a real restructuring and durable competitiveness improvements**.

I also agree that:
- savings are necessary,
- productivity has to improve,
- future-product investment must be protected,
- and burden-sharing has to be real and not one-sided.

So no one should pretend we are denying the problem.

## Where I disagree sharply

### 1) I reject the idea that closures must remain “on the table”
Management keeps saying closures cannot be ruled out if alternatives fail.

From the works council’s perspective, that is exactly the wrong starting point.

You are not asking for a solution. You are using closure threats to force concessions. That destroys trust and undermines any chance of a stable settlement. If you begin with irreversible damage, you cannot claim to be building a durable future.

If management believes there is a savings gap, then show a plan that closes it **without** closing plants.

### 2) I reject compulsory redundancies as a legitimate instrument
You keep calling them a last resort. For us, they are not an acceptable tool at all.

If a company with co-determination cannot adjust through internal measures, then management has failed to use the instruments available:
- working-time flexibility,
- internal redeployment,
- training and reskilling,
- voluntary exits,
- natural attrition,
- productivity measures,
- temporary labor-cost moderation,
- and capacity adjustment through production shifts, not plant shutdowns.

Compulsory layoffs are not “efficiency.” They are social destruction.

### 3) I reject the claim that a blanket site guarantee is “unrealistic”
The employee side is being told that binding guarantees are too rigid.

That argument is backwards.

The real rigidity comes from management’s insistence on keeping the option to break historic protections whenever it wants. That creates permanent insecurity and makes workers pay for strategic mistakes they did not make.

If you want employees to contribute, then the company must first commit clearly:
- all German plants remain open,
- no forced layoffs,
- co-determination stays intact,
- and site security is guaranteed through 2030.

That is the basis for any contribution from the workforce.

## On the numbers
I am not rejecting numbers. I am rejecting the idea that the numbers automatically justify closures.

Management says it needs a substantial and durable reduction in labor cost and capacity. Fine. Put the numbers on the table **properly**, site by site:
- how much savings come from working-time tools,
- how much from productivity,
- how much from overhead restraint and management cuts,
- how much from voluntary exits,
- how much from redeployment and training,
- how much from investor and owner contribution,
- and what remains after all that.

Do not hide behind a vague “equivalent savings” formula and then point to closures as the benchmark. That is not neutral analysis; that is a pre-decided outcome.

## To management
You say the current structure cannot remain unchanged. I agree. But there is a huge difference between **change** and **closure politics**.

If you want a serious counterproposal from the works council, here it is:
1. **No German plant closures**
2. **No compulsory redundancies**
3. **Binding investment commitments for German sites**
4. **Temporary and fair employee contributions**
5. **Internal capacity adjustment through flexible production, not shutdowns**
6. **Management and owners also take visible, quantified burden-sharing**

If you can prove that this package is impossible, then what you are really saying is not that the workforce is refusing to cooperate. You are saying management has chosen a restructuring model that depends on threatening livelihoods. That is a political choice, not an economic necessity proven in this room.

## To the union
I support the union’s insistence that the workforce is not the sole shock absorber.

I also support the view that a negotiated settlement must be binding, not a promise that evaporates once pressure subsides.

But let me be equally clear: any “temporary” contribution from employees must be matched by **real** contributions from management and owners, not just by speeches about competitiveness.

## To the state government
I appreciate your support for site security, but I will say this clearly: the state should not let itself be drawn into a framework where closures are treated as normal instruments and then “limited” by political goodwill.

If the state wants to defend the industrial base, it must defend it in a concrete way:
- no closures,
- no compulsory layoffs,
- no erosion of job security,
- and no weakening of co-determination.

Anything less leaves the workforce exposed and the region vulnerable.

## To the family holding and investors
You keep asking for a durable, margin-accretive solution. So do we.

But durability will not come from destroying trust, breaking guarantees, and forcing out skilled workers. That may create a short-term accounting effect, but it weakens the industrial backbone that makes long-term value possible.

If you want a company that still has expertise, loyalty, and production capability in 2030, then you need to support a settlement that preserves its sites and workforce.

## What we are prepared to discuss
We are not refusing adjustment. We are refusing social dumping.

We are prepared to discuss:
- working-time reductions and flexibility,
- redeployment across plants,
- training and reskilling,
- voluntary exit programmes,
- temporary bonus restraint or labor-cost moderation,
- productivity measures,
- capacity balancing within the existing German footprint,
- and investment commitments tied to future products.

But this only works if the framework is non-negotiable on the essentials:
- **every German plant stays open**
- **no compulsory redundancies**
- **job security preserved through 2030**
- **co-determination protected**

## What I need next from management
Bring a **site-by-site non-closure plan** with numbers.

Not a threat.
Not a “closure-inclusive” baseline.
Not a demand that the workforce prove a negative under duress.

I want:
- the savings required,
- the savings from each internal instrument,
- the management and owner contribution,
- the investment commitments,
- and the binding guarantees that would make a settlement possible.

If that plan cannot be produced, then the issue is not that the workforce is unreasonable. It is that management is unwilling to use the tools a co-determined company is supposed to have.

## Bottom line
The works council’s position is clear and remains firm:

- **No German plant closures**
- **No compulsory redundancies**
- **No dismantling of the job-security framework**
- **Preserve co-determination**
- **Protect jobs and sites through 2030**

We are ready for a serious restructuring.  
We are not ready to sign away the company’s industrial future under pressure.
