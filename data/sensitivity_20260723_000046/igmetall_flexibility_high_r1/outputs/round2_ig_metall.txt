I’ll speak plainly.

## To the Works Council
We are aligned on the essentials:
- **no plant closures in Germany**
- **no compulsory redundancies**
- **no unilateral dismantling of the job-security framework**
- **no solution that makes workers carry all the pain**

I also agree that a deal must be **binding**, not a promise on paper that management can later evade. Trust here is already fragile; we do not rebuild it with vague assurances.

Where I want to sharpen the line is this: if management keeps treating closures and forced layoffs as the “reference case,” then talks are being conducted under coercion. That is not a fair basis for restructuring. The workforce will not accept being negotiated down from a threat.

## To management
Your message is consistent, but it still misses the point in a way that matters.

Yes, the company has a competitiveness problem.
Yes, the cost base must come down.
Yes, savings must be durable.

But you have **not yet demonstrated** that plant closures or compulsory redundancies are necessary rather than preferred. That distinction matters. Saying “the arithmetic does not work” is not enough if you have not fully used the instruments available in a co-determined company.

What I will not accept:
- turning job insecurity into a permanent lever,
- using plant closures as a bargaining club,
- or demanding labor cost relief while refusing meaningful burden-sharing elsewhere.

If management wants a real settlement, it must show that it is also prepared to contribute through:
- management restraint,
- overhead reductions,
- investment commitments,
- and a credible long-term production plan.

## To the state government
Your position is important and, frankly, closer to reality than management’s ultimatum logic.

I agree that the company is a regional industrial anchor and that closures would have irreversible consequences for skills, suppliers, and trust. I also agree that the state cannot be a passive observer while the workforce is made the sole adjustment variable.

But I want to be equally clear: the state should not just defend “industrial base” in general terms. It should insist on a **negotiated package that protects jobs and sites in practice**, not only in rhetoric.

## To the family owners
You say closures cannot be ruled out if they are the only way to secure the future. That is exactly the kind of statement that keeps the conflict open.

From the union’s perspective, **forced layoffs and closures are not the first choice, and they are not the benchmark**. If you want a durable settlement, then the owners must also be willing to back a path that preserves industrial capability rather than extracting a short-term restructuring gain at the expense of the workforce.

A serious owner contribution means more than saying you prefer negotiated solutions. It means accepting that:
- employees cannot be the shock absorber,
- site protection has real value,
- and competitiveness must be restored through shared adjustment, not unilateral downsizing.

## What the union is prepared to discuss
We are not refusing contribution. We are refusing one-sided sacrifice.

We remain prepared to negotiate:
- **temporary or targeted labor-cost moderation**
- **bonus restraint**
- **working-time adjustments**
- **capacity reductions without closures**
- **internal redeployment and training**
- **voluntary exit instruments**
- **productivity measures**
- **a solidarity-based employee contribution**, if it is matched by real contributions from management and owners

But this only makes sense if the package includes hard protections:
1. **No compulsory redundancies**
2. **No German plant closures**
3. **Binding site commitments**
4. **A durable collective agreement**
5. **A fair burden split across all stakeholders**

## What I need from management now
If you want to test whether a negotiated path exists, then stop speaking in generalities and put forward a **numbers-based alternative to closures**:

- how much savings are required,
- which measures deliver them,
- what employee contribution is expected,
- what management and owner contribution is included,
- and how sites and jobs are secured.

If the answer is that this cannot be done without closures and forced layoffs, then I say the proposal is not an objective necessity — it is a political choice to shift the burden onto labor.

## Bottom line
The union is ready for a serious compromise, but **not** for a deal that:
- sacrifices jobs first,
- weakens the collective wage framework,
- and asks workers to pay for the crisis alone.

If a fair, shared-sacrifice package is on the table, we will engage constructively. If not, the workforce should understand that escalation remains very much available, and the peace obligation is over.
