I’ll be equally direct.

We are still too far apart on the central question: **how this company restores competitiveness in a durable way**.

## Where I agree
I agree with all of you on three points:

1. **The company needs a negotiated settlement**
   - Nobody benefits from a prolonged confrontation for its own sake.
   - Strikes, legal deadlock, and public escalation destroy value.

2. **Burden-sharing is necessary**
   - Employees cannot be the only ones contributing.
   - Management, owners, and the company must also show restraint and commitment.

3. **Investment in future products and technology matters**
   - We do need funds for 2030.
   - But those funds do not appear magically; they must be created by a lower cost base and less overcapacity.

## Where I disagree
I do **not** accept the idea that:
- **all German plants must remain open**
- **no compulsory redundancies can ever be considered**
- and **the existing cost and capacity structure can be preserved**
while still expecting this company to become competitive.

That is not a restructuring. That is a delay mechanism.

The market is not waiting for us to resolve this politically. European demand is shrinking, competitors are leaner, and our domestic cost base is too high. If we do not make structural changes now, we will simply compound the problem and leave even less room for investment later.

## To the Works Council
You say closures and forced layoffs are ruled out. I understand why you say that, but I do not accept it as a governing principle.

A blanket veto on closures and compulsory redundancies makes it impossible to fix a structural overcapacity problem when negotiated alternatives do not deliver enough savings.

You ask for numbers. Here they are in principle:
- We need a **substantial and durable reduction in labor cost**
- We need a **meaningful reduction in capacity**
- We need a **lasting improvement in productivity and flexibility**
- We need a **package that materially improves returns by 2030**

If your answer is “only internal measures, regardless of whether they are sufficient,” then we are not discussing a solution, only a refusal to confront the arithmetic.

## To the union
You say you are prepared to discuss wage moderation, bonus restraint, working-time tools, redeployment, and voluntary exits. That is welcome, but it is still not enough unless it produces **real, quantified savings**.

I am not interested in a package that looks cooperative but leaves the cost base structurally uncompetitive.

And let me be very clear: management will not sign up to a scheme where:
- labor gives temporary concessions,
- the company keeps all its overcapacity,
- and the structural gap remains in place.

That would merely postpone the next crisis.

## To the state government
I respect your role and the importance of the regional industrial base. But I need to say plainly: if the state uses its position to block any serious restructuring, it will not be preserving the company — it will be protecting inertia.

A state shareholder has a responsibility not just to employment optics, but to the long-term viability of the enterprise. If the company’s economics do not improve, the region’s position becomes weaker, not stronger.

## To the family owners
You are right that the outcome must be value-accretive and that cosmetic restructuring is not acceptable. But I also need clarity from you: are you prepared to back a package that actually reduces overcapacity and labour costs, even if that means difficult decisions at site level?

Because if the answer is “yes, but not any visible pain,” then we are back to square one.

## What management is prepared to support
We remain open to avoiding closures **if** the alternative delivers equivalent structural savings. That means:
- substantial working-time and flexibility changes,
- permanent cost reductions,
- capacity rationalisation,
- voluntary exit and redeployment instruments,
- targeted labor-cost relief,
- and binding investment commitments tied to performance.

But I need to say this firmly: **if those alternatives do not close the gap, then closures and compulsory redundancies cannot be ruled out.**

That is not a threat for drama. It is a statement of what it takes to restore competitiveness.

## On guarantees
I am not rejecting guarantees in principle. But guarantees must be **conditional and credible**.

A guarantee that freezes the footprint irrespective of economics is not a guarantee; it is a blockage. If we are to make commitments on sites and jobs, then they must be tied to:
- measurable savings,
- productivity improvements,
- and a path back to sustainable margins.

## On industrial action
If the response is to escalate into strikes, that will damage output, brand reputation, and trust — but it will not change the underlying need for structural change.

I would prefer not to go there. But I will not pretend that pressure alone can make a weak business model strong.

## Bottom line
Management is ready for a serious settlement, not a cosmetic one.

The test for any deal is simple:
- Does it **durably** lower costs?
- Does it **meaningfully** reduce overcapacity?
- Does it **restore margin** and investment capacity?
- Does it do so **in time** to keep the company competitive through 2030?

If the answer is yes, we can negotiate the instruments.
If the answer is no, then we are being asked to accept a compromise that postpones the problem and weakens the company further.

So I am asking again for a real counterproposal from the employee side — one that achieves the required savings at a scale the business can actually use. If that cannot be done without structural change at the site level, then that reality has to be faced, not denied.
