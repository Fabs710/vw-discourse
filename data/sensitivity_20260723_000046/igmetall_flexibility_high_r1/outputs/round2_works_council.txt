The Works Council’s position remains unchanged, and I will state it plainly:

**No plant closures in Germany. No compulsory redundancies. No dismantling of the job-security framework.**

If management wants a solution, it must stop treating closures and forced layoffs as the benchmark and then asking the workforce to “find alternatives” under threat. That is not a negotiation; that is pressure politics.

## Where I agree
I do agree on one point with management, the family owners, and investors:  
the company does need a **credible, durable competitiveness plan**. We are not denying the market reality.

I also agree that:
- there must be **real savings**,
- there must be **productivity gains**,
- there must be **investment in future products and technologies**,
- and **burden-sharing** cannot be placed on employees alone.

## Where I disagree
I disagree fundamentally with the idea that plant closures or compulsory redundancies are acceptable “last resorts” that can simply be kept on the table as a threat.

That is exactly the kind of logic that destroys trust and makes any settlement unstable.

If you want a negotiated solution, then start from the premise that:
- **all German plants remain open**
- **no one is forced out**
- **capacity is adjusted through negotiated internal measures**
- **co-determination remains intact**
- **site and job security are protected through 2030**

Anything less is not a solution the workforce can accept.

## On the numbers
Management keeps saying the arithmetic does not work. Then put the arithmetic on the table.

We are prepared to discuss a serious package based on:
- working-time reductions or flexibility,
- internal redeployment,
- training and reskilling,
- natural attrition and voluntary exits,
- temporary or targeted labor-cost moderation,
- efficiency gains,
- and capacity adaptation **without plant closure**.

But I will be very clear:  
**we will not sign up to “equivalent savings” if the only way to get them is closures or forced layoffs.**

If management says equivalent savings are impossible without closures, then I say management has not done its job properly or is refusing to use the real instruments available in a co-determined company.

## On guarantees
The only credible basis for any contribution from the workforce is this:
1. **binding site guarantees for all German plants**
2. **no compulsory redundancies**
3. **investment commitments tied to future products**
4. **durable protection of co-determination and the job-security framework**
5. **a negotiated contribution that is temporary, fair, and reversible**

Without those guarantees, there is no reason for workers to give up pay, flexibility, or security so that management can preserve its freedom to restructure against them later.

## To management
Your argument boils down to: “accept closures now, or competitiveness suffers later.”  
We reject that false choice.

Competitiveness must be restored by:
- smarter production planning,
- better utilization,
- productivity measures,
- investment,
- and fair burden-sharing.

Not by sacrificing plants and livelihoods to satisfy a cost target.

If you want a serious counterproposal, then bring one that **proves** you can close the gap **without** plant closures and **without** forced redundancies. Not slogans. Not threats. Numbers, site-by-site, and a legally enforceable framework.

## To the family owners and investors
You both keep saying no option can be ruled out. I say this clearly:  
**for the workforce, plant closure and compulsory layoffs are ruled out.**

You may call that rigid. I call it responsible. Once a plant is closed and skilled workers are lost, that capability does not come back. The damage is permanent.

If capital wants durability, then it should support a settlement that preserves industrial capability instead of demanding a short-term accounting fix that weakens the company’s backbone.

## To the state government
I appreciate that you are drawing a line against industrial disarmament. But I want to be equally clear: the state should not open the door to “conditional” closures by accepting the premise that they are legitimate tools if the numbers are inconvenient.

If the state wants to defend the regional industrial base, then it must stand behind:
- no German plant closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- and a serious industrial policy for the existing sites.

## Bottom line
The Works Council is ready to negotiate a **real restructuring** — but only one that:
- keeps every German plant open,
- avoids forced layoffs,
- preserves co-determination,
- and secures jobs and investment through 2030.

If management cannot deliver that, then it should not pretend there is a compromise. There is only conflict, and the workforce knows how to defend itself.
