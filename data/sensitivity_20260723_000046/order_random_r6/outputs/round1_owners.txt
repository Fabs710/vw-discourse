We share one key premise: the company is under real pressure and cannot continue with an uncompetitive cost base. Protecting jobs in the long run requires restoring competitiveness first.

That said, I do **not** accept the idea that plant closures or deep structural reductions are automatically off the table. If the German footprint is materially oversized, then pretending otherwise would be irresponsible. As controlling owners, we have to look at the enterprise as a whole, not just at preserving every site in its current form.

Where I agree with you:
- A solution that destroys trust and industrial capability would be value-destructive.
- A negotiated employee contribution is preferable to a prolonged confrontation.
- The company needs investment, product commitment, and a durable settlement, not a temporary patch.

Where I disagree:
- **No forced redundancies and no closures as an absolute principle** is too rigid for the situation as described. If savings cannot be achieved otherwise, structural measures must remain available.
- The burden cannot fall only on management or owners, but neither can the workforce veto every meaningful change while the business continues to lose competitiveness.
- Guarantees are only credible if they are linked to real, enforceable contributions on cost and capacity.

My position is therefore:
1. **We support a serious restructuring package** that restores margins and competitiveness.
2. **We are open to negotiated alternatives to outright closures** if they deliver equivalent savings and capacity reduction.
3. **If those alternatives do not close the gap, deeper structural measures must stay on the table.**

So I would encourage the union side to come forward with a package that is measurable, durable, and commensurate with the scale of the problem:
- working-time flexibility,
- temporary wage restraint,
- bonus moderation,
- capacity reductions,
- site-specific productivity improvements,
- and, where unavoidable, orderly restructuring rather than denial of reality.

In return, the company should make credible commitments on future investment and the long-term role of German operations. But those commitments can only be justified if the cost side is genuinely fixed.

Bottom line: I am **not** aligned with a position that rules out closures or compulsory reductions in advance. I am aligned with a settlement that protects the company’s long-term value, preserves labour peace if possible, and restores competitiveness on a durable basis.
