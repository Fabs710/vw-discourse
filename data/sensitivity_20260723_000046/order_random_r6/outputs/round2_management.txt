We are past the stage where generalities help. So let me respond directly.

### To the union
I acknowledge two things:
- you are prepared to discuss a real employee contribution;
- you understand that a cosmetic deal is not enough.

That is a start. But your core line remains unacceptable: **“no plant closures” and “no compulsory redundancies” as absolute preconditions**.

That is not a restructuring offer. It is an attempt to preserve the existing footprint while shifting the cost problem elsewhere. I cannot accept that, because it does **not** solve the structural overcapacity and cost-gap problem we are facing.

You say closures are irreversible and therefore should not be “on the table.” Precisely: they are serious, which is why I am not using them lightly. But seriousness is the point. If the numbers show we need a smaller footprint to restore the brand’s return-on-sales and fund future products, then closure must remain a real option. Otherwise we are pretending the German cost base can be fixed with voluntary gestures alone.

I do agree that:
- forced layoffs are not the preferred route,
- negotiated exits are better,
- working-time flexibility, wage moderation, and bonus restraint should be part of the package,
- and investment commitments matter.

But none of that is sufficient unless the result is a **durable, quantified reduction in cost and capacity**. If your counterproposal cannot close the gap, then it is not credible.

### To the works council
You are demanding binding site guarantees to 2030 before we have even agreed on the restructuring mechanics. That reverses the logic.

Guarantees are only credible if they sit on top of a restructuring that actually restores competitiveness. Otherwise we are locking in an uncompetitive structure and asking capital markets to finance it indefinitely. That is not responsible management, and it is not fair to the company or the wider workforce.

I also reject the idea that discussing closures is “pressure politics” while insisting on permanent site protection is somehow neutral. It is not neutral. It is a hard constraint on the company’s ability to adapt. If we cannot adapt, the longer-term outcome for jobs is worse, not better.

### To the controlling family
I am aligned with your core message: **the enterprise value problem is real, and the cost base must come down structurally**. Where I differ is only on sequencing and execution.

You are right that alternative measures should be tested. But you should not pretend that those alternatives are automatically enough. If they do not deliver equivalent and verified savings, then deeper structural measures remain necessary. That includes closures if required.

I support your call for scenarios. Let’s be explicit:
1. a negotiated package without closures;
2. a hybrid package;
3. a closure-inclusive package if needed.

That is the right framework. But the point of the exercise is not to find a politically painless answer. It is to find one that actually restores competitiveness.

### To the state government
Your position is understandable politically, but I need to be blunt: **“no closures as a first resort” is not a solution to the problem of uncompetitive factories**.

You cannot simultaneously insist that:
- German sites stay open,
- employment is protected,
- the state’s veto must remain decisive,
- and yet the company must somehow achieve a major cost reset without touching the footprint.

That is exactly the kind of constraint stack that leads to delay, strikes, and eventually a weaker company.

I am not dismissing regional responsibility. I am saying regional responsibility also means preventing a slow decline through underpowered compromises. If we do not restore margins, there will be less investment, less product allocation, and ultimately less employment security.

### Where I stand
My position is unchanged and clear:

- **We need a structural reduction in cost and capacity.**
- **Employee contribution is necessary.**
- **Management and owners must contribute.**
- **Investment commitments will follow a credible restructuring, not replace it.**
- **Closures and compulsory reductions cannot be ruled out in advance.**

### What I am prepared to do
I am prepared to negotiate the form of the restructuring. I am **not** prepared to negotiate away the need for one.

So I am asking the other sides to stop using absolute red lines as if they were a business plan. Bring forward something that actually closes the gap:
- quantified savings,
- durable labour-cost reductions,
- capacity reduction,
- productivity improvement,
- and a credible path to return to target margins.

If you can do that without closures, fine — but it must be proven, not assumed. If you cannot, then we will have to use the structural tools that remain available.

That is the reality of this situation.
