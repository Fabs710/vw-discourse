We agree on one central point: **the current cost base is not acceptable, and a cosmetic settlement would be value-destructive**. From an investor perspective, that matters above all else. If margins remain structurally impaired, we have no reason to reward the stock with a higher valuation, and capital will continue to move elsewhere.

Where I differ is on the **way** this gets done.

### What we support
- A **durable reduction in costs and capacity**.
- A settlement that materially improves the return-on-sales trajectory.
- **Employee contribution** as part of the package, including working-time flexibility, wage moderation, and bonus restraint.
- **Binding investment commitments** if they are tied to real restructuring rather than replacing it.

### What we do not support
- A deal that **preserves the status quo under the label of “stability.”**
- A package that relies on short-term labour peace while leaving the cost structure uncompetitive.
- Any outcome that postpones hard decisions and leaves the company back in crisis in 12–24 months.

### On the union position
I understand the concern about forced redundancies and plant closures, because prolonged conflict destroys value too. But an absolute “no closures, no compulsory redundancies” line is not credible if the footprint is genuinely oversized. If the alternatives do not deliver equivalent savings, then refusing structural change simply shifts the burden into lower margins, weaker investment, and eventually worse outcomes for everyone.

At the same time, I do **not** think management should treat closures as the first and only answer. If equivalent savings can be achieved through negotiated capacity reduction, working-time changes, and a real employee contribution, that is preferable because it reduces disruption and execution risk.

### On the state government position
We respect the role of regional stability and the industrial base. But the state cannot insist on protecting every site while expecting the market to accept underperformance. If German plants remain structurally too expensive, then the state is not protecting jobs by blocking change — it is delaying a deterioration that will show up in margins, investment, and eventually employment anyway.

### On management’s position
We support the principle that the company needs a **structural reset**. But “closures must remain on the table” is only acceptable if management can show:
- the savings are real and durable,
- the plan is executable,
- the restructuring is not just a headline move that triggers strikes and damages the business,
- and there is a credible path to restore competitiveness without destroying future capacity unnecessarily.

### Bottom line
Our support is conditional on **credible, quantified, durable restructuring**. We are not interested in symbolic compromise or political theatre. If the final package delivers:
- meaningful capacity reduction,
- real labour-cost improvement,
- clear accountability from management and owners,
- and a credible path back to competitive margins,

then we can support it.

If it does **not** do those things, then we will treat it as insufficient and value-destructive — regardless of whether it is presented as a peace settlement.
