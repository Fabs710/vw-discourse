Here is the independent restructuring assessment.

## Short answer
**A no-closure, no-forced-layoff package can sometimes close a meaningful part of the gap, but whether it can close the full gap depends on the size of the underlying cost overhang.** In a German automotive plant network, **wage moderation, working-time changes, bonus restraint, productivity gains, and internal redeployment can usually generate savings in the low-to-mid hundreds of millions annually across a large workforce** if implemented broadly and durably.  

However, if management’s diagnosis is correct that the company has **structural overcapacity plus a materially uncompetitive fixed-cost base**, then **those measures alone are often not enough** to restore a credible return-on-sales target. In that case, **some combination of capacity reduction, product simplification, and footprint adjustment is usually required**—though not necessarily immediate plant closure or compulsory redundancies.

## What can be closed by “internal” measures
The following instruments can produce real savings:

### 1) Wage moderation / temporary concessions
- Freezes, reduced special payments, bonus restraint, or other negotiated labor-cost concessions can reduce annual cash outflow.
- But these are usually **time-limited** unless they are embedded in a broader structural deal.
- On their own, they tend to improve liquidity and EBITDA, but **do not permanently fix excess capacity**.

### 2) Working-time reductions and shift flexibility
- Fewer paid hours, lower overtime, shift consolidation, and higher flexibility can reduce labor cost per vehicle.
- This can be powerful if the plants are underutilized.
- But if volume is structurally below network capacity, the saving is partly offset by **high fixed costs spread over fewer units**.

### 3) Productivity measures
- Line balancing, logistics simplification, indirect headcount reduction, shorter changeovers, lower scrap, and better automation utilization can raise output per worker.
- These are economically valuable, but they usually require **time, management discipline, and capital investment**.
- Typical turnaround risk: productivity plans are often **overstated ex ante** and delayed in execution.

### 4) Internal redeployment and voluntary exits
- Voluntary severance, retirement bridges, and transfers can reduce headcount without forced layoffs.
- This is often the most workable labor-adjustment tool in co-determined German structures.
- But it has limits: if the workforce is aging, the short-run cost can be high; if skill profiles don’t match, redeployment is incomplete.

### 5) Management overhead and owner contribution
- Senior management cuts, lower discretionary spending, and investment prioritization can contribute.
- These are important for credibility, but in a large automotive restructuring they are usually **not the main source of savings**.

## The key economic issue: fixed cost and capacity
The core question is not just “can wages be reduced?” It is:

**Is the company carrying too much installed capacity for the volume and product mix it can realistically sell at acceptable margins?**

If yes, then the company has a **structural fixed-cost problem**:
- factories,
- tooling,
- logistics,
- indirect labor,
- depreciation,
- and plant-level overhead

remain too high relative to output.

In that case, internal labor concessions can help, but they do **not fully solve** the mismatch. You can lower unit cost only so far if the denominator—utilization—remains weak.

## Can the gap be closed without closures and forced layoffs?
### My judgment
**Possibly, but only if the required margin improvement is modest and the company’s overcapacity is not severe.**  

If the target is to recover, say, a **small to moderate margin gap**, then a negotiated package of:
- wage moderation,
- bonus restraint,
- working-time flexibility,
- voluntary exits,
- redeployment,
- utilization improvements,
- and overhead cuts

could plausibly get there.

If the target is to move from a **clearly loss-making or near-breakeven position to a robust automotive target margin**, then **internal measures alone are usually insufficient** unless they are extraordinarily deep and the company can also improve:
- mix,
- pricing,
- volumes,
- and platform productivity.

## What would need to be quantified to answer definitively
I would need at minimum:

1. **Current and target operating margin / return on sales**
2. **Annual volume by plant and platform**
3. **Fixed vs variable cost split**
4. **Headcount by site, function, and skill group**
5. **Average labor cost per vehicle**
6. **Capacity utilization by plant**
7. **Overtime, shift patterns, and working-time arrangements**
8. **Voluntary exit potential by age cohort**
9. **Forecast demand by model and market**
10. **The savings already identified by management**
11. **The cash cost of any severance or early-retirement program**

Without those, any claim that the gap can definitely be closed without structural footprint change is **not yet proven**.

## Practical restructuring conclusion
### Most likely outcome in industrial terms
The economically credible middle ground is usually:
- **no immediate mass closure program**
- **no forced layoffs if avoidable**
- **a significant employee contribution**
- **voluntary workforce reduction**
- **productivity and utilization commitments**
- **and some capacity rationalization through shifts, lines, or site-role changes**

That is different from saying “nothing closes.” It means **avoid closure as the first instrument**, but do not exclude **selective capacity exit** if the numbers still do not work.

## Bottom line
From a turnaround perspective:

- **Labour-only adjustment is unlikely to be enough if the company truly has structural overcapacity.**
- **A negotiated no-closure package may be feasible if the savings gap is moderate and productivity gains are real.**
- **If the gap is large, some form of footprint rationalization is usually economically necessary**, even if implemented through softer tools first.
