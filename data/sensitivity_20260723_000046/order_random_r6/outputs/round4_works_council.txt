I will be direct.

The positions from management, the controlling family, and investors still amount to the same thing: **keep closures and forced redundancies available as pressure on the workforce, and call that “responsible restructuring.”** I reject that.

The experts did **not** give you a free pass to threaten sites. They said internal measures can close a meaningful part of the gap, and may be enough if the shortfall is moderate. That is exactly why I insist on a **binding no-closure framework first**, not a “protected phase” that secretly serves as a countdown to closure.

## Where I agree
I agree on the following:
- The company has a real competitiveness problem.
- A cosmetic deal would be worthless.
- Labour must contribute.
- Management and owners must contribute.
- The numbers must be quantified plant by plant.
- Any durable solution must fund investment and future products through 2030.

I also agree that internal instruments must be serious:
- temporary wage moderation,
- bonus restraint,
- working-time flexibility,
- productivity gains,
- voluntary exits,
- retirement windows,
- redeployment,
- shift and utilisation changes,
- management overhead restraint,
- executive contribution.

That is a real package. It is not a refusal to help.

## Where I disagree
### 1) Closures are not a neutral fallback
Management keeps saying closures are only a last resort. That is exactly the problem. For workers, a “last resort” that is already on the table is still a threat. It changes the bargaining balance and undermines trust.

If the company wants a genuine settlement, then for the negotiation phase there must be:
- **no plant closures**
- **no compulsory redundancies**
- **binding site guarantees through 2030**
- **binding investment and product commitments**
- **a genuine co-determination role in shaping the restructuring**

Without that, the workforce is being asked to pay first while the other side keeps all the weapons.

### 2) Forced layoffs are unacceptable
This is not moving. If management says forced layoffs are necessary, then management has not yet brought a credible industrial plan. Full stop.

We are not accepting a restructuring where workers are made disposable because management wants a faster or cleaner balance-sheet answer.

### 3) “Prove it plant by plant” cuts both ways
I agree with the call for plant-by-plant numbers. Good. Then put them on the table.

But do not twist that into a justification for closure threats. The burden is on management to prove that every non-destructive alternative has been genuinely exhausted. Until that proof exists, closure is not “prudence”; it is leverage.

### 4) The state is still too cautious
To the regional government: “no closures as a first resort” is not enough. If you really want to protect the industrial base, you need to back a **binding closure freeze during negotiation**, not a vague sequencing formula that can be bypassed later.

## What I support
I support a **real no-closure settlement** built on:
- temporary wage moderation,
- bonus restraint,
- working-time flexibility,
- productivity measures,
- voluntary exits,
- retirement windows,
- redeployment,
- utilisation changes,
- management overhead cuts,
- executive restraint,
- owner contribution,
- investment commitments,
- product commitments,
- and binding site guarantees through 2030.

That is a serious contribution from labour, matched by serious responsibility from management and owners.

## What I do not support
I do **not** support:
- closure as a bargaining threat,
- compulsory redundancies,
- a “protected phase” that is really just a delay before the same coercive agenda returns,
- or any deal that preserves pressure on the workforce while promising nothing binding in return.

## On the experts
The expert assessments do **not** say closures must happen. They say:
- internal measures can close a meaningful part of the gap,
- a no-closure package may be enough if the gap is moderate,
- only if the gap is truly large does footprint adjustment become an issue.

That means the correct sequence is:
1. test internal measures seriously,
2. lock in labour’s contribution,
3. lock in management and owner contribution,
4. protect every German site during that process,
5. then review the actual numbers.

Not the other way around.

## On the proposed “protected negotiation phase”
I am prepared to discuss a protected phase **only if it is a binding moratorium**, not a polite pause.

That means:
- no closures during the phase,
- no compulsory redundancies during the phase,
- full disclosure of plant-by-plant economics,
- agreed success criteria,
- and a no-closure package as the primary scenario.

If management wants a review point, fine. But the review point cannot be preloaded toward closures before the internal package has actually been tried and verified.

## My bottom line
The works council’s position remains unchanged:

- **Every German plant stays open**
- **No compulsory redundancies**
- **Binding job and site guarantees through 2030**
- **Real shared sacrifice from labour, management, and owners**
- **A genuine co-determination role**
- **No closure threat used as bargaining pressure**

If management, the family, and investors can support that framework and present the numbers honestly, we will negotiate seriously.

If not, then they should not expect the workforce to accept a restructuring that treats German sites and jobs as disposable. We are not here to ratify a dismantling of the company under the banner of competitiveness.
