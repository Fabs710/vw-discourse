From an industrial finance and capacity-planning perspective, the discussion is now at the point where the deciding variable is not rhetoric but **residual gap after internal measures**.

## My assessment of the current state

### 1) What the bargaining positions imply economically
- **Management / family / investors** are asking to preserve option value: closures and redundancies remain available if savings fall short.
- **Union / works council / state** are asking to remove the most destructive options up front and rely on internal adjustments first.
- Those positions are not just political; they imply different **risk allocations**:
  - capital wants the risk of under-delivery shifted to labor unless the package closes the gap,
  - labor wants the risk of restructuring failure shifted back to management/owners unless necessity is proven.

### 2) What is economically plausible
Based on the facts given, it is plausible that:
- **internal measures can close a meaningful part of the gap**, especially through:
  - wage moderation,
  - bonus restraint,
  - working-time changes,
  - utilization improvement,
  - redeployment,
  - voluntary exits,
  - overhead and management cuts.
- But if the company has **material structural overcapacity** and a high fixed-cost burden, then a no-closure package may be **insufficient** unless the required savings are moderate.

So the real question is not “closures or no closures,” but:

> **After internal measures, what annual savings remain unclosed, and do they justify footprint rationalization?**

## What would make a no-closure package financially credible
A no-closure package is credible if it can show all of the following:
1. **Quantified savings by measure**
   - not broad promises, but euro amounts and timing.
2. **Plant-by-plant model**
   - to show where capacity, labor cost, and utilization changes land.
3. **Durable margin improvement**
   - enough to restore the brand’s return-on-sales target and fund future investment.
4. **Workforce reduction via soft tools**
   - attrition, voluntary exits, retirement windows, redeployment.
5. **Management/owner contribution**
   - overhead cuts, executive restraint, capex discipline, investment commitments.
6. **Trigger-based review**
   - if savings miss target, the parties already know what happens next.

If those elements are present and the residual gap is small or moderate, then a no-closure solution can be economically rational.

## When closures become economically justified
Closures become more defensible if:
- a plant is structurally underutilized,
- cost per unit stays uncompetitive after internal measures,
- duplicate capacity exceeds what demand can support,
- or the company cannot otherwise reach a financeable margin structure.

In that case, closure is not a first choice, but it may be the least costly way to remove fixed cost.

## Main risk in the current debate
The biggest risk is a **half-turnaround**:
- some concessions are obtained,
- some internal savings are delivered,
- but the cost base remains too high,
- so the company exits the dispute with less conflict but still weak competitiveness.

That is a common failure mode in heavily blocked governance systems.

## What would change the analysis
I would need the following to make a firmer judgment:
- current and target return-on-sales by brand and plant,
- utilization rates by site,
- fixed vs. variable cost split,
- the euro value of each proposed employee measure,
- one-time closure/shutdown costs,
- severance and redeployment costs,
- investment requirements through 2030,
- and liquidity/covenant constraints.

Without those, no one can say with confidence whether internal measures are enough.

## Bottom line
My neutral industrial-finance view is:

- **A negotiated internal-adjustment package is plausible**
- **if the residual gap is moderate**
- **but if the gap is large, some footprint or capacity reduction will likely be required**

So the right next step is not a principle statement. It is a **scenario model**:

1. no-closure package  
2. hybrid package  
3. closure-led package  

For each:
- annual savings,
- headcount effect,
- capacity effect,
- one-time costs,
- implementation risk,
- and impact on margin/investment capacity.
