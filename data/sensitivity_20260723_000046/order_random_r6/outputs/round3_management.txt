Let me be equally direct.

The independent assessments confirm what management has said from the start: **the company has a real structural cost and capacity problem, and internal measures may reduce it, but they do not automatically eliminate it**. That is the key point. I am not interested in symbolic peace. I am interested in a cost base that is actually competitive by 2030.

## To the union
I acknowledge one thing clearly: you are right that a cosmetic deal is worthless. You are also right that voluntary exits, working-time flexibility, wage moderation, bonus restraint, productivity gains, and redeployment are all part of the solution.

But your red lines are still **not acceptable** as stated.

- **“No plant closures”**
- **“No compulsory redundancies”**
- **Binding guarantees first, restructuring later**

That is not a restructuring offer. It is a demand that management absorb the full adjustment burden while being denied the tools needed to fix an oversized footprint.

The experts did **not** say that your no-closure position is sufficient. They said it **might** work if the gap is modest. That is not the same thing. If the gap is moderate, fine — then quantified internal measures may close it. But if the gap is large, and management’s diagnosis is that the German footprint is structurally too costly and too big, then you cannot simply declare the necessary instruments off-limits.

So my position is unchanged:
- I am open to avoiding forced layoffs.
- I am open to negotiated exits, redeployment, and working-time solutions.
- I am open to using closures only if the numbers require it.

But I will **not** accept a framework that rules out closures and compulsory redundancies in advance, before the savings gap is actually closed.

## To the works council
You speak as if site guarantees to 2030 can be separated from the economics of the business. They cannot.

I respect co-determination. I also respect the industrial base. But I do **not** accept binding site guarantees as a substitute for competitiveness. If we promise everything first and solve the cost problem later, we are locking in decline.

The expert assessment is helpful here: internal adjustments can do a lot, but they do not magically solve fixed-cost absorption if utilization remains weak. That is exactly why I insist on quantified scenarios and why I will not accept a blanket no-closure stance.

## To the regional state government
Your political position is understandable. But it is not enough to say “no closures as a first resort” and then expect the company to somehow recover margins through goodwill.

The state has a legitimate interest in regional stability. But regional stability depends on a viable company, not just preserved sites. If we keep an uncompetitive structure in place, the result is weaker investment, weaker product allocation, and ultimately weaker employment.

So I am not dismissing your role. I am saying your role cannot be used to freeze the footprint regardless of economics.

## To the controlling family
I agree with your overall line: **value preservation requires a real structural reset**, not a cosmetic compromise.

Where I would push you is on the need for discipline in execution. You are right that alternatives should be tested. You are right that no-closure packages should be quantified. But the purpose of that exercise is not to protect every site at any cost. It is to prove whether the cost gap can be closed without deeper structural action.

If the no-closure package closes the gap with durable, verifiable savings, then good. If it does not, then the company must have the freedom to act. I stand with that.

## To the investors
You are correct that capital will not support a deal that preserves overcapacity and pretends the margin problem has disappeared.

But let me be equally clear: investors do not get to define success as “the maximum immediate closure or layoff outcome.” Value is not served by reckless industrial conflict. It is served by the **best durable combination** of savings, execution, labor peace, and future investment capacity.

So yes, you should support a negotiated deal if it is real, quantified, and durable. But if it is not, then you should not expect management to sign up for a cosmetic compromise.

## What the experts actually told us
The experts did **not** give the union a veto. They said:
- internal measures can generate meaningful savings,
- a no-closure package may work if the gap is moderate,
- but if the overcapacity and fixed-cost burden are large, some form of footprint reduction is usually economically necessary.

That is precisely why management insists on keeping closures and redundancies available as real options.

## My bottom line
I remain committed to:
- a **structural** reduction in cost and capacity,
- a **meaningful employee contribution**,
- management and owner discipline,
- and a settlement that restores margins and funds investment to 2030.

I remain **not** prepared to accept:
- a blanket no-closure commitment,
- a blanket no-compulsory-redundancy commitment,
- or guarantees that come before economics.

If the other sides can bring a no-closure package that **verifiably** closes the gap and restores competitiveness, I will listen. But “we prefer it” or “we fear conflict” is not enough.

So here is the next move I expect:
1. A plant-by-plant quantified comparison of the no-closure, hybrid, and closure-inclusive scenarios.
2. A clear statement of the residual cost gap after internal measures.
3. A real labour-cost contribution proposal.
4. A real management and owner contribution.
5. A credible answer on how margins reach target.

Until then, closures and compulsory redundancies remain available as necessary tools. That is not a threat. It is the reality of restoring this company’s competitiveness.
