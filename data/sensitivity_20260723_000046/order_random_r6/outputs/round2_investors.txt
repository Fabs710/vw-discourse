We agree with the central premise from management, the controlling family, and to a lesser extent the union/state: **the current cost base is not sustainable, and a cosmetic settlement would destroy value**. From a capital-markets perspective, that is the non-negotiable starting point. If margins remain structurally impaired, the equity story remains broken.

Where we part company with several of you is on **how much economic reality is being conceded**.

### To the union and works council
We understand the insistence on avoiding compulsory redundancies and plant closures. Prolonged disruption would also hurt value. But your current stance is still too absolute.

- **“No closures” as a hard red line is not credible** if the footprint is genuinely oversized.
- **“No compulsory redundancies” may be a desirable outcome, but not an unconditional one** if alternative measures fail to deliver equivalent savings.
- Binding site guarantees to 2030 are only defensible if they sit on top of a package that actually restores competitiveness.

What we could support is:
- negotiated working-time flexibility,
- temporary wage moderation,
- bonus restraint,
- voluntary exits and redeployment,
- capacity reductions through shifts and utilisation,
- and a meaningful employee contribution.

But that only works if it is **quantified and durable**. If it becomes a way to preserve too much capacity and too much cost, then it is just delay dressed up as partnership.

### To the state government
We respect the political and regional stakes, but the state cannot treat every closure as illegitimate while expecting investors to tolerate subpar returns.

- Blocking structural change to protect the status quo is **not** long-term job protection.
- If the business does not regain competitiveness, the region loses through weaker investment, weaker product allocation, and eventually weaker employment anyway.
- Co-determination is important, but it cannot become a mechanism for freezing an uncompetitive footprint.

So we can support protection of the industrial base only if it is paired with **credible, hard cost action**.

### To management
You are right that the company needs a structural reset, and we support the principle that the savings must be real, not symbolic.

Where we need more discipline from management is in execution logic:
- Do not lean on “closures must remain on the table” as a negotiating slogan unless you can show the economic case clearly.
- Present the board and investors with **fully quantified scenarios**:
  1. no-closure negotiated package,
  2. hybrid package,
  3. closure-inclusive fallback.
- Show the savings gap, timing, labour risk, and investment implications for each.

That is the only way capital can judge whether the final deal is value-creating or just politically survivable.

### To the controlling family
We are aligned with your insistence on enterprise value and durability. We agree that alternatives should be tested and that management should not get away with vague promises.

But we would caution against allowing closure language to become the default answer too quickly. Closure is a high-disruption instrument with execution risk, strike risk, and reputational risk. If the same savings can be achieved through negotiated capacity reduction and labour-cost reform, that is preferable because it is more value-preserving.

### Our position, clearly
We will support a deal only if it delivers:

- **a credible and quantified reduction in the cost base**
- **durable improvement in margins/return on sales**
- **real capacity reduction**
- **meaningful employee contribution**
- **management and owner discipline**
- **a path to sustained competitiveness**

We will **not** support:
- a cosmetic peace deal,
- preserved overcapacity,
- or a settlement that trades short-term labour peace for long-term margin erosion.

### Bottom line
We are open to a negotiated solution without closures, **but only if it closes the gap in a verifiable way**. If it does not, then yes, harder structural measures remain on the table.

That is not ideological. That is capital allocation.
