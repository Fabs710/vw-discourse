I will respond plainly.

## My position
As the regional state government and anchor shareholder, I support a **durable restructuring** that restores competitiveness. But I do **not** accept a restructuring logic that starts from plant closures or forced layoffs as the default answer.

Our responsibility is not only to balance a spreadsheet. It is to protect:
- jobs,
- industrial capability,
- regional stability,
- and the company’s role as an economic anchor in this state.

That means: **internal measures first, structural destruction only if truly unavoidable and proven.**

---

## Where I agree with management, the family, and investors
I agree on the following:

- The company has a real cost and competitiveness problem.
- A cosmetic deal is not acceptable.
- The status quo is not sustainable.
- Employees should contribute.
- Management and owners must also contribute.
- We need quantified scenarios, not slogans.
- A durable solution must restore margins and fund investment.

The independent assessments also confirm something important:  
**internal measures can close a meaningful part of the gap, and may be enough if the shortfall is moderate.**

That matters. It means the case for closures is **not established by assumption**.

---

## Where I disagree
### 1) I do not accept closures as a bargaining threat from the outset
Management, the family, and investors keep saying closures must remain available. I understand the logic of optionality. But in this company and this region, closures are not a neutral instrument. They are a direct threat to jobs, supplier networks, and the industrial base.

So I reject the idea that the negotiation should begin with the workforce under a closure threat. That is not a fair or stable way to reach a solution.

### 2) I do not accept forced redundancies as a first-line tool
Forced layoffs may be discussed only if every softer and reversible option has genuinely failed. They cannot be the starting point of a responsible restructuring.

### 3) I do not accept a preservation-at-all-costs posture either
At the same time, I agree with the experts that we cannot pretend every existing structure is automatically viable. If the numbers show a large residual gap after internal measures, then the company may need some form of capacity adjustment.

But that is a **later question**, and it must be proven plant by plant.

---

## To the union and works council
I support your core red lines:
- no forced layoffs as the starting instrument,
- no closure-led restructuring,
- and protection of the state’s co-determination role.

That said, I need to be clear: if we want to avoid closures, then the labour side must be ready for a **substantial, quantified contribution**. Not symbolic concessions.

That means real discussion of:
- temporary wage moderation,
- bonus restraint,
- working-time flexibility,
- productivity improvements,
- voluntary exits,
- retirement windows,
- redeployment,
- and utilization changes.

A no-closure path will only be credible if labour’s contribution is real enough to close most of the gap.

---

## To management
You are right to ask for numbers. But you have not yet earned the right to treat closures as a normal instrument.

Before any footprint decision, I expect:
1. a **plant-by-plant model**,
2. a **no-closure scenario**,
3. a **hybrid scenario**,
4. a **fallback structural scenario**,
5. and a clear statement of the **residual gap after internal measures**.

I also expect management and owners to show what they are contributing:
- overhead cuts,
- executive restraint,
- capex discipline,
- and investment commitments.

If you want workers to accept sacrifices, then leadership and ownership must visibly share them.

---

## To the controlling family
I acknowledge your point that a blanket no-closure rule cannot be a governing principle if the numbers truly do not work. Fair enough.

But you are overreaching when you treat closures as something that must remain openly available from the beginning. In this company, that is not just “flexibility.” It is a destabilizing threat.

So I will say this clearly:
- **I support a time-limited protected negotiation phase with no closures and no compulsory redundancies.**
- During that phase, the parties must produce the numbers and test the alternatives.
- Only if the internal measures demonstrably fail should deeper structural options be discussed.

That is the proper sequence.

---

## To investors
You are correct that value is not preserved by pretending overcapacity does not exist. But you also underestimate the value of:
- labour peace,
- industrial capability,
- skills retention,
- and regional legitimacy.

A closure-led approach may look efficient on paper and still destroy long-term value through conflict, delays, and reputational damage.

So I would support a no-closure package **if and only if** it is quantified, durable, and closes the gap. If it does not, then we will revisit the question. But not before the evidence is there.

---

## What I can support
I can support a package with:
- a **time-limited closure freeze during negotiations**,
- **no compulsory redundancies during that phase**,
- binding disclosure of the numbers,
- a serious employee contribution,
- management and owner contributions,
- investment and product commitments,
- and strict supervisory-board monitoring.

I can also support a **structured review point** after the quantified package has been tested.

---

## What I will not support
I will not support:
- closure as the opening move,
- forced layoffs as a default instrument,
- or any deal that guts employment in the state while preserving all the pressure on workers.

And I will be explicit about one further point:  
I will not accept any outcome that sidelines the state’s co-determination and veto role. That role is part of this company’s governance balance, and it is there for a reason.

---

## My conclusion
The correct path is:

1. **Protect the negotiation phase**
2. **Quantify the gap plant by plant**
3. **Test no-closure and hybrid options seriously**
4. **Require real contributions from labour, management, and owners**
5. **Only then decide whether any deeper structural step is truly unavoidable**

If the numbers show that a no-closure package can work, I will support it.

If the numbers do not show that, I still will not accept a closure-led solution as the first political answer. The burden will remain on management and ownership to find a restructuring that protects the company **and** the regional industrial base.
