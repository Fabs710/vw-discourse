I will respond directly.

## To management, the family, and investors
You still want to keep closures and compulsory redundancies as standing bargaining options. We do **not** accept that. Once again: that is not a neutral “option set”; it is leverage against the workforce.

The expert input did **not** change the basic bargaining logic:
- internal measures can close a meaningful part of the gap,
- a no-closure package may be sufficient if the gap is moderate,
- and only if the gap is truly large does footprint adjustment become an issue.

That means the burden is still on you to **prove necessity**, not on workers to accept destructive measures preemptively.

## Where we agree
We do agree on several points:
- the company needs a durable solution,
- a cosmetic deal is useless,
- labour must contribute,
- management and owners must contribute too,
- the numbers must be quantified plant by plant,
- and the solution must be credible over the medium term.

We are prepared to discuss:
- temporary wage moderation,
- bonus restraint,
- working-time flexibility,
- productivity measures,
- voluntary exits,
- retirement windows,
- redeployment,
- shift and utilization changes,
- and a contribution package tied to site and job security.

That is a real contribution. It is not a refusal to act.

## Where we disagree
### 1) A “protected negotiation phase” is only acceptable if it is real
I am open to a **time-limited negotiation window** with no closures and no compulsory redundancies during that period. But it must not be a trick where management simply waits for expiry and then returns with the same threat.

So if there is a protected phase, it must include:
- a clear duration,
- full disclosure of the figures,
- a genuine no-closure scenario,
- a hybrid scenario,
- and a fallback scenario,
- plus binding criteria for what counts as success.

If management wants this phase, fine. But then it must be a **binding moratorium**, not a public-relations pause.

### 2) “Residual gap” cannot become a moving target
Management keeps implying that if savings are not enough, closures become inevitable. But what counts as “enough” has not been fixed clearly enough.

So here is our position:
- define the target margin and savings gap transparently,
- define what management and owners contribute,
- define what labour contributes,
- and define what internal measures are expected to achieve.

Only then can anyone honestly judge whether a further review is necessary.

### 3) Forced redundancies remain unacceptable
This is not changing.  
If management says forced layoffs are necessary, then it has not exhausted fair instruments. We will not trade away collective bargaining standards just because management prefers a quicker solution.

## What we can support
We can support a package built on:
- **temporary wage moderation**
- **bonus restraint**
- **working-time flexibility**
- **productivity measures**
- **voluntary exits**
- **retirement windows**
- **redeployment**
- **overhead restraint at management level**
- **owner contribution**
- **investment and product commitments**
- **binding site security through 2030**
- **no compulsory redundancies**

That is a serious shared-sacrifice package.

## What we cannot support
We cannot support:
- closures as a bargaining threat,
- a “review later” that is already preloaded toward closure,
- or any settlement where workers carry the main pain while management, owners, and capital preserve all their options.

## To the regional state government
We appreciate that you want sequencing and evidence, not just slogans. Fine. Then use your governance role to make the protected phase meaningful.

The state should back:
- a binding closure moratorium during negotiations,
- transparent scenario modelling,
- and a no-forced-layoff framework.

If the state says it stands for the industrial base, then it should not allow workers to be negotiated under a gun.

## To the controlling family
You asked whether we would accept a time-limited closure freeze if there is a binding labour and management contribution package.

**Yes — but only if the freeze is genuine and only if the package is fair.**

That means:
- no closures during the protected phase,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- quantified contributions from labour, management, and owners,
- and a binding commitment that the company will first pursue internal measures and utilization improvements.

What we will **not** accept is a freeze that merely postpones the same closure agenda.

## To management specifically
You ask for a minimum residual gap threshold. Fine — table it.

But do not misuse that as a blank cheque for plant closures. If internal measures and shared contributions can close the gap, then the case for closures falls away. If you later claim otherwise, you will need a much stronger proof than “we want flexibility.”

So here is the challenge back to you:
1. show the quantified no-closure package,
2. show the labour contribution we have signaled,
3. show management/owner contribution,
4. and show the margin effect by plant and by year.

If that package works, then closures and forced layoffs are off the table. If you want more, prove why.

## To investors
If a no-closure deal delivers target margin through verified savings, then yes, it should be supported even if it preserves more capacity than you prefer.

But you must also accept that value is not only short-term margin. Value includes:
- labour peace,
- skill retention,
- execution reliability,
- and the ability to invest in future products without an industrial war.
