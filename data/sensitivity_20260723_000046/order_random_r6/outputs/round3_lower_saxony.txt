We appreciate the independent assessment, because it confirms the central point: **the numbers matter**. But it does **not** justify turning plant closures and compulsory redundancies into the default solution.

### To management
I agree with you on one thing: this company needs a **durable restructuring** and a real cost reset. A cosmetic agreement would be irresponsible.  
Where I disagree is your insistence that closures and forced layoffs must stay openly available as bargaining leverage. That is exactly what makes the process politically and socially toxic in a company like this.

The expert has said a no-closure package may be feasible if the gap is moderate, and that internal measures can close a meaningful part of it. That is enough to require management to **prove necessity before touching sites or jobs**. Until that proof is there, I will not treat closures as a normal instrument.

I want the quantified scenarios, yes:
1. no-closure package,
2. hybrid package,
3. fallback structural package.

But I also want those scenarios to be tested against a **real protection framework**:
- no compulsory redundancies as the starting point,
- no closure-led restructuring as the first resort,
- binding employment and site security during the negotiation phase,
- and real management/owner contributions.

### To the controlling family
You are right that the expert does not rule out footprint rationalization in all cases. I accept that.  
But you are overreaching when you convert that into a broad license for closures. The expert’s message was conditional: **if the gap is large enough**, structural adjustment may be necessary.

That is not the same as saying closures should be kept on the table from the outset as pressure on workers. I will not support a process where the company signals to employees that their sites are expendable before exhausting alternatives.

So: yes to quantified review, no to using closure as the opening move.

### To the union and works council
I support your core red lines:
- no forced layoffs,
- no closure-led restructuring,
- binding guarantees matter.

That is consistent with the state’s responsibility as an anchor shareholder and with the regional interest.  
Where I would press you is this: you cannot rely only on principles. The expert has made clear that internal measures have to be **material**, not symbolic. If we are asking management and owners to take a no-closure path seriously, then the labour side must be prepared to discuss a **substantial, quantified contribution**.

That means real discussion of:
- working-time flexibility,
- wage moderation,
- bonus restraint,
- productivity gains,
- voluntary exits,
- redeployment,
- utilization changes.

Not because I want to weaken labour protections, but because that is the only way to make a no-closure package credible enough to stand up against the management case.

### To the institutional investors
You are right that the company cannot preserve every inefficiency forever. But capital discipline does not give you the right to discount regional stability, co-determination, and industrial capability as “soft” factors. In this company, they are part of value.

I would support a negotiated solution without closures **if** it is:
- quantified,
- durable,
- and genuinely closes the margin gap.

But I will not accept an investor logic that says, “if margins are not instantly restored, close sites.” That is too crude for a company with this governance structure and this regional role.

### What I can support
I can support a package that includes:
- measurable employee contribution,
- management overhead and executive restraint,
- owner discipline,
- investment commitments,
- product commitments,
- internal capacity adjustment,
- and strong monitoring through supervisory-board oversight.

I can also support a **time-limited negotiation phase** during which closures and compulsory redundancies are not pursued, while the scenarios are tested seriously.

### What I will not support
I will not support:
- closure as a bargaining threat from the start,
- forced layoffs,
- a restructuring that guts employment in the state,
- or any deal that weakens the state’s co-determination and veto role.

That last point is not negotiable. The state’s role is part of the governance balance of this company, and it exists precisely because this company is more than a short-term capital asset.

### My position
The right sequence is:
1. quantify the gap,
2. test no-closure and hybrid options,
3. require contributions from all sides,
4. only then consider whether any deeper structural step is genuinely unavoidable.

If the numbers show that a no-closure package can work, then that is the path I will support.  
If the numbers do not support that, then we will still not accept a closure-led solution as the first political answer. The burden will remain on management and owners to find a restructuring that protects the company **and** the regional industrial base.
