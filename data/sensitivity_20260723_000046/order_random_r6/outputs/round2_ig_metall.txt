We are prepared to talk about **real numbers and real sacrifices**. But let me be equally clear: if the discussion starts from **closures and compulsory redundancies as the baseline**, then we are not negotiating a shared solution — we are negotiating under threat. That is not acceptable.

### First, where we agree
- The company needs a **durable** solution, not a cosmetic one.
- A **negotiated employee contribution** is on the table.
- Management, owners, and labour must **all contribute**.
- We need **investment and product commitments** if we are to preserve a German industrial base.

### Where we disagree sharply
#### 1) Plant closures are not an “instrument” we can casually keep on the table
A plant closure is irreversible destruction of jobs, skills, supplier networks, and trust. Once a site is shut, it is gone. So no, I do not accept the argument that closures are simply a neutral option to improve bargaining leverage.

If the company wants a negotiated settlement, it must work within a framework that **protects sites** and uses other instruments first:
- working-time adjustments,
- temporary wage moderation,
- bonus restraint,
- capacity reduction through internal means,
- voluntary exits and retirement,
- redeployment,
- productivity improvements,
- and management/owner contributions.

#### 2) Compulsory redundancies are a red line
We are not accepting forced layoffs as the price of management’s strategic failure. If management cannot close the gap without compulsory redundancies, then management has not yet presented a credible industrial plan.

### On the “numbers and options” request
If you want a serious settlement, then here is what the union side requires as the starting framework:

1. **No compulsory redundancies**
2. **No plant closures**
3. **Binding site guarantees**
4. **Binding investment and product commitments**
5. **A fair burden-sharing package across all sides**
6. **Protection of the collective bargaining framework**

Within that framework, we are willing to discuss a **measurable employee contribution**, for example:
- temporary wage restraint,
- bonus waiver or reduction,
- working-time flexibility,
- capacity reduction through shifts and utilisation,
- and a contribution fund linked to job security.

But that only makes sense if management and owners also make real concessions:
- executive and overhead cost reductions,
- capital and dividend restraint where appropriate,
- binding commitments on production allocation,
- and clear guarantees against plant closure and forced layoffs.

### To management
You say the answer must be structural. Fine — but structural change does **not** automatically mean closure-led restructuring. Show us the exact gap, and show us that you have exhausted all alternatives before asking workers to pay.

If you want our agreement, bring forward a package that demonstrates:
- the required cost reduction,
- the capacity adjustment needed,
- the management contribution,
- the owner contribution,
- and the employment safeguards.

Without that, your “open on the instrument, not on the outcome” position is simply code for shifting the burden onto labour.

### To the controlling shareholder and investors
You both keep saying the company needs competitiveness. We do not dispute that. What we dispute is the idea that competitiveness must come through breaking the workforce’s security first.

If you want durable value, then accept that:
- trust matters,
- industrial capability matters,
- and a workforce that feels betrayed will not deliver stability.

### To the state government
We appreciate your clear stance against closure-led restructuring. But that stance has to translate into a concrete settlement, not just a veto posture. If you want a durable outcome, then support a framework that protects sites, jobs, and investment while still allowing the company to make the operational adjustments it needs.

### Our bottom line
We are ready to negotiate **a serious shared-sacrifice package**.

We are **not** ready to accept:
- compulsory redundancies,
- plant closures,
- or a restructuring where employees carry the pain while management and owners remain insulated.

So the next step is straightforward:
- put the savings gap on the table,
- quantify the alternatives,
- and show what management and owners are prepared to contribute.

If those numbers can be met **without closures and without forced layoffs**, then we can talk. If not, then you should expect the workforce to resist with the full means available to us.
