We appreciate the independent assessments because they finally move this out of slogans and into economics. From our perspective, that is the only basis for value creation.

## Where we agree
We agree with management, the controlling family, and the experts on the central point: **the current cost base is not competitive, and a cosmetic settlement would be value-destructive**.

We also agree that:
- labor should contribute,
- management and owners should contribute,
- voluntary exits, working-time flexibility, bonus restraint, and productivity measures are all valid tools,
- and the final package must be durable, not just a temporary truce.

That said, we are not prepared to accept a “peace” that leaves the business structurally impaired.

## Where we disagree
### 1) The union and works council still overstate the case for absolute protection
We understand the political and social resistance to closures and forced layoffs. But **“no closures” and “no compulsory redundancies” as fixed red lines are not credible if the footprint is genuinely oversized**.

The experts were clear: internal measures can close a meaningful part of the gap, but **not necessarily all of it**. If the residual gap remains material, then the company must retain the ability to resize its footprint.

A no-closure package is acceptable **only if it is proven to deliver equivalent, durable savings**. Otherwise it is just delay with nicer language.

### 2) The state government cannot be the guarantor of an uncompetitive structure
We respect co-determination and the state’s role, but blocking every meaningful footprint adjustment is not job protection; it is deferred deterioration.

If the company cannot restore margins, it will invest less, allocate less product, and ultimately support fewer jobs. That is the outcome we want to avoid.

### 3) Management is right on the need for options, but must now quantify them cleanly
We support management’s insistence that closures and compulsory redundancies cannot be ruled out in advance. But we also expect discipline:
- no vague “structural necessity” rhetoric,
- no bluffing,
- no using closure language as a negotiating club without evidence.

The next round should include a **plant-by-plant model** showing:
1. no-closure package,
2. hybrid package,
3. closure-inclusive fallback,
with savings, timing, labor risk, and investment implications for each.

## Our position on the expert input
The expert input is helpful, but it does **not** justify the union’s red lines.

What it says, in plain terms, is:
- labor concessions can do a lot,
- voluntary exits and internal adjustment are preferable,
- but if the overcapacity problem is large, some footprint rationalization is usually necessary.

That is exactly our view.

We would support a negotiated no-closure package **if and only if** it closes the gap in a verifiable way and restores a credible return to target margins. If it does not, then the company must be free to take deeper structural action.

## What we will support
We will support a deal that contains:
- a real employee contribution,
- management and overhead discipline,
- owner restraint,
- investment and product commitments,
- and a credible path back to competitive margins.

We will also support a time-limited negotiation phase where the parties test whether internal measures are enough.

## What we will not support
We will not support:
- a cosmetic peace deal,
- a settlement that preserves too much overcapacity,
- or a package that trades short-term labor peace for long-term margin erosion.

## Bottom line
We are open to a no-closure outcome **only if the numbers prove it works**. If the company’s residual cost gap remains significant after internal measures, then closure and compulsory restructuring cannot be taken off the table.

That is not ideology. That is capital discipline.
