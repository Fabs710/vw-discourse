Let me be equally clear in this round:

**The works council does not accept plant closures or compulsory redundancies as the answer.**  
Not as a “fallback.” Not as a “last resort” that is already being normalized. Not as a bargaining threat to force labour into submission.

The expert assessments have added useful detail, but they have **not** changed the core conclusion: there are still substantial internal instruments available, and management has not proved that destructive measures are unavoidable.

## Where I agree
I agree with one thing from almost everyone here: the company needs a **durable, quantified solution**. A cosmetic deal would help no one. And yes, workers can contribute — but only in a framework that **protects jobs, sites, and co-determination**.

I also agree with the experts that:
- wage moderation,
- bonus restraint,
- working-time flexibility,
- productivity gains,
- voluntary exits,
- redeployment,
- and management overhead cuts

can produce real savings.

That is exactly why I reject the argument that closures and forced layoffs must remain on the table from the outset.

## Where I disagree — and why

### 1) Management is still treating “closure” as if it were a neutral planning instrument
It is not neutral. It is a threat to livelihoods, skills, supplier networks, and the long-term industrial base.

You keep saying “we are not using it lightly.” That does not make it acceptable. If you put closures on the table before proving that all non-destructive alternatives are insufficient, then you are not negotiating a restructuring — you are negotiating under pressure.

The expert did **not** say closures are the correct starting point. The expert said internal measures may be enough if the gap is moderate. That is a direct reason to **start there and stay there unless proven otherwise**.

### 2) The claim that “no closures” is unrealistic is not a business case
It is a preference.  
Management, the family, and investors keep repeating that the footprint may be too large. Fine — **show it plant by plant**. Show the gap plant by plant. Show the utilisation. Show the fixed-cost burden. Show exactly which savings cannot be achieved internally.

Until that is done, “closures must remain available” is not analysis. It is leverage.

### 3) Compulsory redundancies remain unacceptable
This point is not moving.

If management cannot restructure without forced layoffs, then management has not yet brought a credible industrial plan to the table. We are prepared to discuss:
- voluntary exit programs,
- retirement windows,
- redeployment,
- working-time changes,
- temporary wage moderation,
- bonus restraint,
- and productivity measures.

But **not forced redundancies**.

### 4) The state government is right to reject closure-led restructuring, but too cautious in language
“No closures as a first resort” is fine as far as it goes, but the works council will say plainly: **first resort is not enough**.

Workers cannot be asked to make sacrifices while management keeps the option to close sites open in the background. If the state truly stands with the German industrial base, then it should support a **binding closure freeze** during the negotiation phase and use its governance power to make that real.

### 5) Investors keep reducing value to short-term margin logic
You say you will support a no-closure deal if the numbers prove it works. Good. Then support the deal **when it is proven**, even if it preserves more capacity than you like.

What you cannot do is treat jobs, skills, regional stability, and co-determination as optional extras. In a company like this, they are part of the value proposition. A “successful” restructuring that destroys trust and detonates industrial relations is not value creation.

## What the experts actually mean for this discussion
The independent experts said:
- internal measures can close a meaningful part of the gap,
- a no-closure package may be sufficient if the shortfall is moderate,
- and only if the gap is large does footprint reduction become economically necessary.

That is not a mandate for closures.  
That is a mandate to **prove the need for them** — and that proof has not been delivered.

And let me say something else very plainly: even if the gap were large, that would **still not justify forced layoffs as the first instrument**. There are still softer, fairer, more responsible instruments:
- utilization changes,
- internal reallocation,
- phased workforce reduction through attrition,
- voluntary programs,
- and negotiated productivity improvements.

## What we want, concretely
If management wants any serious discussion, the package must be built on these non-negotiables:

- **No plant closures in Germany**
- **No compulsory redundancies**
- **Binding site guarantees through 2030**
- **Binding investment and product commitments**
- **Shared sacrifice from management, owners, and labour**
- **A genuine co-determination role in shaping the restructuring**

That is the starting point. Not the end point.

## To management
If you want the workforce to contribute, then stop talking like the destruction of plants is merely one option among many. Bring a plan that proves:
1. what savings are needed,
2. what internal measures can achieve,
3. what management and owners will contribute,
4. and how every German site can remain open while the gap is closed.

If you cannot do that, then you are asking labour to pay for a strategic failure it did not create.

## To the controlling family
You say you are focused on value preservation. Then preserve the company’s real value:
- skilled labour,
- industrial know-how,
- regional legitimacy,
- social peace,
- and co-determination.

A closure-led strategy may look “disciplined” on a slide deck. In reality it risks destroying the very foundation that makes this enterprise strong. If you force that path, you will not get partnership from the workforce.

## To the state government
Do not stop at rhetoric. If you believe in protecting the industrial base, then use your governance power to support a binding no-closure framework and a no-forced-layoff solution. The workforce needs more than sympathy; it needs commitment.

## To the investors
If you want a durable return, understand this: stability is not a luxury. A workforce that feels betrayed does not create sustainable value. A company that tears up its job-security framework may win a temporary cost gain but lose long-term capability and trust.

## Bottom line
We are **ready to negotiate a serious, quantified, shared-sacrifice package**.

We are **not** prepared to accept:
- plant closures,
- compulsory redundancies,
- or a restructuring that turns the workforce into the buffer for management’s misjudgments.

If the company wants a real solution, then it should table a plan that keeps every German plant open, protects jobs, preserves co-determination, and closes the cost gap through fair internal measures and contributions from management and owners.

If it does not, then the works council will continue to resist — forcefully and with all rights available to us.
