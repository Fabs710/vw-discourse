## My position
I support a **serious, quantified restructuring** that restores competitiveness and margins.  
I do **not** support a settlement that pre-commits the company to preserving every site regardless of economics, nor one that leaves management without credible tools if the residual gap proves material.

At the same time, I am **not** interested in a reckless closure program or an industrial war that destroys value. Long-term ownership means I care about durable savings, labour peace, and execution risk. But that is not the same as accepting a blanket no-closure veto.

---

## Where I agree

### With the experts
The expert assessments were helpful because they clarified the real issue:
- internal measures can close **part** of the gap,
- a no-closure package may work if the shortfall is **moderate**,
- but if the overcapacity and fixed-cost burden are large, then some form of footprint rationalization is likely necessary.

That is the correct framework. It supports my view that we need **numbers, not slogans**.

### With management
Management is right that:
- the company has a structural cost problem,
- labour must contribute,
- and the board cannot rule out every structural option in advance.

I also agree that a purely symbolic compromise would be value-destructive.

### With labour, to a point
I agree with the union and works council that:
- a cosmetic deal is useless,
- workers can and should contribute,
- and trust matters.

I also agree that **voluntary exits, working-time flexibility, bonus restraint, productivity gains, redeployment, and management restraint** should all be part of the package before we even talk about harsher measures.

---

## Where I disagree

### 1) A blanket no-closure, no-forced-layoff stance is not credible as a governing principle
The union and works council keep presenting this as a starting rule. It is not. It may be a bargaining position, but it cannot be the company’s governing constraint if the numbers do not support it.

If a site can only be preserved by permanently weakening the company’s competitive position, then that is not stewardship — it is slow value destruction.

### 2) “Prove necessity first” is correct in principle, but only if it is tied to a real model
I do accept the need for proof. But proof means:
- plant-by-plant economics,
- fixed versus variable cost,
- utilisation,
- volume assumptions,
- and the residual gap after internal measures.

It does **not** mean that closures disappear from consideration simply because some stakeholders dislike them.

### 3) Compulsory redundancies should not be the first tool, but they cannot be pre-banned
I understand why labour rejects forced layoffs. I would also prefer to avoid them. But I will not accept a framework that removes them absolutely before the company has shown whether enough savings can be achieved through softer instruments.

That would give one side a veto over restructuring while shifting all adjustment risk to shareholders.

### 4) The state’s role matters, but it cannot freeze economics
I respect the state government’s concern for regional stability. But regional stability depends on a viable company. If the structure remains uncompetitive, the eventual damage to jobs and investment is worse, not better.

So I support sequencing and evidence, but not a political blockade against necessary restructuring.

---

## On the proposed protected negotiation phase
I am open to a **time-limited negotiation phase** with no closures and no compulsory redundancies **during the phase**, provided three things are true:

1. **Full quantified disclosure** is delivered immediately  
   No vague claims. I want:
   - a no-closure scenario,
   - a hybrid scenario,
   - a fallback structural scenario,
   with savings, timing, and risk.

2. **Clear milestones and triggers** are agreed  
   If internal measures and employee contributions close the gap, good.  
   If they do not, then structural options must remain available.

3. **The phase is not used to pre-lock the outcome**  
   A freeze is acceptable as a negotiation tool. It is not acceptable as a disguised permanent ban.

So: yes to a protected phase, **no** to using it as a route to eliminate restructuring options altogether.

We need someone to design a process that is credible for all sides, because the current trust gap is too large to manage on rhetoric alone.

## What I expect from management
Management must table, in hard numbers:
- the current cost gap,
- the target margin gap,
- what internal measures achieve,
- what the residual gap is,
- and what the fallback options would do.

That is the only way this discussion becomes real.

I also expect management to specify:
- what management overhead cuts it will make,
- what executive restraint it will accept,
- what capex discipline it will enforce,
- and what investment commitments it can credibly make.

Owners and management must contribute. That is not optional.

---

## What I expect from labour
If labour wants a no-closure outcome to be seriously considered, then it must be prepared to put real numbers on the table:
- wage moderation,
- bonus restraint,
- working-time flexibility,
- productivity commitments,
- voluntary exits,
- retirement windows,
- redeployment,
- and utilisation improvements.

Not aspirations. Quantified commitments.

If those commitments can close the gap, then I will support a no-closure solution. If they cannot, then I will not support preserving all sites at any cost.

---

## What I expect from the state government
I respect the state’s concern for jobs and industrial capacity. But the state should not confuse that with a blanket preservation doctrine.

If the state wants to be constructive, it should support:
- a time-limited protected phase,
- transparent scenario modelling,
- and a disciplined test of whether non-destructive measures are enough.

What it should **not** do is turn co-determination into a permanent veto over any footprint adjustment.

---

## What I expect from investors
I agree with investors that capital discipline matters. But value is not just headline margin. It also includes:
- labour peace,
- execution reliability,
- skills retention,
- and the ability to invest in future products without prolonged conflict.

So yes, investors should support a no-closure deal **if it truly closes the gap**. But they should not pretend that a closure-led plan is automatically superior if it triggers industrial disruption and weakens long-term capability.

---

## My bottom line
I support:
- a quantified restructuring,
- a time-limited protected negotiation phase,
- serious employee contribution,
- management and owner contribution,
- and a credible test of whether internal measures can close the gap.

I do **not** support:
- a permanent no-closure rule,
- a permanent no-forced-layoff rule,
- or any framework that makes necessary structural adjustment impossible.

If the no-closure package really works, I will back it.  
If it does not, then the company must retain the freedom to act.

That is the stewardship position.
