We are now at the point where broad principles are not enough. If we are serious about value preservation, we need to talk about what actually closes the cost gap without destroying the company in the process.

I agree with several things the union has said:
- the solution must be durable,
- a cosmetic deal would be value-destructive,
- labour should not carry the entire burden,
- and a prolonged confrontation would damage everyone.

Where I do **not** agree is your attempt to make **“no closures” and “no compulsory redundancies” absolute preconditions**. That is not a credible position if the company’s German footprint is structurally too large. As controlling owners, we cannot endorse a framework that rules out the tools needed to restore competitiveness before we even see whether the alternatives are sufficient.

### On plant closures
A plant closure is a serious step, not a bargaining chip. I do not treat it lightly. But I also will not accept the claim that closures are by definition illegitimate or that they can never be part of a solution. If a site is persistently uneconomic and alternatives cannot deliver equivalent savings, then keeping it open simply postpones the problem and weakens the whole enterprise.

That said, I am **not** asking for closure as the first move. I am saying:
- closures must remain available if the numbers require them,
- but management should first exhaust credible negotiated alternatives,
- and any closure proposal must be backed by a clear business case, not symbolic pressure.

### On compulsory redundancies
I am open to avoiding forced layoffs if — and only if — the savings and capacity reduction are genuinely achieved through other means. Voluntary exits, retirement windows, redeployment, working-time adjustments, and internal flexibility are all preferable. But I will not accept a settlement where “no compulsory redundancies” becomes a veto on any real restructuring.

### On the state government’s position
I understand the concern for regional employment and the industrial base. That concern is legitimate. But a policy of effectively blocking all meaningful structural change is not protecting jobs; it is risking a slower deterioration that will eventually hit harder. A company that cannot restore margins will not invest, and without investment there is no long-term employment security.

### On the numbers and options
The union is right about one thing: we should move from slogans to quantified options.

I would expect management to table, for supervisory-board review, at least three scenarios:
1. **A negotiated package without closures**, showing exactly how much cost and capacity reduction can be achieved through labour flexibility, productivity, redeployment, and compensation restraint.
2. **A hybrid package**, where targeted site adjustments are combined with labour contributions and investment commitments.
3. **A structural package with closures**, only if the first two do not close the gap.

Each scenario must show:
- the cost savings,
- the impact on capacity,
- the investment required,
- the timeline,
- and the labour peace risk.

### What would be acceptable from my side
I can support a package if it contains all of the following:
- a **credible and quantified** reduction in the cost base,
- a **durable** improvement in margins,
- a meaningful **employee contribution**,
- management and overhead discipline,
- and a settlement that avoids unnecessary disruption where possible.

I can also support strong investment commitments, but only if they are tied to a serious restructuring. Investment promises are not a substitute for fixing the cost base.

### What I will not support
I will not support:
- preserving every site at any cost,
- a deal that leaves the cost structure uncompetitive,
- a settlement that relies on wishful thinking instead of hard savings,
- or an outcome that protects the appearance of peace while eroding enterprise value underneath it.

### To the works council
Your demand for binding guarantees until 2030 may be understandable, but it is not persuasive unless paired with a restructuring that actually restores competitiveness. Guarantees without economic discipline are not guarantees; they are postponements.

### To management
Do not mistake my openness to alternatives for support for delay. If alternatives are real, quantified, and durable, use them. If not, do not pretend the problem has been solved by avoiding hard decisions.

### My bottom line
I support a **serious restructuring** that restores competitiveness and margins. I am open to negotiated alternatives to closures and forced layoffs, but only if they deliver **equivalent, verified savings**. If they do not, then deeper structural measures remain necessary.

That is the position from the controlling shareholder side: not ideological closure, not sentimental preservation, but a disciplined outcome that protects the company’s long-term value.
