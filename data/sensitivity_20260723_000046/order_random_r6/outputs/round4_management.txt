I’ll be equally direct.

We are not in a debate about whether the company needs restructuring. Everyone here agrees it does. The question is whether we actually solve the cost problem or simply postpone it behind language about “protection,” “review,” and “sequencing.”

My answer remains: **this company needs a durable structural reset, not a comfort arrangement that preserves an uncompetitive cost base.**

## To the union and works council
I hear your insistence on no closures and no forced redundancies. I understand why you prefer that position. But I do **not** accept it as a governing premise.

You keep saying the expert assessments prove internal measures may be enough. That is **not** what they said. They said internal measures **can close a meaningful part of the gap**, and may be enough **if** the shortfall is moderate. That conditional language matters.

If the gap is moderate, then a no-closure package may work. Fine. Then show me the numbers.

If the gap is large, then a no-closure promise becomes a promise to keep an oversized, expensive structure in place. That is not restructuring. That is denial.

You also say closures are a bargaining threat. I say closures are a legitimate tool if the economics require them. I am not interested in using them lightly, but I will not remove them from consideration before the numbers are proven. That would be irresponsible to shareholders, employees, and the company’s future.

## To the regional state government
I respect your concern for regional stability. But stability without competitiveness is temporary.

I agree with you on one point: we need a **time-limited protected negotiation phase** with clear data. That is sensible. What I do **not** accept is turning that into a de facto permanent closure freeze.

A protected phase is only useful if it is a path to a real outcome. If it becomes a vehicle to block necessary footprint adjustment, then it is not protection. It is delay.

So yes:
- quantified scenarios,
- plant-by-plant disclosure,
- defined milestones,
- and a review point.

But no:
- blanket exclusion of closures,
- blanket exclusion of compulsory redundancies,
- or a governance doctrine that prevents the company from resizing if the economics demand it.

## To the controlling family
You are right about one thing: we need numbers, not slogans. On that we agree.

But I need to push back on your tendency to frame this as a choice between value preservation and industrial conflict, as if the only rational answer is to avoid disruption at any cost. That is too soft and too risky.

If the residual gap remains material after internal measures, then the board must retain the right to act structurally. I am open to avoiding forced layoffs if there are credible alternatives. I am open to internal measures first. I am even open to a protected negotiation window.

I am **not** open to a framework that permanently disarms management before the cost gap is closed.

## To investors
You are right to reject a cosmetic settlement. That is exactly my position.

Where I differ slightly is this: I do not think the binary is “closure-led restructuring or failure.” A credible no-closure package can work **if it is materially sufficient**. But it must be materially sufficient.

So support it if:
- it closes the gap,
- it is durable,
- and it improves return on sales to a level that funds investment through 2030.

Do **not** support it if:
- it preserves excess capacity,
- it only delays hard decisions,
- or it relies on optimistic productivity assumptions without hard delivery mechanisms.

## On the experts
The experts have done their job: they have shown the range of possibilities, not handed anyone a veto.

Their central message is simple:
- internal measures can deliver real savings,
- a no-closure solution may work if the gap is moderate,
- but if overcapacity and fixed costs are large, footprint rationalization is usually required.

That is exactly why management insists closures and redundancies cannot be ruled out in advance.

## What I am prepared to do
I will support:
- a **time-limited protected negotiation phase**,
- full plant-by-plant disclosure,
- a quantified no-closure scenario,
- a quantified hybrid scenario,
- a quantified fallback structural scenario,
- a serious employee contribution,
- management and owner contributions,
- executive and overhead restraint,
- and investment commitments tied to the restructuring.

I will also support avoiding compulsory redundancies **if** the package delivers the required structural savings by other means.

## What I will not do
I will not:
- accept a permanent no-closure rule,
- accept a permanent no-forced-layoff rule,
- accept site guarantees that are disconnected from economics,
- or accept a deal that leaves us with the same uncompetitive cost base and calls it a solution.

## The real test
If the union, works council, and state government believe a no-closure path is enough, then they should not fear the numbers.

Let’s see:
1. what savings your package truly delivers,
2. what the residual gap remains,
3. whether it restores the brand’s return-on-sales target,
4. and whether it frees enough capital to invest in future products and technology to 2030.

If it does, then it is viable.

If it does not, then the company must retain the ability to take deeper structural action. That is not a threat. It is the minimum condition for restoring competitiveness.
