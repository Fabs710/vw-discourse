The independent assessment is useful, and it actually sharpens the issue rather than softens it.

### Where I agree
I agree with the expert on the central point: **wage moderation, working-time changes, bonus restraint, productivity measures, and voluntary exits can close a meaningful part of the gap, but not necessarily all of it**. That is exactly why I have been saying we need quantified scenarios rather than ideological red lines.

I also agree that:
- labour-only adjustment is unlikely to be enough if the overcapacity problem is real,
- a negotiated package is preferable to unmanaged conflict,
- and a durable solution must protect long-term competitiveness, not just short-term peace.

### Where I disagree
What I do **not** accept is the union and works council’s attempt to turn the expert’s conclusion into a veto on structural change.

The expert did **not** say:
- closures are never needed,
- or that no-closure packages will definitely solve the problem,
- or that the company should freeze its footprint indefinitely.

The expert said something more demanding: **the numbers must be proven**. If the gap is modest, internal measures may suffice. If the gap is large, footprint rationalization becomes economically necessary.

That is the correct framework.

### To the union
Your position remains too absolute.

You say:
- no closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- binding guarantees first,
- and only then maybe sacrifices.

That is not a viable restructuring logic if the company’s cost base is structurally too high. The expert just confirmed that. You cannot insist that the company remain fully intact and still demand that it somehow becomes globally competitive through internal adjustments alone.

I do **not** dismiss your concern about trust, skills, and industrial capability. Those are real value factors. But they do not override economics. If the savings gap is large enough, then refusing all footprint adjustment is just another way of preserving decline.

### To the works council
I understand your insistence on protecting sites and co-determination. But you are still presenting “no closures” as if it were a governance principle rather than a bargaining position. It is not.

What I would support is this:
- a **time-limited no-closure negotiation phase**,
- a fully quantified comparison of scenarios,
- and a serious attempt to solve the gap through internal means first.

What I will **not** support is any framework that converts a temporary negotiation stance into a permanent prohibition on structural action.

### To the state government
Your concern for regional stability is legitimate, and I do not take it lightly. But the expert has confirmed the obvious tension: if the footprint is too large, then protecting all sites forever can become a form of managed decline.

So I say this plainly: **I cannot accept a state-led position that effectively neutralises the company’s ability to resize itself if the economics require it**. That would protect headlines, not value. And if value erodes, the eventual employment and regional impact is worse, not better.

### To management
You now have what I wanted: an external confirmation that the answer depends on the size of the gap. Good. Now use it properly.

I expect management to present:
1. a quantified no-closure package,
2. a quantified hybrid package,
3. a quantified fallback structural package,
with clear assumptions on:
- labor cost,
- capacity,
- utilization,
- capex,
- savings,
- execution timing,
- and strike risk.

Do not hide behind generic “structural necessity” language. Show the board what is actually required and what each option buys or costs.

### To investors
The expert’s assessment supports a disciplined capital approach:
- if a negotiated package can close the gap, support it;
- if not, do not pretend capacity can be preserved at no cost.

But I would add one caution: closure itself is not a free lunch. It carries execution, industrial relations, and reputational risk. So the question is not “closures or no closures” in the abstract. The question is what combination of measures produces the best long-term value after all risks are priced in.

### My position, clearly
I support:
- a serious restructuring,
- a quantified comparison of options,
- a meaningful employee contribution,
- management and owner discipline,
- and a durable restoration of margins.

I do **not** support:
- precommitting to no structural adjustment regardless of the numbers,
- compulsory redundancies as the first instrument,
- or value-destructive conflict.

### What would move me
Only one thing: **a credible, quantified no-closure or hybrid package that demonstrably restores competitiveness and margins without undermining the business.**

If that can be shown, I will support it.

If it cannot be shown, then I will not accept that the company is obliged to carry uncompetitive capacity forever just because some stakeholders prefer that outcome.

That is the boundary.
