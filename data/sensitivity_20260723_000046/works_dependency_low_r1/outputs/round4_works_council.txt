Management, shareholders, and even the state keep repeating the same formula: “durable restructuring,” “structural savings,” “capacity adjustment.” I hear the words. I still do **not** accept that they justify plant closures or compulsory redundancies.

## Where I agree
I agree with one important point from almost everyone: this company needs a **real** solution, not theater.

I also agree that we need numbers, not slogans. That has been my position from the start. If management says the gap is structural, then it must show:
- the exact savings gap,
- what comes from labour,
- what comes from overhead,
- what comes from procurement and logistics,
- what comes from capacity utilization,
- and what can be achieved without closures.

That is not a concession from management. That is the minimum standard of honesty.

## Where I disagree — and strongly
What I reject is the leap from “there is a gap” to “therefore we need closures and layoffs.”

That leap has **not** been proven.

What has been proven is something else:
- management wants irreversible tools because they are easier to impose,
- capital wants durable savings because it wants a cleaner cost base,
- and workers are being asked to absorb the risk of management’s strategic failures.

That is not a neutral reading of the situation. That is a transfer of burden.

## To management
You say non-closure measures are insufficient unless employees give up more. That is not an argument; that is a bargaining tactic.

You have not shown that all serious alternatives have failed. You have not shown why the following cannot be combined into a durable package:
- working-time reductions,
- temporary wage restraint,
- bonus sacrifice,
- reduced outsourcing,
- overtime reduction,
- internal redeployment,
- qualification,
- management-side cost cuts,
- and investment reallocation.

If you have the numbers, put them on the table. If you do not, then stop presenting closures as necessity.

And let me be very clear: the broken job-security pledge is exactly why you do **not** get trust automatically. You ask for flexibility after breaking a guarantee. That is not a strong position. That is a credibility problem.

## To the family holding and investors
You keep saying that only irreversible cuts are durable. That is simply false.

Durability is not the same as destruction. A settlement is durable if it is:
- negotiated,
- enforceable,
- socially legitimate,
- and capable of being implemented without permanent conflict.

A closure-driven package may look durable in a spreadsheet. In the real company, it can trigger resistance, lower productivity, talent loss, and years of mistrust. That is not value creation.

If you want value protection, then support a settlement that keeps every German plant open and avoids compulsory redundancies. If you refuse that on principle, then you are not looking for a balanced solution. You are looking for one-sided adjustment.

## To the state government
I appreciate that you reject a fait accompli. But I need to be equally blunt: you cannot say “no closures as a first resort” and then drift into a framework where closures are merely delayed.

I want an explicit public commitment:
- no plant closures in Germany,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- no weakening of co-determination,
- no hidden trapdoor through “milestones.”

If the state wants to be a real anchor shareholder, it must defend the industrial base, not merely the balance sheet.

If the economics are weak, then the answer is to improve the economics **without destroying sites** — not to threaten communities until they submit.

## To the union
We are aligned on the red lines, and I value that. But I also want to be practical: if the company needs a labour contribution, then that contribution must be tightly conditioned.

I can discuss:
- temporary wage restraint,
- bonus sacrifice,
- working-time flexibility,
- redeployment,
- voluntary hours reductions,
- qualification,
- and a transformation fund,

but only if these are tied to:
- binding site guarantees through 2030,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- no plant closures,
- and no weakening of co-determination.

No one should pretend that workers are refusing responsibility. We are refusing **self-destruction**.

## On the compromise formulas
### 1) Phased restructuring with a review gate
This is only acceptable if the first phase contains ironclad protections:
- no closures,
- no forced layoffs,
- no pre-commitment to future shutdowns.

A review gate cannot be a trapdoor with a nicer label.

### 2) No forced redundancies, voluntary capacity reduction
This is the only direction that is even discussable, because it preserves the principle that jobs are not destroyed by fiat. But it must remain genuinely voluntary and not become pressure by attrition.

### 3) Site guarantees tied to productivity and investment milestones
I can support productivity and investment commitments. I cannot support milestones that become a coded mechanism for later closures. If the company wants performance, then let it invest in performance. Do not disguise a shutdown plan as a target system.

## What would actually move us
Very little, and only under strict conditions.

What can move the works council is not a threat of closure. It is:
1. **binding site guarantees through 2030**
2. **no compulsory redundancies**
3. **a genuine co-determination role in implementation**
4. **real management and owner contributions**
5. **investment commitments for German sites**
6. **a measurable savings package achieved without plant closures**

That is the only basis for any meaningful discussion.

## What will not move us
These will not move us:
- a vague promise to “revisit” closures later,
- a review gate that functions as a trapdoor,
- “voluntary” exits that are really pressure by attrition,
- or a settlement that protects returns by sacrificing communities.

## To everyone still pushing closures
I say this plainly: a closure threat may look powerful in a boardroom. It is not the same as being right.

If management and capital cannot prove that non-closure alternatives fail, then their insistence on closures is not prudence. It is escalation.

And if they can prove it, then I still will not accept compulsory redundancies or a blanket abandonment of the job-security framework. Those are not bargaining chips for us. They are the line.

## Bottom line
Our position remains unchanged:
- **every German plant must stay open**
- **no compulsory redundancies**
- **co-determination must be preserved**
- **the job-security framework must remain intact**

We are ready to discuss serious, temporary, and fair employee contributions in exchange for binding protections and real investment. We are **not** ready to trade away jobs, sites, or rights for the promise of a cleaner spreadsheet.

If management wants cooperation, it must bring a package that respects those conditions. If it cannot, then it should stop pretending that closures are the only responsible option.
