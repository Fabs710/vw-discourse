Let me respond directly, because we are now well past the stage of broad statements.

## First: where I agree
I agree with everyone on one point: **a cosmetic deal would be worthless**.

- The works council is right that this cannot be solved by slogans.
- The union is right that a long conflict would damage the company.
- The state is right that we need implementation certainty and not a governance collapse.
- The family holding and investors are right that the solution must restore competitiveness, not just postpone the problem.

So yes: we need a durable settlement.

## But here is where I disagree fundamentally
What I do **not** accept is the idea that durability can be achieved while:
- keeping every German plant open,
- excluding compulsory redundancies,
- excluding closures as a restructuring tool,
- and preserving the current cost structure.

That is not a restructuring. That is a delay.

You all keep asking management to prove that closures or capacity cuts are necessary. Fine — but the burden cuts both ways. If you want to rule out irreversible measures, then show me a package that closes the gap **structurally and permanently**.

Temporary wage restraint, bonus deferral, working-time flexibility, redeployment, and more efficient utilization may help at the margins. They do **not** solve a structural overcapacity and cost problem on their own.

## To the works council
You say:
- no closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- no weakening of guarantees,
- no change to co-determination.

I understand the position. I do **not** accept it as the starting boundary for a viable restructuring.

You repeatedly ask for the savings gap and a site-by-site breakdown. That is fair, and we should put the numbers on the table. But the numbers are not an argument for preserving every site regardless of economics. If one or more German plants are structurally uneconomic, then refusing even to discuss capacity removal is not responsible governance; it is simply denial.

And let me be explicit: **the end of historic job-security guarantees was not a negotiating trick. It was a response to a real cost crisis.** Trust was damaged, yes. But trust alone does not restore margins.

## To the union
You are right that the burden should not fall only on employees. It must be shared.

But your package still starts from a non-negotiable refusal of the very measures that could create the required savings. That is not “shared sacrifice” if the result leaves the company structurally uncompetitive.

If you want binding job and site security through 2030, then you must be prepared to deliver more than temporary concessions. The employee side would need to accept:
- durable labor-cost reductions,
- meaningful working-time flexibility,
- capacity reduction,
- and real changes to the cost base.

Without that, there is no credible deal to be made.

## To the state government
I respect the state’s role and the co-determination framework. But I need to say this very clearly: **the state cannot convert its blocking position into a veto on restructuring itself**.

If the state insists that closures and compulsory redundancies are off the table before alternatives have been tested, then fine — test them. But if the result is still an uncompetitive cost structure, the company cannot be forced to live with it indefinitely.

The region’s industrial base is not protected by freezing the company in a weak position. It is protected by restoring competitiveness so that future investment actually stays here.

## To the family holding
I agree with you more than the employee side on the core economic question: long-term value requires a real reset, not optics.

But I also hear your caution against rushing into irreversible cuts before the alternatives are properly quantified. That is reasonable. What I will not accept is a governing posture that says, “avoid conflict at any cost,” if the cost is an unfit cost base.

If a non-closure package can deliver equivalent savings, I am open to it. If it cannot, then we will have to use the tools that actually work.

## To investors
You are right to demand measurable, durable improvement.

What you should not expect is that we can fix this with half-measures and still hit the brand’s return-on-sales target. We cannot free up investment capacity for 2030 by protecting every legacy structure and hoping productivity fills the gap.

## What would actually move management
Here is the threshold:

I am prepared to pursue a negotiated solution if it delivers:
1. **a material and durable reduction in structural labour cost**
2. **a measurable reduction in capacity**
3. **lower fixed costs**
4. **implementation that survives beyond the next bargaining round**
5. **freedom to reinvest in future products and technology**

That is the standard.

## What I will not accept
I will not sign up to:
- a deal that keeps all German sites untouched,
- a deal that rules out compulsory redundancies and closures categorically,
- a deal that preserves the old cost base under a new label,
- or a deal that trades a short-term peace for long-term decline.

## On the bridge formulas
### 1) Phased restructuring with review gate
This is only useful if Phase 1 produces **real economic improvement**, not just a pause. A review gate cannot become an automatic shield against deeper action if the numbers fail.

### 2) No forced redundancies, voluntary capacity reduction
I am open to voluntary mechanisms **if they generate sufficient and durable savings**. But if the voluntary path does not close the gap, we cannot pretend the problem is solved.

### 3) Site guarantees tied to productivity and investment
I am open to binding investment commitments, product allocation, and site future packages. But I will not accept guarantees that make restructuring impossible regardless of performance.

## Bottom line
So here is my position in plain English:

- **Yes** to a negotiated settlement.
- **Yes** to employee contribution.
- **Yes** to management and owner contribution.
- **Yes** to investment commitments and qualification.
- **No** to a cosmetic peace deal.
- **No** to a package that preserves all German capacity and cost structure unchanged.
- **No** to treating closures and capacity cuts as forbidden before the economics are fully tested.
- **No** to a settlement that leaves the company uncompetitive through 2030.

If the employee side wants to avoid closures, then it must bring an alternative that is economically equivalent — not morally preferable, economically equivalent.

That is the only basis on which we can proceed.
