We are at the point where vague phrases are no longer enough.

## First, where I agree
I agree with the works council on the core red lines:
- no compulsory redundancies,
- no plant closures as the default restructuring tool,
- no dismantling of the collective bargaining framework,
- and no solution that shifts the whole burden onto workers.

I also agree with management, the family holding, investors, and the state on one thing: **this cannot be a cosmetic deal**. If the company has a real competitiveness gap, then it needs a real response.

So the question is not whether change is needed. The question is **who pays, how much, and under what guarantees**.

## Where I disagree with management and capital
What I do **not** accept is the claim that closures or layoffs are somehow the only credible form of durability.

That is not established. It is asserted.

You keep saying “structural” and “durable,” but you have not yet demonstrated that a negotiated package of:
- working-time measures,
- temporary wage restraint,
- bonus sacrifice,
- reduced outsourcing,
- internal redeployment,
- qualification,
- management-side cost cuts,
- and investment commitments

cannot close the gap.

If you want employee concessions, then show the numbers transparently:
- exact savings gap,
- labor cost share,
- overhead share,
- procurement and logistics share,
- capacity/utilization gap,
- site-by-site impact.

Until that is on the table, “closure” is not a necessity claim. It is a bargaining threat.

## To management
I am prepared to discuss a serious contribution from the workforce, but only in exchange for **binding job and site security**.

That means:
- no compulsory redundancies,
- no plant closures,
- no weakening of the collective wage framework,
- and a durable settlement, not a one-year pause.

If you want savings from labour, then the package must be fair and shared. That could include:
- temporary wage restraint,
- bonus renunciation or deferral,
- working-time flexibility,
- a transformation fund,
- and voluntary mechanisms for capacity adjustment.

But I will not endorse a plan where workers give up income and security while management reserves the right to close plants later anyway.

## To the works council
I support your insistence on hard protections. But I want to be equally clear: if we are going to defend jobs and sites, then we must offer a **credible alternative** that actually reduces costs.

That is why I am not dismissing:
- temporary reductions in pay components,
- a limited tariff contribution,
- flexible working time,
- voluntary reductions in hours,
- redeployment,
- and a jointly governed transformation fund.

Those are legitimate bargaining tools.

What I will not support is any arrangement where worker concessions are open-ended, while management’s commitments are vague. If employees contribute, management and owners must contribute too.

## To the state government
Your role matters because the company is not a normal corporation in this respect; co-determination and the special governance structure are part of the reality. I appreciate that you reject a fait accompli.

But I need to be blunt: “no closures, no forced redundancies” is not enough if no one explains how the economics are fixed.

If you want to be part of a responsible settlement, then support:
- a quantified restructuring plan,
- management-side overhead reductions,
- binding investment commitments,
- qualification and redeployment,
- and a negotiated labour contribution.

If the state simply freezes all structural adjustment, then it is not solving the problem; it is postponing it.

## To the family holding and investors
You are right to insist on durability. You are not right to assume that durability only comes through irreversible cuts.

A settlement that destroys trust, triggers prolonged conflict, or permanently weakens the company’s social foundation will be expensive in exactly the way you say you want to avoid.

So the burden of proof is on management and capital:
- prove the gap,
- prove why non-closure options are insufficient,
- and prove that the alternative to closures is not just temporary optics.

If you cannot do that, then insisting on closures is not prudence — it is coercion.

## My position on the bridge formulas
### 1) Phased restructuring with a review gate
This is only acceptable if the first phase contains **hard protections**:
- no closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- no hidden pre-commitment to later site shutdowns.

A review gate must be a genuine review, not a trapdoor.

### 2) No forced redundancies, voluntary capacity reduction
This is the most realistic route if management is serious. We can discuss:
- voluntary severance,
- early retirement,
- internal transfer,
- natural attrition,
- reduction of overtime,
- and working-time adjustments.

But this must remain voluntary and cannot become indirect compulsion.

### 3) Site guarantees tied to productivity and investment milestones
I can support productivity and investment commitments. But not as a disguised closure mechanism.

If milestones are used to reopen the question of closures or forced layoffs, then employees are being asked to sign a blank cheque.

## What would move me
I said this already, and I mean it:

I can support a serious labour contribution if, and only if, it is tied to:
1. **binding job and site security**
2. **management and owner contribution**
3. **a credible investment and product commitment**
4. **a measurable savings target achieved without forced redundancies or closures**

That means:
- temporary wage restraint, yes,
- bonus sacrifice, yes,
- working-time flexibility, yes,
- transformation fund, yes,
- voluntary capacity reduction, yes,

but **no compulsory redundancies and no plant closures**.

## Bottom line
We are not bargaining over whether the workforce should pay for management’s mistakes through permanent job destruction. We will not accept that.

If management wants a settlement, it needs to come back with:
- the numbers,
- a shared-sacrifice package,
- binding job and site guarantees,
- and a credible path to competitiveness **without** forced redundancies or closures.

If it cannot do that, then it should not be surprised if the union continues to mobilize the workforce. The peace obligation is gone, and we will use the leverage we have.
