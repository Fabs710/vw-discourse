We are at the point where positions are clear, so I will speak plainly.

## Where I agree

I agree with all parties on two essentials:

1. **The company has a real competitiveness problem.**
   German cost levels, structural overcapacity, and weak margins cannot be ignored.

2. **A prolonged conflict would destroy value.**
   A strike spiral, governance deadlock, or reputational damage would hurt the company, the workforce, and the ownership base.

Those two facts are not in dispute for me.

## Where I disagree

### To the works council
I do **not** accept the absolute line that there can be no plant closures in Germany under any circumstances.

I understand why you are taking that line, but as a governing shareholder I cannot treat it as an economic principle. If a site is structurally uneconomic, refusing to touch it forever simply shifts the problem into the future and destroys more value later.

I also do **not** accept that co-determination means a veto against all meaningful restructuring. Co-determination is a governing reality, yes — but it is not a shield for permanent underperformance.

That said, I also do **not** support management using closures as a first reflex or as a slogan. If closures are to be on the table, they must be justified by numbers, not by convenience.

### To the union
I agree that workers should not carry the full burden. But I do **not** accept a package that gives employees only temporary concessions while preserving the current cost base and capacity structure.

If the company needs a durable reset, then the labor contribution must be real and lasting enough to matter. Temporary wage restraint alone is not enough.

### To the state government
I respect the state’s role and its blocking position. But I do **not** accept turning that position into a permanent freeze on restructuring.

Protecting the regional base does not mean freezing the company in a weak competitive position. If the business remains structurally unfit, the region loses anyway.

### To management
I support your diagnosis more than I support your current method.

You are right that cosmetic savings are insufficient. You are **not yet** convincing enough that closures or irreversible capacity reduction are the only serious path. If you want support for hard measures, show the numbers:

- exact savings gap,
- labor cost share,
- overhead share,
- procurement and logistics share,
- capacity/utilization gap,
- and the impact by site.

Without that, “closure” sounds like a tool, not a necessity.

### To investors
You are right to insist on durability and margin repair. But I do **not** accept the idea that durability only comes through immediate plant closure. A negotiated package can be durable if it is enforceable, measurable, and backed by real commitments.

## What would move me

I can support a serious restructuring if it meets these conditions:

1. **Durable competitiveness improvement**
   - measurable structural savings
   - lower fixed and labor costs
   - improved capacity utilization
   - credible margin recovery

2. **Avoidance of value-destructive conflict**
   - no open-ended confrontation
   - no industrial chaos
   - no governance paralysis

3. **A negotiated package with real burden sharing**
   - employees contribute
   - management contributes
   - owners contribute
   - investment and future products are secured

## On the bridge formulas

### 1) Phased restructuring with a review gate
This is acceptable only if Phase 1 is real and not cosmetic:
- immediate labor-cost relief,
- capacity/utilization improvements,
- overhead reduction,
- and a clear savings target.

A review gate cannot be a trapdoor, but neither can it be a permanent shield against harder choices if the economics fail.

### 2) No forced redundancies, voluntary capacity reduction
I am open to voluntary mechanisms:
- early retirement,
- voluntary severance,
- internal transfer,
- natural attrition,
- working-time adjustments.

That is preferable to compulsory redundancies. But if the voluntary path does not close the gap, then it cannot be the whole answer.

### 3) Site guarantees tied to productivity and investment milestones
I can support binding investment commitments, product allocation, and site-future packages. But they must be tied to performance and not become a blank cheque that prevents any adjustment regardless of economics.

## My line

I will support **a serious negotiated restructuring that restores competitiveness and margins**.

I will **not** support:
- a cosmetic peace deal,
- a permanent preservation of every structure unchanged,
- or a governance arrangement that makes restructuring impossible.

If the employee side wants to avoid closures, it needs to bring an alternative that is economically equivalent — not morally preferable, economically equivalent.

## What I want next

I want management to put the numbers on the table, in a board-usable form:
- minimum savings gap,
- what is truly structural,
- what can be achieved through non-closure measures,
- and what remains after that.

I want the employee side to specify:
- which concrete concessions they can make,
- for how long,
- and what binding protections they require in return.

And I want the state to be explicit about:
- what restructuring it can support,
- and where its red line actually sits if the numbers do not work.

## Final point

The family holding will back a settlement that:
- restores competitiveness,
- protects long-term enterprise value,
- and avoids destructive instability.

We will not back a hollow compromise. We will not back permanent impairment of the company’s value. And we will not back a settlement that leaves the business uncompetitive through the next cycle.

So: bring us the numbers, and bring us a package that is durable in economic terms, not only in political language.
