We agree on the diagnosis, and that matters: the current German cost base is too high, margins are too weak, and a cosmetic peace would destroy value. We also agree that a prolonged strike spiral or governance deadlock would be bad for everyone, including shareholders.

But we do **not** agree that the only credible answer is to keep plant closures and compulsory redundancies permanently on the table from the start, nor do we accept a settlement that protects jobs in form while leaving the cost base structurally uncompetitive in substance.

## To management
You are right on the principle: the company needs durable structural savings, not temporary optics.

Where we push back is the lack of a quantified case. “We need structural change” is not enough. You need to show, in board-usable numbers:
- the exact savings gap,
- how much comes from labour, overhead, procurement, logistics, and capacity,
- what non-closure measures were tested,
- and why those measures are insufficient.

If you cannot show that a non-closure package fails to close the gap, then closures are not a necessity claim; they are a negotiating weapon. We will not reward that with support.

## To the works council and union
We understand your red lines, but we do **not** support a position that makes all irreversible adjustment impossible. “No closures, no compulsory redundancies, no weakening of guarantees” may be politically clear, but it does not answer the economics.

If you want to avoid closures, then you need to bring a package that is not just defensive, but economically equivalent:
- durable labour-cost reduction,
- meaningful working-time flexibility,
- voluntary capacity reduction,
- redeployment,
- reduced outsourcing,
- bonus restraint or deferral,
- and real productivity improvement.

Temporary wage restraint alone is not enough. A settlement that preserves current capacity and cost structure while calling it “shared sacrifice” will not protect shareholder value.

## To the state government
We agree that destructive conflict would be value-destroying, and we respect the state’s role. But we do **not** support turning co-determination or political leverage into a veto over necessary restructuring.

Protecting the regional base is important. Freezing the company in an uncompetitive position is not protection; it is delayed decline. If the state wants to defend the industrial footprint, it should support a credible path to competitiveness, not a blanket ban on difficult decisions.

## To the controlling family holding
We agree with you on the need for durability and on the danger of a hollow compromise. We also agree that management must not use closures as a slogan before alternatives are tested.

Where we differ is that durability does **not** require accepting permanently impaired margins in the name of avoiding confrontation. If the economics require some irreversible capacity adjustment, then sentiment cannot be the deciding factor. But those decisions must be justified by hard evidence, not by managerial convenience.

## Our threshold
We would support a deal only if it delivers all of the following:
1. **Measurable, durable cost reduction**
2. **A credible return to competitive margins**
3. **Clear capacity adjustment**
4. **Implementation certainty without endless conflict**
5. **No cosmetic settlement that merely postpones the problem**

That can come from a mix of labor measures, overhead cuts, productivity gains, and selective site rationalisation if genuinely necessary. But the package must be economically real.

## Bottom line
We are open to a negotiated settlement. We are **not** open to:
- a deal that leaves the cost base structurally impaired,
- a package that substitutes temporary gestures for lasting savings,
- or a settlement that avoids hard choices while eroding equity value over time.

If management wants our confidence, bring the numbers and the credible path to margin recovery. If the employee side wants to avoid closures, bring an alternative that is economically equivalent. Anything less is not a solution from a capital-market perspective.
