## Where I agree

I agree with management on the core economic point: **the company has a genuine competitiveness problem**. If German operations remain structurally too expensive and capacity is mismatched to demand, then margins will keep eroding and the brand will lose room to invest. That would damage long-term value, and as controlling owner I cannot support that.

I also agree with the works council, union, and state on one important point: **a prolonged industrial conflict would be deeply value-destructive**. A strike spiral, reputational damage, and governance deadlock would weaken the business more than any negotiated adjustment should.

So yes: the status quo is not acceptable, and open conflict is not acceptable either.

## Where I disagree

### 1) To the works council
I do **not** accept your absolute line that there can be no plant closures in Germany under any circumstances.

I understand the political and human concern. But from an ownership perspective, a blanket taboo is not a restructuring strategy. If some sites or capacities are genuinely uneconomic, then refusing to touch them regardless of economics would simply transfer the crisis into the future and destroy more value later.

The same applies to compulsory redundancies: I do not want them as the first resort, but I also will not pre-commit to ruling them out if there is no economically credible alternative. A rigid exclusion may sound protective, but it can also lock the company into weakness.

What I do **not** accept from management, however, is using closures as a slogan before alternatives have been fully tested. That is not good governance either.

### 2) To the union
I agree that employees should not be asked to carry the full burden. But I do **not** accept a package that protects all current guarantees, rejects structural capacity reduction, and offers only temporary or symbolic concessions.

If the deal does not produce durable savings, then it is not a real contribution. Shared sacrifice must be measurable and lasting, not a pause button.

### 3) To the state government
I respect the state’s role and the co-determination framework. But I do **not** support turning the state’s blocking position into a permanent freeze on restructuring.

If every meaningful adjustment is treated as politically off-limits, the company will simply continue to underperform. That helps nobody in the long run — not the region, not the workforce, and not the shareholders.

### 4) To management
I agree with your diagnosis, but I will challenge your method.

You are right that cosmetic measures are not enough. You are **not** yet convincing enough that closures or layoffs are the only path. Before I would support irreversible measures, I would need to see a quantified case that non-closure alternatives cannot close the gap.

So far, what I hear is a demand for structural change, but not yet a sufficiently precise demonstration of:
- the exact savings required,
- the split between labour, overhead, procurement, and capacity,
- and why a negotiated package cannot reach the same end state.

## What would move me

As controlling shareholder, I would support a serious restructuring package if it meets these conditions:

1. **Durable competitiveness improvement**
   - real and measurable cost reduction
   - lower fixed costs
   - reduced structural overcapacity
   - better margin trajectory

2. **Labour peace and implementation certainty**
   - a settlement that can actually be implemented
   - no endless escalation
   - no governance paralysis

3. **Preservation of long-term industrial value**
   - no unnecessary destruction of core capability
   - no value-destructive confrontation
   - no weak compromise that only postpones the crisis

## My position on the current alternatives

- I **do not** support a cosmetic peace deal.
- I **do not** support preserving every structure unchanged.
- I **do not** support plant closures as a first reflex.
- I **do** support a negotiated package if it delivers comparable, durable savings.
- I **do** support management insisting on structural improvement, but not at any cost.

## What I want next

We need to move from principle to numbers.

I would ask management to provide:
- the minimum savings gap that must be closed,
- which elements are truly structural,
- and what non-closure package would be considered economically equivalent.

I would ask the employee side to identify:
- which real, durable concessions it can make,
- and what level of labour contribution it would accept in exchange for binding stability and investment commitments.

And I would ask the state to be explicit about:
- what it would support as a realistic adjustment,
- and where exactly it considers the red lines to be.

## Bottom line

The family holding will support **a serious negotiated restructuring that restores competitiveness and margins**.

We will **not** support:
- a hollow compromise,
- a permanent impairment of the company’s value,
- or a governance deadlock that leaves the business weaker.

If there is a credible alternative to closures that achieves the same economic result, I am open to it. If not, then management cannot be asked to carry an uncompetitive structure indefinitely.
