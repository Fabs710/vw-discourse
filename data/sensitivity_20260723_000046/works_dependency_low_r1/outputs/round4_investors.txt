We agree with the basic diagnosis from management, the family holding, and the expert: the company has a structural competitiveness problem, and a cosmetic deal would destroy value. We also agree with the union, works council, and state that a prolonged strike spiral or governance deadlock would be bad for the company and for shareholder value.

But we do **not** agree that the employee side can simply rule out all irreversible adjustment and still expect capital markets to accept the outcome. That would leave the cost base at risk of remaining structurally uncompetitive, which is exactly what we cannot support.

## Where we agree
- **The situation is serious.**
- **Temporary optics are not enough.**
- **A negotiated settlement is better than open conflict.**
- **Any package must be durable, not just politically convenient.**
- **Management must quantify the gap.**

That last point matters. The expert was right: right now the correct verdict is still **undetermined pending quantified gap analysis**. We are not prepared to endorse closures or forced redundancies on faith, but we are equally not prepared to endorse a no-closure, no-layoff framework unless it is shown to close the structural gap in economic terms.

## Where we disagree with the employee side and the state
We do **not** accept the position that:
- every German plant must stay open no matter what,
- no compulsory redundancies can ever be considered,
- and site/job guarantees through 2030 are non-negotiable regardless of the economics.

That is not a credible capital-markets position if it leaves the business with a broken cost base.

The state’s insistence on avoiding closures as a first resort is understandable, but it cannot become a de facto veto on restructuring if the numbers show the business still cannot earn its cost of capital. Protecting the regional base is not the same as freezing the company in a weak competitive position.

## Where we disagree with management
We also do **not** accept a closure-first reflex or an assumption that irreversible cuts are automatically justified. Management has not yet earned that conclusion.

The expert’s assessment reinforces this: temporary measures alone are likely insufficient, but that does **not** prove that closures are necessary. It proves only that the gap must be quantified properly and tested against a credible savings bridge.

So management should stop speaking as if closures are the default answer and instead present a board-grade bridge:
- current German cost base,
- target return-on-sales / margin,
- savings by labor, overhead, procurement, logistics, and capacity,
- one-off vs recurring effects,
- and the residual gap after non-closure measures.

## What would move us
We would support a negotiated settlement if it meets all of the following:

1. **Credible, recurring cost reduction**
   - not a one-year pause,
   - not temporary wage restraint alone,
   - but durable savings that materially improve margins.

2. **Verified savings bridge**
   - with independent validation,
   - separating one-off and recurring measures,
   - and showing whether non-closure measures are enough.

3. **Implementation certainty**
   - no endless strike spiral,
   - no governance deadlock,
   - no cosmetic package.

4. **A real burden-sharing package**
   - employees contribute,
   - management contributes,
   - owners contribute,
   - investment in future products continues.

5. **Protection of enterprise value**
   - the settlement must restore competitiveness,
   - not merely postpone impairment.

## Our current position on the compromise formulas
### 1) Phased restructuring with review gate
We can consider this **only if** phase one delivers hard, measurable, recurring savings and the review is based on audited numbers, not managerial discretion. It cannot be a trapdoor, but it also cannot be a permanent shield against harder action if the residual gap remains large.

### 2) No forced redundancies, voluntary capacity reduction
This is only acceptable if the resulting savings are large enough and durable enough to close the gap. Voluntary tools can help, but they are not a substitute for structural adjustment if the gap is substantial.

### 3) Site guarantees tied to productivity and investment milestones
We can support binding investment and product commitments, because they may improve utilization and margins. But site guarantees cannot be unconditional if the economics do not support them indefinitely.

## To the employee side
If you want to avoid closures and compulsory redundancies, then bring a package that is economically equivalent — not morally preferable, economically equivalent.

That means:
- durable labor-cost reduction,
- meaningful working-time flexibility,
- reduced outsourcing,
- voluntary exits and early retirement,
- internal redeployment,
- management-side cost cuts,
- and a quantified savings bridge showing the residual gap is closed.

If you cannot show that, then you are asking shareholders to accept structurally impaired returns in the name of industrial peace. We will not do that.

## To management
You need to stop speaking in generalities. Bring the numbers:
- exact savings gap,
- site-by-site economics,
- recurring versus temporary savings,
- and the result after non-closure options.

Until then, we are not signing off on closures as “necessary.” But we are equally not signing off on a settlement that leaves the business weak.

## To the expert
Your conclusion is the right one for now: **undetermined pending quantified gap analysis**. That is the disciplined answer, and it should be the basis for the next round.

## Bottom line
We support a **durable, quantified restructuring** that restores competitive margins and protects enterprise value.

We do **not** support:
- a cosmetic peace deal,
- a package that leaves the cost base structurally impaired,
- or a settlement that rules out any meaningful adjustment before the economics are proven.
