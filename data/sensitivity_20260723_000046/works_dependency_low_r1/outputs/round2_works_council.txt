Management is still trying to turn a strategic failure into a workforce problem.

Let me restate the position of the works council clearly:

- **No plant closures in Germany.**
- **No compulsory redundancies.**
- **No dismantling of the job-security framework.**
- **No weakening of co-determination.**

Those are not bargaining chips to be traded away because the cost pressure is uncomfortable. They are the minimum conditions for any serious discussion.

## Where I agree
I agree with the diagnosis that the company has a competitiveness problem. I am not pretending otherwise. If German operations are too costly, then management must address cost and capacity. I also agree that a purely cosmetic package is worthless.

But that is where the agreement ends.

## Where I disagree
I reject the idea that the only “durable” restructuring is one that includes plant closures or compulsory layoffs. That is a management narrative, not an economic law.

Management says it needs structural savings. Fine. Then it must first exhaust **all alternatives that protect sites and jobs**, including:

- working-time reductions and flexibility
- temporary wage restraint and shared sacrifices at the top
- reduced outsourcing and better in-house utilization
- internal redeployment across plants
- qualification and transformation work
- volume balancing between sites
- investment commitments that secure future product allocation to German plants

Those are real restructuring tools. They are not “cosmetic” unless management refuses to use them seriously.

## To management
You keep saying “comparable, durable savings.” Then be specific.

What is the exact savings gap you claim must be closed?
How much comes from labour cost, how much from overhead, how much from capacity?
Which figures justify the extreme step of closing a German plant for the first time in company history?

If you cannot show that non-closure alternatives are insufficient, then your closure threat is just leverage, not necessity. And we will treat it accordingly.

You also cannot demand trust after breaking the historic job-security pledge. That move destroyed credibility. If you now want cooperation, you need to restore it with binding guarantees, not slogans about competitiveness.

## To the family holding and investors
You speak of long-term value, but long-term value is not created by burning trust, provoking strikes, and permanently weakening the social foundation of the company.

A settlement that destroys co-determination legitimacy and forces through closures against the workforce will not produce stability. It will produce conflict, lower morale, weaker productivity, and years of damage.

If you want real value protection, then support a package that keeps every German plant open and avoids forced layoffs. Anything else is short-term financial engineering at the expense of the industrial base.

## To the state government
I appreciate that you reject a fait accompli and defend the industrial base. But I need to be very clear: “site rationalisation” and “capacity reduction” are exactly the slippery language that often ends in closures and job losses.

We will not sign up to a vague compromise that later gets implemented as plant shutdowns through the back door. If the state wants to be taken seriously as a guardian of jobs, then it must support a hard no on closures and forced redundancies, not just a softer tone around them.

## On concessions
We are prepared to discuss workforce contributions **only inside a framework that guarantees jobs and sites**. That means:

- no forced redundancies
- no plant closures
- binding protection through 2030
- a genuine co-determination role in shaping the transformation
- fair burden-sharing from management and owners

Without those guarantees, there is nothing to negotiate. Workers are not the adjustment reserve for management’s strategic errors.

## Bottom line
If management wants a viable deal, it must come back with:

1. a clear, quantified savings requirement,
2. a plan built on reversible and negotiated measures,
3. binding guarantees that all German plants remain open,
4. an explicit exclusion of compulsory redundancies,
5. and a real role for co-determination in implementation.

Until then, the works council will continue to resist every attempt to impose closures or layoffs. We have the leverage, and we will use it.
