I will be equally clear in round 4: **I agree with the works council on the red lines, and I disagree with management and capital on the claim that closures or compulsory redundancies are the only durable answer.**

## Where I agree
I agree with the works council, the state, and even management on one point: **this cannot be a cosmetic deal**.

The company does need a durable competitiveness solution. I also agree that we need numbers, not just language. If management says there is a structural gap, then it must show:
- the exact savings gap,
- what comes from labour,
- what comes from overhead,
- what comes from procurement and logistics,
- what comes from capacity utilization,
- and what can be achieved without closures or forced layoffs.

That is not an unreasonable demand. It is the minimum basis for any serious bargaining.

## Where I disagree
What I do **not** accept is the repeated attempt to turn “durable restructuring” into a synonym for:
- plant closures,
- compulsory redundancies,
- and permanent insecurity.

That is not a proven necessity. It is a preferred tool.

And I do not accept the argument that if we do not accept irreversible cuts now, then we are somehow refusing reality. We are not refusing reality. We are insisting that **employees should not be forced to carry the whole burden while management and owners escape with a cleaner balance sheet**.

## To management
You say temporary measures are insufficient and that the company needs a structural reset. Fine. Then show it.

But until you have done that, closures are not “the answer.” They are leverage.

I am prepared to discuss real employee contributions, including:
- temporary wage restraint,
- bonus sacrifice or deferral,
- working-time flexibility,
- voluntary reductions in hours,
- redeployment,
- qualification,
- reduced outsourcing,
- and a transformation fund.

Those are meaningful contributions. They are not cosmetic.

What I will **not** accept is a package where workers give up pay, flexibility, and guarantees while management keeps closures and layoffs in reserve as the final word. That is not shared sacrifice. That is one-sided risk transfer.

## To the works council
I support your core line:
- no compulsory redundancies,
- no plant closures,
- no weakening of co-determination,
- no dismantling of the collective framework.

That is exactly right.

Where I would sharpen it is this: if employees are asked to contribute, then the contribution must be **tied to binding protections**, not to vague promises.

So yes to:
- binding job and site security,
- no forced layoffs,
- investment commitments,
- and a genuine role for worker representation in implementation.

No to:
- hidden trapdoors,
- “voluntary” measures that become pressure by attrition,
- or a review gate that is just a delayed closure decision.

## To the state government
I agree that the state should not allow a fait accompli. But I also need to be blunt: saying “no closures as a first resort” is not enough if the state then accepts a process that leaves closures as a standing threat.

If the state wants to be a credible anchor shareholder, it should support:
- a quantified restructuring plan,
- management-side cost cuts,
- qualification and redeployment,
- investment commitments,
- and a negotiated labour contribution.

But it should also say clearly:
- no forced redundancies,
- no closure-first strategy,
- and no use of milestones as a disguised shutdown mechanism.

## To the family holding and investors
You are right that the company needs competitiveness and not illusions. I do not dispute that.

What I do dispute is the idea that only irreversible plant closures can create durability. That is not established. It may be your preference, but it is not yet your proof.

A negotiated package can be durable if it is:
- economically measurable,
- enforceable,
- socially legitimate,
- and backed by real contributions from management and ownership.

If you want employees to contribute, then the owners and management must contribute too:
- overhead reductions,
- management cost cuts,
- investment commitments,
- and a clear industrial plan for German sites.

## On the compromise formulas
### 1) Phased restructuring with review gate
This is only acceptable if the first phase contains **hard protections**:
- no closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- no hidden pre-commitment to shutdowns.

A review gate must be a real review, not a trapdoor.

### 2) No forced redundancies, voluntary capacity reduction
This is the most realistic route if management is serious. We can discuss:
- early retirement,
- voluntary severance,
- internal transfer,
- natural attrition,
- overtime reduction,
- and working-time adjustments.

But it must remain truly voluntary and not become indirect compulsion.

### 3) Site guarantees tied to productivity and investment
I can support productivity and investment commitments. I cannot support them if they are just a coded mechanism to reopen the closure threat later.

If milestones are used to force later plant shutdowns, then they are not a partnership tool. They are a delayed ultimatum.

## What would move me
Very specifically, this is what can move the union:

1. **Binding no-compulsory-redundancy commitments**
2. **Binding protection against plant closures**
3. **Binding site and investment commitments**
4. **A fair employee contribution**
5. **Management and owner contribution**
6. **A measurable savings package that does not rely on forced layoffs**

That is the framework.

## What will not move me
These will not move me:
- “temporary” concessions with no real guarantees,
- a promise to revisit closures later,
- a review gate that functions as a trapdoor,
- or a settlement that asks workers to pay for restructuring with their jobs.

## Bottom line
We are prepared to contribute. We are not prepared to be sacrificed.

If management wants a serious settlement, it needs to come back with a package that:
- keeps German sites open,
- excludes compulsory redundancies,
- preserves the collective wage framework,
- and shares the burden fairly across workforce, management, and owners.

If it cannot do that, then it should not be surprised if the union continues to mobilize the workforce. We have not exhausted our leverage, and we will use it if necessary.
