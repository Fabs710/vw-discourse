We agree on the diagnosis, but not yet on the credibility of the solution.

### Where we agree
- The company has a real competitiveness problem.
- The current German cost base is too high.
- A cosmetic settlement would be value-destructive.
- Prolonged strikes or governance deadlock would also destroy value.
- Any package must be durable, not just a pause button.

From a capital-market perspective, those are the right starting points.

### Where we disagree
We do **not** accept a settlement that preserves the current structure, avoids meaningful capacity adjustment, and leaves margins structurally impaired. If the outcome is only:
- temporary wage restraint,
- flexible working time,
- internal redeployment,
- and vague future commitments,

then that is not enough. It may reduce short-term conflict, but it does not restore risk-adjusted value.

We also do **not** support an outright taboo on closures or compulsory redundancies if those are genuinely necessary to close the cost gap. Jobs cannot be protected indefinitely by keeping an uncompetitive cost base in place. That would simply erode equity value, investment capacity, and ultimately the company’s long-term industrial position.

### Our position on the current debate
To the works council and union: your insistence on no closures and no compulsory redundancies is understandable, but if it blocks real restructuring, it becomes unacceptable. We need a package that closes the gap in measurable, lasting terms. If you want to avoid closures, then the alternative must still produce comparable savings and capacity reduction.

To management: you are right that symbolic savings are not enough. But you still need to prove, with numbers, that closures or headcount reduction are truly required after serious testing of alternatives. “Structural change” is not the same as “closure first.” Show the gap, show the measures, and show why the non-closure path is insufficient.

To the state and family: we agree that a destructive conflict would be value-destructive. But support for stability cannot become support for inertia. If the company remains structurally uncompetitive, long-term value will keep leaking out. We would rather see a hard but credible restructuring than a politically comfortable compromise that leaves the economics broken.

### What would move us
We would support a package if it includes all of the following:
- **measurable durable cost reduction**
- **clear capacity adjustment**
- **a credible return to competitive margins**
- **implementation certainty**
- **limited disruption relative to open conflict**

That can come from a mix of:
- labor-cost concessions,
- working-time changes,
- overhead cuts,
- site rationalisation where necessary,
- and investment commitments tied to performance milestones.

But the package must clearly and durably improve the economics. Anything that leaves the company with structurally impaired margins is not supportable.

### Bottom line
We are open to a negotiated deal, but only if it is economically real. We will not back a cosmetic peace settlement, and we will not back indefinite preservation of an uncompetitive structure. If the final package does not materially close the cost gap, then it does not deserve capital-market support.
