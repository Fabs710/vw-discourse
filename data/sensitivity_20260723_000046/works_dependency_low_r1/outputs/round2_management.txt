Let me respond directly, because we are now past generalities.

I agree with one thing the works council and union keep repeating: this company needs a **durable** solution, not a symbolic one. I also agree with the state, the family holding, and investors that a prolonged conflict would destroy value. On that, we are aligned.

But let me be equally clear on the core point: **a deal that excludes closures, excludes compulsory redundancies, and preserves the existing cost structure is not a restructuring**. It is a delay tactic.

## To the works council
You say “no closures, no compulsory redundancies, no weakening of guarantees.” I understand the political line. I do **not** accept it as an economic answer.

You keep asking for the exact savings gap. The gap is simple: **German factory costs are too high relative to target and to competitors, and the brand is missing its return-on-sales target**. That is not a marginal issue that can be solved by redeployment slogans and temporary flexibility. We need a **structural, lasting reduction in cost and capacity**.

If you want to avoid closures, then show me a package that delivers the same economic result:
- materially lower labour costs,
- measurable capacity reduction,
- lower fixed costs,
- and a credible path back to margin improvement.

Not promises. Numbers.

If the answer is just:
- working-time tweaks,
- temporary wage restraint,
- and internal redeployment,

then that does **not** close the gap. It may soften it, but it does not fix it. And I will not sign up to a cosmetic compromise while the company continues to bleed competitiveness.

## To the union
You say employees should not carry the burden alone. On that, I agree. Management and owners must contribute too. But your position still starts from an absolute refusal of the measures that could actually restore competitiveness.

You want:
- no compulsory redundancies,
- no closures,
- no dismantling of the collective framework,
- and binding protection through 2030.

That is not compatible with the scale of the crisis **unless** the employee side is prepared to deliver a very substantial concession package. At the moment, I do not see that from your side.

If you want to avoid closures, then “shared sacrifice” must mean real sacrifice:
- durable wage restraint, not just temporary optics,
- substantial flexibility on working time,
- meaningful reductions in labour cost,
- and acceptance that some capacity must come out of the system.

Anything less is asking management to carry the full adjustment while continuing to fund an uncompetitive structure. That is not negotiable.

## To the state government
I respect the state’s role and its veto power. I also understand the concern for jobs and industrial capacity in the region.

But I need to say this plainly: **using the state’s position to freeze restructuring would be a mistake**. If the company is forced to preserve every site and every legacy arrangement regardless of economics, then the cost crisis will remain. The result will be weaker investment, weaker products, and ultimately a weaker industrial base.

So yes, we want negotiated solutions. But no, I will not accept a political veto over any meaningful structural adjustment. If the state wants to help, it should help secure a deal that restores competitiveness, not one that postpones hard choices.

## To the family holding
I appreciate your focus on long-term value and stability. But stability cannot be confused with inertia.

You are right that a destructive confrontation would be value-destroying. You are also right that cosmetic compromise is not acceptable. Where I push back is your reluctance to confront the need for irreversible measures if the economics demand them.

We cannot leave capacity untouched and simply hope a bit of flexibility and goodwill will solve the problem. Long-term value is protected by:
- lower fixed cost,
- better utilization,
- and a structure that matches demand.

That may require plant closures or at minimum equivalent, binding capacity removals. If a non-closure package truly delivers the same effect, I’m prepared to look at it. But it must be credible and enforceable, not aspirational.

## To investors
You are right to focus on margin improvement and implementation certainty. That is exactly why I am insisting on a structural package, not a symbolic one.

I also agree that long strikes and uncertainty are damaging. But the answer is not to accept a weak compromise in order to stop the noise. The answer is to secure a deal that actually changes the economics.

## What would move management
I remain open to alternatives to closures. But only if they deliver **comparable, durable savings**. Concretely, that means a package with:
- a genuine reduction in structural labour cost,
- measurable capacity reduction,
- a reduction in fixed overhead,
- and implementation that survives beyond the next bargaining cycle.

I am not asking for one-off pain. I am asking for a sustainable reset.

## Bottom line
So here is where I stand:

- **I support a negotiated settlement.**
- **I do not support a cosmetic peace deal.**
- **I do not support preserving all German plants, all guarantees, and the current cost base unchanged.**
- **I remain prepared to consider non-closure solutions only if they close the gap structurally and durably.**

If the employee side wants to avoid closures, it needs to bring an alternative that is economically equivalent. If it cannot, then management will continue to pursue structural remedies, because the company cannot compete on sentiment.
