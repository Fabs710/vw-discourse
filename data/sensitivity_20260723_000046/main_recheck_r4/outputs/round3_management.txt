Let me be equally direct.

I hear the workforce side’s insistence on **no closures, no compulsory redundancies, and binding guarantees through 2030**. I understand why you are drawing that line. But from where I sit, that line still does **not** answer the core problem: **our German cost base and capacity are structurally too high for the market we face.**

### Where I agree
I agree with a few things that have been said:
- A **cosmetic** or temporary deal would be useless.
- We do need **durable**, quantified savings.
- We do need a package that allows **investment in future products and technology**.
- Social conflict would be damaging for everyone.

So yes, we are aligned on the need for a real solution.

### Where I disagree
I do **not** agree that the solution can start with the premise that every German plant must stay open and every job must be protected to 2030 regardless of economics.

That is not a restructuring plan. That is a constraint that may make the company’s problems worse.

And let me be clear: if the answer is “we will only discuss measures that preserve the current footprint,” then the employee side is effectively asking management to guarantee the status quo while the market keeps deteriorating. I cannot accept that.

### On closures and compulsory redundancies
I am not saying closures are the first choice. I am saying they remain **on the table** if — and only if — no alternative package closes the gap with the same durability.

That is not a threat for its own sake. It is the reality of the economics.

If the workforce wants closures off the table, then it must present a package that is:
- **quantified**
- **durable**
- **legally and operationally enforceable**
- and capable of delivering the same structural savings

Not just “internal flexibility,” not just “voluntary measures,” and not just aspirational commitments.

### On the union’s and works council’s framing
You both keep saying that management is turning workers into the adjustable shock absorber. I reject that framing.

What we are actually saying is this: the company as a whole must adjust to reality. That includes labor cost, capacity, and operational footprint. If one side insists that only management and owners adjust while the industrial structure stays untouched, then that is not shared sacrifice either.

### To the state shareholder
I appreciate the concern about regional employment and legitimacy. I do not dismiss that. But the state’s responsibility is not to freeze the footprint at any cost. Its responsibility is to help ensure there is a viable company in the future.

A company that continues to miss its return-on-sales target, with an uncompetitive cost base, will not protect jobs in the long run. It will simply delay a deeper crisis.

### What I would need to see
If the employee side is serious about avoiding closures, then the next step is not another statement of principle. It is a concrete alternative package with numbers.

Specifically:
1. **How much savings do you deliver?**
2. **How much capacity do you take out?**
3. **How quickly?**
4. **What exact labour-cost contribution do you make?**
5. **What legal certainty makes it durable through 2030?**

Without that, “no closures” is not a solution; it is a refusal to engage with the economics.

### On burden-sharing
I remain open to burden-sharing, but it must be real burden-sharing, not a one-sided protection package. That means:
- employee contribution,
- management restraint,
- and owner-side discipline.

But burden-sharing does **not** mean that management gives up the restructuring mandate. It does not mean we accept guarantees that prevent necessary structural change.

### My position remains
- We need a **material reduction in overcapacity**.
- We need a **material reduction in labor cost**.
- We need a **durable path back to margin**.
- We need **investment capacity to 2030**.

If those conditions can be met without closures, I will support that. If they cannot, then closures and deeper structural measures remain legitimate options.

So I will say it plainly: **bring a real alternative, or accept that structural action remains necessary.**
