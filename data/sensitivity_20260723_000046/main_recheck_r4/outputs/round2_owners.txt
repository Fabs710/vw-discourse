I will respond plainly.

I agree with several points raised by the employee side and the state shareholder: this cannot become a brute-force social rollback, and a cosmetic settlement would be value-destructive. They are right that prolonged conflict, loss of trust, and political backlash can destroy value just as surely as bad cost discipline.

But I disagree with their central red lines **as absolute positions**.

### Where I agree
- The company needs a **durable** restructuring, not a temporary patch.
- **Shared sacrifice** is preferable to making employees carry everything.
- A deal that triggers open-ended industrial conflict would damage the company’s value and reputation.
- Investment in future products and technology must remain part of the outcome.

### Where I disagree
The repeated insistence on:
- **no plant closures ever**,
- **no compulsory redundancies ever**,
- **binding guarantees through 2030** regardless of economics,

is not compatible with the ownership duty to protect the enterprise as a whole.

I understand the social and regional concerns, but a line that protects every structure regardless of cost is not a restructuring plan. It is a veto on necessary change. If alternatives truly close the savings gap, I will support them. But I will not support a settlement that preserves all sites and leaves the company structurally uncompetitive.

### On management
Management is right to insist that the current cost base and capacity are too high. But management must also be disciplined in how it proceeds.

I do **not** want a strategy that needlessly escalates conflict or creates a permanent legitimacy problem. If closures or forced measures are used, they must be because they are genuinely necessary after alternatives have been exhausted, not because they are the easiest path.

So my support for management is conditional:
- show me **quantified, durable savings**,
- show me a **credible path back to margin**,
- and show me that the chosen measures are proportional and executable.

### On the works council and union
If you want us to avoid closures, then bring a package that is economically equivalent, not just politically preferable.

That means:
- real capacity reduction,
- real productivity improvement,
- real labour-cost contribution,
- and enough durability to matter.

Temporary gestures, voluntary measures alone, or vague promises do not satisfy that test. If the answer is simply “keep all plants open and give us guarantees,” then there is no basis for a serious agreement.

### On the state shareholder
I share the concern about the regional base and supplier network. That concern is legitimate. But the state’s role should not be to freeze the company’s industrial footprint indefinitely.

The state has a stake in a competitive, investable company, not just in preserving nominal capacity. If the economics require change, the issue is how to achieve it with the least long-term damage — not whether change can be denied outright.

### My position
I can support:
- a negotiated package with real savings,
- a strong employee contribution if matched by meaningful management and owner discipline,
- investment commitments tied to measurable performance,
- voluntary measures and flexibility where they work,
- and, if unavoidable, structural measures that are necessary to restore competitiveness.

I cannot support:
- cosmetic compromise,
- open-ended guarantees that lock in uncompetitive structures,
- or a settlement that permanently weakens the company’s value by avoiding hard decisions.

So the burden is on the other parties now: if you want to avoid closures, bring an alternative that closes the gap in a durable and enforceable way. If you cannot, then the company must retain the option of structural action.
