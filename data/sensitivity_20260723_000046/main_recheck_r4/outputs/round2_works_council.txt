Let me respond directly to management, the owners, and the capital market side.

You all keep saying “credible,” “durable,” and “structural.” I hear that. But let me be equally clear: **a solution that closes German plants, breaks the historic job-security framework, or imposes compulsory redundancies is not credible to the workforce — it is a declaration of conflict.**

### To management
You say the current cost base is too high. Fine. But your conclusion is not the only possible one, and it is not acceptable as stated.

- **We do not accept plant closures in Germany.**
- **We do not accept compulsory redundancies.**
- **We do not accept a restructuring that hands workers the bill for management’s strategic failures.**

If you need savings, then bring us a plan that achieves them **without closing plants and without forced layoffs**. That means:
- internal capacity adjustments,
- working-time instruments,
- temporary and voluntary measures,
- retraining and redeployment,
- product and investment commitments,
- and real burden-sharing from management and owners.

If you cannot close the savings gap without resorting to closures or forced job cuts, then you are not presenting a restructuring plan — you are presenting a social rollback.

### To the controlling family and investors
You keep repeating that economics must prevail. I do not dispute the need for economic viability. But you are reducing viability to a single metric: immediate cost cutting. That is too narrow and too risky.

A company like this does not survive by destroying the very workforce, skills base, and industrial stability that make it strong. **Closing plants may look “durable” on a spreadsheet, but it creates long-term damage in trust, know-how, industrial peace, and regional legitimacy.**

And let’s be honest: if the logic is “no closure can be ruled out,” then employees are being asked to negotiate under threat. That is not a fair basis for a settlement.

### To the state shareholder
Your concerns about regional employment and supplier networks are exactly right. But concern is not enough. If you mean what you say, then you must use your blocking position to stop a package that destroys German industrial capacity. The workforce will not accept that the state watches from the sidelines while historic guarantees are broken.

### On the “minimum acceptable outcome”
Our minimum is unchanged:

1. **Every German plant stays open.**
2. **No compulsory redundancies.**
3. **Binding job and site guarantees through 2030.**
4. **Real co-determination in shaping the restructuring.**
5. **A fair contribution from management, owners, and capital.**

### What we can discuss
We are not refusing restructuring. We are refusing destructive restructuring.

We can discuss:
- reduced volumes and capacity use,
- flexible working-time arrangements,
- voluntary exit programs,
- internal transfers and retraining,
- temporary, negotiated employee contributions,
- management restraint,
- and investment commitments tied to future models and technologies.

But any package must be built on **site security and job security first**. Not as a side note, not as a later promise, but as the foundation.

### What will not move us
What will not move us is a demand that we accept closures “if needed,” with no real alternative on the table. If management and owners want our consent, they must show a serious package that closes the savings gap **without breaking German sites and jobs**.

If they do not, then they should not expect agreement — they should expect resistance in the supervisory board, in the plants, and publicly.

If needed, we should now put hard numbers on the alternative path:
- exact savings target,
- exact non-closure measures,
- exact investment commitments,
- and exact guarantees on employment to 2030.

But let us not pretend that closures and forced layoffs are simply “one option among many.” For us, they are a red line.
