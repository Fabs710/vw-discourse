[Works Council (Betriebsrat) (Plant-level employee representation under co-determination) — Round 1]
As Works Council, I will be very clear from the start:

We will not accept plant closures in Germany and we will not accept compulsory redundancies. That is the line.

The workforce has already carried a great deal of adjustment, and we are not prepared to let management solve strategic mistakes by sacrificing jobs, sites, and the social commitments that have existed for decades. A restructuring is one thing; dismantling the German industrial base is another.

Our priorities are:

1. **Every German plant stays open.**
2. **No forced layoffs.**
3. **Binding job and site guarantees through 2030.**
4. **A real co-determination role in shaping any restructuring, not a fait accompli.**
5. **Fair burden-sharing from management and owners, not one-sided pressure on employees.**

If savings are needed, then they must come through alternatives that protect jobs and sites: capacity adjustments, internal flexibility, working-time instruments, voluntary measures, retraining, product and investment commitments, and negotiated contributions that are limited, temporary, and proportionate. But we will not trade away the workforce’s future for short-term cost cutting.

Management has to understand this: if it comes with closures, forced layoffs, or the breaking of historic job-security guarantees, it will face decisive resistance. We are prepared to fight this in the boardroom and, if necessary, in the plants. We want a viable future for the company, but not at the expense of tens of thousands of employees and their families.

[Industrial Union (Trade union and collective-bargaining counterpart) — Round 1]
I agree with the Works Council on the core line: **no plant closures in Germany and no compulsory redundancies**. That is not a decorative preference; it is the minimum requirement for any credible settlement.

Management’s framing is too one-sided. Yes, the company faces cost pressure and market challenges. But shifting the burden mainly onto employees through closures, job cuts, and dismantling protections is not acceptable. Workers did not create the strategic missteps, and they should not be asked to pay for them alone.

What we can discuss is a **fair restructuring package** that shares the pain and preserves the industrial base:

- **Capacity reduction through working-time tools and internal flexibility**, not closures.
- **Voluntary measures only** for any staffing adjustment.
- **A temporary employee contribution** if, and only if, management and owners also contribute substantially.
- **Investment and product commitments** that secure the future of German sites.
- **Binding job and site guarantees** for a meaningful period, not vague promises.

Let me be equally clear on what will not work:  
If the proposal depends on plant closures, forced redundancies, or open-ended insecurity, then there is no basis for agreement. In that case, the workforce will resist—also industrially, if necessary.

We are open to a serious compromise, but it must be built on **shared sacrifice, not worker sacrifice alone**. Management and the owners need to put something real on the table: lower dividends, management restraint, and a credible long-term commitment to the sites and the collective bargaining framework. Without that, there is nothing to negotiate.

## Facilitator proposals for possible compromise paths

### 1) Binding “no closure during restructuring period” with objective trigger review

- monthly or quarterly monitoring,

If milestones are missed, the parties do **not** reopen unilateral closure rights immediately; instead they enter a **second-stage bargaining process** with mandatory mediation and a defined list of additional non-closure measures to exhaust first.

This may bridge the gap between:
- employee/state demands for real security, and
- management/owners’ demand for economic discipline.

- **No compulsory redundancies** as a binding rule,

- enhanced severance / bridge-to-retirement options,
- retraining guarantees,
- internal transfer rights,
- and management/owner contributions that visibly reduce the burden on labor.

This could reduce resistance because it removes the most contested labor instrument while still allowing some footprint adjustment.

- German plants remain open for the agreed period,

- **Industrial Union**: collective-bargaining actor with strike power; high leverage on labour peace.
- **Regional State Government**: anchor shareholder and political actor; high legitimacy and blocking influence.
- **Controlling Family Holding**: majority voting-rights holder; high formal governance power.
- **Institutional Investors**: dispersed capital-market shareholders; weaker formal power, but important for value and market credibility.

## 3. Final positions
### Group & Brand Management
Management holds that the current German cost base and capacity are structurally uncompetitive and that the restructuring must remain able to include plant closures and compulsory redundancies if internal measures do not close the savings gap. It is open to voluntary exits, flexibility, redeployment, retraining, and temporary contributions, but rejects an absolute no-closure framework or a 2030-style blanket guarantee.

### Works Council
The works council maintains a hard red line: no German plant closures, no compulsory redundancies, and no “review later” mechanism that merely postpones closure threats. It accepts internal measures such as working-time flexibility, voluntary exits, redeployment, retraining, and productivity changes, but only within a binding no-closure, no-forced-layoff framework.

### Industrial Union
The union aligns with the works council on the core red lines and supports burden-sharing rather than one-sided worker sacrifice. Unlike the works council, it shows limited openness to a genuinely binding phased process, but only if it removes forced layoffs and closures during the restructuring period and contains enforceable measures, monitoring, and contribution from management and owners.

### Regional State Government
The state shareholder strongly backs a binding no-closure and no-forced-layoff approach to protect regional employment, supplier networks, and industrial stability. It is willing to support serious internal restructuring and investment commitments, but rejects any model that treats closures as a normal fallback or uses review clauses as exit ramps.

### Controlling Family Holding
The family holding agrees that the company needs a durable, non-cosmetic solution and recognizes the need for quantified savings and investment capacity. It will not accept freezing the footprint regardless of economics; closures must remain available if alternatives do not deliver equivalent savings, though it is open to shared sacrifice and non-closure measures first.

### Institutional Investors
Investors support management’s diagnosis and insist that a cosmetic compromise would destroy value. They are open to non-closure measures only if they are quantified, legally enforceable, durable, and sufficient to restore margins; otherwise, they insist closures cannot be ruled out.

## 4. Genuine conflicts (structurally incompatible positions)
- **Closures as fallback vs. closures off-limits**: management, family, and investors want structural options preserved; labour and the state want a binding no-closure framework.
- **Compulsory redundancies**: labour/state reject them categorically; capital-side actors will not rule them out if needed.
- **Sequencing**: labour wants security first, then contributions; management and investors want quantified savings first, then reduced closure risk.
- **Meaning of “durable”**: labour defines durability as protected sites and jobs; capital defines it as economically sufficient, enforceable cost reduction.
- **Review mechanisms**: labour/state view them as backdoors; management/capital view them as necessary discipline and contingency planning.

## 5. Areas of convergence
- All parties agree the status quo is unsustainable and temporary fixes are insufficient.
- There is broad support for shared sacrifice rather than burdening only one side.
- All parties endorse some combination of internal flexibility, working-time tools, voluntary exits, redeployment, retraining, and investment in future products.
- Everyone recognizes that prolonged conflict would be value-destructive and harmful to the company’s future.

## 6. Key trade-offs for the decision-maker
- **Economic sufficiency vs. industrial/social stability**: deeper structural cuts may improve margins but trigger conflict and long-term damage to legitimacy and capability.
- **Binding job security vs. management flexibility**: strong guarantees could secure labour peace but may constrain necessary adjustment.
- **Immediate certainty vs. conditionality**: a hard no-closure promise gives workers certainty but leaves management worried about underdelivery; conditionality preserves adaptability but erodes trust.
- **Short-term conflict avoidance vs. long-term credibility**: a compromise without enforceable savings risks merely postponing the crisis.
- **Regional-political legitimacy vs. enterprise value**: the state can preserve industrial presence, but not at any cost to competitiveness.

## 7. Integrative pathways
1. **Time-limited binding no-closure period with objective monitoring**  
   A restructuring period could be defined during which closures and compulsory redundancies are ruled out, while agreed internal measures are implemented and independently verified. If milestones are missed, the process would move to mandatory mediation and a second-stage negotiation, rather than immediate unilateral closure rights.

2. **No compulsory redundancies, but voluntary structural adjustment**  
   Separating the two most contested tools may be the most workable bridge: permanent rejection of forced layoffs, combined with enhanced voluntary exits, retraining, redeployment, and capacity reductions.

3. **Site-security package with quantified contributions**  
   Sites remain open for a defined period in exchange for measurable labour flexibility, temporary employee contributions, management restraint, owner-side sacrifice, and binding investment commitments.

4. **Arbitrated escalation mechanism**  
   If targets are missed, a jointly governed arbitration step could decide what additional non-closure measures must be exhausted before any structural discussion reopens.

## 8. Open questions
- What exact savings gap remains after all non-closure measures each side is willing to accept?
- Which internal measures are truly sufficient to replace closure-driven savings?
- What level of job/site security is compatible with a durable restructuring?
- What contributions will management, owners, and the state each make in concrete terms?
- What monitoring and enforcement mechanism would both sides regard as credible?
- How long can any no-closure commitment last without becoming economically unrealistic?

## 9. Recommended next step
Request a single, quantified options paper from management, with matching counterproposals from labour and the state, showing:
- exact savings target,
- exact non-closure measures,
- exact employee/management/owner/state contributions,
- exact job and site protections,
- implementation timetable,
- and a credible enforcement mechanism.  
The next negotiation should focus only on packages that can demonstrably close the gap without immediately reintroducing closure threats.

## 10. Emergent insights that only appeared through multi-turn interaction
- The real issue is not simply “restructuring or not,” but **who bears residual risk** when the savings gap remains after internal measures.
- Labour’s rejection of “review later” clauses is not just rhetorical; it reflects a belief that conditionality destroys trust, investment planning, and bargaining legitimacy immediately.
- Management, family, and investors are not merely demanding cuts; they are demanding a governance-safe way to preserve **optionality** in case non-closure measures underperform.
- The union appears more open than the works council to a genuinely binding phased process, creating a possible internal labour-side nuance.
- The state shareholder’s position is strongest when framed as regional-industrial stewardship, not just labour solidarity; it wants a binding no-closure framework, but only if it is backed by serious quantified adjustment.
- A viable compromise likely depends less on proclaiming principles than on constructing an enforcement architecture that makes either side trust the package’s durability.
