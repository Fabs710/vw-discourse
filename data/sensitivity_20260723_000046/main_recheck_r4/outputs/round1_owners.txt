I will be direct.

I agree with management on the diagnosis: **the current cost base and capacity are not sustainable** if the company is to remain competitive over the long term. A durable margin recovery is not optional. If we avoid hard choices now, we risk a slower erosion of the brand, lower investment capacity, and ultimately worse outcomes for everyone, including the workforce and the regions.

At the same time, I also recognize that **a brute-force solution built only around closures and compulsory redundancies would be highly destructive**. It would risk industrial conflict, political backlash, and long-term damage to the enterprise. So I am not endorsing any simplistic “cut at all costs” approach.

Where I differ from the employee representatives and the state shareholder is on the premise that **no plant closures and no compulsory redundancies can be absolute lines**. I understand why those lines are drawn, but from an ownership perspective they are not automatically compatible with restoring competitiveness. If an alternative package can achieve the same structural effect, then that is preferable. But if it cannot, then the company cannot rule out structural measures that are necessary to protect the whole enterprise.

That said, I am also not prepared to support a plan that **permanently weakens the company’s industrial footprint or governance stability**. I do not want a value-destroying conflict, and I do not want a solution that creates open-ended unrest or damages the company’s reputation as a long-term employer and national industrial asset.

So my position is:

- **Yes** to a credible restructuring that restores margins and competitiveness.
- **Yes** to serious burden-sharing from all sides, including labor and management.
- **Yes** to alternatives to closures if they deliver real, durable savings.
- **No** to cosmetic measures that preserve every structure while leaving the economics unchanged.
- **No** to any outcome that permanently impairs enterprise value or undermines governance control.

To the works council and union: if you want me to support avoiding closures, then you need to show how the savings gap is closed in a way that is not just temporary deferral. That means real productivity gains, meaningful labor-cost adjustment, and operational flexibility that is durable enough to matter.

To management: if you want backing for deeper restructuring, you need to demonstrate discipline, proportionality, and a plan that preserves stability. A deal that triggers prolonged conflict or damages the company’s social license is not a success.

To the state shareholder: I share the concern about regional employment and industrial spillovers, but the company cannot be frozen in its current structure simply because it is politically difficult to change. The state’s interest is not served by preserving capacity that steadily loses competitiveness.

In short: I am open to an agreement, but only if it is **credible on competitiveness and stable in execution**. If the parties bring me a package that achieves that without unnecessary damage, I will support it. If not, I will not endorse a settlement that merely postpones the reckoning.
