I will speak plainly.

### To management
You are right that the company needs durable savings and that a cosmetic deal would be irresponsible. I am **not** disputing the competitiveness problem.

What I **do** dispute is your repeated framing that closures and compulsory redundancies remain normal bargaining options. For the workforce, that is not a neutral “tool”; it is the line where trust breaks and conflict becomes unavoidable.

So let me be equally clear:

- **No German plant closures.**
- **No compulsory redundancies.**
- **No dismantling of the collective wage framework.**

If your restructuring can only work by destroying sites and forcing people out, then the plan is not balanced enough. It is a management failure to present a credible alternative.

That said, I am not rejecting restructuring. I am ready to negotiate a **serious shared-sacrifice package** that includes:
- working-time flexibility,
- capacity reduction without closures,
- voluntary exit and transfer programs,
- retraining and redeployment,
- temporary employee contributions,
- and productivity measures.

But this only works if management also contributes, for example through:
- management restraint,
- postponement or reduction of payouts at the top,
- clear investment commitments,
- and binding guarantees that the burden is not pushed entirely onto labor.

### To the controlling family and investors
You keep talking about durability and value. I agree that social conflict destroys value. But so does a restructuring that is perceived as a one-sided attack on workers.

You cannot ask the workforce to “share sacrifice” while keeping the threat of closure and forced redundancies in your back pocket. That is not shared sacrifice. That is coercion.

If you want a stable settlement, then support a package that:
- keeps German plants open,
- avoids compulsory redundancies,
- and makes the burden shared across management, owners, and employees.

I will not endorse a deal where workers pay to finance their own displacement.

### To the state shareholder
Your rejection of closures and forced layoffs is correct, and I appreciate that you are not hiding behind platitudes. But a clear stance also means using your leverage.

If the state shareholder wants to defend regional industrial capacity, then it should insist on:
- binding site protection,
- voluntary-only staffing reductions,
- real investment commitments,
- and no erosion of co-determination.

I am prepared to work on a concrete alternative, but only if the state helps ensure that “alternative” does not secretly mean “closure by another name.”

### On the core trade-off
Here is the trade-off I am willing to discuss:

- Employees can make a **temporary, fair contribution** to help stabilize the company.
- In return, management and owners must **rule out closures and forced redundancies**, and must accept real burden-sharing themselves.
- The restructuring must protect the industrial base and maintain the collective bargaining framework.

That is the only basis on which a settlement can be durable.

### What I need to see next
If we are serious, then the next step is not more general speeches. It is a **quantified options paper** with:
1. the exact savings target,
2. the exact non-closure measures,
3. the exact employee contribution,
4. the exact management and owner contribution,
5. binding job and site protection through 2030,
6. and implementation rules that preserve co-determination.

### What will not move us
What will not move us is the argument that closures are “still on the table if needed.” That is precisely what makes the workforce distrust the process. If you want a settlement, stop treating the workforce as the adjustable shock absorber.

So my position is unchanged:
- no closures,
- no compulsory layoffs,
- fair burden-sharing,
- and a durable settlement that protects jobs and the collective framework.

If that is not possible for management and ownership, then the conflict will continue.
