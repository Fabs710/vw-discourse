I will be equally direct in round 4.

### To the Works Council
I agree with your core red lines:
- no German plant closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- no one-sided burden on the workforce,
- no weakening of co-determination.

That part is non-negotiable for me as well.

Where I do **not** fully follow you is your rejection of any phased or conditional process. A settlement must be durable, not just declaratory. If we simply write “no closures” and stop there, without quantified internal measures, then we will not have solved the economic problem — we will only have postponed the next confrontation.

What I cannot accept is a setup in which employees contribute immediately while management keeps a hidden exit hatch. So if a phase-based model is discussed, it must be **genuinely binding**:
- no closures in the agreed period,
- no compulsory redundancies in that period,
- clear internal savings measures now,
- and a jointly governed verification of whether the package is actually delivering.

But I agree with you on the key point: a “review later” clause must **not** become a backdoor to break the deal.

### To management
You keep returning to “structural overcapacity” and “economics first.” I hear the diagnosis. I do **not** accept the conclusion that closures and forced layoffs must remain normal bargaining instruments.

If your restructuring case only works by threatening plants and jobs, then you are not presenting a balanced transformation strategy. You are asking labor to absorb the risk while capital keeps full optionality.

That is precisely what will trigger industrial conflict.

You asked for quantified non-closure alternatives. Fine — but then you must be concrete too. Put the full savings gap on the table and separate:
1. what can be achieved through working-time and capacity tools,
2. what can be achieved through management restraint,
3. what owners are prepared to contribute,
4. what investment commitments are real,
5. and only then what remains.

Do **not** start from closure and then call everything else “insufficient” by definition.

### To the controlling family
I agree that the company needs a durable solution and that symbolic compromises will not work. I also agree that the enterprise must remain competitive.

But I disagree strongly with your assumption that credibility requires keeping closures and compulsory redundancies available as a backstop. That is exactly the kind of logic that destroys trust and makes a settlement unstable.

If you want value preservation, then value the workforce as part of the company’s productive core, not as a cost block to be trimmed until the numbers look better.

I can support burden-sharing, but only if it is genuinely shared:
- labor contributes,
- management contributes,
- owners contribute,
- and the employment framework is protected.

### To institutional investors
You are right about one thing: a cosmetic compromise would be value-destructive. We are not proposing that.

But you are wrong if you believe the only credible discipline comes from keeping closures on the table. That may look tidy from a spreadsheet, but it ignores conflict costs, reputational damage, and the long-term loss of industrial capability.

A deal that preserves sites and avoids forced layoffs can still be economically serious if it includes:
- verifiable capacity reduction,
- productivity gains,
- working-time flexibility,
- temporary employee contributions,
- management restraint,
- and real capital-side sacrifice.

What I will not support is a package where workers pay for a “solution” that simply transfers the crisis onto them.

### To the state shareholder
Your role matters here. If you want to defend the regional industrial base, then do not allow the discussion to be framed as “either accept closures later or accept irresponsibility now.”

That is a false choice.

Use your blocking position to insist on a real alternative:
- no closures in the agreed framework,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- measurable internal restructuring,
- investment in future products,
- and a fair distribution of sacrifice.

### On the actual bargaining substance
Let me be very concrete about what could move us:

We are prepared to negotiate a package with:
- working-time flexibility,
- internal capacity reduction,
- voluntary exits only,
- retraining and redeployment,
- temporary employee contribution,
- productivity measures,
- and management restraint.

But the package must also include:
1. **a binding no-closure commitment for the restructuring period,**
2. **no compulsory redundancies,**
3. **protection of the collective wage framework,**
4. **a fair contribution from management and owners,**
5. **and investment commitments that are not just promises on paper.**

That is the basis for any durable settlement.

### What we reject
We reject:
- closures “if needed” as a default bargaining threat,
- compulsory redundancies “if alternatives fail,”
- review clauses that are really exit ramps,
- and any arrangement that makes labor bear all downside risk.

### Bottom line
I am not moving from the core position:
- protect jobs,
- protect the collective agreement,
- avoid forced layoffs,
- and achieve a durable restructuring through shared sacrifice.

If management and ownership want a real agreement, they need to stop treating closure as a normal tool and instead commit to a binding non-destructive restructuring path. If they refuse that, then they should not expect the union to smooth the path for cuts that workers alone must pay for.
