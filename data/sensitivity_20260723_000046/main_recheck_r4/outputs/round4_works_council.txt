### To management
You keep saying the cost base is too high and that closures must remain available if savings fall short. That is exactly the logic we reject.

You are treating **industrial destruction** as a normal planning tool. We do not accept that. If your restructuring case only works when you hold plant closures and compulsory redundancies over the workforce, then the plan is not a restructuring plan — it is a blackmail instrument.

You have not answered the core question:
- What exact savings gap remains **after** you fully use internal tools, management restraint, owner contributions, and investment commitments?

Until you answer that with real numbers, there is no basis for talking about closures at all. And let me say this plainly: **there will be no German plant closures and no compulsory redundancies.**

I agree with you completely on the red lines:

- no dismantling of job security,

I also agree that the company needs a serious alternative package and that we must not hide behind slogans.

### To the state shareholder
You are right that the company needs durability and that social conflict is damaging. But durability does not come from threatening the workforce. It comes from trust, investment, and binding commitments.

If you really mean to defend the regional industrial base, then use your blocking position to **rule out closures and forced redundancies**, not to invite them back through a review mechanism. A review gate is just a dressed-up exit ramp for management.

I expect the state shareholder to back:
- every German plant remaining open,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- meaningful investment commitments,
- and a negotiated employee contribution only if it is matched by management and owner-side sacrifice.

### To the controlling family and investors
You both keep saying you need an economically credible solution. Fine. Then stop assuming that credibility requires the right to close plants.

That assumption is exactly what destroys trust and triggers resistance. A workforce that has been betrayed by the breaking of job-security guarantees will not accept a “trust us, we may close later” framework.

If you want a durable settlement, then commit to:
- all German plants staying open,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- no erosion of collective protections,
- and real burden-sharing from ownership and top management.

If you refuse that, then you are not asking for a plan. You are asking for submission.

### On the proposed “phased” compromise
I reject it.

Why? Because it does not remove the central threat. It merely says:
1. accept internal cuts now,
2. accept management’s right to reopen closure talks later.

That is not security. That is a staged attack.

And let us be honest: once you normalize the idea that a plant can be “reviewed” for closure later, the workforce is never really secure. Investment, morale, supplier confidence, and regional planning all suffer immediately.

### On internal measures
We are not refusing restructuring. We are refusing **destructive restructuring**.

We are prepared to discuss, in a serious quantified package:
- working-time flexibility,
- capacity reduction through internal tools,
- voluntary measures,
- redeployment,
- retraining,
- productivity improvements,
- management restraint,
- and owner-side contributions.

But these measures must operate **within a binding framework of site and job security**. Not as a probation period before closure.

Not “where the gap remains after we preserve the right to close,” but the actual non-destructive package.

Then we can assess whether management is serious about alternatives or simply dressing up an ultimatum.
