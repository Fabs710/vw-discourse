I will be equally direct.

## My position
I support restructuring. I do **not** support using the restructuring to normalize plant closures or compulsory redundancies in our state before the alternatives have been rigorously tested and the numbers made transparent.

The company must become competitive again. But it must do so in a way that preserves its industrial base, protects regional employment, and respects co-determination. Those are not sentimental add-ons; they are part of the company’s legitimacy and long-term stability.

## Where I agree
I agree with several points raised by others:

- **Management** is right that doing nothing is not an option.
- **Investors** are right that the restructuring must be quantified and durable.
- **The controlling family** is right that cosmetic compromise would just postpone the problem.
- **The union and works council** are right that employees should not carry the full burden alone.
- **The expert** is right that the burden of proof is still on management to show the residual gap site by site.

On that last point: yes, we need hard numbers. We need a plant-by-plant operating case, not slogans.

## Where I disagree
### To management
You are still treating closures and compulsory redundancies as if they must remain a live fallback from the outset. I reject that as the starting point.

If you want us to consider anything beyond voluntary and internal measures, then first show:
- the exact savings gap,
- site-by-site fixed and variable costs,
- utilization and break-even volumes,
- logistics and complexity costs,
- what voluntary exits and redeployment achieve,
- what management itself is contributing,
- what the owners are contributing,
- and what remains after all that.

Until then, “we may need closures” is not a business case. It is leverage.

### To the controlling family
I understand your insistence on value and durability. But stewardship is not served by making closure a permanent governance instrument before alternatives are exhausted.

If you want long-term enterprise value, then you need:
- binding investment commitments,
- a real owner contribution,
- and support for a restructuring that preserves viable sites wherever possible.

A framework that tells the workforce that jobs and sites are always contingent will destroy trust faster than it restores margins.

### To institutional investors
You are right that the package must be credible. But credibility does **not** mean accepting plant closures as an automatic right. It means proving the economics.

If a site-preserving package closes the gap, then that is the credible solution. If it does not, then management must prove that with data, not force the issue through threat.

### To the union and works council
I support your insistence that workers should not pay alone, and I support a real site-preserving test first.

Where I stop is at absolute formulas. I cannot endorse a permanent principle of “no closure, no compulsory redundancy under any circumstances,” because I have a duty to the company’s long-term health as well as to the region. But that does **not** mean closures should be presumed or front-loaded. It means they must be justified, not assumed.

## On the mediator’s proposals
### 1) Time-limited moratorium + independent plant audit
This is the right first step, and I would support it, provided it is real and not cosmetic.

For me, “real” means:
- no closure announcements during the moratorium,
- no compulsory redundancy triggers during the moratorium,
- no parallel pressure campaign by management,
- and a fixed deadline for the independent audit.

That gives the workforce and the state a proper alternatives test, while giving management and investors the quantified basis they say they need.

### 2) Alternatives first with quantified residual-gap trigger
This is the most sensible sequence so far.

The order must be:
1. voluntary exits and retirement,
2. redeployment and internal capacity measures,
3. working-time and productivity changes,
4. temporary labor-cost contribution,
5. owner and management contributions,
6. then an honest assessment of any residual gap.

Only if a documented residual gap remains should deeper measures even be discussed. And even then, the discussion must be site-specific, not a blanket license.

### 3) Site preservation for viable plants, controlled restructuring for non-viable ones
This is the only framework I see as potentially workable.

But the classification must be based on hard evidence, not bargaining positions. No plant should be labeled “non-viable” until the audit proves:
- current economics,
- improvement potential,
- and the limits of alternatives.

That is the standard I will insist on.

## What I need next
I want the independent audit now — and not a management-friendly summary, but a genuine plant-by-plant restructuring and valuation analysis covering:

- fixed and variable costs by site,
- utilization rates,
- break-even volumes,
- logistics and complexity costs,
- model allocation,
- capex needs,
- savings from each proposed measure,
- and the residual gap after alternatives.

## My bottom line
I am prepared to back a restructuring that includes:
- voluntary exits,
- early retirement,
- redeployment,
- working-time flexibility,
- productivity improvements,
- temporary labor-cost moderation,
- and binding management/owner contributions,

if and only if it is paired with:
- a genuine independent audit,
- a real alternatives-first sequence,
- and protection of viable sites and regional employment.

What I will **not** support is:
- a blanket closure-first logic,
- a blanket acceptance of compulsory redundancies,
- or a package that asks the region to absorb industrial loss before the numbers justify it.

We need a durable solution, not a fast one. And durable means economically sound **and** socially legitimate.
