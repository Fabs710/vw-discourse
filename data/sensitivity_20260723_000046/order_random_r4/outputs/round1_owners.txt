We agree on one point: this cannot be a one-sided workforce solution. If the burden falls only on employees while the company, management, and owners remain insulated, that will not be acceptable and it will not be stable.

That said, I need to be equally clear: **doing nothing structural is not an option**, and neither is a settlement that preserves every site, every guarantee, and every legacy constraint if the company’s cost base remains uncompetitive. The brand’s margins are under real pressure, and long-term value will erode if we protect structures that the market no longer supports.

So my position is:

- **Yes** to a credible competitiveness program that restores margins and preserves the industrial base over the long term.
- **Yes** to negotiated solutions that avoid unnecessary disruption.
- **No** to an outcome that simply postpones the problem.
- **No** to a package that leaves the company trapped with an unaffordable German cost structure.

On your specific points:

1. **No compulsory redundancies / no plant closures**  
   I understand why this is your opening line, but I cannot endorse it as an absolute. A permanent guarantee that rules out closures or workforce reductions regardless of business reality would handcuff the company. If sites are structurally unviable, we cannot pretend otherwise. The question is whether there is a way to achieve the necessary savings without immediate closures — but that requires real concessions and measurable results, not just declarations.

2. **No destruction of the collective wage framework**  
   I am not seeking gratuitous confrontation over labour standards. But if legacy pay rules and guarantees are materially out of line with competitiveness, they have to be revisited. There can be protection of core bargaining standards, but not preservation of every historic privilege.

3. **No workforce paying the whole burden**  
   On this we agree. Management must contribute, and owners must support a credible turnaround. But contribution from owners is not the same as accepting permanent value destruction. We can support investment, bridge measures, and a disciplined restructuring plan. What we cannot support is an arrangement that spends capital without fixing the structure.

What I would consider a serious basis for discussion is this:

- a **durable cost-reduction package**
- **capacity adjustments** that are economically justified
- **job reductions mainly through attrition, redeployment, retirement, and voluntary measures where possible**
- a **negotiated labour-cost contribution**
- **binding productivity and investment commitments**
- and above all, a plan that is credible enough to restore competitiveness, not merely delay a larger crisis

So I am not joining the “no closures, no redundancies, no sacrifice” position as a fixed rule. I am open to a socially responsible solution, but only if it produces real structural improvement. If the package does not materially change the economics, then it is not a solution — it is an expensive delay.
