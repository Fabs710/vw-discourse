## 2) Minimum data needed for a real plant-by-plant viability test

For each German plant, I would require the following schedules:

### A. Operating base
- Annual output, by model and program
- Installed capacity
- Capacity utilization, current and projected
- Shift pattern and effective operating hours
- Headcount by function: direct labor, indirect labor, maintenance, logistics, overhead

### B. Cost structure
- Direct labor cost per vehicle
- Indirect labor and site overhead per vehicle
- Energy, maintenance, and consumables
- Logistics in/out and internal transport cost
- Scrap, rework, and quality cost
- Allocated corporate overhead, separately shown and not mixed with plant economics

### C. Break-even analysis
- Fixed cost base by site
- Contribution margin per vehicle
- Break-even volume at current and post-restructuring cost levels
- Sensitivity to volume changes, especially a 10%, 20%, and 30% downside case

### D. Investment needs
- Capex needed to keep the site technically competitive over 3–5 years
- Capex needed for EV or platform conversion
- Maintenance capex to avoid underinvestment
- Cost of delay if investment is deferred

### E. Alternatives savings bridge
For each measure, quantify annual savings:
- voluntary exits
- early retirement
- redeployment
- working-time reduction
- wage moderation / temporary labour contribution
- productivity improvement
- management overhead reduction
- purchasing/logistics simplification
- product allocation changes

### F. Residual gap
- Savings remaining after all feasible alternatives
- Time profile of savings: immediate, 12 months, 24 months
- One-off restructuring charges
- Payback period

Without these items, closure claims are still policy assertions, not audited economics.

## 3) Decision threshold: when a site becomes economically hard to defend

A plant becomes difficult to preserve if most of the following are true:
- utilization remains structurally low even after product allocation changes;
- break-even volume is materially above credible forecast demand;
- the site requires substantial capex just to remain competitive;
- logistics or complexity costs are structurally high and not fixable;
- labor cost per vehicle remains well above peer benchmarks even after concessions;
- the residual gap after realistic measures is too large to be closed by non-destructive means.

By contrast, a site is defensible if:
- utilization can be lifted materially;
- fixed-cost absorption improves;
- break-even volume falls into a range supportable by the allocated product plan;
- capex need is manageable;
- and the residual gap is narrow enough that labor and operational measures can plausibly close it.

I would place plants into four bins:

### 1. Clearly viable
- Positive contribution after restructuring
- Manageable capex

### 2. Viable with measures

  - labor contribution
  - working-time changes
  - overhead cuts
  - volume allocation
  - modest capex
- Preserve, but monitor milestones

### 3. Borderline / conditional
- Viability depends on:
  - product allocation
  - future platform assignment
  - higher utilization
  - specific capex
- Preserve only if conditions are contractually and operationally credible

### 4. Structurally non-viable
- Persistent losses or very weak contribution even after realistic measures
- High capex with poor payback
- Break-even volume too high
- Limited operational route to competitiveness
- These sites are the only ones where closure or major downsizing becomes economically plausible

## 5) On closures versus downsizing

From a restructuring-valuation standpoint, closure is not the first question. The first question is:

**Can the site be made to contribute positively after realistic restructuring?**

If yes, closure is usually value-destructive unless there is a very strong alternative use of capital and volume elsewhere.

If no, then the next question is whether:
- downsizing,
- line consolidation,
- partial closure,
- or full exit

produces the least value destruction.

Often, **partial downsizing** is economically more efficient than full closure if:
- a plant has some good programs and some weak ones;
- the site can be simplified;
- fixed cost can be cut materially without losing all strategic value.

Full closure becomes more likely when:
- the plant is too small,
- too complex,
- too capex-intensive,
- or too far from competitive break-even.

## 7) My valuation lens on the dispute

From a valuation perspective:
- A credible restructuring package should improve **enterprise value** by lowering fixed cost, reducing overcapacity drag, and improving margin durability.
- A settlement that avoids all hard decisions but preserves legacy cost structures can raise short-term labor peace but **depress long-term value**.
- Conversely, premature closures can destroy option value if the plants are not truly non-viable.

Therefore the right valuation test is:
1. measure each site’s standalone and post-restructuring contribution,
2. compare the NPV of:
   - preserve and invest
   - preserve with downsizing
   - partial exit
   - full closure
3. choose the option with the best durable value, not the easiest political outcome.
