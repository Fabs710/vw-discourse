We are now past the stage of generalities. Let me respond directly.

## Where I agree

I agree on three points:

- **Doing nothing is not an option.**
- **Employees should not carry the burden alone.**
- **A negotiated solution is preferable to a strike spiral.**

I also agree that the restructuring must be **durable**, not cosmetic. If we do not materially change the cost base and capacity, we will simply be back here in a worse position.

## Where I disagree

### 1) “No plant closures” cannot be a fixed rule
To the union, works council, and state government: I understand why you want to defend sites. But I cannot accept a principle that rules out closures before we have even looked at the economics.

If a plant is structurally uncompetitive, then keeping it open is not a social solution — it is postponing the problem and forcing the rest of the company to subsidise overcapacity. That weakens the whole group and ultimately puts *more* jobs at risk.

We are not talking about closures for their own sake. We are talking about a **structural correction** where the current footprint is too large for the market we now face.

### 2) “No compulsory redundancies” is not acceptable as an absolute
I prefer voluntary measures, redeployment, retirement schemes, and phased exits wherever possible. But I will not bind the company to a rule that makes necessary workforce reductions impossible if voluntary tools do not deliver enough.

If we need a real reset, then the company must retain the ability to act. Otherwise the savings will not be credible, and neither will the turnaround.

### 3) Capacity reduction without hard consequences is not enough
Several of you keep pointing to working-time changes, flexibility, and redeployment. Those can help, and I am willing to discuss them. But by themselves they do **not** yet answer the core problem:

- German factory costs are too high.
- Our European market is shrinking.
- Competition is intensifying.
- The brand is missing its return-on-sales target.

That means we need a package that actually reduces:
- **capacity**
- **headcount**
- **labour cost**
- and therefore the long-term cost base

Not just a package that sounds balanced.

### 4) The governance veto does not solve the business problem
To the state government and the controlling family: co-determination and governance rights are real, and I respect them. But they do not change the economics. They cannot be used to freeze the company in an unaffordable structure.

If the board is serious about protecting long-term value, then it has to confront the cost gap head-on. We need a package that protects the company’s future, not one that preserves every legacy constraint.

## What I need to hear

If the employee side wants me to rule out closures and forced reductions, then it must put something equally concrete on the table:

- a **quantified labour-cost contribution**
- **material productivity gains**
- a **meaningful capacity reduction**
- binding implementation timing
- and a package large enough to restore competitiveness through 2030

That is the test. Not statements of principle.

## On the state government
You say closures are not a default option. Fine — I am not saying they are the default. I am saying they must remain available if the numbers require it. If you want me to explore a site-preserving solution, then show me how it delivers the same savings at the same scale. So far, I have not heard that.

## On the union and works council
You are right that employees should not pay the whole bill. But “shared sacrifice” cannot mean that management and owners make symbolic contributions while the workforce is asked to preserve everything else. That will not close the gap.

If you want to avoid closures, then the alternative must be:
- credible,
- quantified,
- durable,
- and economically equivalent.

Anything less is a delay.

## On the controlling family and investors
I appreciate the support for real restructuring. But let me be equally clear: if we start watering this down into a purely site-preserving exercise, the market will treat it as a cosmetic compromise. That would damage confidence, investment capacity, and the brand’s future.

## Bottom line
I am prepared to discuss:
- voluntary exits,
- working-time flexibility,
- redeployment,
- productivity measures,
- investment commitments,
- and a negotiated labour contribution.

But I am **not** prepared to accept:
- a blanket ban on closures,
- a blanket ban on compulsory redundancies,
- or a package that leaves the German cost base structurally uncompetitive.

If the other parties want a real settlement, they need to help build a package that actually restores competitiveness. If not, then we are not restructuring — we are simply delaying the inevitable.
