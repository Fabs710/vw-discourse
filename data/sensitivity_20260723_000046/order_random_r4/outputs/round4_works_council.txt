I’ll be blunt: I welcome the audit and the numbers, but I do **not** accept the repeated attempt to keep plant closures and compulsory redundancies as a standing fallback. That is not “responsible flexibility” from our point of view. It is a way of leaving the workforce under permanent threat while asking us to concede first and trust later.

## Where I agree
I agree with the expert, the state government, and even parts of management on one point: **we need a quantified, plant-by-plant basis for any restructuring discussion**.  
I also agree that:
- doing nothing is not an option,
- employees cannot be asked to carry the full burden alone,
- voluntary exits, retirement, redeployment, working-time flexibility, and productivity measures belong on the table,
- and a cosmetic deal would help no one.

That is the shared starting point. But it is not where the deal ends.

## Where I disagree

### To management
You keep saying the company has too much capacity and too high a cost base. Fine. Then **prove, plant by plant, that your alternatives are insufficient**. Until you do that, closure is not an economic necessity; it is your preferred threat.

And let me be very clear: **I do not accept a restructuring model where the company first demands concessions and then keeps the right to close plants or force layoffs if those concessions are not enough.** That turns “negotiation” into extortion by timetable.

You want credibility? Then be credible about your own contribution:
- management restraint,
- overhead cuts at the top,
- investment commitments,
- product allocation,
- and binding guarantees.

Not just “flexibility” from workers.

### To the controlling family
You talk about enterprise value and stewardship. Stewardship means protecting the industrial base, not threatening it into submission.  
If you want our cooperation, then you need to back:
- binding site commitments,
- real investment in Germany,
- and a future product plan that secures work at the plants.

What you cannot expect is: workers give up security, and owners keep all strategic options open. That is not partnership. That is asymmetric risk transfer.

### To institutional investors
You demand “credibility” and “durable savings.” I agree with the standard, but not with your conclusion that closures must remain live from day one.  
A site-preserving package is credible **if it is built on measurable, enforceable measures** and if management and owners contribute materially. What is not credible is treating closures as the default proof of seriousness before the audit has even shown a residual gap.

Your logic still sounds like: if we cannot immediately extract enough from labor, then we must reserve the right to shut sites. That is not a neutral economic position. It is a power position.

### To the state government
I appreciate your support for a site-preserving alternatives test. But I need to say this plainly: **a test is only meaningful if it actually protects the sites unless and until management proves otherwise.**  
I do not want a “test” that becomes a procedural runway to the same closure decision later.

If you are serious about regional employment, co-determination, and industrial continuity, then use your blocking power to insist that:
- no closure is pursued before the alternatives are fully implemented and measured,
- and no forced layoffs are introduced as a shortcut.

### To the expert
Your assessment is useful because it says what we have said all along: **closures are not proven necessary yet.**  
You also say the burden is on management to show the residual gap after alternatives. Exactly. That is why I reject any attempt to make closure the default fallback before that proof exists.

But I also want to be equally clear: I do **not** accept the expert’s framing that closures merely remain “economically admissible” if a gap remains large. That may be a financial view. It is not an acceptable social settlement from the workforce side. We are not here to predetermine which German plant gets sacrificed if management cannot make the numbers work fast enough.

## On the facilitator’s proposals

### 1) Time-limited moratorium + independent plant audit
This is the only proposal that is even in the right direction. But it must be a **real moratorium**, not a pause with the closure gun still loaded on the table.

For us, real means:
- no closure announcements,
- no compulsory redundancy announcements,
- no side-channel pressure that one site is next,
- no bargaining with a hidden fallback.

And crucially: during the moratorium, the company must implement the alternatives seriously, not just wait for the audit to justify an outcome already chosen.

### 2) Alternatives first with a quantified residual-gap trigger
I can accept **alternatives first**. I cannot accept **alternatives first, closures later if management says so**.

If the trigger is simply “management decides the gap is still too large,” then the trigger is not objective. It is just a delayed threat.  
If there is to be any trigger at all, it must be tied to a genuine site-preserving implementation process, not to a pre-licensed exit route.

### 3) Site preservation for viable plants, controlled restructuring for non-viable ones
This is where I draw the line.

Who decides “non-viable”? Management? Investors? An auditor using management assumptions?  
I do **not** accept a classification that can become a polite label for closure. The only legitimate criterion is whether the site can be made viable **through internal measures, investment, product allocation, and fair burden-sharing**.

If the answer is yes, it stays open.  
If the answer is no, then management has not proven a labor problem — it has proven an industrial and strategic failure that must not be dumped onto the workforce.

## What we are prepared to do
We are prepared to discuss:
- temporary labour-cost moderation,
- working-time flexibility,
- redeployment,
- early retirement,
- voluntary exit programs,
- productivity improvements,
- product and investment commitments,

**provided that** they come with:
- binding job and site guarantees,
- preservation of co-determination,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- no plant closures,
- and protection of the collective framework.

That is not maximalism. That is the minimum acceptable basis for a legitimate restructuring.

## What we will not accept
Let me put this plainly so nobody can claim ambiguity later:
- no closures as a “last resort” dressed up as inevitability,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- no hidden fallback mechanism,
- no conditional guarantees that disappear when the numbers become inconvenient,
- no restructuring that uses the workforce as the adjustment reserve.

If management and owners need a reminder: the workforce has leverage here. We have already shown we are prepared to use it. We do not need lectures about realism from people who want flexibility only in one direction.

## [SUMMON: independent labour-market and plant-competitiveness auditor]
I want an independent auditor, not just a restructuring adviser, to test whether a **site-preserving package with voluntary measures, working-time changes, productivity gains, and owner/management contributions** can in fact close the savings gap **without** plant closures or compulsory redundancies.

That audit must answer:
- what the required savings are,
- what the alternatives actually deliver,
- what management and owners contribute,
- and whether any site is truly unsalvageable **after** all reasonable measures.

## Bottom line
Our position remains unchanged:
- **No plant closures**
- **No compulsory redundancies**
- **Binding job and site guarantees**
- **Binding management and owner contributions**
- **A genuine alternatives-first process under co-determination**

If the other sides want a durable settlement, they need to stop treating job destruction as a normal management instrument.  
If they insist on keeping closures and forced layoffs alive as bargaining leverage, then they should expect resistance — including industrial action — not consent.
