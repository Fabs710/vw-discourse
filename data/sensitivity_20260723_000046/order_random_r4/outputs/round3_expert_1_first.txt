From an industrial restructuring perspective, the key question is not whether closures are “desirable,” but whether management has shown that **site-preserving alternatives can close the economic gap with enough durability and speed**.

## My assessment

### 1) The burden of proof is still on management
Based on what has been described, management has asserted a structural cost and capacity problem, but I have not seen a defensible **site-by-site operating case** with:
- current fixed-cost burden by plant,
- contribution margin by model and site,
- utilization rates,
- incremental labor cost by site,
- logistics and complexity costs,
- and a quantified bridge from current economics to target economics.

Without that, claims that closures are “necessary” are not yet proven. In restructuring work, closure is a last-resort tool when the gap is persistent and alternatives do not close it. It should not be treated as a rhetorical default.

### 2) A “no closures / no compulsory redundancies” package can work only if the savings gap is modest
A site-preserving deal can be credible if the required adjustment is mainly:
- labor-cost moderation,
- productivity improvement,
- working-time flexibility,
- internal redeployment,
- voluntary exits and retirement,
- SKU/platform rationalization,
- and some capacity reduction via shift or line changes rather than plant shutdowns.

But if the company truly has **material overcapacity plus a structurally high German cost base**, then alternatives alone usually struggle to deliver the full adjustment. In that case, the likely savings gap is too large to close with concessions only, unless the company is willing to accept a very large employee contribution and still preserve enough volume to keep the sites efficient.

### 3) The decisive issue is the size of the savings gap
A credible restructuring decision depends on three numbers:
1. **Required annual savings** to restore target margin.
2. **Savings achievable without closures**.
3. **Residual gap after voluntary and internal measures**.

If the residual gap is small enough, then a site-preserving package is feasible. If it remains large, then management’s fallback to closures has economic logic.

What I cannot say from the information provided is the actual size of that gap. That would require:
- plant-level P&Ls,
- headcount and labor-hour data,
- planned volume by site,
- product allocation,
- utilization assumptions,
- and the cost of each proposed measure.

### 4) Alternatives are feasible, but only up to a point
The alternatives mentioned are all standard restructuring tools and can be effective:
- voluntary exits / retirement bridges,
- redeployment,
- working-time reductions,
- temporary pay moderation,
- productivity programs,
- investment commitments tied to flexibility.

However, these measures have limits:
- they are slower to realize than closures,
- they depend on continued volume,
- they often produce partial rather than structural savings,
- and they can be eroded if demand weakens further.

So yes, they are feasible. But whether they are sufficient is a quantitative question, not a moral one.

### 5) Plant closures become rational only under certain conditions
Closure is economically defensible when a plant has one or more of the following:
- chronically low utilization,
- high fixed-cost absorption,
- unfavorable logistics,
- weak model allocation prospects,
- excessive retooling needs,
- persistent labor-cost disadvantage that concessions cannot offset,
- or duplication that cannot be removed otherwise.

If none of those conditions are severe, then closure is harder to justify. If several are present, then preserving the site may simply postpone the restructuring.

## Practical conclusion
My neutral assessment is:

- **A site-preserving alternative is plausible and should be tested rigorously first.**
- **It is not yet proven that closures are unavoidable.**
- **But if management’s quantified gap is large, alternatives alone are unlikely to be sufficient.**
- **The right sequence is to force a transparent site-by-site economics case before anyone treats closures or compulsory redundancies as inevitable.**

## What data is needed to form a defensible view
To validate the economics, I would need:
- site-by-site fixed and variable cost structure,
- utilization and break-even volumes,
- labor cost per vehicle by plant,
- logistics and complexity costs,
- product allocation and platform timing,
- capex required to keep sites competitive,
- quantified savings from each restructuring lever,
- and the time profile of those savings.
