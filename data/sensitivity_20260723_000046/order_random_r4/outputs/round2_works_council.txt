Let me respond directly and without euphemisms.

## To management
You still have **not** shown that closures or compulsory redundancies are unavoidable. You keep repeating “competitiveness” and “structural mismatch,” but that is not a business case — it is a conclusion. If you want the workforce to accept painful measures, then put the numbers on the table:

- exact cost gap by site,
- exact savings from each proposed measure,
- what has already been exhausted,
- and why a site-preserving package cannot deliver the same effect.

Until then, your closure talk looks like a lever to force concessions, not an unavoidable necessity. We reject that.

## To the controlling family
You say you do not want a one-sided workforce solution. Fine. Then stop supporting a framework that makes jobs the easy target. A real owner contribution means more than bridge financing or vague “support for a turnaround.” It means:

- binding investment commitments,
- protection of German sites,
- and a willingness to back a social restructuring that does **not** destroy the workforce base.

I also reject your framing that site guarantees are somehow a “legacy privilege.” These are the foundation of trust in this company. Breaking them would not create value; it would destroy it.

## To the state government
You are right to oppose closures as a default tool. But I want to be even clearer: the co-determination and special governance rights are not decorative. They exist precisely to prevent a purely financial logic from tearing apart industrial capacity and jobs.

I expect you to use your blocking position accordingly. If management tries to force a fait accompli, then the answer should be no.

## To the institutional investors
You keep demanding a “credible, quantified path” and insisting closures must remain on the table. I hear the market logic. I reject the idea that the only credible path is one that puts plants and jobs on the chopping block.

A site-preserving alternative is not “cosmetic” if it actually delivers savings through:
- working-time adjustments,
- internal redeployment,
- voluntary exits,
- retirement schemes,
- productivity improvements,
- and a fair negotiated labour contribution matched by management and owners.

What is cosmetic is pretending that closure is the only serious instrument before the alternatives have been fully developed. You have not shown that.

## To the union
We are aligned on the essentials:
- no compulsory redundancies,
- no plant closures,
- no one-sided burden on employees,
- protection of the collective framework.

I support your insistence on a fair shared package, and I will say openly: if management wants concessions, they must be matched by **binding** commitments on sites, jobs, and investment. Not promises. Binding commitments.

## My position
The works council will not accept:
- plant closures in Germany,
- forced layoffs,
- tearing up job-security guarantees,
- or a restructuring that shifts the crisis onto the workforce.

What we **can** discuss is a serious alternative package that keeps all German plants open and preserves co-determination, including:

- capacity reductions without closures,
- voluntary exit programmes,
- early retirement,
- internal redeployment,
- working-time flexibility,
- productivity measures,
- and a negotiated employee contribution only if management and owners contribute equally and visibly.

## The core point
If management’s answer is “closures first, layoffs first, employees pay,” then there is no agreement.

If the company wants a future in Germany, then that future must be built on:
- **sites kept open,**
- **jobs protected,**
- **co-determination respected,**
- and **burden-sharing that is genuinely fair.**

Anything less is not a restructuring worth supporting. It is an assault on the workforce dressed up as strategy.
