We are at the point where the discussion must be judged against one question only: **does the package restore competitiveness in a durable way, or does it merely postpone the crisis while preserving all the structural weaknesses?**

## Where I agree

I agree with the union, works council, state government, management, and investors on the basic premise that:
- doing nothing is not an option,
- the company needs restructuring,
- employees should not carry the burden alone,
- and a negotiated solution is preferable to a strike spiral.

I also agree that a one-sided solution would be unstable and value-destructive. From an ownership perspective, prolonged conflict is not acceptable. It destroys margins, trust, and ultimately the enterprise value we are responsible for stewarding.

## Where I disagree

### 1) “No closures” as a fixed principle
I cannot accept that. Not because closures are desirable, but because **an absolute ban on closures is economically irresponsible** if a site is structurally uncompetitive.

The union and works council are asking for a guarantee that no site can ever be challenged, regardless of the numbers. I understand the industrial logic of that position, but I do not accept it as a governance principle. If a plant cannot compete even after alternatives are exhausted, then preserving it by force is not social responsibility — it is value erosion.

### 2) “No compulsory redundancies” as an absolute
I prefer voluntary exits, retirement, redeployment, and phased reductions. That remains my first choice. But I will not endorse a formula that removes the company’s ability to act if those tools do not deliver enough.

A restructuring package must be credible to capital markets and to the long-term owner. If it contains a hard prohibition on all workforce reductions beyond voluntary measures, then I do not see how it can be durable unless the savings gap is trivially small — and the evidence so far suggests it is not.

### 3) A “review trigger” is not enough on its own
I appreciate the moderator’s attempt to create sequencing. But I share the concern that a review trigger can become either:
- a disguised pre-authorization for closure, or
- a disguised way to freeze the company in place.

Neither is good enough.

If we are to accept a pause, that pause must be tied to **real, measurable, and time-bound delivery**, not just more discussion. And if the package fails, the company must still retain the ability to make economically necessary decisions. Otherwise the process becomes a holding pattern for the status quo.

## To management
You still owe us the hard numbers in a form that can be governed properly:
- the exact savings gap,
- by site and by measure,
- what voluntary measures achieve,
- what productivity gains are realistic,
- what management contributes,
- and what the owner side contributes.

Without that, the debate is still too abstract. You cannot ask the board and shareholders to support painful measures without a quantified case. “Competitiveness” is not a magic word.

## To the union and works council
You are right that employees should not pay the full bill. I support that. But you are wrong if you think that means every site and every employment guarantee can be insulated from economic reality.

A site-preserving package is only credible if it actually delivers the required savings. If it does not, then it is not a solution; it is a delay. And if you refuse even to allow the possibility of deeper action later, then you are not bargaining over restructuring — you are trying to pre-decide the outcome.

I also want to say plainly: if the workforce insists that closure can never be contemplated under any circumstances, you should not expect the owners to sign away governance responsibility. We have a duty to the long-term health of the enterprise.

## To the state government
Your role matters, and I respect that. But your blocking power is not a tool to preserve every existing structure indefinitely. It is there to protect the long-term interest of the company and region.

That means you should not use it to prevent any economically necessary adjustment. At the same time, I agree that closures must not be the default starting point. They must be justified, not assumed. That is a real distinction, and it matters.

## To institutional investors
I hear your demand for credibility and quantified savings. That is legitimate. But I would caution against treating closure as the only marker of seriousness. A package can be serious without being maximalist, provided it is:
- quantified,
- durable,
- enforceable,
- and sufficient to restore margins.

If the site-preserving alternative actually closes the gap, then it deserves consideration. If it does not, then I will not support pretending otherwise.

## My position on the compromise proposals

### Proposal 1: “No immediate closure / no immediate compulsory redundancy”
This is acceptable only as a **temporary sequencing measure**, not as a substantive guarantee that removes all flexibility. I could support a time-limited implementation period if it is tied to:
- real cost reductions,
- measurable productivity gains,
- voluntary exits,
- redeployment,
- and a defined review point.

But I cannot support a formulation that makes later action impossible regardless of results.

### Proposal 2: Site-specific package with hard thresholds
This is the most reasonable approach on the table, but only if the thresholds are real and objective. I would support:
- site-by-site economics,
- transparent targets,
- and documented proof that alternatives have been tested.

What I will not support is a process where “site preservation” becomes an argument for ignoring economics entirely.

### Proposal 3: Burden-sharing matrix
Yes. This is essential. Any settlement must show that:
- employees contribute,
- management contributes,
- owners contribute,
- and the company becomes more competitive.

But burden-sharing is not real if it is paired with a structure that still leaves overcapacity untouched. The burden must buy a durable reset, not just a temporary truce.

## My bottom line
I am prepared to support a socially responsible restructuring if it:
- materially restores competitiveness,
- is quantified and time-bound,
- includes management and owner contributions,
- uses voluntary and internal measures first,
- and remains capable of decisive action if the plan fails.

I am **not** prepared to support:
- a blanket ban on closures,
- a blanket ban on compulsory redundancies,
- or a package that protects every legacy guarantee while leaving the cost base structurally uncompetitive.

That would not be stewardship. It would be a slow-motion destruction of value.

## What I need next
Before I move further, I want management to present a **site-by-site quantified restructuring case** and I want the employee side to state clearly:
- what level of labour-cost contribution they consider possible,
- which productivity measures they will actually implement,
- and what measurable savings would make a site-preserving solution credible.

If the answer is “no closures, no compulsory redundancies, regardless of the numbers,” then we are not close to a governance solution.
