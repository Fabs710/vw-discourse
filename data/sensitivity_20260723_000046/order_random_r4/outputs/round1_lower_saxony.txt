I agree with both of you on one central point: **the company needs a credible restructuring, and the burden cannot fall on employees alone**. If management and owners ask for sacrifices while staying untouched themselves, that will not be politically or socially sustainable.

But I need to be equally clear about where I draw the line.

### Where I agree
- **A pure “status quo” defense is not viable.** The company’s cost base and capacity have to be brought into line with the market.
- **A negotiated solution is preferable to escalation.** A lasting settlement is better than a cycle of strikes, confrontation, and uncertainty.
- **Employees can be part of the solution**, including through flexibility, productivity gains, and a fair contribution — but only if that is matched by management and ownership.

### Where I disagree
I **do not accept plant closures in the state as a default restructuring tool**. A closure is not just a line item; it destroys jobs, supplier networks, public confidence, and the regional industrial base. Once that is done, it is very hard to reverse. As a shareholder and as a regional government, I cannot support a path that treats sites as disposable when alternative measures have not been exhausted.

I also **will not accept a restructuring that sidelines the co-determination and veto role**. These governance rights are not a nuisance to be bypassed; they are part of the company’s stability architecture. Any attempt to force through an outcome by ignoring that reality will create a deeper conflict and more risk, not less.

### What I think is required
A serious solution has to be built around:
- **site security wherever economically possible**
- **capacity reduction without blanket closures**
- **job reduction primarily through voluntary, phased, and socially responsible means**
- **a negotiated employee contribution**
- **management restraint and owner support**
- **binding commitments on future investment and product allocation in Germany**

If management believes it needs savings, then it should first present a package that protects the industrial core and regional employment. That means using the full range of alternatives before closure: working-time adjustments, internal redeployment, voluntary exits, early retirement, investment shifts, and temporary capacity rationalisation.

### My bottom line
I am **open to painful restructuring**, but I am **not open to a solution that permanently weakens the company by gutting regional employment or turning closures into the first resort**. That would be economically shortsighted and politically unacceptable.

If the question is whether I would support a balanced package that restores competitiveness **while preserving sites and jobs as far as possible**, the answer is yes.  
If the question is whether I will back deep cuts that sacrifice the regional industrial base and the state’s co-determination role, the answer is no.
