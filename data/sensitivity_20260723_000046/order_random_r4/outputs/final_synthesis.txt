## 1. Decision context

The company faces a restructuring under severe economic pressure: overcapacity in Germany, high fixed costs, weak returns, and a shrinking European market. The live decision is not whether to restructure, but how far to go on plant closures, job cuts, wage concessions, and flexibility measures. Governance makes the choice unusually constrained: co-determination, family control, and state shareholding all create effective veto points, so any durable outcome must be both economically credible and politically survivable.

## 2. Stakeholder assembly

- **Group & Brand Management**: driving the restructuring and arguing for a structural cost reset.
- **Works Council**: plant-level employee representation with supervisory-board power, defending sites and jobs.
- **Industrial Union**: collective-bargaining counterpart with strike power, opposing closures and forced layoffs.
- **Controlling Family Holding**: controlling shareholder, focused on enterprise value and long-term stewardship.
- **Regional State Government**: anchor shareholder and political actor, protecting regional employment and co-determination.
- **Institutional Investors**: dispersed capital-market shareholders, prioritizing margin improvement and restructuring credibility.
- **Independent Expert**: plant-by-plant restructuring and valuation advisor, clarifying what the economics can actually support.

## 3. Final positions

### Group & Brand Management
Management insists the company has too much capacity and too high a fixed-cost base to restore competitiveness without hard structural measures. It supports a time-limited audit and first-wave alternatives, but refuses to preclude plant closures or compulsory redundancies if the residual gap remains material.

### Works Council
The works council accepts the need for restructuring and some employee contribution, but rejects plant closures and compulsory redundancies as acceptable fallback tools. It demands binding job and site guarantees, management and owner contributions, and a genuine alternatives-first process under co-determination.

### Industrial Union
The union is prepared to negotiate serious concessions on working time, redeployment, voluntary exits, and temporary labour-cost moderation. But it is explicitly unwilling to bargain in a framework where closures and forced layoffs remain a live weapon; it views that as coercive leverage rather than credible negotiation.

### Controlling Family Holding
The family supports restructuring and hard data, but will not endorse a framework that permanently removes closure or redundancy options. It is willing to back site-preserving solutions if they are proven to close the gap, but insists that economically necessary action must remain available.

### Regional State Government
The state supports restructuring, but only after rigorous alternatives testing and transparency. It wants to use its governance power to require a real site-preserving assessment first, while stopping short of a blanket prohibition on deeper restructuring if the evidence ultimately supports it.

### Institutional Investors
Investors want a durable margin reset, not a symbolic peace deal. They support an independent audit and staged alternatives, but oppose any blanket ban on closures or compulsory redundancies because they see those as necessary fallback options if the residual gap remains too large.

### Independent Expert
The expert concludes that restructuring is necessary, but that no defensible closure decision can yet be made without plant-by-plant operating data and a quantified residual gap after alternatives. The expert’s position is procedural rather than ideological: closures are not yet proven necessary, but they are not ruled out.

## 4. Genuine conflicts (structurally incompatible positions)

1. **Whether closures/forced redundancies may remain a live fallback now**
   - Labour and the state want them removed from the table until alternatives are fully tested.
   - Management, family, and investors want them preserved as credible options from the start.

2. **Who bears the burden of proof**
   - Labour argues management must first prove why alternatives are insufficient.
   - Management argues it cannot give up hard options before the numbers establish which sites are viable.

3. **Whether a moratorium is protection or delay**
   - Labour views a moratorium as meaningful only if it suspends closure pressure entirely.
   - Management and investors see it as acceptable only if it is temporary and without prejudice to later action.

4. **Meaning of “non-viable”**
   - Labour treats it as a potentially political label for preferred closures.
   - Management/investors treat it as an economic conclusion that must remain available.

5. **Absolute site protection vs. economic flexibility**
   - The labour side seeks binding job and site guarantees.
   - Capital and ownership sides reject any framework that would freeze structural adjustment regardless of economics.

## 5. Areas of convergence

- Restructuring is necessary; doing nothing is not an option.
- Employees should not carry the whole burden alone.
- Voluntary exits, retirement, redeployment, productivity gains, and working-time flexibility are acceptable tools.
- An independent, plant-by-plant audit is necessary.
- Management and owners must make visible contributions.
- Any settlement must be durable, not cosmetic.
- Site-by-site differentiation is preferable to an all-or-nothing fight over every plant.

## 6. Key trade-offs for the decision-maker

- **Short-term labour peace vs. long-term competitiveness**
  - A site-protective settlement may reduce conflict now but fail if it does not close the cost gap.

- **Economic credibility vs. social legitimacy**
  - Keeping closures on the table strengthens management’s bargaining position but undermines trust and increases strike risk.

- **Uniform treatment vs. differentiated site solutions**
  - A one-size-fits-all settlement is politically easier but economically weaker; site-specific outcomes are more defensible but harder to negotiate.

- **Flexibility vs. commitment**
  - Management wants option value; labour wants binding guarantees. The balance will determine whether the deal is accepted at all.

- **Evidence-first sequencing vs. decision urgency**
  - The audit can reduce error risk, but delay itself may worsen competitiveness if implementation stalls.

## 7. Integrative pathways

1. **Time-limited moratorium + independent audit**
   - Suspend closure and forced redundancy announcements during a fixed audit period.
   - Implement first-wave measures immediately.
   - Reassess only after the audit.

2. **Alternatives-first with a quantified residual-gap trigger**
   - Apply measures in sequence: voluntary exits, redeployment, working-time changes, productivity, temporary labour-cost moderation, management/owner contributions.
   - Only if a documented residual gap remains do deeper measures re-enter the decision.

3. **Site classification model**
   - Categorize plants as:
     - viable,
     - viable with measures/investment,
     - structurally non-viable.
   - Preserve the first two categories; treat the third separately with enhanced mitigation and evidence standards.

4. **Binding burden-sharing matrix**
   - Tie employee concessions to explicit commitments by management and owners:
     - investment,
     - product allocation,
     - overhead cuts,
     - compensation restraint,
     - site guarantees where economically supportable.

## 8. Open questions

- What is the actual savings gap, by site, after realistic alternatives?
- Which plants are genuinely viable under revised product allocation and capex?
- What management and owner contributions are large enough to be credible?
- What threshold turns a site from “difficult” to “structurally non-viable”?
- How long can the company afford a moratorium without damaging competitiveness further?
- What fallback mechanism would labour accept short of immediate closure or layoffs?
- What decision rule will govern the state’s blocking power in practice?

## 9. Recommended next step

Commission a **fixed-deadline independent plant-by-plant audit** with full access to operating, cost, utilization, break-even, capex, and savings-bridge data. Pair that with a **time-limited moratorium on closure and compulsory redundancy announcements** and immediate implementation of agreed first-wave measures. The audit should return a site classification and a quantified residual-gap assessment that can support a final negotiated package.

## 10. Emergent insights that only appeared through multi-turn interaction

- The real dispute is not whether restructuring is needed, but **what must be proven before closure or forced layoffs become legitimate**.
- “Closure as fallback” is functioning as both an economic tool and a bargaining weapon; labour rejects the latter even if it concedes the former may exist in principle.
- A simple yes/no framing misses the more useful middle ground: **site-by-site classification plus residual-gap testing**.
- Management’s credibility problem is not only about numbers; it is also about whether it can separate **evidence-based necessity** from **strategic leverage**.
- Labour’s “no closures” position became more precise over the rounds: it is not just opposition to closure, but opposition to **pre-negotiated destruction rights** embedded in the process from the start.
- The most workable compromise is procedural rather than substantive at first: **pause, audit, sequence, quantify, then decide**.
- The expert’s role shifted from abstract validation to a concrete standard-setting function: defining the missing data and threshold tests needed to turn a political dispute into a decisionable economic one.
