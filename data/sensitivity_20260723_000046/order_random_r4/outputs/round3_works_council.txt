Let me be absolutely clear: **I do not accept plant closures or compulsory redundancies as the price of restructuring.** Not as a “fallback,” not as a “last resort,” and not as a blank cheque hidden behind phrases like “economic necessity.”

## Where I agree
I agree with the expert and with the parts of the other parties that say:
- doing nothing is not an option;
- the company needs a durable restructuring;
- employees should contribute;
- management and owners must also contribute;
- numbers matter, and a site-by-site view is necessary.

That is the point where we start. Not where we end.

## Where I disagree

### To management
You still have **not** proven that closures or forced redundancies are necessary. Repeating “competitiveness” does not make it true. Repeating “structural overcapacity” does not identify a plant that must be shut. You need to show:

- the exact savings gap,
- the economics of each site,
- what voluntary measures can achieve,
- what management is contributing,
- what owners are contributing,
- and why a site-preserving package cannot close the gap.

Until that is on the table, closure is not a solution — it is a threat used to force concessions.

And I say this plainly: **the workforce will not accept a restructuring where management’s answer to every problem is to cut jobs and close plants while the company’s strategic errors are socialized onto workers.**

### To institutional investors
You keep saying “credibility” and “capital markets will not accept cosmetic deals.” Fine. But a deal that keeps every German plant open and avoids compulsory redundancies is not cosmetic if it truly delivers savings through:
- working-time changes,
- productivity gains,
- voluntary exits,
- retirement schemes,
- redeployment,
- and a fair labour contribution matched by management and owners.

What is not credible is demanding irreversible destruction of jobs and sites before you have demonstrated that alternatives fail. Your logic turns every restructuring into a pretext for shrinking the industrial base. We will not endorse that.

### To the controlling family
You talk about enterprise value and structural reform. I understand that. But enterprise value is not protected by breaking the historic trust contract with the workforce.

If you want a real turnaround, then your contribution must be real:
- binding investment commitments,
- protection of German sites,
- support for future products,
- and a clear commitment that the workforce is not the default adjustment variable.

Saying you support a turnaround while keeping closures and layoffs as routine options is not enough. It tells the workforce that the promise of partnership ends the moment it becomes inconvenient.

### To the state government
Your role is not decorative, and I appreciate that you are resisting closures as a default. But let me be blunt: if you really mean it, then use your blocking power to insist on a **site-preserving solution first**, not a “review” that quietly prepares the ground for closure later.

I do not accept the argument that co-determination or state rights are just obstacles to “discipline.” They exist precisely because this company is too important to be treated like a disposable cost block.

### To the expert
Your assessment is useful, but I want to be equally clear: you did **not** prove that closures are unavoidable. You said the burden of proof is still on management, and that a site-preserving package is plausible if the gap is modest enough.

That supports my position, not management’s.

What you also said matters:
- closures are a **last resort**;
- the decisive issue is the **residual gap after alternatives**;
- alternatives are feasible and standard tools.

Exactly. So let’s test them properly — and stop pretending that closure is the default answer.

## My position on the moderator’s proposals

### 1) “No immediate closure / no immediate compulsory redundancy”
I can support a **real moratorium** on closures and forced layoffs during the negotiation and implementation phase.

But let me define what “real” means:
- no closures announced as a threat in parallel;
- no compulsory redundancies in the package;
- no hidden pre-authorization for future closures;
- and no language that makes the moratorium just a pause before the same demand returns.

If management wants trust, it must stop negotiating with a knife on the table.

### 2) Site-specific package with thresholds
A site-specific review is acceptable only if the purpose is to prove how to keep the plants open, not to build a cleaner case for shutting them later.

The threshold I care about is simple:
- Can the site be made viable through internal measures, productivity, and fair burden-sharing?
If yes, it stays open.
If management says no, then it must prove that with hard numbers, not with pressure.

### 3) Burden-sharing matrix
Yes, burden-sharing must be real. But it has to be **equal in substance**, not equal in rhetoric.

That means:
- employee contribution only in exchange for binding site and job security,
- management restraint on pay and overhead,
- owner investment and guarantees,
- and no unilateral workforce sacrifice.

## What I would actually accept
I am prepared to discuss:
- temporary labour-cost moderation,
- working-time flexibility,
- redeployment,
- early retirement,
- voluntary exit schemes,
- productivity measures,
- product and investment commitments,
- and a negotiated future framework.

But only if the settlement contains:
- **no plant closures**
- **no compulsory redundancies**
- **binding job and site guarantees to 2030**
- **preservation of co-determination**
- **protection of the collective framework**

That is not maximalism. That is the minimum for a socially legitimate restructuring.

## What I will not accept
I will not accept:
- a “review trigger” that is just a disguised closure mechanism,
- a package that keeps closure alive as an automatic fallback,
- a settlement that weakens job-security standards,
- or a model where workers pay for management’s failure through plant shutdowns and forced job loss.

## The practical way forward
If management is serious, then put this on the table:

1. **Independent plant-by-plant operating audit**
2. **Transparent savings bridge**
3. **Binding management and owner contributions**
4. **Negotiated employee contribution**
5. **No closures / no forced redundancies during the term**
6. **Long-term site and employment guarantees**
7. **Joint monitoring under co-determination**

If the numbers truly support a site-preserving solution, then there is no need for closures. If the numbers do not, then management must still explain why it believes destroying jobs and sites is a better answer than a deeper industrial plan.

And I will repeat: **we will not give up the workforce as the adjustment reserve.**
