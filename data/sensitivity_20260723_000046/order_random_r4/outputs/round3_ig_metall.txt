We are willing to talk about a serious restructuring package. But I need to be equally clear: **a “review trigger” that normalizes plant closures and compulsory redundancies is not a compromise for us. It is just postponing the threat.**

## Where I agree
I agree with the moderator and with several of you on one important point:
- the company needs restructuring,
- the current cost base is too high,
- and employees can make a contribution.

I also agree that **a negotiated, quantified package** is better than an uncontrolled escalation. That remains our preference.

## Where I disagree

### To management
You still have not answered the central question: **why are closures and forced redundancies being treated as the default fallback before the alternatives are fully proven?**

You keep saying “the numbers require it,” but you have not put forward a transparent site-by-site case that shows:
- the exact gap,
- the exact savings from each alternative,
- what management is contributing,
- what owners are contributing,
- and why a site-preserving package cannot close the gap.

Until you do, your closure option looks less like necessity and more like leverage.

And let me be explicit: if management insists on keeping closures and compulsory redundancies as a live weapon, then the workforce has every reason to prepare its own counterpressure. We are not powerless here.

### To institutional investors
You say you want credibility and quantified savings. Fine. So do we. But credibility is **not** the same thing as handing management a blank cheque to close plants if talks get difficult.

If you can show that a site-preserving package delivers the same savings, then that is the credible route. If you cannot prove that closures are uniquely necessary, then you are asking for an industrial and social rupture on the basis of assumption, not evidence.

### To the controlling family
You talk about enterprise value and structural reform. We understand that. But enterprise value is not protected by destroying trust in the workforce and ripping up the collective framework.

If you want our cooperation, then you must commit to:
- binding site security,
- binding investment,
- and a real owner contribution.

Not rhetorical support. Not “we support a turnaround.” Binding commitments.

### To the state government
Your position is closer to ours, but I need more than general opposition to closures as a default. If you are serious about co-determination and regional responsibility, then use your blocking power to insist on a real alternatives test **before** any closure or forced layoff is even discussed.

### To the works council
We are aligned on the fundamentals:
- no compulsory redundancies,
- no plant closures,
- no one-sided burden on workers,
- protection of the collective framework.

I want to be careful here: I appreciate the works council’s insistence on binding guarantees. But I do not want us to get trapped into a procedural game where management says, “we reviewed alternatives,” and then returns with the same closure demand. A review trigger only works if the alternative package is genuinely allowed to run and prove itself.

## Our position on the moderator’s proposals

### Proposal 1: “No immediate closure / no immediate compulsory redundancy”
This is the only part of the moderator’s package that points in the right direction. But we would only consider it if it means:
- a real moratorium,
- a binding social package,
- and no pre-authorization of closures as the fallback.

A “pause” is not enough if the underlying threat remains automatic.

### Proposal 2: Site-specific package with hard thresholds
This can be useful **only if the threshold is about proving the alternatives first**, not about building a cleaner path to closure later.

We can accept site-specific analysis. We cannot accept site-specific analysis as the prelude to shutting down German plants if management simply prefers that route.

### Proposal 3: Burden-sharing matrix
Yes — but only if it is truly balanced. So far, the burden-sharing discussion has too often meant:
- employees give up wages and flexibility,
- management promises restraint,
- owners promise support,
- and then the company still reserves the right to cut jobs and close sites.

That is not equal burden-sharing. That is employees paying twice: once through concessions, and again through insecurity.

## What we would actually support
We are prepared to discuss a package with:
- a temporary labour-cost contribution,
- working-time flexibility,
- productivity measures,
- redeployment,
- voluntary exits,
- early retirement,
- and a negotiated capacity reduction,

**if and only if** the package is tied to:
- no compulsory redundancies,
- no plant closures,
- binding site and employment guarantees,
- management restraint,
- owner contribution,
- and protection of the collective agreement.

## What we will not accept
Let me be explicit about the line:
- no hidden closure option as a “fallback”
- no forced layoffs after an arbitrary review date
- no conditional guarantees that evaporate the moment the numbers become inconvenient
- no restructuring that shifts the structural burden onto the workforce alone

## My answer to the pointed question
To the moderator: if a package includes binding employment and site commitments plus measurable savings targets, **the fallback mechanism we would accept is further negotiated internal measures, not immediate closure or layoffs**.

That means:
- deeper working-time adjustments,
- temporary additional labour-cost measures,
- accelerated voluntary exit schemes,
- internal redeployment,
- delayed capacity reduction through non-destructive means,
- and renewed bargaining with management and owners.

If the company insists that the only fallback is closure or compulsory redundancies, then there is no real shared solution — only a countdown to conflict.

## Bottom line
We are ready for a hard but fair settlement. We are **not** ready to sign up to a restructuring model where:
- management sets the target,
- owners keep their flexibility,
- and the workforce carries the risk of plant closure and forced job loss.

If the other parties want a durable deal, then they need to prove that competitiveness can be restored **without sacrificing sites and compulsory employment security as the price of entry**.

If that proof is not provided, then the union will use the means at its disposal.
