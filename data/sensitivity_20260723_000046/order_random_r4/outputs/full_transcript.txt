A site-preserving deal can be credible if the required adjustment is mainly:

### 3) The decisive issue is the size of the savings gap
A credible restructuring decision depends on three numbers:
1. **Required annual savings** to restore target margin.
2. **Savings achievable without closures**.
3. **Residual gap after voluntary and internal measures**.

If the residual gap is small enough, then a site-preserving package is feasible. If it remains large, then management’s fallback to closures has economic logic.

What I cannot say from the information provided is the actual size of that gap. That would require:

- planned volume by site,
- product allocation,
- utilization assumptions,

### 4) Alternatives are feasible, but only up to a point
The alternatives mentioned are all standard restructuring tools and can be effective:
- voluntary exits / retirement bridges,
- redeployment,
- working-time reductions,
- temporary pay moderation,
- productivity programs,
- investment commitments tied to flexibility.

However, these measures have limits:
- they are slower to realize than closures,
- they depend on continued volume,
- they often produce partial rather than structural savings,
- and they can be eroded if demand weakens further.

So yes, they are feasible. But whether they are sufficient is a quantitative question, not a moral one.

### 5) Plant closures become rational only under certain conditions
Closure is economically defensible when a plant has one or more of the following:
- chronically low utilization,
- high fixed-cost absorption,
- unfavorable logistics,
- weak model allocation prospects,
- excessive retooling needs,
- persistent labor-cost disadvantage that concessions cannot offset,
- or duplication that cannot be removed otherwise.

If none of those conditions are severe, then closure is harder to justify. If several are present, then preserving the site may simply postpone the restructuring.

The specific question is whether a **site-preserving restructuring** can plausibly close the economic gap, or whether **plant closure / compulsory workforce reduction** becomes economically unavoidable after alternatives are exhausted.

## 3) How to test whether a site-preserving package works
A site-preserving package is credible only if the following bridge closes:

1. **Required annual savings** to restore the target margin  
2. minus **savings from voluntary exits, retirements, redeployment**
3. minus **working-time and productivity measures**
4. minus **temporary labor-cost contribution**
5. minus **product / volume / complexity actions**
6. equals a **residual gap**

If the residual gap is:
- **small**: a site-preserving solution may be economically defensible;
- **material**: closures or deeper capacity cuts become more likely;
- **large and persistent**: closure becomes a rational restructuring tool.

## 4) Likely economics of the alternatives
The alternatives listed are standard and real, but they have limits:

### Usually effective
- voluntary exits
- early retirement
- redeployment
- shift rationalization
- working-time flexibility
- temporary wage moderation
- productivity programs
- simplification of variants / complexity reduction

### Often insufficient on their own if overcapacity is severe
- permanent overstaffing reduction without site closure
- legacy cost reductions without volume removal
- “concession-only” deals when fixed-cost absorption is the problem

In other words: if the problem is mainly **labor cost**, alternatives may work.  
If the problem is **structural overcapacity plus low utilization**, alternatives alone often do not.

## 5) When closure becomes economically defensible
A closure case becomes stronger when a plant shows several of the following:
- chronically weak utilization,
- poor break-even economics,
- high logistics cost,
- limited product pipeline,
- major future capex needs,
- high complexity / low flexibility,
- inability to reach competitive unit costs even after concessions.

If one plant is clearly the weakest on these dimensions, closure may be the least-bad economic choice.  
If several plants are marginal, a partial footprint reduction may be needed.

- It is **not yet proven** that closures are unavoidable.

- I cannot say that no German plant should close.

## 5) On closures versus downsizing

From a restructuring-valuation standpoint, closure is not the first question. The first question is:

**Can the site be made to contribute positively after realistic restructuring?**

If yes, closure is usually value-destructive unless there is a very strong alternative use of capital and volume elsewhere.

If no, then the next question is whether:
- downsizing,
- line consolidation,
- partial closure,
- or full exit

produces the least value destruction.

Often, **partial downsizing** is economically more efficient than full closure if:
- a plant has some good programs and some weak ones;
- the site can be simplified;
- fixed cost can be cut materially without losing all strategic value.

Full closure becomes more likely when:
- the plant is too small,
- too complex,
- too capex-intensive,
- or too far from competitive break-even.
