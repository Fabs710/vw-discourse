I hear the points about shared sacrifice and the political and social consequences of closures. But I need to be direct: **a package that avoids structural plant closures, deep capacity reduction, and a real labour-cost reset is not enough**.

### Where I agree
- **Doing nothing is not an option.** We are in an immediate cost and competitiveness crisis.
- **Employees should not carry the full burden alone.** Management, owners, and the broader group must all contribute.
- **A negotiated solution is preferable to prolonged escalation.** I would rather reach a durable settlement than destroy value through a strike cycle.

### Where I disagree
#### 1) “No plant closures” cannot be a blanket rule
I understand the concern about regional impact. But if we keep every site open regardless of economics, we simply lock in an uncompetitive cost base. That is not industrial responsibility; it is delay.

We are not talking about symbolic cuts. We are talking about a structural mismatch between capacity, costs, and market demand. If a plant is not economically viable, then we have to be honest about that. I will not endorse a solution that preserves overcapacity under the label of “social responsibility.”

#### 2) Capacity reduction without hard consequences risks becoming cosmetic
Working-time changes, redeployment, and voluntary exits can help, but they are not sufficient on their own if they do not deliver the scale of savings required. I am not interested in a paper exercise that leaves the German cost base still above target two years from now.

We need:
- a **durable reduction in capacity**
- a **meaningful reduction in headcount**
- a **material labour-cost contribution**
- and a structure that supports returns through 2030, not just through the next bargaining round

#### 3) The burden must be shared, but not neutralised
I accept that management and owners must contribute. That can mean investment commitments, disciplined capital allocation, and restraint. But owner support cannot substitute for structural reform, and it cannot be used to avoid the hard decisions indefinitely.

### On the state government’s position
I respect the political realities, but I cannot accept the idea that co-determination or regional preference can override economic reality. Governance rights do not create competitiveness by themselves. If the board is going to act responsibly, it has to confront the cost problem head-on.

### What I am prepared to consider
I am still open on **how** the restructuring is achieved, provided the outcome is real. That means I can discuss:
- phased capacity adjustments
- voluntary measures and retirement schemes
- redeployment where it is credible
- productivity changes and working-time flexibility
- a negotiated labour contribution
- investment and product commitments in Germany

But I need to be explicit: these are only viable if they add up to a **structural** cost reduction at the scale required. If they do not, then we are just postponing closures rather than solving the problem.

### My bottom line
I am not here to demand closures for their own sake. I am here to secure a company that can compete. If that requires closures, then we cannot rule them out. If there is a credible alternative that delivers the same durable savings and capacity correction, I am prepared to examine it. But I will not sign up to a settlement that protects every site, avoids hard decisions, and leaves the cost base broken.

We need a package that is **measurable, durable, and large enough to restore competitiveness**. Anything less is not a restructuring.
