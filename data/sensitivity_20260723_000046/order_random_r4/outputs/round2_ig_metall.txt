We need to get specific, because general statements about “competitiveness” are too easy to use as a cover for shifting the whole crisis onto the workforce.

### Where I agree
I agree with everyone on three points:
- **Doing nothing is not an option**
- **A negotiated settlement is better than escalation**
- **Employees can contribute, including through flexibility and a negotiated labour-cost element**

That is already more than the workforce is obliged to give if management and owners do not also step up. But I am prepared to talk about a real package.

### Where I disagree
I disagree with the repeated framing that **plant closures and compulsory redundancies must remain on the table as normal tools**. For the union, that is not a neutral technicality. It is a direct attack on jobs, sites, skills, and trust.

If the company starts from “closures may be necessary,” then the burden of proof is on management and investors to show, in hard numbers, that all viable alternatives have been exhausted. So far I do not hear that proof — I hear a demand that the workforce accept the threat first.

I also disagree with the idea that the collective framework can be chipped away “partially” without consequences. Once you weaken wage standards and guarantees, you do not just create savings; you create a precedent that future restructurings will use again. That is not a durable settlement.

### What I need to hear before moving
If management wants our cooperation, it needs to stop speaking in abstractions and put forward a concrete social package. The core elements must be:

1. **No compulsory redundancies**
2. **No plant closures**
3. **Binding site and employment guarantees**
4. **A negotiated employee contribution only in exchange for equivalent management and owner contributions**
5. **Capacity reduction through internal measures**
   - working-time adjustments
   - voluntary exits
   - retirement solutions
   - redeployment
   - productivity measures
6. **Protection of the collective bargaining framework**

That is not an unrealistic wish list. It is the minimum for a settlement that does not make employees bear all the pain.

### On the other parties
#### Management
You say you need “hard decisions.” Fine. But hard decisions cannot mean that the easiest way to balance the books is to cut the workforce and close sites while leaving the strategic errors untouched. If you want flexibility from employees, show us what management is giving up.

#### Controlling family
You rightly reject a one-sided workforce solution. Then do not support one. If you want structural reform, your side must bind itself to real investment, restraint, and site security. “Support for a turnaround” is not enough by itself.

#### State government
Your position is closer to ours than to management’s, but I need you to be more explicit. If you truly reject closures as a default, then use your blocking power to force serious alternatives onto the table. Co-determination is not decorative.

#### Institutional investors
You keep saying the market will not accept a cosmetic deal. I agree that cosmetics are useless. But a deal with no plant closures is not automatically cosmetic if it actually delivers savings through other routes. The question is not whether a closure is possible in theory. The question is whether there is a package that achieves the required result without destroying sites and jobs. You have not shown that closure is the only path.

### The union’s position
We are not blocking restructuring. We are blocking **one-sided restructuring**.

We are prepared to discuss:
- a temporary or phased labour-cost contribution
- working-time flexibility
- productivity improvements
- voluntary and socially responsible headcount reduction
- capacity rationalisation where it does not destroy sites
- a negotiated future product and investment plan

But we will not sign up to:
- compulsory layoffs
- plant closures
- wage dumping
- or a settlement that makes employees absorb the crisis while management and owners remain protected

### The real test
If management wants to claim that closures are unavoidable, then it must present:
- the savings gap
- the alternatives considered
- why voluntary measures are insufficient
- why closures are the only way to close the gap
- and what management and owners are putting on the table first

Until then, we should treat closures and forced redundancies as **a threat, not a solution**.

If you want, I can help build a concrete counter-package around voluntary exits, reduced hours, and owner/management contributions — but only on the basis that **jobs, sites, and the collective agreement remain intact**.
