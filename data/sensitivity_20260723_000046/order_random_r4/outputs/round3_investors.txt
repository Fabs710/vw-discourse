We are focused on one thing: **credible, quantified margin restoration**. Not symbolism, not endless process, and not a deal that preserves structural overcapacity while hoping the market becomes more forgiving.

## Where we agree

### To management
We agree that **doing nothing is not an option**. The current cost base and capacity structure are not acceptable if the brand is to return to target margins. We also agree that the case needs to be **quantified by site and by measure**. Without that, capital cannot judge whether the restructuring is real or just rhetoric.

### To the expert

### To the controlling family and state government
We agree that a purely cosmetic compromise would be value-destructive. We also agree that management and owners must contribute visibly, and that prolonged conflict would destroy value.

### To labour representatives
We agree that employees should not carry the whole burden alone. A settlement that simply shifts the pain onto the workforce while leaving the company’s structure unchanged would not be durable.

## Where we disagree

### 1) “No plant closures” cannot be an absolute
We do **not** accept a principled ban on closures before the numbers are known. If a plant is structurally uncompetitive, then refusing closure on principle is not social responsibility; it is **subsidising overcapacity** and impairing the return on capital.

That said, we are **not** arguing for closure as a default. We are arguing for **economic reality**. If site-preserving alternatives can fully close the gap, excellent. But that has to be demonstrated, not asserted.

### 2) “No compulsory redundancies” cannot be an absolute
We prefer voluntary exits, retirement, redeployment, working-time changes, and productivity measures. But we will not support a settlement that removes the company’s ability to achieve the necessary savings if voluntary tools fall short.

An absolute ban on compulsory workforce reduction risks making the restructuring non-credible. Capital markets will treat that as delay dressed up as peace.

### 3) Review triggers are only useful if they are real
The moderator’s sequencing ideas are sensible in principle, but only if they do **not** become a mechanism to lock in the status quo. A review trigger must be tied to:
- hard numbers,
- a fixed timetable,
- and a real decision consequence.

If it just means “we review again later,” that is not acceptable.

## Our position on the current proposals

### On a time-limited moratorium
We could support a **short, defined implementation period** with no immediate closures or forced redundancies, provided that:
- savings targets are quantified,
- the package is implemented quickly,
- and there is an explicit, credible fallback if the targets are missed.

This is acceptable only as sequencing, not as a substitute for hard decisions.

### On a site-specific alternatives test
This is the right next step. We support a **plant-by-plant operating case**. But the test must be genuinely economic:
- current fixed and variable costs,
- utilization,
- labor cost per vehicle,
- logistics and complexity costs,
- capex needs,
- savings from each alternative measure,
- and the residual gap.

If the residual gap remains large, then the market will expect deeper action.

### On the burden-sharing matrix
This is essential. We need a package where:
- employees contribute materially,
- management contributes materially,
- owners contribute materially,
- and the result is a real reset in costs and margins.

But burden sharing does not mean everyone gives a little while the core structure stays intact. It must buy a **durable competitiveness gain**.

## What would move us

We would back a package if it includes all of the following:

- **quantified, site-by-site savings**
- **a durable capacity reduction**
- **a meaningful labor-cost reset**
- **management and owner contributions**
- **a clear path to target return-on-sales**
- **implementation certainty**
- **an explicit fallback if targets are missed**

That is the minimum standard.

## What would not move us

We will not support:
- a cosmetic settlement,
- a site-preserving deal that leaves the cost base structurally too high,
- a permanent ban on closures,
- or a permanent ban on compulsory redundancies if the economics do not work.

That is not a hard line for its own sake. It is a value line. If the company cannot get back to competitive margins, the share price and access to capital will remain under pressure.
