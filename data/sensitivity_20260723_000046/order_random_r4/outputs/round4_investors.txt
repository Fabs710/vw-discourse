We are still at the same point: we support restructuring, but only if it produces a **credible and durable reset of margins**. We are not interested in a symbolic settlement that preserves structural overcapacity, leaves the cost base uncompetitive, and then asks capital markets to pretend the problem is solved.

## Where we agree

### With management
We agree that:
- doing nothing is not an option,
- the company needs a structural reset,
- employees should not carry the full burden alone,
- and the case must be quantified site by site.

We also agree that a cosmetic compromise would be value-destructive. If the settlement does not materially improve margins and fixed-cost absorption, then it is not a turnaround; it is delay.

### With the expert
Your core point is exactly right: **closures are not yet proven necessary, but neither are they ruled out**. The burden of proof sits with management to show:
- the exact savings gap,
- what alternatives deliver,
- and what residual gap remains.

That is the right analytical standard. We support it.

### With the controlling family and state government
We agree that the company needs durable competitiveness, not theatre. We also agree that management and owners must contribute visibly.

### With labour representatives
We agree that employees should not pay the whole bill alone. A one-sided adjustment would be unstable and likely to provoke conflict and prolonged value destruction.

## Where we disagree

### 1) A blanket ban on closures is not acceptable
I am being explicit here: we do **not** support a principle that excludes plant closures in advance.

Why? Because if a site is structurally uncompetitive after a proper audit, then preserving it by political or bargaining fiat would simply entrench losses. That would impair returns, weaken the business, and ultimately damage all stakeholders, including employees.

That does **not** mean we want closures for their own sake. We do not. But they must remain available if the economics require them.

### 2) A blanket ban on compulsory redundancies is also not acceptable
We prefer voluntary exits, retirement schemes, redeployment, working-time flexibility, and productivity gains. But if those measures do not close the gap, then a hard prohibition on compulsory reductions would make the restructuring non-credible.

Capital markets will not support a package that preserves every employment guarantee while leaving the company structurally uncompetitive.

### 3) “No closures now” is only acceptable if it is genuinely temporary and evidence-based
A moratorium can be useful as sequencing. It becomes unacceptable if it turns into a de facto veto on economic reality.

If there is a time-limited pause, it must be tied to:
- immediate implementation of first-wave measures,
- an independent audit,
- a fixed deadline,
- and a real decision consequence if the residual gap remains too large.

Anything less is just postponement.

## On the facilitator proposals

### Time-limited moratorium + independent plant audit
This is reasonable as a next step, provided it is not a stealth guarantee. We would support it only if:
- the audit is truly independent,
- the timeframe is short and fixed,
- and no one pretends the outcome is predetermined.

### Alternatives first with a quantified residual-gap trigger
This is the right sequence. We support a staged approach:
1. voluntary exits and retirement,
2. redeployment and internal measures,
3. working-time and productivity changes,
4. temporary labor-cost contribution,
5. management and owner contributions,
6. then a hard review of the residual gap.

If that residual gap is still material, deeper restructuring must remain available.

### Site preservation for viable plants, controlled restructuring for non-viable ones
This is the most sensible framework so far. But the classification must be economic, not political.

We would support site preservation where the numbers work. We would not support preserving every site regardless of cost.

## What would move us

We would back a package if it includes:
- a **plant-by-plant quantified savings bridge**
- **real and durable capacity reduction**
- **meaningful labor-cost reset**
- **management and owner contributions**
- **a credible path to return-on-sales target**
- **implementation certainty**
- and **a real fallback if targets are missed**

That is the minimum for a value-preserving restructuring.

## What will not move us

We will not support:
- a cosmetic compromise,
- a site-preserving deal that leaves the cost base structurally too high,
- a permanent prohibition on closures,
- or a permanent prohibition on compulsory redundancies if the economics do not work.

Those positions are not “socially responsible” if they simply transfer the restructuring problem into the future and into the share price.

- fixed and variable costs,
- utilization,
- break-even volumes,
- logistics and complexity costs,
- capex needs,
- and savings by measure.

We want an independent audit to validate:

## To the union and works council
If a site-preserving package truly closes the gap, we will support it. But if the audit shows a material residual gap, then insisting on absolute job and site protection will not be acceptable. The market will treat that as delay, not discipline.

## To the controlling family and state government
Your support matters, but it cannot become a shield for structural inefficiency. Binding contributions are welcome; blank checks are not.

## Bottom line
Our position remains:
- **Yes** to a disciplined, quantified restructuring that restores competitiveness.
- **Yes** to alternatives first, if they are real and measurable.
- **Yes** to management and owner contributions.
- **No** to cosmetic settlement.
- **No** to preserving every site and guarantee if that leaves the company structurally uncompetitive.
- **No** to removing closures and compulsory redundancies from consideration before the numbers are known.

Show us the audited economics. Then we can judge whether a site-preserving solution is sufficient or whether deeper action is required.
