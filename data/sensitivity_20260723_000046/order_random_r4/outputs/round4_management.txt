I’ll be equally direct.

We have now spent several rounds agreeing on the diagnosis while still avoiding the central operational truth: **the company has too much capacity, too high a fixed-cost base, and too little time to fix it.** I am willing to test alternatives rigorously, but I am **not** willing to enter a process that pre-decides the outcome in favor of preserving every site and every legacy guarantee regardless of economics.

## Where I agree

I agree with the expert, the state government, investors, and even the union on several important points:

- **Doing nothing is not an option.**
- **Employees should not pay the whole bill alone.**
- **We need a site-by-site quantified case.**
- **Voluntary exits, retirement, redeployment, productivity gains, and working-time flexibility must be used.**
- **A deal that is merely cosmetic would be fatal.**

That is the useful part of the discussion. We are aligned on the need for restructuring and on the need for numbers.

## Where I disagree

### 1) I do not accept “alternatives first” as “alternatives only”
The union and works council continue to frame closures and compulsory redundancies as if even mentioning them were illegitimate. That is not a responsible bargaining position.

If a plant is structurally uncompetitive after a proper audit, then closure is not a threat tactic. It is a legitimate restructuring tool. The same applies to compulsory reductions if voluntary tools do not deliver enough.

I am not saying those measures are the first choice. They are not. But I **will not** accept a framework that removes them from consideration before we even know the residual gap.

### 2) A moratorium cannot become an industrial veto
I can support a time-limited pause while we complete the audit and implement first-wave measures. I cannot support a moratorium that becomes a de facto veto on hard decisions.

If the audit shows the gap remains too large, the company must retain the ability to act. Otherwise we are just buying time and calling it a strategy.

### 3) “No closures, no compulsory redundancies” is not a restructuring plan
It is a wish list.

The works council and union keep saying that the fallback must be further internal measures, not closures or layoffs. I respect the intent, but if internal measures are insufficient, then insisting on site permanence anyway is not responsible co-determination. It is denying the economic reality that will ultimately hit jobs, investment, and competitiveness much more broadly.

### 4) The state government cannot use blocking rights to freeze economics
I respect the state’s role and its legitimate concern for regional employment. But blocking rights do not create competitiveness. They can force discipline in the case, which I welcome. They cannot substitute for a viable operating model.

If the numbers support a site-preserving solution, fine. If they do not, then using veto power to prevent necessary adjustment would be damaging in the long run.

### 5) The controlling family is right about value, but not enough about urgency
I agree that a cosmetic compromise would destroy enterprise value. I also agree that the company needs a durable turnaround, not a temporary truce.

But that means we need more than general support for “structural reform.” We need the willingness to back actual structural change: capacity reduction, cost reset, and, where required, site action.

## On the proposed compromise logic

### Time-limited moratorium + independent audit
This is acceptable only if it is:
- truly time-limited,
- tied to immediate implementation of measures,
- and explicitly without prejudice to later economic decisions.

It must not become a holding pattern designed to avoid the real decision.

### Alternatives first with a residual-gap trigger
This is the right sequence. But I want to be very clear: the trigger must be **real**.

That means:
- site-by-site economics,
- fixed and variable costs,
- utilization,
- break-even volumes,
- logistics and complexity costs,
- capex needs,
- savings from each measure,
- and the residual gap after all agreed actions.

If that residual gap is still material, then the company must be able to consider deeper restructuring. No one gets to declare the answer in advance.

### Site preservation for viable plants, controlled restructuring for non-viable ones
This is the most sensible framing on the table, and I support it.

But it only works if “non-viable” is determined by hard data, not by political preference and not by a refusal to count closure as an option.

## To the union and works council
You say closure and compulsory redundancies are merely leverage. That is exactly the problem: if they are removed too early, the restructuring loses credibility; if they remain taboo forever, then no one is negotiating the actual economics.

You want binding job and site guarantees to 2030. I understand why. But if the business case cannot support that, then promising it anyway is not protection — it is pretending the numbers do not matter.

We are prepared to discuss:
- temporary labour-cost moderation,
- working-time flexibility,
- productivity measures,
- redeployment,
- early retirement,
- voluntary exits,
- and a negotiated capacity reduction.

But I am not prepared to give you a permanent veto over closure or compulsory reductions if the numbers say the sites cannot be kept competitive.

## To the state government
I hear your demand for a rigorous alternatives test, and I support it. But your role is not to secure a site-preservation outcome regardless of economics. Your role is to ensure the decision is disciplined, transparent, and fair.

That means:
- no closure as the starting assumption,
- but also no closure prohibition as a principle.

The audit should determine which sites are viable with measures and which are not. That is the only responsible basis.

## To the controlling family
You are right that enterprise value must be protected. But value is protected by hard choices, not by slogans.

If you want a credible turnaround, then support the full restructuring logic:
- a real labor-cost reset,
- capacity reduction where needed,
- and the ability to deal with structurally weak sites.

Symbolic support will not be enough.

## To investors
You are right to insist on quantified margin restoration. We will provide the site-by-site case. But let’s not pretend that seriousness only exists if closure remains on the table from minute one. Seriousness exists when the numbers are honest and the response matches them.

## My position, plainly
I support:
- an independent plant-by-plant audit,
- a time-limited implementation period,
- voluntary exits and retirement,
- redeployment,
- working-time and productivity measures,
- temporary labor-cost contribution,
- management and owner contributions,
- and a site-specific assessment of viability.

I do **not** support:
- a blanket ban on closures,
- a blanket ban on compulsory redundancies,
- or binding 2030 guarantees that may make the company structurally uncompetitive before we know the economics.

That is not a compromise position from me. It is the line between restructuring and self-deception.

## What I need next
I want the independent audit delivered quickly, with plant-by-plant economics and a quantified residual savings gap after the first wave of measures.

If the gap is small enough, a site-preserving solution may work.

If it is not, then we must be honest enough to say that deeper action is required.
