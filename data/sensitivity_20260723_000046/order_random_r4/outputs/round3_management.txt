Thank you. The expert input helps in one important respect: it confirms that **the burden of proof is on management to quantify the gap**. We will do that. But it does **not** change the underlying reality that the company needs a structural reset, not a temporary truce.

## Where I agree
I agree with the expert, and with several of you, on these points:

- **Doing nothing is not an option.**
- **Employees should not carry the full burden alone.**
- **A quantified, site-by-site view is necessary.**
- **Voluntary exits, retirement, redeployment, productivity, and working-time tools should all be used.**
- **Any package must be durable and financially credible.**

That is exactly why we are pressing for hard decisions now, not later.

## Where I disagree

### 1) The union and works council are still trying to turn “alternatives first” into “alternatives only”
I hear the insistence on no closures and no compulsory redundancies. I reject it.

A restructuring that is constrained in advance to preserve every site, every guarantee, and every legacy rule is not a restructuring strategy. It is a demand that management absorb the full burden of overcapacity while the operating structure remains untouched.

That is unacceptable.

You say closures are a threat, not a solution. Sometimes that is true. But if a site is structurally uncompetitive after a proper review, then closure is not a threat — it is an economically necessary outcome. I will not sign up to a framework that makes that impossible regardless of the numbers.

### 2) The state government cannot use co-determination to freeze industrial reality
I respect your role. I do not accept your premise.

Your blocking position does not create competitiveness. It only forces us to be more disciplined in proving the case. Fine. We will prove it. But I will not accept the idea that regional politics can override the company’s long-term viability.

If the package leaves German capacity too high, then the state may protect jobs briefly while actually weakening the company and the region over time. That is not stewardship; that is delay.

### 3) The controlling family is right on value, but not on preserving flexibility at all costs
I agree with the family on one core point: a cosmetic compromise would destroy enterprise value. But I also need the family to be clear-eyed.

If you want value creation, then you must support a package that actually reduces fixed cost and capacity enough to restore margins. “Support for a turnaround” is not enough if the turnaround cannot touch structurally weak sites.

### 4) The investor position is correct on credibility, but too abstract on execution
You are right that the market will not accept a settlement that simply postpones the problem. But credibility comes from **executability**, not slogans.

So here is the reality: we will provide the plant-by-plant economics. If the site-preserving alternative truly closes the gap, then we can discuss it seriously. If it does not, then you cannot ask management to pretend that it does.

## What the expert really confirmed
The expert did **not** say closures are unnecessary. The expert said:

- closures are **not yet proven**,
- but they remain economically admissible if the residual gap stays large,
- and the real test is a quantified bridge after alternatives.

That is exactly where I stand.

So let me be equally direct: I am not prepared to accept a permanent ban on closures or compulsory redundancies as a principle. If the numbers show a plant is not viable, then we must have the ability to act.

I will support a rigorous alternatives test, but it must be paired with honesty about the fallback. That means:

- site-by-site economics,
- break-even volumes,
- fixed-cost absorption,
- product allocation,
- logistics and complexity costs,
- capex requirements,
- quantified savings from each measure,
- and the residual gap.

That analysis is not a delaying tactic. It is the basis for deciding whether internal measures are sufficient or whether deeper restructuring is required.

## My position on the proposed sequencing
### “No immediate closure / no immediate compulsory redundancy”
I can accept a time-limited negotiation and implementation period. I cannot accept it as an open-ended shield against necessary change.

If a moratorium is proposed, it must be:
- time-bound,
- tied to measurable deliverables,
- and explicitly **without prejudice** to economically necessary decisions if targets are missed.

### Site-specific package with thresholds
This is the right direction, but only if the thresholds are economic, not political. Thresholds must answer:
- Is the site viable at competitive cost?
- Can alternatives close the gap?
- What is the residual gap after all measures?

Not: “How can we preserve every site regardless of economics?”

### Burden-sharing matrix
Yes, and it must be real.

Employee contributions must be matched by:
- management restraint,
- owner support,
- and structural action.

But I will be blunt: management and owners cannot make symbolic contributions while the workforce makes the substantive ones. If we are asking people to accept change, the change has to change something.

## To the union directly
You say a review trigger merely postpones the threat. I say that without a credible fallback, your position simply preserves the status quo under a different name.

If your alternative package can truly deliver the required savings, then closures will not be needed. If it cannot, then refusing even to contemplate them is not responsible bargaining. It is obstruction.

And let me be clear: if you force us into a corner where the company cannot restructure structurally, then you are increasing the risk of a much harsher outcome later.

## To the works council directly
You keep saying “site-preserving” as if the word itself solves the problem. It does not.

I am willing to test site preservation. I am not willing to let site preservation become a substitute for economics. If a plant cannot be made competitive, then keeping it open by decree does not preserve value; it transfers losses into the future.

## To the state government directly
You say your role is to protect industrial capacity. Agreed. But protecting industrial capacity requires keeping the company viable. If the domestic footprint remains too large, we will not be able to fund future products, technology, and platform investment to 2030.

That is the trade-off you must confront.

## Bottom line
I am prepared to proceed with a **plant-by-plant quantified restructuring review** and to test a site-preserving alternative rigorously.

But I am **not** moving on these core points:

- no blanket ban on closures,
- no blanket ban on compulsory redundancies,
- no deal that leaves the German cost base structurally uncompetitive,
- no cosmetic compromise dressed up as social peace.

If the numbers support a site-preserving solution, fine — show me.  
If they do not, then the company must be able to act.

That is the standard we need.
