I’ll be direct.

## Our position
We are still prepared to negotiate a serious restructuring package. But we are **not** prepared to accept a framework in which **plant closures and compulsory redundancies remain a live bargaining weapon** from the start. That is not “credibility.” It is using the threat of destruction to force one-sided concessions.

If this company wants the workforce to contribute, then the package must be based on **shared sacrifice, binding commitments, and job protection** — not on keeping layoffs and closures on the table as an ever-present fallback.

## Where I agree
I agree with several points made in this round:

- The company needs restructuring.
- The burden cannot fall on employees alone.
- Numbers matter, and a site-by-site view is necessary.
- Voluntary exits, retirement, redeployment, productivity measures, and working-time flexibility should be used.
- An independent audit can be useful.

Those are not controversial for us. We have already signaled openness to a labour-cost contribution and capacity adjustment if it is part of a fair package.

## Where I disagree

### To management
You still frame the issue as if the only way to make a restructuring “credible” is to preserve the option to close plants and force layoffs. We reject that logic.

If you want a credible package, then **show the savings bridge first**:
- what the exact gap is,
- what voluntary and internal measures deliver,
- what management is contributing,
- what owners are contributing,
- and what remains after that.

Until that is transparent, closure is not a necessity. It is leverage.

And let me say clearly: if you insist on keeping closure and compulsory redundancy as a ready-made fallback, then you should expect the workforce to defend itself with the means available to it. We are not going to sign up to a deal that legalizes future plant destruction in advance.

### To investors
You keep saying “capital markets need flexibility.” Fine. But capital also needs **social peace, industrial capability, and operational continuity**. A settlement that permanently undermines the collective framework and destroys trust is not credible; it is volatile.

If the site-preserving alternative delivers the savings, then it is the credible route. If you have not proven that it fails, then demanding closure rights now is premature and one-sided.

### To the controlling family
You speak about enterprise value and stewardship. Then act like a steward.

That means:
- binding investment commitments,
- visible owner contribution,
- support for future products,
- and no attempt to offload the adjustment entirely onto the workforce.

You cannot ask employees to moderate wages, accept flexibility, and absorb uncertainty while ownership keeps full strategic freedom to close sites later. That is not partnership.

### To the state government
Your insistence on a site-preserving alternatives test is the right instinct. But I need to be blunt: a test only matters if it has consequences.

If the audit shows a plant can be preserved through fair burden-sharing, then that plant stays open.  
If the audit becomes a prelude to “we checked, now we close,” then it is not protection — it is staging.

We support using co-determination and your blocking power to require real alternatives first. We do **not** support turning that process into a formality.

### To the expert
Your contribution confirms something important: **closures are not yet proven necessary**.

You also said that a site-preserving solution may be feasible if the residual gap is modest enough. That is exactly why we reject any premature closure logic. The burden of proof is still on management.

## On the facilitator’s compromise proposals

### 1) Time-limited moratorium + independent plant audit
This is the only proposal on the table that moves in the right direction — but only if the moratorium is real.

For us, “real” means:
- no closure announcements,
- no compulsory redundancy announcements,
- no parallel pressure campaign,
- no hidden pre-authorization of later closure,
- and no bargaining with a knife on the table.

If management wants a moratorium to be credible, it must also commit to **genuine alternatives implementation during that period**, not just wait for the audit to justify what it already wanted.

### 2) Alternatives first with a quantified residual-gap trigger
This is better than the current “closure as default” logic, but only if the trigger is not designed to make closure automatic later.

Our concern is simple: if “alternatives first” is just code for “employees concede now, plants close later if management says so,” then there is no fair bargain.

We can accept a structured sequence of:
- voluntary exits,
- retirement,
- working-time changes,
- productivity measures,
- redeployment,
- temporary labour-cost moderation,
- and management/owner contributions.

But the fallback cannot be an open-ended right to close plants or force layoffs after a deadline.

### 3) Site preservation for viable plants, controlled restructuring for non-viable ones
This sounds reasonable in words, but the devil is in the classification.

Who defines “non-viable”?  
Management? Investors? An external auditor with management assumptions baked in?

We will not accept a model where “non-viable” is just a polite label for “management prefers to shut this site.” If there is to be any differentiation, it must be based on:
- hard, transparent data,
- full disclosure,
- and a genuine test of alternatives and owner contribution.

## What we are prepared to put on the table
We are not saying “no contribution.” We are saying “no one-sided sacrifice.”

We are prepared to discuss:
- temporary labour-cost moderation,
- flexibility in working time,
- productivity improvements,
- redeployment,
- early retirement,
- voluntary exit schemes,
- and capacity reduction through non-destructive measures,

**if and only if** this is tied to:
- no compulsory redundancies,
- no plant closures,
- binding site and job guarantees,
- management restraint,
- owner contribution,
- and preservation of the collective framework.

## What is non-negotiable for us
Let me be explicit:

- **No compulsory redundancies**
- **No plant closures**
- **No hidden fallback that normalizes either**
- **No deal where workers carry the structural pain alone**
- **No dismantling of collective wage standards as the price of entry**

If the company cannot live with that, then it is not asking for restructuring. It is asking the workforce to underwrite its restructuring by sacrificing its own security.

## On the independent audit
Yes, we support an independent plant-by-plant audit — but it must be truly independent and not management-led in substance.

We would want the audit to examine:
- current and projected utilization,
- fixed-cost absorption,
- labor cost per vehicle,
- logistics and complexity costs,
- model allocation,
- capex needs,
- and the savings from each proposed measure.

And one more thing: the audit must also test the **value of owner and management contributions**. Too often these are treated as symbolic while employee concessions are treated as material.

## What would move us
What could move us is simple:
- a real burden-sharing package,
- binding guarantees,
- no closures,
- no forced layoffs,
- owner and management contributions that are actually meaningful,
- and a durable collective agreement.

That is the basis for a deal.

## What will not move us
What will not move us is:
- repeated appeals to “credibility” while preserving closure threats,
- a “review” that is just a countdown to layoffs,
- or a restructuring that uses social pressure to achieve what management cannot justify openly.

If the other side wants peace, then it must stop treating job destruction as a normal management instrument.

If that is refused, then we will use the instruments we have — including industrial action — to defend jobs, sites, and collective standards.
