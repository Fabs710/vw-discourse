I will be equally direct.

We all agree on one basic point: **the company needs a real restructuring**. But I do **not** accept the way this is being framed by management, the investors, and partly also the controlling family: as if closures and layoffs are the natural starting point and everything else is merely an exception.

That is not acceptable from the perspective of the state as anchor shareholder, regional government, and co-determination stakeholder.

## Where I agree
I agree that:
- doing nothing would be irresponsible;
- the company needs a durable improvement in competitiveness;
- employees cannot be asked to carry the burden alone;
- voluntary exits, working-time flexibility, redeployment, productivity measures, and a negotiated contribution can all be part of a serious package;
- management and owners must also make visible contributions.

## Where I disagree
### 1) Plant closures in the state are not a default option
I do **not** accept closures as the first or even normal tool of restructuring. A closure is not just a cost item. It means:
- permanent job losses,
- damage to supplier networks,
- weakening of regional value creation,
- political rupture,
- and a long-term loss of trust in the company’s social partnership model.

If a site is claimed to be unviable, that claim must be proved with hard numbers and after all alternatives have been exhausted. Until then, closures remain a threat, not a solution.

### 2) Compulsory redundancies are the wrong path
I do not support forced layoffs as the basis of a settlement. If the company wants a stable outcome, it must use socially responsible tools first:
- natural attrition,
- early retirement,
- voluntary departure schemes,
- internal redeployment,
- working-time adjustments,
- phased capacity reduction.

A deal built on forced redundancies would be politically explosive, industrially destabilizing, and economically short-sighted.

### 3) The state’s co-determination role is not negotiable
I reject any attempt to treat our veto and board role as an obstacle to be bypassed. These rights exist precisely because the company is too important to the region to be managed with a narrow short-term logic. They are part of the stability of the company, not an inconvenience.

## On the union’s position
I largely support the union’s insistence that:
- employees should not pay the whole bill,
- no one should be forced out as the first resort,
- and a settlement must include binding guarantees.

But I also need to be honest: if we insist on absolutes without any room for quantified alternatives, we risk leaving the company without a workable path. So I am not saying “nothing can ever change.” I am saying: **change must be socially responsible, reversible where possible, and balanced**.

## On the controlling family
You are right that the company cannot simply preserve every legacy structure forever. But I do not accept your implication that preserving sites and jobs is a political luxury. In this company, that industrial base is part of the long-term value. If it is torn up too aggressively, the company may become leaner on paper and weaker in reality.

If you want real restructuring, then I would expect:
- concrete owner support,
- a genuine industrial plan,
- and clear investment commitments in Germany.

Not just rhetorical backing for “turnaround.”

## On management
Management still has not made the case that closures are necessary rather than preferred. That distinction matters. I want:
- a transparent presentation of the cost gap,
- the specific measures considered,
- the expected savings from each,
- and why site-preserving alternatives would not suffice.

Without that, asking for closures or forced redundancies is premature.

## My bottom line
I am open to a **hard but socially responsible restructuring** if it:
- protects sites and jobs **as far as economically possible**,
- uses voluntary and internal measures first,
- includes a real contribution from management and owners,
- preserves co-determination,
- and leaves the company stronger in the long term.

I am **not** open to a package that makes plant closure the default answer, sidelines the state’s role, or transfers the main burden onto the workforce.

If management wants my support, it must come forward with a **site-preserving, quantified, and binding alternative package**. If it cannot do that, then I will not give it a free pass to restructure by cutting out the region that helped build this company.
