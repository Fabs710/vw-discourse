We are now at the point where the discussion has to become concrete rather than ideological.

## Where I agree
I agree with the union on several important points:
- the company needs a real restructuring, not theatre;
- employees should not carry the full burden;
- voluntary exits, retirement, redeployment, working-time flexibility, and productivity measures should all be used;
- an independent plant-by-plant audit is the right next step.

I also agree with management, investors, and the expert on one core point: **the company must restore competitiveness in a durable way**. A deal that merely preserves the status quo while pushing the problem into the future would destroy value.

## Where I disagree strongly
### 1) A blanket “no closures, no compulsory redundancies” position is not acceptable
I cannot support turning that into an absolute principle. If a site is structurally uncompetitive after a proper test, then refusing even to consider deeper action is not stewardship — it is value erosion.

I am not saying closures are the goal. They are not. But I will not endorse a governance framework that removes economically necessary options before the numbers are known.

### 2) A moratorium cannot become a veto
I can support a time-limited pause while the audit is done and alternatives are implemented. But I will not accept a moratorium that is effectively a permanent shield against action.

If the audit shows that the residual gap remains too large, the company must retain the ability to act. Otherwise the restructuring is not credible.

### 3) “Shared sacrifice” must mean real structural savings
The union is right that workers should not pay alone. But management and owners also cannot make symbolic contributions while expecting the workforce to absorb all the meaningful pain.

That said, employee contributions must actually move the cost base. If the alternatives do not close the gap, then insisting on preserving every site regardless of economics is not fair either.

## To the union and works council
You say closures and compulsory redundancies are merely leverage. I understand why you see it that way. But from an ownership and governance perspective, leverage cuts both ways. If the company cannot structurally reset its cost base, then it risks long-term decline that would damage jobs, investment, and the regional footprint anyway.

So my answer is: I am prepared to support a site-preserving solution **if it is proven to close the savings gap**. I am **not** prepared to pre-commit to no closures or no compulsory redundancies in all circumstances.

## To management
You still owe the board and shareholders a proper quantified case. The expert was clear: the burden of proof is on management. I support that demand.

Bring forward:
- site-by-site P&Ls,
- utilization rates,
- break-even volumes,
- logistics and complexity costs,
- capex needs,
- and the savings contribution of each measure.

Without that, closure talk is premature. With that, we can judge whether a site-preserving package is sufficient or not.

## To the state government
I respect your role and your blocking power. But that power should not be used to freeze the industrial structure indefinitely. It should force discipline, transparency, and fairness.

I support:
- an independent alternatives test,
- binding consultation,
- and a strong social mitigation package.

I do **not** support a position that makes economically necessary adjustment impossible if the audit proves the gap remains.

## To institutional investors
You are right to demand credibility and a real margin reset. I agree with that. But credibility is not the same as insisting on closures from day one.

If the site-preserving route closes the gap, it should be accepted. If it does not, then we cannot pretend otherwise.

## On the facilitator proposals
### Time-limited moratorium + independent plant audit
This is acceptable to me **if** it is truly time-limited and without prejudice to later action if the numbers require it.

### Alternatives first with a quantified residual-gap trigger
This is the right sequence. I support it.

### Site preservation for viable plants, controlled restructuring for non-viable ones
This is the most sensible framing so far. But the classification must be based on hard data, not bargaining positions.

## My bottom line
I am prepared to support a serious restructuring package that includes:
- voluntary exits,
- retirement schemes,
- redeployment,
- working-time flexibility,
- productivity gains,
- temporary labour-cost moderation,
- management restraint,
- and owner contribution,

provided that:
- there is a genuine plant-by-plant audit,
- the residual gap is measured honestly,
- and the company retains the ability to make deeper structural decisions if alternatives fail.

What I will **not** support is:
- a blanket ban on closures,
- a blanket ban on compulsory redundancies,
- or a deal that preserves all legacy structures while leaving the company structurally uncompetitive.

That would not be a compromise. It would be a slow erosion of the enterprise value we are meant to protect.
