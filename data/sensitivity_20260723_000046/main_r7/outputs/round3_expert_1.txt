Below is the economics-based test the parties are asking for: **how much of the margin/cost gap a strongest-possible no-closure package can plausibly close, and what residual gap would remain before closures or forced redundancies become economically justified.**

## 1) The right way to judge this
For a large German auto OEM, the question is not whether a no-closure package creates savings — it usually does. The question is whether it closes **enough of the structural gap** to restore target returns **durably**.

A practical test is:

- **Annual recurring savings needed:** typically in the high hundreds of millions for a brand with a major German cost gap
- **One-off cost tolerance:** whether the firm can absorb restructuring charges without damaging liquidity
- **Residual gap after internal measures:** if still large, closures become more likely to be economically justified

## 2) Strongest-possible no-closure package: realistic savings
If management and labor actually implement the upper end of internal adjustment, a **serious no-closure package** can plausibly produce about:

### Annual recurring savings
**€500m to €900m per year**

This is the realistic “strong package” range, not a symbolic one. It would usually require all of the following:

- voluntary exits / retirement windows
- internal redeployment across German sites
- working-time reduction or more flexible shift arrangements
- pay / bonus concessions or temporary wage moderation
- management and indirect overhead cuts
- reduced contractor use
- productivity improvements and simplification
- possibly some outsourcing or insourcing changes

### Headcount effect
**2,500 to 6,000 FTE** reduction over 2–4 years is plausible without closures if the company is willing to use large voluntary programs and attrition aggressively.

### One-off costs
**€400m to €1.2bn** is a realistic range.

That is not trivial. Severance, early retirement support, retraining, redeployment, and temporary ramp inefficiency all cost money.

## 3) What remains after that package?
If the company’s structural cost/margin gap is, say, in the rough range of **€1.0bn to €1.5bn per year** in recurring improvement needed to restore acceptable returns, then a strong no-closure package may close:

- **about one-third to two-thirds** of the gap, depending on the actual starting point and implementation quality

That means the **residual gap** could still be roughly:

### Residual gap after strong no-closure package
**€200m to €700m per year** in many plausible cases

If the true overcapacity problem is large, the residual can be larger than that.

## 4) When do closures become economically justified?
Closures or major capacity reduction become economically stronger if all three are true:

1. **The residual gap remains material** after a serious internal package  
   - usually at least several hundred million euros annually

2. **The business has permanently excess capacity**
   - not just a cyclical downturn
   - and not a temporary demand dip

3. **The fixed-cost savings from removing capacity exceed the execution costs and disruption**
   - including ramp-down, transfer, relocation, and conflict costs

If those conditions hold, closures often have better long-run economics because they remove **fixed plant cost**, not just labor cost.

## 5) Why a no-closure package may still be insufficient
A no-closure package can cut labor expense, but it does **not fully eliminate underutilized plant economics**. Open plants still carry:

- maintenance
- utilities
- supervision
- logistics
- engineering overhead
- complexity costs
- partially loaded indirect labor

So if a plant is structurally underfilled, internal measures can lower the unit cost, but only up to a point. That is why no-closure packages often become a **bridge** rather than the final solution.

## 6) My judgement
### Economically plausible conclusion:
A **strong no-closure package is credible as a first step** and may close a large share of the gap.

### But:
If the company truly has **large structural overcapacity**, it is **unlikely** that no-closure measures alone will fully restore competitiveness on a durable basis.

So the likely outcome is one of two:

- **Best case for no-closure advocates:**  
  the residual gap after internal measures is modest enough that it can be bridged by further productivity gains, portfolio changes, or owner support

- **More likely in a severe overcapacity case:**  
  the residual gap remains large enough that **some capacity reduction, site consolidation, or closure** becomes economically justified

## 7) Practical threshold
If after a strong no-closure package the company still faces a residual recurring gap of roughly:

- **€300m+ per year**, and especially
- **€500m+ per year**

then the case for deeper structural adjustment becomes materially stronger.

At that point, the question is no longer whether labor can contribute — it has. The question becomes whether the remaining gap is due to **excess installed capacity** that only closures or major consolidation can remove.

## 8) Bottom line
- **No-closure package:** plausible, serious, and potentially large
- **But likely incomplete** if the overcapacity is truly structural and sizeable
- **Residual gap after strong internal measures:** likely still meaningful in a severe case
- **Closures/forced redundancies:** economically justified only if that residual gap is large, persistent, and cannot be bridged by further internal measures without unacceptable ongoing cost
