I’ll be equally clear.

## Where I agree
I agree with all parties on one thing: this company needs a **durable** solution, not a cosmetic pause. I also agree that:
- the package must be quantified,
- it must be enforceable,
- and it must support future investment and product capability.

I also agree that a prolonged strike cycle would damage output, reputation, and trust. That is exactly why we need a settlement that actually fixes the cost base.

## Where I disagree — and why

### To the works council and union
You keep saying management has not “proven necessity.” But the economist already did the useful work here: alternatives can reduce the gap, yes, but **they are unlikely to eliminate a large structural overcapacity problem** on their own.

That is the central point you keep avoiding.

If the company’s German footprint is carrying fixed costs and capacity well above what the market can support, then:
- working-time tweaks,
- voluntary exits,
- redeployment,
- qualification,
- and productivity measures

may help, but they do **not** automatically remove the structural burden. They can be part of a package, not the whole package.

So no, I am not prepared to promise:
- all German plants remain open,
- no compulsory redundancies under any circumstances,
- and binding job guarantees through 2030,

while expecting the company to become competitive again. That is not restructuring. That is freezing the problem in place.

You say closures are a bargaining weapon. I say closures are a **real option** if they are necessary to restore competitiveness. Those are not the same thing.

### To the regional state government
I respect the regional responsibility. I do **not** accept that regional sensitivity can override industrial reality.

If we preserve every site regardless of utilization and cost, we end up protecting a structure that erodes margins, delays investment, and weakens the company’s future. That is not responsible stewardship of the region either. A weak company is a worse employer than a restructured one.

If the state wants to be constructive, then it should help test a serious alternative package — but not insist that closures are politically forbidden before the numbers are even matched against the savings target.

### To the controlling family
I agree with you on the need for long-term value and on rejecting symbolic compromise. But I need the same discipline applied consistently.

“Value” is not protected by preserving legacy structures that keep the cost base uncompetitive. Value is protected by:
- removing excess capacity,
- lowering labour cost structurally,
- restoring margins,
- and securing investment for future products.

So yes, I agree with you that a cosmetic deal is unacceptable. But that is exactly why I will not sign up to a package that is socially comfortable and financially insufficient.

### To the investors
I agree with your core message: the market will not reward a deal that leaves the company structurally weak. A temporary bridge with no real reset would be value destructive.

But let me be clear: I will not accept the implication that the harshest possible restructuring is automatically the best one. The right outcome is the one that is:
- sufficiently deep,
- executable,
- and durable.

If that requires closures or stronger workforce reductions, then so be it. The standard is not comfort; the standard is whether the company reaches a cost structure that can compete through 2030.

## The real issue
The employee side keeps framing this as a choice between “social responsibility” and “destruction.” That is false.

The real choice is between:
1. a **real structural reset**, or
2. a **slow decline disguised as social protection**.

If overcapacity remains, then margins remain under pressure. If margins remain under pressure, then investment capacity remains constrained. If investment capacity remains constrained, the company falls further behind. That is the chain we are trying to break.

## What I would accept
I remain open to a negotiated package that includes:
- voluntary exits at scale,
- internal redeployment,
- working-time adjustments,
- qualification and productivity measures,
- management contribution,
- owner contribution,
- and targeted site protection where it supports the industrial plan.

But I need one thing that the employee side still refuses to acknowledge:  
**the package must be large enough to remove the structural burden, not just soften it.**

That means:
- real capacity reduction,
- real labor-cost reduction,
- and real durability.

If you cannot deliver that without closures and stronger workforce reduction, then closures remain on the table.

## What I reject outright
I reject:
- a blanket no-closure promise,
- a blanket no-redundancy promise,
- a settlement that protects every existing structure regardless of economics,
- and any framework that asks management to guarantee competitiveness without the tools needed to achieve it.

## My position remains unchanged
I am not here to negotiate away the need for deep restructuring. I am here to ensure the company can compete, earn, and invest again.

So the next step is not another round of general principles. It is this:

- Put forward a **no-closure alternative** with hard numbers.
- Put forward a **structural adjustment scenario** with hard numbers.
- Show the annual savings, one-off costs, headcount impact, and capacity impact of each.
- Then we will see which one actually restores competitiveness.

Until then, I will not pretend that closures and meaningful workforce reduction can be ruled out. They cannot.
