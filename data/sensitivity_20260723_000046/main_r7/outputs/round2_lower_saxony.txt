I will be very clear from the perspective of the regional state government and anchor shareholder:

I **accept** that the company needs a serious restructuring. I **do not** accept that the only credible route is plant closures and forced redundancies in Germany.

The economist’s input is useful, but it does not change my core position. It actually confirms something important: there may be a real savings gap, but that does **not automatically justify sacrificing sites and jobs in the state**. It means the parties must prove whether the gap can be closed by a broader package before anyone reaches for closures as the first or only instrument.

## Where I agree
I agree with management, the controlling family, and investors on one point: **doing nothing is not an option**. If the company stays structurally uncompetitive, that would threaten the future of the entire industrial base, including regional employment.

I also agree that we need:
- durable savings,
- a credible product and investment plan,
- and a restructuring that is actually executable.

And I agree with the works council and union that **the workforce cannot be the only party paying the bill**.

## Where I disagree
### To management
You are treating plant closures and workforce cuts as if they were the default answer. I reject that framing.

The economist’s assessment says alternative measures can be substantial. That matters. It means you have not yet shown that closures are unavoidable. Before you press for a decision that would damage regional jobs and supplier networks, you owe us a **fully quantified alternative**.

If the company is serious about a negotiated solution, then put numbers on the table for:
- internal capacity reduction,
- working-time adjustments,
- voluntary exits,
- redeployment,
- productivity measures,
- management contribution,
- and owner contribution.

If those measures do not close the gap, then we can discuss what additional steps are necessary. But I will not support a process that starts with closures and uses “competitiveness” as a blanket justification.

### To the controlling family and investors
You are right that the company must remain competitive. But competitiveness is not served by detonating the regional footprint and then hoping the workforce will accept it as discipline.

A settlement that triggers prolonged strikes, reputational damage, and political backlash is **not** value-maximizing. It is a high-risk gamble. Long-term value depends on social peace, stable production, and preserved skills — not just on forcing the harshest possible cut.

So I reject the idea that “harder” automatically means “better.” The test is whether the package is durable **and** compatible with the company’s role as a regional anchor.

### To the works council and union
I understand your red lines. I share the goal of protecting jobs and sites. But I also need to be honest: simply repeating “no closures, no compulsory redundancies” does not yet answer the economic problem.

The economist has made clear that a no-closure package could work only if it is unusually broad, measurable, and enforceable. That means the workforce side also has to engage seriously with:
- temporary flexibility,
- productivity improvements,
- voluntary exit arrangements,
- and, if needed, time-limited employee contributions.

What I cannot support is a position that protects every existing structure without providing a credible mechanism for the necessary savings.

## My bottom line
From the state’s perspective, the minimum acceptable path is:

1. **No plant closures as the starting point**
2. **No compulsory redundancies**
3. **A serious alternative package first**
4. **Binding protection for the regional footprint where possible**
5. **Shared burden from management, owners, and workforce**
6. **Clear investment commitments for future products and transformation**

If management can demonstrate, with verifiable numbers, that comparable savings cannot be reached without closures, then we are at least in a harder discussion. But we are **not there yet**.

And I want to state one further point plainly: this company’s legitimacy in the region depends on more than balance-sheet arithmetic. It depends on whether it behaves as a responsible industrial anchor or as a site that can simply be trimmed away when pressure rises. I will not allow the state’s co-determination and blocking role to be bypassed by presenting closures as a fait accompli.

## What I would ask for next
Before any decision on closures, I want a concrete package on the table that shows:
- what savings are achievable without plant shutdowns,
- what additional state, owner, and management contributions are possible,
- which sites are truly strategic,
- and how job security can be preserved while competitiveness is restored.

If the capital side wants my support, it must show more than pressure. It must show a credible, quantified restructuring that protects the company **and** the region.
