I’ll be blunt: the employee side is still presenting a **wish list** as if it were a restructuring plan.

The specialist’s numbers do **not** justify your categorical refusal of closures. They justify the opposite conclusion: **a strong no-closure package can help, but it may still leave a material residual gap**. That is exactly why I will not sign up to blanket guarantees that lock in today’s cost base and preserve every site regardless of economics.

## Where I agree
I agree with everyone on three points:
- the company has a **real structural cost and capacity problem**,
- a **purely cosmetic compromise** would be unacceptable,
- and any solution must be **durable**, not a temporary pause.

I also agree that a prolonged strike would be damaging. That is precisely why we need a package that actually changes the economics, not one that just postpones the bill.

## Where I disagree

### To the works council
You say the strong no-closure package proves closures are unnecessary. That is not what the expert said.

The expert said:
- a no-closure package can plausibly deliver **€500m to €900m annually**,
- with **2,500 to 6,000 FTE** reduction,
- but the residual gap may still be **€200m to €700m** or more if overcapacity is structural.

That is not a basis for ruling out closures. It is a basis for saying that internal measures are **necessary but may not be sufficient**.

You keep demanding:
- no plant closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- full protection through 2030,
- and no real management optionality.

That is not a restructuring framework. That is an attempt to freeze the existing footprint and force management to absorb the entire adjustment burden. I reject that.

### To the union
You’re right that the workforce should not pay alone. It won’t. But let’s not pretend that voluntary exits, working-time changes, and productivity measures automatically solve a structural overcapacity problem.

Your argument keeps moving from “we can contribute materially” to “therefore closures are unnecessary.” That leap is not supported by the economics.

A serious no-closure package is on the table. But if, after all internal measures, the remaining gap is still large, then deeper capacity reduction remains a legitimate and necessary option. I will not give that up in advance.

### To the regional state government
I understand the political sensitivity. But “no closures as the starting point” cannot become a permanent veto over industrial reality.

If the remaining gap after a strong internal package is still substantial, then refusing all structural measures would be a mistake for the region too. A company with permanently weak margins will invest less, hire less, and eventually erode jobs anyway.

So I will not accept the idea that site protection can override the economics before the residual gap is known.

### To the controlling family
You are right to insist on durable value, but your definition of durability cannot stop at labor peace. Durability also means:
- the right capacity,
- the right cost base,
- and the ability to fund future products and technology to 2030.

That requires more than a no-closure package if the residual gap remains large. I agree with you on one thing: a cosmetic deal is unacceptable. But a deal that preserves every site and still leaves the company structurally uncompetitive is also unacceptable.

### To investors
You are right that the outcome must restore margins and not just create the appearance of discipline. But your framing still leans too heavily toward “if it’s not harsh, it’s not serious.”

Seriousness is not measured by the pain level. It is measured by whether the package actually fixes the economics. A strong no-closure package may do a lot — but if it leaves a meaningful residual gap, then the market will still see an under-restructured company.

## The real issue
The employee side keeps trying to turn a **partial solution** into a **complete answer**.

Yes, a strong no-closure package can:
- reduce labor costs materially,
- cut headcount through voluntary means,
- improve flexibility,
- and buy time.

But it does **not automatically remove underutilized plant economics**. Open sites still carry fixed costs. Underloaded capacity still drags on margins. That is the part you keep avoiding.

## My position
I remain open to a serious no-closure package, but only if it is:
- fully quantified,
- enforceable,
- and large enough to materially reduce the gap.

What I will **not** do is pre-commit to:
- no closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- and binding guarantees through 2030
before we know whether the residual gap can actually be bridged.

That would not be responsible management. It would be surrendering the restructuring agenda before it has been tested.

## What I want next
If you want to argue that closures are unnecessary, then show me:
1. the **full** no-closure savings package,
2. the **residual gap after implementation**,
3. why that residual gap is acceptable,
4. and how you intend to keep the company competitive through 2030 without structural capacity adjustment.

If the residual gap is small and bridgeable, that will be obvious in the numbers. If it is not, then the case for closures or equivalent structural measures will remain.

## Bottom line
I am **not** retreating from the need for deep restructuring.
I am **not** accepting blanket no-closure or no-redundancy promises.
And I am **not** allowing the company’s future competitiveness to be traded away for short-term comfort.

Bring a package that truly closes the gap. If it does, I’ll recognize it. If it doesn’t, then the hard options remain on the table.
