## Where I agree

### With management
I agree that:
- the company needs durable cost reduction,
- a cosmetic deal would be irresponsible,
- and investment capacity for future products must be protected.

I also agree that the company cannot simply freeze the status quo and call that a solution.

### With the controlling family and investors
I agree that the package must be:
- quantified,
- enforceable,

You are right that a weak compromise would just delay the problem.

### With the works council and union
I agree that the workforce cannot be the only party carrying the burden.  
I also agree that a negotiated solution is far better than open escalation.

## Where I disagree

### To management
You keep saying the residual gap may still justify closures. That may be true in theory, but you have **not yet shown** that it is true here.

The expert said a strong no-closure package can plausibly deliver:
- **€500m to €900m** in annual savings,
- **2,500 to 6,000 FTE** reduction,
- and material cost relief without plant closures.

That is a serious restructuring contribution. It is not “wishful thinking.”  
So I reject the idea that closures should remain the default threat while the alternative is only half-developed.

If you want credibility, then do the hard work:
- quantify the residual gap after the strongest internal package,
- show it site by site,
- show why voluntary exits, flexibility, redeployment, productivity, and management contribution are insufficient,
- and only then discuss whether any additional structural measures are truly unavoidable.

Until that is done, closures are **not proven necessary**.

### To the controlling family
You are right that value must be protected. But value is not protected by using plant closures as a first instrument.

A company like this does not build long-term value by:
- destroying trust,
- weakening co-determination,
- and turning the workforce into the first adjustment variable.

The specialist also made clear that closures carry:
- very high one-off costs,
- long execution times,
- ramp-down risk,
- and conflict risk.

So let us not pretend closures are the clean, disciplined option. They are a high-risk instrument, not a sign of strength.

### To institutional investors
You are right to insist on durability. But durability is not the same as harshness.

A durable solution can also be:
- a serious no-closure package,
- binding productivity measures,
- voluntary exits at scale,
- management and owner contributions,
- and a clear review point.

What I will not accept is the idea that anything short of closures is somehow unserious. That is not economics; that is ideology.

### To the works council and union
I support your core position: **no closures as the starting point, no compulsory redundancies, and real burden sharing**.

But I will also be frank with you: a no-closure package must be large and enforceable. It cannot be just a statement of principle. It must include:
- voluntary exits,
- working-time flexibility,
- internal redeployment,
- qualification,
- productivity improvements,
- and a fair, time-limited contribution from the workforce, management, and owners.

That is the standard if we want a deal that is both socially responsible and economically real.

## My position
As regional state government and anchor shareholder, my line remains unchanged:

- **No plant closures in Germany as the starting point**
- **No compulsory redundancies**
- **A serious no-closure alternative must be tested first**
- **Binding protection for sites and jobs wherever possible**
- **Shared burden from workforce, management, and owners**
- **Investment commitments for the German footprint**

That is not sentiment. It is responsible stewardship of a regional industrial anchor.

## What the expert means for the next step
The expert has given us the right sequence:
1. build the strongest possible **no-closure package**,
2. measure the actual savings and the residual gap,
3. only then decide whether any further structural measures are justified.

That is the only defensible order. Not closures first, alternatives later.

## What I want on the table now
I want a **side-by-side comparison** of:
- the strongest possible no-closure package,
- and any structural adjustment scenario,

with the same metrics:
- annual savings,
- one-off costs,
- headcount impact,
- capacity impact,
- timing,
- durability,
- and residual gap.

If the no-closure package materially closes the gap, then closures are **not** a first resort.  
If a residual gap remains after serious internal measures, then we can discuss what additional tools are necessary — but not before.

## Bottom line
I will not support a process that treats plant closures as the default answer before alternatives are exhausted in a serious, quantified way.

I am prepared to support a **real restructuring**.  
I am **not** prepared to support the gutting of the regional industrial base under the label of restructuring.

If management wants my support, it should bring a package that:
- protects jobs and sites,

That is the standard.
