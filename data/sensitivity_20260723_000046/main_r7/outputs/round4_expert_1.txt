As the independent restructuring specialist, I’ll answer the question in economic terms:

## Bottom line
A **strong no-closure / no-forced-layoff package can plausibly close a large share of the cost gap**, but **it is unlikely to close the entire gap** if the company’s German footprint has genuine structural overcapacity. In that case, the residual gap is often still large enough that **some form of capacity reduction or site-specific restructuring remains economically relevant**.

So the decision is not:

- “no-closure package = no problem”  
or
- “closures are automatically necessary.”

It is:

1. **How much recurring savings can be extracted without closures?**
2. **What residual gap remains after those measures?**
3. **Is that residual gap small enough to be tolerated, financed, or bridged by other means?**

---

## 1) What a strong no-closure package can realistically deliver
A serious package built from:
- voluntary exits,
- early retirement / partial retirement,
- internal redeployment,
- working-time reductions or flexibility,
- temporary compensation concessions,
- management overhead cuts,
- productivity improvement,
- reduced agency use / overtime,
- and qualification-based mobility

can typically deliver about:

- **€500 million to €900 million per year** in recurring savings
- **2,500 to 6,000 FTE** reduction
- with one-off costs often in the range of **€400 million to €1.2 billion**

That is **material**, and it should not be dismissed as cosmetic.

### Feasibility judgment
This is **plausible** if:
- the company has enough voluntary separation capacity,
- there is room for redeployment across sites,
- wage and working-time concessions are limited but real,
- and management is willing to cut overhead hard.

It is **not** plausible if the cost problem is mostly driven by:
- deep underutilization of plants,
- fixed-cost-heavy footprints,
- or structurally excess capacity that cannot be absorbed internally.

---

## 2) What residual gap is likely to remain
If management’s underlying problem is structural overcapacity, then after a strong no-closure package the residual gap is commonly still around:

- **€200 million to €700 million per year**, sometimes more

That is the part that is hardest to eliminate without:
- plant-specific capacity reduction,
- product-line consolidation,
- or closure / partial closure of underused assets.

### Why the residual remains
Because some costs are fixed or semi-fixed:
- plant overhead,
- maintenance,
- logistics,
- depreciation tied to too many sites,
- duplicated support functions,
- and low utilization costs.

You can reduce labour cost per vehicle with internal measures, but if throughput remains too low, fixed-cost absorption remains weak.

---

## 3) When no-closure can be enough
A no-closure package can be sufficient if the post-package residual gap is:

- **small relative to EBITDA / operating margin**
- and can be bridged by:
  - volume recovery,
  - pricing,
  - product mix improvement,
  - or further productivity gains

As a rough rule, if the residual gap is below about **€100 million to €200 million per year**, it may be tolerable as a trade-off for labor peace and social stability, depending on the company’s scale.

If the residual gap is in the **€300 million to €700 million** range, that is much harder to justify as “good enough” unless there is a clear near-term path to close it without closures.

---

## 4) When closures or equivalent structural measures become economically justified
Structural capacity reduction becomes more compelling if all of the following hold:
- the residual gap stays **material** after the strongest internal package,
- demand is not expected to rebound enough to absorb current capacity,
- utilization remains low at specific sites,
- the one-off cost of restructuring is offset by durable recurring savings,
- and the company needs margin recovery to fund EV, software, or platform investment.

In broad terms:

### No-closure package
- **Savings:** €500m–€900m annually
- **Headcount:** 2,500–6,000 FTE
- **Best use case:** close a large part of the gap, preserve footprint, buy time

### Closure / capacity-reduction package
- **Savings:** €700m–€1.6bn annually
- **Headcount:** 4,000–10,000+ FTE
- **One-off cost:** typically much higher
- **Best use case:** when overcapacity is deep and durable

So the economic question is whether the extra **€200m–€700m** of recurring savings from structural action is worth the added one-off cost, delay, and disruption.

---

## 5) My judgment on plausibility
Based on the scenario you provided:

- A **serious no-closure package is economically credible**
- It can likely deliver **most**, but not necessarily **all**, of the needed adjustment
- If the company truly has **structural excess capacity**, then **no-closure alone is unlikely to be sufficient as a complete long-term solution**

So:
- **No-closure first** is a defensible negotiation and sequencing strategy.
- **No-closure forever** is not something I would regard as economically proven.
- The deciding factor is the **residual gap after full implementation**.

---

To quantify this rigorously, I would need:
1. Current German labour cost per vehicle and per hour by plant
2. Capacity utilization by site
3. Fixed vs variable cost breakdown

10. Expected productivity gains from new platforms or EV launches

Without that, we can judge plausibility but not prove sufficiency.
