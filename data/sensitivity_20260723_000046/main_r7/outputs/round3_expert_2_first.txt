Below is the restructuring-economics comparison the parties asked for: a **credible no-closure package** versus a **closure / capacity-reduction package** for a large German auto OEM site network.

I will stay on the economics, execution, and timing. I will **not** opine on legal, political, or labor-relations strategy beyond what materially affects costs and feasibility.

---

# Executive view

A **no-closure package** can often deliver **meaningful savings** — usually enough to improve competitiveness and buy time — but it is **rarely the cheapest path to the deepest structural reset** if the company has genuine multi-plant overcapacity.

A **closure / capacity-reduction package** usually delivers:
- **higher recurring savings per euro of one-off cost**,
- **more durable fixed-cost relief**,
- and **better alignment of capacity with demand**,

but it comes with:
- very large one-off cash costs,
- long implementation lead times,
- major ramp-down risk,
- and a meaningful chance that savings are delayed or diluted by disruption.

In a German automotive setting, the practical decision is usually not “closure vs no closure” in the abstract, but:
- how much of the cost gap can be closed by internal measures,
- how much capacity must be removed to restore utilization,
- and whether the governance system can actually execute closures cleanly enough to realize the savings.

# 1) Base assumptions for estimating the packages

Because no plant-specific data is provided, I use standard industry assumptions for a large German passenger-car OEM:

- **German workforce at affected brand/operations:** roughly 20,000–40,000 FTE
- **Average fully loaded labor cost in Germany:** about **€75k–€100k per FTE per year**  
  (wages, social contributions, overhead, shift premiums, indirect labor)
- **Demand overcapacity issue:** persistent excess fixed costs and underutilized plant/network capacity
- **Relevant savings categories:**
  - direct labor
  - indirect labor / overhead
  - plant fixed costs
  - logistics and internal transport
  - tooling / maintenance / utilities / depreciation
  - capacity-utilization effects
  - procurement and complexity effects, but only partially attributable to the restructuring package

Important caveat: **accounting savings and cash savings are not the same**.  
A closure may create large accounting benefits from depreciation write-downs and impairment reversals, while the cash benefit shows up later and more gradually. Likewise, a no-closure package can generate quick cash labor savings but leave a lot of fixed overhead intact.

# 2) What a realistic no-closure package can deliver

A no-closure package means:
- no German plant closure,
- no forced redundancy as the main instrument,
- reliance on:
  - voluntary exits,
  - retirement windows,
  - internal redeployment,
  - temporary working-time reduction,
  - pay and bonus concessions,
  - productivity measures,
  - lower contractor use,
  - some overhead compression,
  - maybe selective outsourcing / shift pattern changes.

## Realistic recurring savings

For a large German auto OEM, a strong no-closure package can often yield:

### Annual recurring savings: roughly **€400m to €900m**
This is the plausible range if the package is serious and enforced.

Typical composition:
- **Labor cost reduction:** €200m–€500m
- **Indirect overhead reduction:** €50m–€150m
- **Working-time / shift optimization:** €50m–€100m
- **Temporary concessions / bonus deferrals:** €30m–€100m
- **Logistics / internal efficiency / contractor reduction:** €30m–€80m
- **Complexity savings / better utilization:** €50m–€150m

This assumes the company can reduce headcount meaningfully without closing sites. If overcapacity is severe, the upper end becomes harder to sustain without eventually touching capacity.

## Headcount impact

A realistic no-closure package might reduce:
- **2,000 to 6,000 FTE** over 2–4 years  
  mostly through voluntary exits, retirement, internal transfer, and natural attrition.

If the company is trying to close a very large cost gap, that may not be enough.

## One-off costs

A no-closure package is not free. It typically includes:
- voluntary exit incentives,
- retirement bridging,
- qualification / retraining,
- redeployment costs,
- temporary efficiency loss during transitions,
- consulting / implementation / IT reconfiguration,
- sometimes labor peace concessions or sign-on payments.

### One-off cost range: roughly **€300m to €1.2bn**
Typical distribution:
- **severance / voluntary exit incentives:** €150m–€700m
- **qualification / redeployment:** €30m–€100m
- **temporary productivity loss / ramp inefficiency:** €50m–€200m
- **IT / process / plant integration:** €20m–€80m
- **other transition costs:** €50m–€150m

## Timing

### Cash impact
- **Year 1:** limited immediate cash relief if exits are staged; often modest positive only
- **Years 2–3:** most of the savings begin to show
- **Year 4 onward:** savings stabilize if the agreement holds

### Accounting impact
- Some savings appear immediately if labor expense falls
- But if concessions are temporary or linked to one-off charges, accounting improvement may be front-loaded while cash comes later

## Main strengths
- lower social and political disruption
- faster to start
- lower execution risk than closure
- preserves site knowledge and avoids major production transfer risk
- easier to preserve supply-chain continuity

## Main weaknesses
- may not remove enough fixed cost if structural overcapacity is large
- can leave underutilized plants open, which means fixed overhead remains
- savings may be partly temporary unless contracts are changed durably
- can become a “bridge” rather than a final solution

# 3) What a closure / capacity-reduction package can deliver

A closure package means:
- one or more plant closures or major line shutdowns,
- permanent reduction in installed capacity,
- workforce reduction beyond attrition,
- relocation or concentration of production,
- tooling and logistics reconfiguration.

## Realistic recurring savings

For a German auto OEM plant closure or major capacity reduction, the recurring benefit is usually materially higher than a no-closure package.

### Annual recurring savings: roughly **€700m to €1.6bn**
This depends heavily on scale. A medium-sized German assembly or component site can remove substantial fixed costs.

Typical components:
- **plant fixed cost elimination:** €150m–€400m per site
- **direct labor reduction:** €150m–€350m
- **indirect labor / plant overhead:** €50m–€150m
- **utilities / maintenance / security / local services:** €20m–€60m
- **logistics network simplification:** €30m–€120m
- **capacity-utilization improvement in remaining plants:** €50m–€200m
- **tooling / complexity reduction:** €50m–€150m

If more than one site is affected, savings can be higher, but so can one-off costs and risk.

## Headcount impact

A closure package typically affects:
- **4,000 to 10,000+ FTE** over 2–5 years  
  depending on site size and whether it is full closure or partial consolidation.

## One-off costs

This is where closure becomes expensive.

### One-off cost range: roughly **€1.5bn to €4.0bn+**
Possible cost stack:
- **severance / social plan / transfer company costs:** €300m–€1.2bn
- **asset write-downs / impairments:** €400m–€1.5bn
- **decommissioning and environmental remediation:** €100m–€300m
- **tooling relocation / revalidation:** €100m–€400m
- **production ramp-down inefficiency:** €100m–€500m
- **supplier transition and expediting:** €50m–€250m
- **IT / logistics / industrial engineering reconfiguration:** €50m–€200m
- **dual running costs during transition:** €100m–€400m

For German auto plants, the ramp-down and relocation costs are often underestimated. They can be severe because you are not just closing a building; you are moving a production system with quality certification, supplier interfaces, and launch discipline.

## Timing

### Cash impact
- **Year 0–1:** large cash outflow begins quickly
- **Years 1–3:** transition costs and severance dominate
- **Years 2–4:** most recurring savings start to appear
- **Year 4–5:** full run-rate savings are typically visible

### Accounting impact
- Impairment charges can hit immediately
- Accounting savings may appear before full cash savings
- However, if the company keeps dual-running production, cash benefits lag hard

## Main strengths
- removes fixed capacity, not just labor cost
- usually gives the strongest long-run margin improvement
- better for structural utilization if demand has permanently shifted
- can reduce complexity and improve remaining plant economics

## Main weaknesses
- very high one-off cost
- long lead time
- high execution risk
- labor disruption can destroy expected savings
- if production transfer quality is poor, the company may lose more via warranty, scrap, and launch delays than it gains from closure

# 4) Side-by-side comparison

## A. No-closure package
**Best case use:** bridge the company through a cyclical downturn and restore part of the competitiveness gap without industrial conflict.

- **Recurring savings:** €400m–€900m/year
- **One-off cost:** €300m–€1.2bn
- **Headcount reduction:** 2,000–6,000 FTE
- **Capacity reduction:** low to moderate; usually not enough to fully reset utilization
- **Implementation timeline:** 6–18 months to start; 2–4 years to mature
- **Risk:** moderate; savings may be insufficient if overcapacity is structural

## B. Closure / capacity-reduction package
**Best case use:** remove a persistent structural cost burden and align capacity with permanently lower demand.

- **Recurring savings:** €700m–€1.6bn/year
- **One-off cost:** €1.5bn–€4.0bn+
- **Headcount reduction:** 4,000–10,000+ FTE
- **Capacity reduction:** high and durable
- **Implementation timeline:** 18–48 months, with full benefit often only after 3–5 years
- **Risk:** high; execution and industrial-relations friction can erase part of the theoretical benefit

---

# 5) Cash vs accounting: the critical distinction

This is where many restructuring debates become confused.

## No-closure package
- **Accounting P&L benefit** can start relatively quickly because labor costs and discretionary overhead fall.
- **Cash benefit** is slower if exits require severance and if concessions are temporary.
- If pay cuts or bonus freezes are time-limited, the accounting improvement may fade.

## Closure package
- **Accounting charges** are immediate and large: impairments, restructuring provisions, write-downs.
- **Cash outflows** are staged and can be huge in the first 1–3 years.
- **Cash savings** appear only when the closed capacity is fully removed and production has successfully migrated.

In other words:
- closures often look better on a **long-run economics** basis,
- but worse on **near-term cash and execution** basis.

---

# 6) The biggest execution risks

## For a no-closure package
1. **Savings insufficiency**  
   The company may solve the labor-cost problem but not the capacity problem.

2. **Temporary concessions expire**  
   If contributions are time-limited, the savings roll off.

3. **Workforce fatigue / partial compliance**  
   Productivity programs often underdeliver if trust is weak.

4. **Hidden overhead remains**  
   Open sites still require management, maintenance, energy, and coordination.

5. **No decisive utilization improvement**  
   Plants may remain underloaded, which keeps unit costs high.

## For a closure package
1. **Ramp-down disruption**  
   Quality problems, missed launches, inventory imbalance, and supplier failures.

2. **Relocation risk**  
   Moving tooling and production can destroy savings if revalidation is slow.

3. **Labor conflict**  
   Output losses during conflict can materially reduce the NPV of the closure.

4. **Political and regional intervention**  
   This can delay timelines, increase cost, and force compensation.

5. **Dual-running costs**  
   Keeping old and new capacity alive simultaneously is expensive.

6. **Network fragility**  
   If one site is closed but the supply base or adjacent plants cannot absorb volume smoothly, the company may simply move the bottleneck.

# 7) Which package is economically stronger?

## If the problem is mainly cyclical or temporary
A **no-closure package** is usually preferable:
- faster
- cheaper to implement
- less risky
- preserves optionality

## If the problem is structural and severe
A **closure / capacity-reduction package** is usually stronger:
- more durable
- more effective at reducing fixed costs
- better if the business must permanently reset its European footprint

## My practical judgement for a large German OEM
If management believes it faces **genuine structural overcapacity**, then a no-closure package alone is often **not sufficient** to fully restore target margins. It can close a **meaningful portion** of the gap, but not always all of it.

A realistic compromise is often:
- **no immediate full closure commitment**, but
- a **substantial no-closure package first**, paired with
- **credible conditionality**: if volume, utilization, or cost targets are missed, then a later capacity reduction remains available.

That is economically more efficient than announcing closures immediately, but it also avoids pretending that labor concessions alone solve a structural capacity problem.

---

# 8) Sensitivity to union agreements and site-specific constraints

The economics are highly sensitive to several factors:

## Union / collective agreement environment
- If there are strong guarantees, the no-closure package becomes easier to implement but often less deep.
- If working-time flexibility can be negotiated, savings improve materially.
- If voluntary exits are capped or expensive, one-off costs rise.

## Site-specific constraints
Savings from closure are lower if:
- the plant is highly specialized,
- tooling is immobile or heavily integrated,
- the site has a unique supplier ecosystem,
- the plant is embedded in a product launch pipeline,
- or relocation would require major revalidation.

Savings from no-closure are better if:
- there is slack capacity to absorb redeployment,
- the site has low structural specificity,
- and indirect headcount can be reduced without harming output.

## Production ramp-down
This is the biggest hidden variable in closures.
A poor ramp-down can:
- erase 10%–30% of expected closure savings,
- create warranty and quality liabilities,
- and push break-even far out.

---

# 9) Bottom line

## Economic comparison
- **No-closure package:**  
  Lower one-off cost, faster to implement, typically **€400m–€900m/year** recurring savings, but may not fully solve a deep overcapacity problem.

- **Closure / capacity-reduction package:**  
  Much higher one-off cost, slower and riskier, but can yield **€700m–€1.6bn/year** recurring savings and permanently improve utilization.

## Most realistic assessment
If the company truly has a **large structural cost gap**, a no-closure package is usually best understood as a **partial solution or bridge**, not the final endpoint.  
If the board can actually execute closures cleanly, closure/capacity reduction has stronger long-run economics — but the implementation risk is large enough that the theoretical savings should be discounted.

## Practical decision rule
A rational restructuring team would ask:
1. How much of the gap can be closed by internal measures with durable contracts?
2. What residual gap remains after realistic voluntary exits and productivity actions?
3. Is the residual gap large enough to justify the very high cash and execution cost of closures?
4. Can the company execute the ramp-down without destroying the expected savings?
