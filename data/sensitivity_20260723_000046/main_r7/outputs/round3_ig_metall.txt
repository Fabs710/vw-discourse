## To management
You still have not answered the decisive question: **why are closures and forced redundancies necessary, rather than simply convenient for management?**

The economist has confirmed what we already knew:
- alternative measures can deliver **material savings**,
- voluntary exits, working-time changes, productivity measures, and contributions can close **part** of the gap,
- and only if the gap is truly large and persistent do closures become the preferred route from a capital perspective.

That is **not** the same as proving necessity. It means you prefer the fastest and harshest lever. I reject the idea that the burden of proof shifts to employees to justify their own job security.

If you want a settlement, then show us a package with:
- exact savings,
- exact sequencing,
- exact duration,
- exact burden-sharing,
- and an explanation of why the alternative measures are supposedly insufficient.

Until then, your “structural necessity” claim remains an assertion, not a case.

## To the controlling family
You say you want long-term value, but you keep treating the workforce as the first and biggest adjustment variable. That is not stewardship. It is a one-sided risk transfer.

A durable industrial company is not built by threatening plants and jobs until people accept cuts. It is built by:
- trust,
- skills,
- stable production,
- and a collective wage framework that employees can rely on.

If you break that trust, you do not create value — you create a conflict that will damage output, morale, recruitment, and implementation for years. That is not “discipline.” It is escalation.

## To institutional investors
You talk about durability and competitiveness, and on principle I agree: we do not want a cosmetic compromise that fails in 12 months.

But your conclusion is too one-sided. You are treating closures as the benchmark of seriousness. They are not. Seriousness is a package that actually works **and** can be implemented without tearing the company apart.

If you want durable savings, then support a deal with:
- voluntary exits at scale,
- working-time adjustment,
- qualification and redeployment,
- management and owner contribution,
- and a time-limited employee contribution.

That can be serious. What is not serious is presenting job destruction as the only “credible” proof of discipline.

## To the regional state government
I appreciate that you are not endorsing closures as a starting point. But I need to be equally clear: “please examine alternatives” is not enough if it is not backed by binding commitments.

If the state wants to use its role responsibly, it should push for:
- no plant closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- protection of the collective bargaining framework,
- and investment commitments for the German footprint.

That is the minimum for a real transformation agreement. Anything vaguer leaves the workforce carrying the risk while others keep optionality.

## On the expert assessment
The economist did **not** prove that closures are inevitable. The expert said:
- alternatives can produce meaningful savings,
- but may not fully match the economics of closing a plant if overcapacity is very large.

That is an important distinction. It means:
1. management has to quantify the actual gap,
2. employee-side measures have to be taken seriously,
3. and closures are only relevant if the gap remains after those measures are fully and transparently assessed.

You have not done that yet.

## What we are prepared to discuss
We remain open to a serious shared-sacrifice package:
- temporary working-time adjustments
- voluntary exit programmes
- internal redeployment
- qualification and reskilling
- targeted productivity measures
- fair, time-limited employee contributions
- management and owner contribution
- investment and product commitments

That is a real restructuring path. It is not a refusal of change.

## What remains non-negotiable
I will say it again plainly:
- **no plant closures in Germany**
- **no compulsory redundancies**
- **no weakening of the collective wage framework**
- **no settlement where employees bear the pain alone**

Those are not bargaining flourishes. They are the boundary of a legitimate deal.

## The burden now
If management and capital side insist that only closures and layoffs can work, then prove it with numbers:
- current overcapacity by site,
- exact savings from internal measures,
- exact residual gap,
- and why that gap cannot be closed without destroying sites and jobs.

If you cannot prove that, then your position is not a necessity-based restructuring plan. It is a demand that the workforce accept a one-sided correction.

We are prepared to contribute.
We are not prepared to capitulate.
We are prepared for a durable settlement.
We are not prepared for one built on closures, forced redundancies, and the erosion of collective standards.

If there is a real package that shares the burden fairly and protects jobs and sites, bring it. If not, then you should not be surprised when the workforce uses every legitimate tool available to defend itself.
