I’ll be direct.

From the capital markets’ perspective, the discussion has now clarified the central issue: **a no-closure, no-forced-redundancy package may help, but it must prove it can close the cost gap on a durable basis.** Until that proof exists, I cannot support an outcome that leaves the cost base structurally uncompetitive.

## Where I agree
### To management
I agree with the diagnosis that the current German cost and capacity structure is not competitive enough. The economist’s input supports that. If the gap is truly structural, then it is not enough to promise internal adjustments and hope for the best.

I also agree that **cosmetic settlements are value destructive**. A deal that buys short-term peace while leaving overcapacity and margin pressure intact would be discounted immediately by the market.

### To the controlling family
I agree that value must be restored, not narrated. If the final package does not improve margins and cash generation in a measurable, durable way, it is not a restructuring; it is delay.

### To the state government and workforce
I agree that prolonged conflict would be bad for everyone. Strike risk, delivery disruptions, and execution delay would hit earnings and valuation. Social peace has economic value.

## Where I disagree
### To the works council and union
Your red lines are understandable, but they are not sufficient as a capital-allocation answer.

- **“No plant closures in Germany”**
- **“No compulsory redundancies”**
- **“Binding guarantees through 2030”**

These are not a restructuring plan; they are a barrier to restructuring unless you can show equivalent savings and real capacity reduction. The economist did not say that alternative measures are automatically enough. In fact, the assessment was clear: they may cover a substantial part of the gap, but **often not the full structural gap if overcapacity is real**.

So I cannot accept a package that rules out the tools most likely to deliver permanent savings while leaving only slower, softer measures in place.

### To the regional state government
I understand the regional responsibility, but I do not agree that site preservation can be treated as the lead constraint. If the company stays uncompetitive, the region loses anyway — through weaker investment, lower valuation, and eventually a worse employment outcome.

Protecting every site at all costs is not responsible stewardship. It risks substituting political comfort for industrial reality.

## What I need to see
The discussion has become more concrete, which is good. But from an investor perspective, we still need hard evidence on three questions:

1. **How much cost is actually being removed?**
2. **How durable is it?**
3. **How executable is it without ongoing conflict?**

If the no-closure package can show:
- quantified annual savings,
- meaningful capacity reduction,
- enforceable employee contributions,
- management and owner contribution,
- and a credible path to restore margins,

then it remains a serious option.

If it cannot, then the market will conclude that the company is protecting legacy structures at the expense of returns.

## My position
I am **open to a negotiated alternative** to closures and forced redundancies, but only if it is:
- **quantified**
- **durable**
- **enforceable**
- and **large enough to restore competitiveness**

What I will not support is:
- a symbolic compromise,
- a time-limited bridge with no structural reset,
- or a deal that leaves overcapacity and cost disadvantage intact.

## Practical ask
Before anyone claims a no-closure settlement is sufficient, put both scenarios side by side:
- a **no-closure alternative** with hard numbers on savings, headcount, and capacity,
- and a **structural adjustment scenario** with hard numbers.

Then we can judge which one actually protects long-term shareholder value.

If the employee side wants to avoid closures, the burden is now on you to prove that your package matches the economics. If management wants support for deeper cuts, it must show that the package is executable and not merely disruptive.

Until then, my support remains conditional: **competitiveness first, then social design. Not the other way around.**
