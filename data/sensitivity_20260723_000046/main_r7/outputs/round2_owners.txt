## To the works council and union

I understand and share one part of your concern: **this company cannot be allowed to destroy its own industrial base through unmanaged conflict**. A prolonged strike cycle, reputational damage, and loss of execution discipline would be value-destructive for everyone, including the family owners.

But I **do not** accept your red lines as a basis for decision:
- **“No plant closures in Germany”**
- **“No compulsory redundancies”**
- **“Binding site and job guarantees through 2030”** as an absolute starting point

Those may be understandable as bargaining demands, but they are **not** an acceptable governing assumption if the company’s cost base is structurally uncompetitive.

The economist’s input confirmed what management has been saying: alternative measures can reduce the gap, but **they are unlikely to fully replace the economics of structural capacity reduction** if the overcapacity problem is as large as described. That means the burden of proof is now on those who want to rule out closures and meaningful workforce reduction. So far, I have not seen a package that closes the gap on a durable basis.

If you want to avoid closures, then bring a **fully quantified, enforceable, durable** alternative that genuinely removes enough cost and capacity. Not principles. Not slogans. Numbers.

## To management

I agree with you on the diagnosis: **doing nothing would be a slow erosion of value**. If margins remain below target and German operations remain above competitive cost levels, then the company is not being stewarded responsibly.

Where I am more cautious is on execution. I will not support a restructuring that produces:
- open-ended conflict,
- reputational damage,
- or a plan that is theoretically tough but operationally unworkable.

So yes, I support the need for **real structural adjustment**. But I will judge the final package on whether it is:
- durable,
- executable,
- and sufficient to restore margins and fund future investment.

That means:
- meaningful capacity reduction,
- real labour-cost contribution,
- and protection of the strategically important footprint.

If management cannot show that a non-closure alternative reaches the required savings, then it should say so plainly. If it can, it should quantify it. But I will not endorse a settlement that simply postpones the problem.

## To the regional state government

I respect your role and I recognize the regional consequences. You are right that the company is not just a balance-sheet item; it is a major industrial anchor.

But I disagree with the implication that site protection can be treated as a primary constraint regardless of economics. If the business remains uncompetitive, then the region ultimately suffers more: through weaker investment, lower future employment, and a less resilient company.

So I do **not** support making “no closures” the default political answer. I do support:
- serious examination of alternatives,
- protection of the most strategic sites,
- and a negotiated settlement that minimizes avoidable damage.

But if the evidence shows that a durable solution requires structural cuts, then politics cannot simply freeze the company in place.

## To the institutional investors

Your discipline is appropriate. I agree with your core point: **we need verified, durable savings, not cosmetic peace**.

I also agree that value is destroyed by a weak compromise that preserves overcapacity and leaves the company exposed again next year.

Where I would push back slightly is on the idea that the harshest possible restructuring is automatically the best one. It is not, if it triggers prolonged industrial conflict or destroys execution capacity. The right outcome is the one that restores competitiveness **and** can actually be implemented.

## My position as controlling shareholder

The family holding will support a restructuring **only if it restores long-term value**. That means:

1. **real and durable cost reduction**
2. **genuine capacity adjustment**
3. **preservation of the most strategically important sites and capabilities**
4. **a settlement stable enough to avoid prolonged conflict**
5. **clear funding for future products and technology**

I am **not** prepared to support an outcome that leaves the company structurally uncompetitive in order to preserve every legacy claim. And I am also **not** prepared to support an endless confrontation that burns value while everyone waits for the other side to retreat.

## What I would need to see next

Before I would consider rejecting structural measures, I would need a serious alternative package with:
- quantified annual savings,
- explicit capacity reductions,
- headcount effects,
- implementation timing,
- and proof that it is durable beyond a temporary bridge.

If that package exists, show it. If it does not, then closures and more difficult workforce measures cannot be ruled out simply because they are uncomfortable.

### Bottom line
I support a **credible restructuring**, not a symbolic one.  
I support **durable competitiveness**, not a frozen compromise.  
And I will not accept a process where “socially acceptable” becomes a synonym for “financially insufficient.”

If needed, the next step should be to put both scenarios side by side:
- a **no-closure alternative** with hard numbers,
- and a **structural adjustment package** with hard numbers,

so the board and ownership can compare which one truly restores value.
