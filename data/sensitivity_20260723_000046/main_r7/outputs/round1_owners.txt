I appreciate the positions laid out, and I will be equally direct from the perspective of the controlling family holding.

### Where I agree
I agree on the need for a **credible industrial solution**. This company cannot drift. If the cost base stays structurally too high and capacity remains misaligned with demand, value will erode, and that hurts everyone: employees, the region, and the long-term owners.

I also agree that a **purely unilateral approach would be wrong**. This must be settled in a way that is durable and that preserves labour peace to the greatest extent possible. A short-lived “win” that triggers prolonged conflict would destroy value.

### Where I disagree
I do **not** agree with the idea that plant closures or workforce reductions can simply be ruled out in advance.

If we start from the premise that:
- every German plant must remain open,
- there can be no meaningful employment reduction,
- and all existing guarantees must remain untouched,

then we are not negotiating restructuring anymore. We are negotiating denial. That is not a responsible way to steward a major industrial asset.

I also do **not** accept a solution that protects form over substance. A site guarantee or job guarantee has value only if the company remains competitive enough to sustain it. Otherwise, we are postponing the problem and potentially making the eventual correction harsher.

### On management’s position
Management is right about one core point: **the current structure is not competitive enough**. If there is a verified gap between cost base and market reality, then that gap must be closed. I am prepared to support difficult measures if they are part of a credible plan to restore margins, fund investment, and secure the company’s future.

But I am not giving a blank cheque either. If management wants support for major restructuring, it must show:
- a **hard, credible business case**,
- a **durable path to competitiveness**,
- and a package that is not simply value destruction dressed up as strategy.

### On the workforce and union demands
I understand the insistence on social responsibility. The workforce should not bear the full burden of years of strategic and structural pressure. But “no closures, no compulsory redundancies” as an absolute line is not a solution; it is a veto on any real adjustment.

If alternatives can deliver comparable savings and real capacity reduction, I am open to them. But they must be **measurable, durable, and sufficient**. Vague promises of working-time changes, redeployment, or voluntary exits are not enough if the underlying overcapacity remains.

### On the state government’s position
I respect the concern for regional employment and the company’s role in the region. That is legitimate. But the state also has to be careful not to defend every existing site and every existing structure irrespective of economics. If the company’s competitiveness collapses, the regional consequences will be worse, not better.

So I do not support a political line that effectively pre-commits the outcome to no closures at any price.

### My position
The controlling family holding will support a restructuring **only if it restores long-term value**. That means:
- real and durable cost reduction,
- genuine capacity adjustment,
- protection of the most strategically important parts of the footprint,
- and labour peace that is stable, not merely temporary.

I am open to a negotiated solution, but not to a settlement that preserves every site and every legacy guarantee while leaving the business structurally uncompetitive.

If the other stakeholders can produce a package that achieves the necessary savings without closures, fine — let it be shown in numbers, not assertions. But if they cannot, then we should not pretend that avoiding every difficult decision is in the company’s interest.
