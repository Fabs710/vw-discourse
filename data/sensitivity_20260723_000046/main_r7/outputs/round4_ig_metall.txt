I will be equally direct.

The works council is right on the core point: **a serious no-closure package is economically real**. The expert has confirmed that it can deliver **material savings** and a substantial headcount reduction **without** plant closures. That matters. It means closures are **not** the automatic or necessary starting point management keeps implying.

But I will also be clear where I differ from the works council: a no-closure package is only acceptable if it is **durable, quantified, and shared fairly**. A one-off gesture or a temporary wage hit with no management/owner contribution is not enough.

## To management
You still have not proven that closures or forced redundancies are necessary.

What the expert said is:
- a strong no-closure package can plausibly deliver **€500m to €900m annually**
- with **2,500 to 6,000 FTE** reduction through voluntary and internal measures
- and that only if the residual gap remains materially large do closures become more attractive from a capital perspective

That is **not** proof of necessity. It is proof that there is a serious alternative which must be exhausted first.

So I reject your attempt to keep closures as a standing threat while asking workers to concede up front. That is not a restructuring plan; it is leverage.

### What I could accept from management
A real package that includes:
- voluntary exits at scale
- internal redeployment
- qualification and reskilling
- working-time flexibility
- productivity improvements
- management and owner contribution
- and a **time-limited** employee contribution

But I would still require:
- **no compulsory redundancies**
- **no plant closures as part of the initial settlement**
- **binding site and job protection for the agreement period**

If you want our cooperation, then put the numbers on the table for the strongest no-closure deal and commit to it.

## To the controlling family
You keep saying you want durable value. Fine — then stop treating the workforce as the first and easiest adjustment variable.

The expert also said closures carry:
- high one-off costs
- long lead times
- ramp-down and transfer risk
- and conflict costs that can destroy expected savings

So no, closures are not some clean management tool. They are a high-risk option with large social and operational damage.

If you want long-term value, then support a settlement that:
- protects the German footprint
- removes costs through internal measures first
- and shares the burden across workers, management, and owners

Anything else is not stewardship. It is a one-sided transfer of risk to employees.

## To institutional investors
You are right about one thing: the deal must be **durable**. I agree completely. We do not want a cosmetic compromise that collapses in 12 months.

But you are wrong if you equate “durable” with “closer to closure.” Durability also comes from:
- stable production
- preserved know-how
- functioning co-determination
- employee buy-in
- and an agreement the workforce can actually live with

A settlement that triggers warning strikes, demoralization, and operational disruption is not durable, even if it looks harsh on paper.

So if you want a credible deal, then support the strongest possible no-closure package and accept that employees are already offering real contribution. The burden cannot rest on the workforce alone.

## To the regional state government
I welcome your rejection of closures as a starting point. But I need more than that.

If you are serious about defending the industrial base, then you should back:
- no closures in the initial agreement
- no compulsory redundancies
- binding investment commitments
- and protection of the collective bargaining framework

The state cannot simply say “test alternatives first” and then leave the company’s option to close plants hanging over the workforce as a permanent threat.

## On the expert assessment
The key point is simple:

- a strong no-closure package can close a **large share** of the problem
- the residual gap may remain, but that does **not** automatically justify closures
- management must prove, site by site and with transparent numbers, that internal measures are truly insufficient

Until then, closures remain a **choice**, not a necessity.

## What I am prepared to discuss
I am prepared to discuss:
- voluntary exits
- redeployment
- qualification
- temporary working-time adjustments
- productivity measures
- a fair, time-limited employee contribution
- management and owner contribution
- investment and product commitments

But only in a framework where:
1. **no German plant closures are part of the agreement**
2. **no compulsory redundancies**
3. **the collective wage framework is preserved**
4. **workers do not bear the pain alone**
5. **the settlement is binding and durable**

## What I reject outright
I reject:
- closures as a bargaining weapon
- compulsory layoffs
- open-ended job insecurity
- any settlement that destroys the collective wage framework
- any “solution” where employees carry the burden while management and owners retain full flexibility

## My bottom line
A **strong no-closure package** is the only legitimate basis for a settlement.

If management and capital side are serious, then bring a fully quantified package that removes the cost gap without breaking the sites and without forced redundancies. If they cannot do that, then they are not asking for a solution — they are asking for capitulation.

And if they choose confrontation, the workforce has the means to respond.
