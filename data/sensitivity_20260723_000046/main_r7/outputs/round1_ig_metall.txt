I agree with the central line you have drawn: **no plant closures, no compulsory redundancies**. That is also my position, and I will say it clearly because the workforce is already under pressure and patience is not unlimited.

At the same time, I want to be precise about what can and cannot be part of a settlement.

### Where I agree
- **The burden cannot fall only on employees.** If management wants restructuring, then management, the owners, and the company must also contribute.
- **There must be a binding framework for jobs and sites.** A vague promise is not enough.
- **Alternatives to closures must be exhausted first**: working-time adjustments, internal redeployment, qualification, voluntary departures, temporary capacity reduction, and a fair internal cost contribution.

### Where I disagree with management’s current approach
If the proposal still relies on:
- closing German plants,
- forced layoffs,
- or stripping away core protections without equivalent guarantees,

then it is unacceptable. That is not restructuring, that is shifting the crisis onto the workforce.

### What a serious negotiated path would require
If management wants a deal, then it must put real substance on the table:

1. **No plant closures in Germany**
2. **No compulsory redundancies**
3. **A binding site and job security agreement**
4. **A fair employee contribution**, but only as part of shared sacrifice
5. **Clear management and owner contributions**
6. **A credible industrial and investment plan**
7. **Protection of the collective bargaining framework**

We are not refusing change. We are refusing a one-sided cut-and-burn strategy. If savings are needed, they must come through a balanced package, not through destruction of jobs and locations.

And to be equally clear: if the company insists on closures and layoffs, then it should expect industrial action, because we will not accept workers being made to pay for this crisis alone.
