I’ll be direct.

The latest round has clarified the economics, but it has **not** removed the need for structural change. I hear the employee side’s argument that a strong no-closure package can generate meaningful savings. I agree with that. But I do **not** agree with the conclusion that this alone is enough to rule out deeper measures in advance.

## Where I agree
I agree with management, investors, and the state government on one central point:  
this company has a **real structural competitiveness problem**. Doing nothing would erode value. A cosmetic deal would be irresponsible.

I also agree with the works council and union that:
- the workforce should not be made to carry the entire burden,
- a negotiated settlement is preferable to open conflict,
- and internal measures should be taken seriously.

## Where I disagree

### To the works council and union
You are treating the expert’s no-closure range as if it proves your case. It does not.

Yes, the specialist says a strong no-closure package could plausibly deliver:
- **€500m to €900m annually**
- **2,500 to 6,000 FTE** reduction
- without plant closures

That is **material**. I accept that. But the same assessment also says that if the structural gap is large, a residual **€200m to €700m** annual gap may remain. That is not a trivial remainder. For governance, that is exactly the kind of gap that keeps a company under pressure and keeps margins too weak to support future investment.

So I reject the idea that “no closures” is established as sufficient. It is not. It is a credible **first step**, not necessarily the final solution.

You are also asking for:
- no closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- binding guarantees through 2030,
- and full protection of all German sites,

while leaving management and owners with no real fallback if the savings prove insufficient. That is not a restructuring framework. It is a demand to pre-empt the entire adjustment risk onto capital while keeping all strategic flexibility off the table.

I will not accept that.

### To management
I agree with you that internal measures may not fully close the gap. But you still need to be disciplined in how you present that case.

Do **not** argue as if closures are automatically the right answer. The specialist did not say that. The specialist said:
- strong internal measures can close a large share,
- closures become economically stronger only if a material residual gap remains after a serious package,
- and execution risk matters a great deal.

That means the right approach is not “closures now,” but “prove the residual gap after the strongest feasible internal package.” On that, I am with you. But if you jump too quickly to closure language, you will trigger unnecessary instability and destroy trust before the numbers are fully tested.

### To the regional state government
I respect your role as anchor shareholder and your political responsibility. I agree that closures should not be the starting point. But “not the starting point” cannot become “never, regardless of economics.”

If after a serious no-closure package there is still a substantial recurring gap, then refusal to consider any structural measure would be value destructive. The region does not benefit from preserving every site today if that means weaker investment, weaker product competitiveness, and eventual decline.

So I support your sequencing instinct, but not a blanket veto on structural adjustment.

### To institutional investors
You are right that the outcome must be durable and not cosmetic. I agree. But durability is not the same as harshness. A deal is durable if it:
- restores competitiveness,
- protects investment capacity,
- and avoids a prolonged destructive conflict.

If a no-closure package materially narrows the gap and keeps the company stable, that is valuable. If it leaves a large residual burden, it is insufficient. The key is not ideology. It is whether the package actually restores the economics.

## My position
As controlling shareholder, I will back a restructuring that does **all** of the following:
1. materially lowers the cost base,
2. restores margin discipline,
3. protects long-term investment capacity,
4. avoids value-destroying instability,
5. and preserves governance control and strategic flexibility.

That means:
- I support a **serious no-closure package** as the first test.
- I do **not** support a blanket no-closure promise.
- I do **not** support a blanket no-redundancy promise.
- I do **not** support binding guarantees that remove all restructuring flexibility through 2030.

## What I want next
Before anyone asks me to rule out structural measures, I want the strongest no-closure package presented in hard numbers:
- annual savings
- one-off costs
- headcount impact
- capacity impact
- implementation timing
- durability beyond the initial agreement cycle
- and the **residual gap** after those measures

If that residual gap is truly modest and bridgeable, then we can judge accordingly.  
If it remains material, then deeper restructuring remains on the table. That is the only responsible governance position.

## Bottom line
I am **not** here to protect legacy structures for their own sake.  
I am here to protect the long-term value of the core asset.

That means I support:
- serious internal adjustment first,
- no symbolic compromise,
- and no pre-commitment that strips management and owners of the tools needed to restore competitiveness.

If the no-closure package truly closes the gap, good. If it doesn’t, then we must not pretend otherwise.
