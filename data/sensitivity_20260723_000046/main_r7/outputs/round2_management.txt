The economist’s contribution is useful because it confirms the basic point management has been making from the start: **alternative measures may reduce costs, but they are unlikely to eliminate a large structural gap if overcapacity is real**.

That matters. Because we are not debating a marginal efficiency tweak. We are debating whether this company remains competitively structured for the next product cycle.

## To the works council and union
I hear your red lines. I understand them politically. But I will not pretend they solve the business problem.

You say:
- no plant closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- no dismantling of job guarantees,
- binding site security to 2030.

My response is unchanged: **I cannot accept those as absolute preconditions** if they block the structural adjustment the business needs.

The expert just made clear why:
- working-time measures and productivity improvements help, but only to a point,
- voluntary exits are slow and costly,
- wage concessions can be temporary,
- and none of that fully removes fixed overhead if capacity remains too high.

So if you are asking me to rule out closures and workforce reductions in advance, you are asking me to accept a package that may be **socially easier but economically insufficient**. I will not do that.

## To the state government
I appreciate the political sensitivity and the regional impact. But regional importance does not cancel arithmetic.

If we preserve every site regardless of utilisation and cost, we may protect the status quo for a while — and then undermine the company’s ability to invest, compete, and employ in the future. That is not protection. That is deferred decline.

I am willing to support a solution that protects the most strategic sites and the regional industrial base, **if** it also delivers real capacity reduction and durable savings. But I will not support a line that turns “no closures” into a veto on meaningful restructuring.

## To the controlling family and investors
You are right on the core issue: value has to be restored, not just narrated. But let me be equally clear: support for tough restructuring is only meaningful if it translates into an actual decision, not just rhetoric about discipline.

I need a package that is:
- durable,
- executable,
- and large enough to close the cost gap materially.

Anything less will leave us with a cosmetic outcome and renewed pressure in 12 months.

## What the expert advice means in practice
The economist’s assessment supports a hard truth:

- A **no-closure, no-forced-redundancy** package may produce meaningful savings.
- But if the gap is as large as management’s diagnosis suggests, it likely **will not be sufficient on its own**.

That is exactly why I am not prepared to rule out structural measures.

## My position
I am open to a negotiated settlement, but only if it contains **real structural content**. That means:

1. **Meaningful capacity reduction**
2. **Material labour-cost contribution**
3. **A credible path to lower unit cost**
4. **Protection of the most strategic investment footprint**
5. **No deal that leaves the company uncompetitive in practice**

What I will not accept is a “deal” that:
- preserves all existing sites,
- avoids all hard decisions,
- and leaves us with the same overcapacity and the same cost base under a different label.

## Bottom line
The expert has not given anyone a free pass. If anything, the expert has confirmed that management is right to insist on **durable structural savings**, and wrong to accept a cosmetic compromise.

So I say this plainly to the employee side:  
if you want to avoid closures, then bring me a package that **matches the economics**, not just the principle. If you cannot do that, then closures remain on the table because the company cannot be governed by a cost structure that is known to be uncompetitive.

I am prepared to negotiate the form of the restructuring. I am **not** prepared to negotiate away the need for deep, durable change.
