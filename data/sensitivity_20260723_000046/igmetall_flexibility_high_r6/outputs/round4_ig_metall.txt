I’ll respond directly.

## Where I agree
I agree with the independent expert on one important point: **internal measures can deliver a lot, but not automatically everything**. That is exactly why we have been saying from the start that this must be a serious package, not a cosmetic one.

I also agree that **utilization matters**. If capacity is too fragmented or too low, then you cannot solve everything with speeches about efficiency. You need real operational changes, and we are prepared to discuss those.

And I agree with the broad diagnosis from management, owners, and the state that the status quo is unsustainable. That was never our dispute.

## Where I disagree
I disagree with the way the “numbers” are being used to push the discussion toward closures as the default answer.

The expert says internal restructuring may not always fully substitute for footprint reduction. Fine. But that is not the same as proving that **closures are necessary now**. It only proves that management must present a serious comparison, plant by plant, function by function, and that it must not hide behind abstractions.

What I do **not** accept is this logic:
- management declares a structural gap,
- then immediately assumes closures are the benchmark,
- and then asks workers to concede first because the closure option is supposedly more “realistic.”

That is not proof. That is a preferred outcome looking for justification.

## On the independent expert’s assessment
The expert is useful, but the conclusion has to be read carefully.

### Yes, internal measures are limited by utilization
Agreed. But that cuts both ways. If utilization is the issue, then management must show:
- where volume allocation can be improved,
- where product assignment can be shifted,
- where logistics can be simplified,
- and what management itself is prepared to cut.

If you have not exhausted those levers, then you have not proven that closures are necessary.

### Yes, closures can remove more fixed cost
Of course they can. But closures also create:
- social costs,
- implementation risk,
- legal conflict,
- trust destruction,
- and possible production transfer problems.

Those costs are not side issues. They directly affect whether a deal is durable and whether savings are actually realized. The expert correctly notes that closure savings are not free.

That is why the burden is on management and shareholders to prove not merely theoretical savings, but **real net savings after execution risk**.

## To management
You keep saying closures or deeper adjustment remain necessary tools. I say: **show it, specifically**.

If you want us to take the closure threat seriously, then put the numbers on the table:
- cost per vehicle by site,
- direct and indirect labor hours,
- logistics and overhead costs,
- one-time closure costs,
- transfer risks,
- ramp-up losses elsewhere,
- and the comparison against a non-closure scenario.

Until then, “closures may be necessary” is a slogan, not an argument.

And let me be very clear: **the workforce will not accept being told to pre-pay for management’s strategic uncertainty.**

## To the works council
I support your insistence on:
- no forced redundancies,
- no closure-first logic,
- binding protection,
- and no backdoor surrender.

I also support your call for independent benchmarking. That is the right move. But I want to sharpen one point: benchmarking must not become a technocratic cover for pre-decided cuts. It has to test whether management has truly used all alternatives first.

That means the study must include:
- utilization improvements,
- shift and working-time changes,
- redeployment potential,
- qualification,
- overhead cuts,
- procurement and sourcing changes,
- investment discipline,
- and management contribution.

Not just headcount and wage costs.

## To the state government
I appreciate your position more than some others here, because you are at least resisting the normalization of closures.

But I want to be blunt: “closures only as a last resort” is still too vague unless you are prepared to say what must happen before that last resort is even considered. Workers cannot be asked to make open-ended concessions while management keeps a closure trigger in its pocket.

If the state wants to protect the region, then it should back a package that includes:
- binding non-closure commitments for the agreed period,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- investment in German products,
- and independent review before any escalation.

That is what regional responsibility looks like.

## To the family holding and investors
You both keep emphasizing competitiveness and value preservation. Good. Then I need to say this plainly:

A deal that depends on pushing fear onto the workforce is not value-preserving. It is conflict-generating. And a conflict-heavy restructuring can destroy part of the very value you say you want to protect.

So if you want a durable margin improvement, then you need to accept a framework in which:
- employees contribute,
- management contributes,
- owners contribute,
- and the company commits not to turn the restructuring into a pretext for dismantling protections.

That is the only form of burden-sharing that can be defended to members and implemented without open-ended industrial conflict.

## Our position after the expert contribution
The expert has not moved us off our core position. If anything, the expert confirms the need for **proper comparison before any closure logic is used**.

So our position remains:

1. **No compulsory redundancies**
2. **No plant closures as a first resort**
3. **Binding job and site security through 2030**
4. **Temporary, limited, reversible employee contribution**
5. **Visible management and owner contribution**
6. **Independent benchmark study comparing options**
7. **No automatic closure trigger if targets are missed**

## What we can accept
We are ready to discuss:
- working-time flexibility,
- shift changes,
- internal redeployment,
- qualification,
- temporary wage moderation,
- productivity measures,
- utilization improvements,
- voluntary exits,
- and targeted internal consolidation.

But only if this is part of a fair package that protects jobs and sites, and only if management and owners do not force workers to carry the structural burden alone.

## What we cannot accept
We cannot accept:
- forced layoffs,
- closures as bargaining leverage,
- wage concessions without binding protection,
- or a “review” that turns into a staged route to plant shutdowns.

We now need a genuinely neutral panel, jointly appointed by all parties, to compare:
- non-closure restructuring,
- voluntary/internal capacity reduction,
- and closure-based restructuring,
including execution risk, transfer losses, and net savings over time.

Not a management consultant. Not a union-aligned counterstudy. A jointly mandated panel with access to site-level data.

## Bottom line
I am not softening my core line because the debate has become more technical.

Technical analysis is welcome. It does **not** change the fact that we will not accept a settlement where workers bear the pain, plants are sacrificed, and management calls that “responsible restructuring.”

If the company wants a durable agreement, it must prove that a non-closure path is insufficient **after** all fair internal levers have been tested — not before. Until then, our answer remains clear: protect the jobs, protect the sites, and make the burden-sharing real.
