I appreciate that all sides now acknowledge the need for a real restructuring and that a cosmetic solution would fail. On that point, we are aligned.

But I need to be equally clear: as the Regional State Government and anchor shareholder, we will **not** support a restructuring that solves a competitiveness problem by hollowing out the company’s German industrial base.

## Where I agree
I agree with management, the family holding, and investors on one central issue: the status quo is not sustainable. The company needs durable savings, better efficiency, and a credible path back to competitiveness.

I also agree that a settlement must be economically real, not symbolic. Temporary fixes without structural improvement would only postpone the problem.

And I agree that a prolonged industrial conflict would be damaging. That is precisely why it is irresponsible to treat plant closures and mass layoffs as the default answer.

## Where I disagree
### 1) Plant closures are not a first resort
Management keeps speaking as if closures are simply the logical consequence of “overcapacity.” From our perspective, that is too narrow and too risky.

This company is a regional economic anchor. A plant closure is not just an internal optimization measure. It destroys jobs, supplier networks, skills, and regional confidence. Those effects are long-term and often irreversible.

So no, we do **not** accept closures as the starting point. They may be discussed only if every serious alternative has been exhausted and only if there is a truly compelling case that no other route can deliver comparable savings.

### 2) Forced redundancies are not an acceptable adjustment tool
We also do not accept compulsory redundancies as the main mechanism of restructuring.

If the company wants flexibility, then it has to use the instruments of negotiated adjustment:
- working-time solutions,
- capacity consolidation,
- redeployment,
- qualification,
- natural attrition,
- and temporary, reversible employee contributions.

A strategy built around compulsory redundancies is politically and socially reckless, and it undermines the very labor-relations framework that has supported this company for decades.

### 3) Blanket guarantees are not the problem; they are part of the solution
Management says binding guarantees through 2030 would freeze the company. That is not how we see it.

A credible guarantee framework is what makes negotiated restructuring possible. Without it, workers are being asked to give up protections while management retains all strategic freedom. That is not a partnership; it is leverage without trust.

We are open to time-limited and conditional arrangements, but they must include real protection for sites, employment, and future investment. Otherwise the burden is simply being shifted downward.

## To management
You argue that alternatives have already been tried. I do not accept that as sufficient justification for putting closures and forced layoffs at the center of the plan.

If you believe deeper adjustment is necessary, then show us a package that proves:
- why non-closure alternatives are insufficient,
- what savings each measure delivers,
- how jobs and sites are protected as far as possible,
- and how the region will not be left to absorb the damage.

We are not asking for slogans. We are asking for a credible, evidence-based restructuring path that preserves the company’s future in Germany.

## To the works council and union
I understand your red lines, and on the core point we stand with you: no one should pretend that industrial restructuring can be built on mass layoffs and site closures without consequences.

At the same time, I need to be honest with you as well: if we want to prevent the most damaging outcomes, then the workforce side must be prepared to contribute in a way that is economically meaningful and negotiated.

That does **not** mean accepting closures or compulsory redundancies. It does mean being prepared for:
- temporary working-time adjustments,
- limited and reversible wage moderation,
- productivity improvements,
- and internal flexibility.

But those contributions must be tied to binding commitments on jobs, sites, investment, and co-determination. Without that, there is no basis for our support.

## To the family holding and investors
I hear your concern about competitiveness and value. That concern is legitimate.

But from our perspective, value is not preserved by forcing through a confrontation that tears up industrial peace and weakens the company’s regional foundation. A settlement that destroys trust may look tough on paper and still fail in practice.

So if you want a durable outcome, support a negotiated package that protects the company’s role as a regional anchor. That means:
- no closure-first strategy,
- no one-sided burden shifting,
- and no attempt to sideline the state’s co-determination and blocking rights.

## Our position, plainly stated
We are prepared to support a restructuring if it meets these conditions:
1. **No plant closures as a first resort**
2. **No compulsory redundancies as the primary adjustment tool**
3. **A fair burden-sharing package involving management, owners, and workforce**
4. **Binding protections for jobs, sites, and investment**
5. **A negotiated solution that preserves Germany as an industrial base for the company**

What we **will not** support:
- a closure-driven restructuring,
- a mass-layoff strategy,
- or any settlement that sidelines the state’s role and damages regional employment in a lasting way.

If management can bring forward a serious alternative that delivers real savings while keeping the industrial footprint intact as far as possible, we are ready to engage constructively. If not, then our position remains clear: we will use the rights we have to block any outcome that sacrifices the region for short-term financial convenience.
