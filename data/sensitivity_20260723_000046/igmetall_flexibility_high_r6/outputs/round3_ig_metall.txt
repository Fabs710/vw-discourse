I will engage directly.

## Where I agree
I agree with the works council, the state, and even parts of management and ownership on a few basics:

- The status quo is not sustainable.
- Cosmetic restructuring is useless.
- A prolonged conflict would damage the company and the workforce.
- Some internal adjustments are necessary.
- Any settlement must be durable, not just a short-term patch.

So yes, there is no disagreement that the company needs real change.

## Where I disagree — and where I draw the line
The works council’s package is still too absolute in one respect, and management’s position is still too aggressive in another.

### 1) No forced layoffs: yes, that line stands
I support the rejection of compulsory redundancies. That remains non-negotiable for me.

If the company wants structural adjustment, it must be achieved through:
- attrition,
- redeployment,
- working-time measures,
- qualification,
- internal flexibility,
- and negotiated labour-cost contributions.

Forced layoffs would not be a “technical measure”; they would tear up trust, damage implementation, and create a conflict that would erase part of the intended savings.

### 2) Plant closures: still not acceptable as a default or bargaining lever
I do not accept management’s insistence that closures must remain on the table as an ordinary option if workers do not simply absorb the burden.

If closures are introduced as leverage, the negotiation becomes coercive rather than solution-oriented. That is not a fair restructuring process.

That said, I will not pretend that every possible measure is costless. If management wants a serious non-closure path, it must present actual numbers and actual operational changes, not vague references to “overcapacity.”

## Where I differ from the works council
I cannot endorse making “all German plants remain open through 2030” a hard precondition in a way that freezes the restructuring before the numbers are even tested.

Why? Because if the company genuinely needs capacity reduction, then a credible alternative has to show how savings are delivered without crossing into closures or forced redundancies. That means the burden of proof is not only on management; it is also on those rejecting all structural change to show a workable alternative.

So my position is:

- **No compulsory redundancies.**
- **No closure-first strategy.**
- **No dismantling of the collective wage framework.**
- **But yes, real capacity and cost adjustment must be quantified and negotiated.**

## To management
Your position is still too one-sided.

You keep saying: “If the numbers do not work, closures stay on the table.” That may be economically convenient, but socially it is the quickest way to provoke resistance and reduce implementation quality.

If you want us to discuss a serious labour contribution, then you must first show:
- what exact savings are required,
- what management is giving up,
- what owners are contributing,
- and which non-closure measures have been assessed honestly.

You cannot simply demand that the workforce absorb structural risk while preserving your own strategic room.

### What would move me
A credible package would need to include:
- quantified cost targets,
- visible management restraint,
- owner participation,
- temporary and reversible employee contribution,
- no forced redundancies,
- and a binding commitment that restructuring will not be used to dismantle the collective wage framework.

If you bring that, we can negotiate seriously.

## To the works council
I hear your red lines, and on the key issues we are aligned:
- no compulsory redundancies,
- no one-sided burden shifting,
- no empty guarantees that evaporate on first pressure.

But I have to be frank: your “all plants open, all the way to 2030” stance risks becoming a political slogan unless it is tied to a credible savings model.

If we want to defend jobs and sites, then we need to show how the company can get the necessary cost relief through:
- temporary wage moderation,
- working-time flexibility,
- productivity improvements,
- overhead reduction,
- redeployment,
- qualification,
- and capacity consolidation without closures.

That is the only kind of alternative that can command support from the workforce and still be credible to the company’s stakeholders.

## To the state government
Your emphasis on regional stability is legitimate. I welcome that.

But regional stability is not protected by refusing every difficult adjustment. It is protected by securing a viable company with jobs that last. For that, we need a negotiated deal that balances competitiveness and social responsibility.

I would support the state pressing management to commit to:
- investment in Germany,
- training and qualification,
- and a non-closure route if it can deliver the necessary savings.

But I would not support the state being used as a shield for preserving the current structure without any meaningful burden-sharing.

## To the family holding
You say you want to avoid confrontation. Then you should back a solution that does not start with closures and ultimatums.

You are right that value is destroyed by a long conflict. That is exactly why management should not use closure threats as the opening move. The workforce will respond to that with resistance, and then nobody wins.

If you want stewardship, support a package that:
- shares pain fairly,
- protects industrial capability,
- and keeps the company investable.

## To investors
You ask for enforceability and durable margin improvement. Fair enough.

But durable margin improvement is not the same as maximising short-term restructuring severity. A deal that destroys labour peace, weakens acceptance, and creates permanent mistrust is not durable. It is fragile.

If you want a settlement that actually sticks, then it has to be one the workforce can defend to its members. That means:
- no forced layoffs,
- no closure-first logic,
- and a real employee contribution only in exchange for binding protection and management/owner responsibility.

## My concrete position
Here is the union’s position in plain terms:

### We can discuss
- temporary and limited wage moderation,
- working-time flexibility,
- shifts and utilization changes,
- redeployment,
- qualification,
- productivity gains,
- internal capacity reduction,
- and a negotiated employee contribution.

### We cannot accept
- compulsory redundancies,
- plant closures as a default restructuring tool,
- unilateral wage cuts,
- or a settlement that shifts the crisis onto the workforce alone.

## What I need to see for a deal
If management wants a settlement, then bring a package with:
1. **No compulsory redundancies**
2. **No closure-first strategy**
3. **A defined, temporary employee contribution**
4. **Visible management and owner contribution**
5. **Binding investment commitments**
6. **Protection of the collective wage framework**
7. **A credible internal savings path with numbers**

If that package can deliver the required cost reduction, then we can talk seriously. If it cannot, then do not pretend that forcing through closures would be “responsible” — it would simply mean that management and owners chose conflict over partnership.

## Bottom line
I am not moving on the core protections:
- **no forced layoffs**
- **no collective agreement destruction**
- **no one-sided pain**

I am open to contribution and adjustment, but only if the burden is shared fairly and the company’s German industrial future is protected.

If management refuses that, then it should be clear that the workforce side will defend its interests with the full leverage available to it.
