I will be direct.

## My position
The family holding will support a **durable restructuring that restores competitiveness and margins**. We will **not** support a settlement that merely postpones the cost problem, preserves every legacy protection unchanged, and leaves the company structurally uncompetitive.

At the same time, we also **will not** back a closure-first confrontation that destroys trust, triggers prolonged industrial conflict, and erodes enterprise value. That is equally unacceptable.

So the test is simple: **Can the non-closure path produce sufficiently large, durable, and enforceable savings?** If yes, that is preferable. If not, then deeper structural adjustment remains necessary.

---

## To the Works Council
I respect the clarity, but your position is still too absolute.

You say:
- no closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- binding guarantees through 2030,
- and no automatic closure trigger.

I understand the logic, but I do **not** accept the premise that these protections can simply be declared as the floor and then treated as sufficient. The burden of proof is still not met.

You keep asking management to prove that alternatives fail. Fair enough. But if you reject closures entirely in advance, then you must prove that your package actually replaces the savings footprint reduction would otherwise deliver.

What I need to see from you is not another statement of principles, but:
- quantified savings by measure,
- expected utilization improvement by plant,
- how much headcount reduction comes from attrition / voluntary exits,
- what management overhead cuts are included,
- what supplier, logistics, and allocation changes are real,
- and how the package restores margins, not just calm.

If that is credible, I can support it.
If it is not, then “all plants open” is not a restructuring plan; it is a political boundary.

I also reject your idea that a review process is illegitimate merely because it could lead to harder decisions. A review without consequences is not governance. It is delay.

## To the Industrial Union
You are more realistic than the works council in acknowledging that internal measures have limits. I agree with that.

But you also want employee contributions to remain strictly temporary and reversible. I do **not** think that is enough if the structural cost gap is substantial.

If the company needs durable margin improvement, then the workforce contribution must be meaningful enough to matter. Temporary moderation can bridge a gap, but it cannot on its own repair a permanently uncompetitive cost base.

Where I agree with you:
- no forced layoffs as the first answer,
- no symbolic restructuring,
- no one-sided burden shifting,
- and no erosion of the collective framework through the back door.

Where I disagree:
- employee contributions cannot be merely short-term optics.
- If the package is too shallow, it will fail in two years and we will be back here again, only weaker.

So yes, I support a negotiated solution, but only if it is materially strong enough to restore competitiveness.

## To Group & Brand Management
You are right on one point: a cosmetic compromise would be a disaster.

You are also right that utilization and structural overcapacity matter. But you are still too quick to treat closures and forced redundancies as the natural benchmark.

That may be economically understandable, but it is governance-poor if it creates a conflict that wipes out the value you are trying to save.

I need from management a real comparison, not a rhetorical one:
- what internal measures were fully tested,
- what savings each one delivers,
- what remains missing after those measures,
- what one-time costs and transition risks closures create,
- and what net savings remain after those costs.

Do not ask the workforce to concede first and promise competitiveness later. That sequence will not produce a stable deal.

If the non-closure package can close the gap, I will back it.
If it cannot, then you must be honest that the business needs deeper structural change.
But do not use the threat of closures as a negotiating habit. It will harden resistance and weaken implementation.

## To the Regional State Government
I appreciate that you are resisting the idea of closures as a first resort. That is consistent with your role and with the region’s interests.

But I also need to be clear: “last resort” is not enough unless the package is actually capable of getting to the required economics.

You are right to insist on protection for the regional base. I support that objective. But you should not create the impression that every structural adjustment is illegitimate. A business that cannot compete will damage the region anyway.

So my position is:
- yes to a serious non-closure test,
- yes to internal measures first,
- yes to investment and qualification commitments,
- no to an automatic closure logic,
- but also no to a guaranteed preservation of every site regardless of economics.

If the numbers support the non-closure route, I will back it.
If they do not, then the state should not pretend that governance can make physics go away.

## To the Institutional Investors
You are focused on enforceability and margin improvement. That is correct.

But I would caution you against treating closure as the clean answer and everything else as soft. It is not that simple. The execution risk of closures is real and can destroy value through legal conflict, labor resistance, transfer disruption, and production instability.

That said, you are right on the essential point: any non-closure package must be **quantitatively credible**. If it only preserves all sites with small temporary concessions, it will not be enough.

We are aligned on the requirement for:
- durable savings,
- real capacity adjustment,
- measurable management contribution,
- and a path back to stronger margins.

Where I differ from a pure capital-market framing is this: the package must also be governable and implementable in a German co-determination setting. A theoretically optimal cost solution that cannot be executed is not value-preserving.

## My answer on the two scenarios

### 1) Maximal site/job protection with internal savings
This is acceptable **only if it is economically sufficient**.

I am open to it if it can demonstrate:
- enough cost reduction,
- enough capacity adjustment,
- enough utilization improvement,
- and enough durability to restore competitiveness through 2030.

If not, then it is too weak.

### 2) Deeper structural adjustment including possible closures
I do not endorse closures as the first or preferred path.

But I will not rule them out in principle if the non-closure path fails to deliver equivalent net savings. That is not a threat. It is the reality of ownership responsibility.

What I will not support is a settlement that:
- locks in an uncompetitive structure,
- delays hard decisions indefinitely,
- and destroys enterprise value under the banner of social peace.
