From the capital markets’ perspective, the discussion has become much clearer: **the company needs a restructuring that is economically real, enforceable, and fast enough to restore margins**. We are not interested in a cosmetic peace that leaves the cost base uncompetitive.

## Where we agree

We agree with management, the family holding, and the independent benchmark expert on the core economic point:

- the status quo is not sustainable,
- internal measures alone may not be enough if utilization is structurally weak,
- and a deal must produce **durable** savings, not just a temporary truce.

We also agree with the union and works council on one important practical point: **a prolonged conflict destroys value**. A strike-heavy stalemate would damage earnings, execution, and sentiment. Deal certainty matters.

## Where we disagree

### 1) With the works council and union: “no closures, no forced redundancies” cannot be a permanent shield
We understand the social logic, but from an owner perspective this cannot be the end state unless the alternative is genuinely sufficient.

A package that preserves all plants and excludes forced redundancy may be acceptable **only if** it delivers savings comparable to footprint reduction. So far, what we have heard is mostly a defence of protections, not a full economic replacement.

If the result is simply:
- temporary wage moderation,
- working-time flexibility,
- and some overhead cuts,

then that is **not enough** if structural overcapacity remains. That would be postponement, not restructuring.

### 2) With management: do not use closures as a reflex or bargaining weapon
Management is right that savings must be deep. But repeatedly putting closures and compulsory redundancies in the foreground risks hardening resistance and making the eventual outcome worse for shareholders.

Closures may be necessary in some cases, but they are not automatically the best answer if they trigger:
- legal delay,
- labor conflict,
- transfer disruption,
- ramp-up losses,
- and long implementation timelines.

We will not support a strategy that destroys more value than it creates.

### 3) With the state: “last resort” is too vague unless it is tied to measurable failure criteria
The state is right to resist closure-first logic. But “closures only as a last resort” is not enough by itself. There must be a clear, audited test of whether the non-closure path actually closes the savings gap.

Otherwise, the negotiation just becomes circular: labour refuses change until it gets guarantees, management refuses guarantees until it sees savings, and investors are left waiting while margins erode.

## Our position on the two scenarios

### Scenario A: maximal site/job protection with internal savings
We could support this **only if it is quantitatively credible**.

That means:
- a quantified savings target,
- a detailed breakdown by measure,
- material and durable labour-cost relief,
- real management and overhead cuts,
- demonstrable utilization improvement,
- and a clear path to competitive margins.

If this package is just a softer version of the status quo, we will not back it.

### Scenario B: deeper structural adjustment including possible closures
We do **not** want closures as the default answer. But we will not rule them out if the numbers show that internal measures cannot deliver the required cost reset.

That is the key point:  
**closures are not desirable, but neither is an uncompetitive cost structure.**

If deeper adjustment is necessary, then it must be:
- targeted,
- net-value positive after transition costs,
- and executed with as little conflict as possible.

## What would move us

A credible package would need to include:

1. **A quantified savings target**
2. **A plant-by-plant and function-by-function breakdown**
3. **A real utilization plan**
4. **Meaningful workforce contribution**
5. **Visible management restraint and overhead reduction**
6. **Owner contribution through governance and capital discipline**
7. **An enforceable implementation mechanism**
8. **A contingency if targets are missed**

## [SUMMON: Jointly appointed independent industrial finance and plant-utilization panel]
We need a genuinely independent, jointly appointed panel with site-level access to compare:

- net savings from internal restructuring,
- net savings from closures or footprint reduction,
- one-time restructuring and social-plan costs,
- transfer and ramp-up losses,
- utilization effects,
- and execution risk.

Not a management consultant. Not a union-aligned counter-study. A neutral panel with access to the actual plant economics.

## Bottom line

We are prepared to support a hard restructuring if it is **real**.

We are **not** prepared to support:
- a cosmetic compromise,
- a settlement that preserves an uncompetitive structure,
- or a prolonged standoff that destroys value without fixing the economics.

So our message is simple: **show the numbers, show the net savings, and show the implementation path.** If the non-closure package can truly restore competitiveness, we can support it. If not, deeper structural adjustment must remain on the table.
