I’ll respond directly.

## First: what the expert has clarified
The independent benchmark discussion is useful because it confirms something management has said from the start:

- **Internal measures can create meaningful savings, but they do not automatically replace footprint reduction.**
- **Utilization is the key variable.**
- If installed capacity is structurally too high, preserving every site may be socially appealing, but it is not necessarily economically sufficient.

That matters. Because it means this is not a debate about whether restructuring is needed. It is a debate about whether the workforce side is willing to accept the real scale of change that the numbers may require.

## Where I agree
I agree with the expert and, in part, with the union and works council on these points:

- A simple closure-first slogan is not enough.
- Closure has real execution risk, transfer cost, and social damage.
- Internal levers must be fully tested:
  - working-time flexibility
  - shift optimization
  - redeployment
  - qualification
  - procurement and overhead cuts
  - product allocation
  - utilization improvement
- A cosmetic compromise would be useless.

So yes: numbers matter. Benchmarking matters. Implementation risk matters.

## Where I disagree
### 1) The “all plants open through 2030” line is not a serious answer to the cost problem
To the works council and union: you keep presenting plant survival as the minimum acceptable outcome. I understand why. But it is not yet a credible industrial answer unless it produces **closure-level savings**.

Right now, you are asking management to pre-commit to site protection before the required savings are secured. That is backwards.

We are not managing a preservation exercise. We are trying to restore competitiveness in a shrinking, high-cost market. If the cost base stays too high, then the company will simply bleed margin and investment capacity.

### 2) “No compulsory redundancies” cannot mean “no real capacity reduction”
I will repeat this clearly: I understand the rejection of forced layoffs. But I will not agree to a framework that rules out meaningful structural workforce reduction in advance.

If headcount and footprint must come down materially, then the instruments can be negotiated — voluntary exits, attrition, redeployment, working-time measures, social plans — but the result must still be real.

A package that only smooths the problem for a few years is not a restructuring package. It is postponement.

### 3) The review mechanism cannot become a permanent veto over necessary change
To the works council: you are demanding that a non-closure phase cannot turn into a closure trigger. I hear that. But if milestone failure never permits escalation, then the review is just a device to lock in the current structure regardless of outcomes.

That is not acceptable.

A review must have consequences. If the agreed savings are not delivered, then deeper structural measures must remain available. Otherwise there is no discipline, only delay.

## To the works council
You asked for a proper comparison. Fair enough. Let’s be precise.

A serious non-closure package would need to show, in hard numbers:

- what savings come from working-time and shift changes,
- what savings come from overhead cuts,
- what savings come from product allocation and utilization,
- what savings come from voluntary exits and attrition,
- what savings come from wage moderation,
- what management is cutting,
- and what owners are contributing.

If that package closes the gap, I’m prepared to support it. If it does not, then “all plants open” is not a solution. It is a constraint that prevents the company from fixing itself.

## To the union
I agree with you that a reckless closure threat can damage trust. I also agree that forced redundancies should not be the first instrument.

But I do **not** accept your insistence that employee contributions must be only temporary and reversible while structural protections stay fixed. That would not restore margins to 2030. It would produce a short-term truce and a long-term cost problem.

If the company is to fund future products and technology, then the labour-cost contribution must be meaningful enough to matter. Durable competitiveness requires durable relief.

## To the state government
You are right to care about regional stability. But regional stability is not protected by freezing the company’s cost structure. A business that cannot compete will ultimately damage the region more severely.

I can support a path that preserves industrial capacity in Germany — but only if it is economically credible. If the non-closure option reaches the needed savings, fine. If it doesn’t, then the state cannot reasonably demand that management carry an uncompetitive structure indefinitely.

## To the family holding
You are right that value destruction from conflict is a real risk. But value is also destroyed by refusing structural adjustment.

A deal that preserves the appearance of social peace while leaving the business uncompetitive is not stewardship. It is drift.

If you want value preservation, then you need to support a package that actually changes the cost base, even if that means difficult site or headcount decisions remain possible.

## To investors
You are asking for something I fully understand: enforceability, durable savings, margin improvement.

That is exactly why I am not willing to sign up to a purely defensive package. A non-closure deal is only acceptable if it delivers:
- real capacity adjustment
- material labour-cost relief
- visible management contribution
- owner support
- and a credible path back to competitive returns

If it does not, then it is not a deal investors can support in good faith.

## My position after the benchmark contribution
The benchmark discussion reinforces our position rather than weakening it.

It shows:
- internal measures are necessary,
- but may be insufficient if structural overcapacity is deep,
- and the real question is whether the workforce side will accept the consequences of that reality.

So here is the line:

### I remain open to alternatives to plant closures
But only if they deliver equivalent, durable savings and restore utilization sufficiently.

### I do not accept a package that rules out deeper structural adjustment in advance
Because that would make the restructuring incapable of fixing the business if internal levers fall short.

## What I need next
If the other sides want to avoid plant closures, then bring me a package with:

1. quantified savings by measure,
2. a credible utilization improvement plan,
3. material workforce contribution,
4. visible management and owner contribution,
5. implementation discipline,
6. and a clear answer to what happens if targets are missed.

Not slogans. Numbers.

## My bottom line
The company needs a restructuring that is **durable, not symbolic**.

That means:
- lower cost
- lower overcapacity
- stronger margins
- and enough free cash to invest to 2030

I will not accept a settlement that preserves every site and every historic protection if that leaves the company unable to compete.

If a non-closure package can truly close the gap, I will consider it seriously. If it cannot, then closures and deeper structural adjustment remain necessary tools. That is not a threat. It is the economic reality we are responsible for.
