From the capital markets’ perspective, the discussion is converging on the right issue, but not yet on a credible solution.

## Where we agree
We agree with management, the family holding, and even the state on the core diagnosis:
- the status quo is not sustainable,
- the German cost base is too high,
- and a cosmetic settlement would damage value.

We also agree that a prolonged, strike-heavy conflict would be bad for earnings, execution, and sentiment. Deal certainty matters. The company needs a path that restores margins and is implementable.

## Where we disagree
We do **not** support the workforce side’s attempt to make broad job and site guarantees the starting point. If those guarantees are used to rule out meaningful restructuring in advance, they risk locking in an uncompetitive cost base. That is not acceptable to shareholders.

At the same time, we are not enthusiastic about management framing the issue as if closures and forced redundancies must be the immediate default. A destructive confrontation that damages operations or leaves a permanently poisoned labour relationship can destroy the savings it is meant to create. So “closure-first” is not automatically value-maximizing either.

## On the two scenarios

### 1) Maximal site/job protection with internal savings
We could support this **only if** it delivers savings that are genuinely comparable to a deeper restructuring.

That means:
- quantified, durable cost reduction,
- meaningful capacity reduction,
- real labour-cost relief,
- and credible implementation.

If “no closures” simply means preserving the current footprint while asking for temporary concessions, then it is too weak. That would not restore competitive margins.

### 2) Deeper structural adjustment including possible closures
We will not rule this out. In fact, if alternatives cannot deliver equivalent savings, then closures and headcount reduction have to remain on the table. A shareholder cannot support a solution that leaves the company structurally impaired just because it is politically easier.

But we would want that path to be:
- targeted, not indiscriminate,
- financially justified,
- and executed in a way that avoids prolonged disruption.

## What would move us
The decisive test is not rhetoric about fairness or tradition. It is whether the package produces:
1. a credible return to competitive margins,
2. durable cost base reduction,
3. clear capacity adjustment,
4. and enough certainty to avoid years of conflict.

If management can show that internal measures alone achieve that, fine. If not, then the ownership and workforce sides should not expect shareholders to support a deal that preserves every site and guarantee while leaving returns structurally weak.

## Our bottom line
We are prepared to back a hard restructuring **if it is real**.
We are not prepared to back:
- a cosmetic compromise,
- a settlement that preserves an uncompetitive structure,
- or a prolonged standoff that destroys value without fixing the economics.

So our position is straightforward:  
**show us the numbers and the enforceability.** If the non-closure package truly restores competitiveness, we can support it. If not, deeper structural adjustment must remain available.
