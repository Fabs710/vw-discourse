We are now in the phase where words have to become numbers and enforceable commitments. Good. Then let me be equally concrete.

## First: what we agree on
I acknowledge three things that are now beyond dispute:

1. **The status quo is not sustainable.**
2. **A cosmetic solution would fail.**
3. **Any serious package must include internal measures, burden-sharing, and real savings.**

That is not where the dispute lies anymore.

## Second: what we do not accept
Let me repeat this plainly, because several parties still seem to be testing the boundaries:

- **No plant closures in Germany.**
- **No compulsory redundancies.**
- **No dismantling of the historic job-security framework.**
- **No “temporary” guarantees that are meaningless the moment management wants to renegotiate them.**

Those are not tactical preferences. They are our red lines.

## To management
You keep saying closures and forced redundancies must remain on the table if the numbers do not work. I say the burden of proof is still yours.

You have not shown that all serious alternatives have been exhausted. Not in a way that justifies irreversible damage.

If you want to claim that a non-closure package cannot work, then show the full comparison:
- what savings each site alternative delivers,
- what capacity can be removed through shifts, utilization, product allocation, and consolidation,
- what management overhead reduction is on the table,
- what owner contribution is real and not cosmetic,
- and what labour-cost contribution you are demanding.

So far, what I hear is mainly this: workers should accept the risk first, and then maybe the company will be competitive. That is not a restructuring plan. That is an ultimatum.

### My position on your “numbers”
If you want a deal, then the minimum package must be based on:
- **all German plants staying open,**
- **no forced layoffs,**
- **binding site and employment guarantees to 2030,**
- **internal capacity adjustment only,**
- **temporary and reversible employee contribution,**
- **visible management and owner contribution,**
- **full co-determination in implementation.**

If that package does not produce enough savings, then management needs to look first at its own assumptions, its overhead, its sourcing, its investment priorities, and its allocation decisions — not at shutting plants or throwing people out.

## To the union
I appreciate your rejection of compulsory redundancies and your insistence on fairness. On that, we stand together.

But I need to correct one point: this is not a situation where workers should be asked to “contribute” in the abstract while the company keeps the right to close plants later. That is not shared sacrifice; that is a trap.

If there is any employee contribution, it must be:
- **limited,**
- **temporary,**
- **reversible,**
- and **strictly tied to binding guarantees**.

Otherwise it is just prepayment for the next round of cuts.

## To the state government
You are right that the company is a regional anchor and that closures would damage supplier networks and communities. That is exactly why the state should not normalize closure-first logic.

You say regional stability is protected by securing a viable company. Agreed. But a viable company is not built by sacrificing one German site after another and calling that modernization.

If the state wants to protect the region, then it should support a plan that keeps the industrial base intact and forces management to solve the competitiveness problem without destroying jobs.

## To the family holding
You speak about value preservation and avoiding confrontation. Then do not back a strategy that creates the very confrontation you claim to fear.

A closure threat is not a neutral instrument. It triggers resistance, destroys trust, and undermines implementation. If you want real stewardship, then support a package that preserves the company’s industrial capability and the trust needed to execute it.

And let me be very clear: value is also destroyed when management tries to buy a quick financial improvement by breaking the social contract that has held this company together.

## To investors
You ask for enforceability and durable margin improvement. Fair enough.

Then the question is simple: **can the company achieve those goals without closures and without forced redundancies?**

Our answer is yes — if management and owners are prepared to act on the real levers:
- overhead reduction,
- management restraint,
- working-time flexibility,
- productivity gains,
- utilization improvements,
- product and allocation discipline,
- and a fair, limited workforce contribution.

If you insist that only closures can restore competitiveness, then you are saying you value a mechanically cleaner cost base more than industrial stability and implementation. That may sound neat in a spreadsheet. It is not how you keep a workforce committed to a turnaround.

## Our concrete minimum package
This is the floor from our side:

1. **All German plants remain open.**
2. **No compulsory redundancies.**
3. **Binding job and site security through 2030.**
4. **Co-determination remains fully involved in design and implementation.**
5. **Employee contribution only if temporary, limited, and reversible.**
6. **Management and owners make visible, quantified contributions.**
7. **Investment commitments for future products and technology in Germany.**

Anything short of that is not a viable settlement for us.

## What we can discuss
We are ready to discuss:
- working-time flexibility,
- shift changes,
- internal redeployment,
- qualification,
- temporary wage moderation with a firm end date,
- productivity measures,
- consolidation of capacity without closures,
- and transparent burden-sharing.

We are **not** ready to discuss:
- closures,
- layoffs,
- the erosion of binding guarantees,
- or any package that turns employee concessions into a one-way street.

## On the “deeper restructuring” scenario
Let me be blunt: that scenario is not acceptable to us, and it will not become acceptable just because it is repeated often enough.

If management and shareholders insist on keeping closures in the package, then they should also be clear that the workforce side will use every co-determination right, every collective bargaining instrument, and every legitimate form of industrial resistance to stop it. That is not a threat; it is the reality of the framework we are in.

## Final point
We are prepared for a hard, negotiated restructuring.

We are **not** prepared to trade away plants, jobs, and guarantees in exchange for vague promises about future competitiveness.

If the other sides want a deal, bring us:
- a non-closure package,
- no forced layoffs,
- binding guarantees,
- real burden-sharing from management and owners,
- and a credible industrial plan for Germany.

Anything else is not a compromise. It is asking the workforce to pay for a crisis it did not create.
