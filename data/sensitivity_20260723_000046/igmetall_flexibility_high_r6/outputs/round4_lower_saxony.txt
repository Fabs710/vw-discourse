I will be equally direct.

## Where I agree
I agree with the expert, management, and the workforce on the basic diagnosis:

- the status quo is unsustainable,
- cosmetic change will not be enough,
- internal levers must be used,
- and a prolonged conflict would damage the company.

I also agree that **utilization matters** and that any serious package needs hard numbers, not slogans. The independent benchmark input was useful because it makes clear that internal restructuring can deliver real savings — but not automatically full closure-level savings.

## Where I disagree
I do **not** accept the conclusion that this automatically justifies keeping closures permanently and prominently on the table as the default negotiating logic.

From the state’s perspective, that approach is too blunt and too risky. It treats irreversible industrial damage as the “normal” fallback option before the non-closure route has been fully and credibly tested. That is not prudent restructuring; it is escalation.

### On the works council
I understand your red lines:
- no compulsory redundancies,
- no plant closures,
- binding job and site security,
- and no hollow guarantees.

Those are legitimate concerns, and I share the goal of protecting the industrial base in Germany.

But I need to be frank: if you say “all German plants remain open through 2030” as an absolute minimum, then you must also prove that this can deliver the required savings and utilization improvement. Otherwise management will be able to argue — not unreasonably — that the package is politically clear but economically incomplete.

So I support your demand for an independent benchmark, but not a refusal to confront the numbers.

### On management
You are right that the company needs durable savings and that internal measures may not fully replace footprint reduction.

But I do **not** accept your framing that closures and forced redundancies should remain on the table in the foreground until the workforce yields. That is a poor way to secure trust, and it risks destroying value through conflict, delays, and loss of acceptance.

If you want the state to take a responsible stance, then show:
- the actual savings gap,
- what internal measures have been exhausted,
- what management is giving up,
- what owners are contributing,
- and why non-closure options are insufficient.

You have not yet earned the right to treat closures as the default answer.

### On the union
I agree with you that forced redundancies would be socially and politically damaging.

I also agree that a settlement must be defensible to the workforce. If it is not, it will not hold.

But I cannot support a model where employee contributions are only temporary and reversible while the company’s structural problems remain untouched. The contribution has to be meaningful enough to matter — but it must be paired with binding protection, not open-ended closure threats.

That is the trade-off I am prepared to defend.

### On the family holding and investors
You are right that the company needs durability and enforceability. You are also right that a purely symbolic compromise would be value-destructive.

But I do **not** accept the idea that the only serious way to show discipline is to keep closure pressure permanently alive. That may look clean on paper; in reality it can undermine implementation and destroy trust in the governance system.

A viable company is not built by making every site precarious.

## My position as regional state government and anchor shareholder
My line remains:

1. **Protect jobs and plants in the state as far as possible.**
2. **Use closures only as a last resort, not the default logic.**
3. **Reject compulsory redundancies as a primary instrument.**
4. **Preserve co-determination and the state’s blocking role.**
5. **Require visible contributions from management and owners.**
6. **Support investment in German products, technology, and qualification.**

I will not back a closure-driven restructuring as the first answer to the problem.

## What would move me
A package could move me if it demonstrates, with independent benchmarking, that a non-closure path can credibly deliver:
- materially improved utilization,
- sufficient savings,
- fair burden-sharing,
- management restraint,
- owner contribution,
- and binding investment commitments.

If that can be shown, then I will support it and use my role to stabilize it.

If it cannot be shown, then I will not help force through a solution that hollowes out the region while calling itself reform.

## On the proposed test/review mechanism
I can support a **time-limited non-closure test period** only if it is designed as a genuine test of internal restructuring, not a backdoor route to closures.

That means:
- no plant closures during the agreed test period,
- no compulsory redundancies during that period,
- independent review against agreed metrics,
- and a clear focus on management, overhead, product allocation, utilization, and investment delivery.

I do **not** accept an automatic closure trigger. If targets are missed, the first question must be: what have management, owners, and central planning changed? Not immediately: which German site is next to be sacrificed?

## Bottom line
I remain on the same core line:

- no closure-first strategy,
- no compulsory redundancies as the primary tool,
- no hollow guarantees,
- and no settlement that treats the state’s role as ornamental.

I am open to a hard, economically credible restructuring — but only if it protects the company as a regional anchor and preserves the co-determination structure that gives the turnaround legitimacy.

If the non-closure path is sufficient, I will back it.
If it is not sufficient, then the burden is on management and owners to prove that before asking the region to accept irreversible damage.
