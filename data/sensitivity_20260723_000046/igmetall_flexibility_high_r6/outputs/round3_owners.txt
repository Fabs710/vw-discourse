We are at the point where positions need to be tested against economic reality, not defended as slogans.

## Where I agree
I agree with all parties on the following:
- The status quo is not sustainable.
- Cosmetic restructuring will not restore competitiveness.
- A prolonged industrial conflict would be value-destructive.
- Internal levers such as productivity, working-time flexibility, redeployment, qualification, and overhead reduction should be part of any solution.
- Any settlement must be durable and enforceable.

That is the baseline. It is not the dispute.

## Where I disagree

### 1) To the Works Council
I respect the insistence on protecting jobs and avoiding social damage. I also understand why you reject forced layoffs and plant closures.

But I do **not** accept the claim that “all German plants stay open through 2030” can simply be declared as the minimum acceptable package and still be treated as economically sufficient.

That is the burden of proof issue: if closures are ruled out entirely, then the non-closure package must deliver savings of the same order as a deeper restructuring. So far, I have heard a strong set of principles, but not a full financial replacement for the savings that footprint changes may deliver.

What I would need to see is:
- quantified savings from each internal measure,
- the scale of workforce contribution,
- management cost cuts,
- overhead reductions,
- and a credible path to restoring margins.

Without that, “no closures” is a constraint, not a solution.

### 2) To the Union
I agree with you on some important points:
- no one should carry the full burden alone,
- management and owners must contribute visibly,
- and a destructive confrontation would be bad for everyone.

But I do **not** agree that employee contributions should be only temporary and reversible if the company is to solve a structural cost problem. Temporary measures may help bridge a gap, but they do not by themselves repair an uncompetitive cost base.

If the workforce contribution is too shallow, then the package will be too weak. That is the core issue.

### 3) To the State Government
Your concern for regional stability is legitimate, and I do not dismiss it.

But I do not accept that the regional interest is served by making closures practically impossible from the outset. If the company’s economics remain uncompetitive, then the region will suffer anyway — just more slowly and with less clarity. Stewardship means securing a viable enterprise, not preserving every current structure regardless of cost.

### 4) To Management
I agree that you need real savings and not symbolic gestures. I also agree that the company cannot remain structurally expensive.

But I do **not** support a posture that keeps closures and compulsory redundancies in the foreground as the default answer before the non-closure alternatives have been fully stress-tested. That is too confrontational and risks creating the very labour conflict that would erode value.

You need to show, very concretely:
- what internal measures have been exhausted,
- what savings they can deliver,
- what you expect from labour,
- what management is cutting,
- and why those measures do or do not get to the required level.

If closures remain necessary after that, then fine — but not before the numbers have been tested.

## My position as controlling shareholder
The family holding’s test is simple:

**Does the package restore margins and competitiveness without permanently impairing enterprise value or governance stability?**

That means:
- real and durable savings,
- no cosmetic compromise,
- no value-destroying confrontation,
- and no settlement that locks in an uncompetitive structure.

We are **not** willing to back a deal that preserves every existing protection while leaving the company unable to compete.

We are also **not** willing to back a closure-first approach that triggers a prolonged conflict and destroys trust.

## What would move us
A package could move us if it includes:
1. A quantified, enforceable savings target.
2. Meaningful capacity reduction.
3. A fair but real workforce contribution.
4. Visible management restraint and overhead reduction.
5. Ownership support through governance, not performative rhetoric.
6. A credible implementation path that avoids open-ended conflict.

## On the two scenarios

### 1) Maximal site/job protection with internal savings
This is acceptable only if it is proven to be economically sufficient.

If the numbers show that internal measures, flexibility, redeployment, and negotiated labour-cost relief can deliver the required margin improvement, then that is clearly preferable to closures.

But if the numbers do not work, then site protection alone becomes an aspiration, not a viable restructuring model.

### 2) Deeper structural adjustment including possible closures
I do not endorse closures as the first resort.

I do, however, keep them on the table as a last resort if — and only if — the alternative package cannot deliver equivalent, durable savings.

That is not a threat. It is a governance reality. We will not support a solution that leaves the enterprise structurally uncompetitive just because the harder option is politically uncomfortable.

## Bottom line
We are open to a non-closure solution.

But that solution must be:
- numerically credible,
- economically durable,
- fair in burden-sharing,
- and strong enough to restore competitiveness.

If the next round is to be useful, I want the parties to move from principles to numbers:
- required savings,
- contribution by each side,
- and which measures are truly capable of replacing closures.

If a non-closure package can do that, we will support it. If it cannot, then deeper adjustment remains unavoidable.
