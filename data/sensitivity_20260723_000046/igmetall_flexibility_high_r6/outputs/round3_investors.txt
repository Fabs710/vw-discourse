From the capital markets’ perspective, this discussion is finally where it needs to be: not abstract principles, but whether the package is financially credible.

## Where we agree
We agree with management, the family holding, and the state on the core diagnosis:
- the status quo is unsustainable,
- the cost base is too high,
- and cosmetic restructuring would be value-destructive.

We also agree that a prolonged strike-heavy conflict would hurt earnings, execution, and sentiment. Deal certainty matters, and the company needs a path back to competitive margins.

## Where we disagree

### 1) On the works council’s “all plants open, no forced layoffs, guarantees to 2030” position
We cannot back that as a starting point if it is used to preclude meaningful restructuring.

That package may be understandable from a labour-relations perspective, but from an owner’s perspective it is only acceptable if it can demonstrably replace the savings that plant closures or deeper footprint changes would otherwise deliver.

So far, I have heard a strong defence of jobs and sites, but not a full financial replacement.

If “no closures” means preserving the existing structure with temporary concessions, then it is too weak. That does not restore competitive margins.

### 2) On management’s tendency to keep closures and compulsory redundancies prominently on the table
We also do not want a blunt closure-first strategy.

A confrontational approach that destroys labour peace can wipe out part of the intended savings and damage the company for years. So while we will not rule out structural adjustment, we do not see plant closures as the automatic first answer.

The test is not whether an option sounds tough. The test is whether it is financially justified and implementable.

### 3) On the state and union’s insistence on hard exclusions
We respect the political and social constraints, but we do not accept hard exclusions that lock in an uncompetitive cost base.

If closures and headcount reduction are ruled out in advance, then the burden of proof shifts heavily onto the non-closure package. It must deliver comparable, durable savings, not just a temporary truce.

## Our position on the two scenarios

### 1) Maximal site/job protection with internal savings
We could support this only if it is quantitatively sufficient.

That means:
- meaningful and durable cost reduction,
- real capacity adjustment,
- measurable labour-cost relief,
- management and overhead cuts,
- and credible implementation.

If the package simply preserves every site while asking for temporary wage moderation and flexibility, then it is not enough.

### 2) Deeper structural adjustment including possible closures
We will not insist on closures as the default answer, but we will not rule them out if the numbers require it.

If alternatives cannot deliver equivalent savings, then deeper structural adjustment remains on the table. Shareholders cannot support a solution that leaves the company structurally impaired just because the harder option is politically difficult.

## What would move us
A credible package would need to show:
1. a quantified savings target,
2. a clear breakdown by measure,
3. meaningful capacity reduction,
4. real management and owner contribution,
5. a workforce contribution that is more than symbolic,
6. and enough certainty to avoid years of conflict.

## Bottom line
We are prepared to support a hard restructuring if it is real.
We are not prepared to support:
- a cosmetic compromise,
- a settlement that preserves an uncompetitive structure,
- or a prolonged standoff that destroys value without fixing the economics.

So our message is simple: **show us the numbers and the enforceability.** If the non-closure package can truly restore competitiveness, we can support it. If not, deeper structural adjustment must remain available.
