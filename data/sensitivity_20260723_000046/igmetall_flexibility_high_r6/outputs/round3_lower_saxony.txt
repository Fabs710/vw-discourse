I will respond plainly, because this is now the stage where positions have to be tested against reality.

## Where I agree
I agree with everyone on three points:
- The status quo is not sustainable.
- Cosmetic measures will not solve the problem.
- A prolonged conflict would damage the company, workers, and the region.

I also agree that internal measures must be part of the solution:
- productivity gains,
- working-time flexibility,
- redeployment,
- qualification,
- and overhead reduction.

That is all sensible.

## Where I disagree
I do **not** accept that the only credible restructuring logic is: “keep closures and forced redundancies on the table until workers cave.”

That is not prudent restructuring. It is escalation dressed up as realism.

From the perspective of the region, that approach is reckless for three reasons:
1. It risks lasting damage to employment and supplier networks.
2. It undermines trust and implementation capacity.
3. It treats Germany’s industrial base as a variable to be reduced first, rather than preserved if at all possible.

As the state government and anchor shareholder, I cannot support that as the default path.

## To management
You have made one important point: the company needs durable savings, not symbolism. I accept that.

But you have not yet justified why plant closures or compulsory redundancies must remain part of the package at this stage. Saying “if alternatives do not work, then closures remain possible” is not enough. Before you ask the region to absorb irreversible damage, you must show:
- what exact savings are required,
- what internal measures have been tested,
- what management is contributing,
- what owners are contributing,
- and why a non-closure path is insufficient.

Right now, I see a demand for flexibility from labour, but too little self-constraint from management.

### What I can support
I could support a serious restructuring if it includes:
- quantified savings,
- capacity adjustment through internal means first,
- clear management restraint,
- visible owner participation,
- and binding investment commitments for Germany.

### What I cannot support
I will not support:
- a closure-first strategy,
- mass layoffs,
- or a settlement that treats the state’s role as decorative while pushing the social costs onto the region.

## To the works council
I support your central warning: closures are not a first resort, and the region cannot be casually hollowed out. That is correct.

But I also want to be honest with you: if you insist on “all plants open, no forced layoffs, guarantees to 2030” as an absolute formula, you must still demonstrate that this can deliver the needed savings. Otherwise management will simply say — plausibly — that the alternative is not credible.

So I am with you on the principle, but I need the numbers to be firm enough to withstand pressure.

### What I need from you
Show a package that proves a non-closure route can work through:
- shift and working-time changes,
- temporary and reversible employee contributions,
- productivity and utilization improvements,
- consolidation without site shutdowns,
- management and overhead reductions,
- and investment commitments.

If that package can genuinely deliver the required savings, then it is the right path. If it cannot, then we are left with a conflict nobody should want.

## To the union
I agree with you that the workforce should not carry the whole burden, and I agree that a settlement must be fair and durable.

I also agree that compulsory redundancies should not be the first answer. They would be socially damaging and politically destructive.

But I do **not** agree that employee contributions can be treated as only temporary, while the company’s structural cost problem remains untouched. If the business needs a durable turnaround, then contributions must be meaningful enough to matter.

That said, I am prepared to back employee contributions only if they are tied to:
- binding site and job protection,
- management contribution,
- owner contribution,
- and a credible industrial plan for Germany.

Without that, there is no legitimacy in asking workers to concede anything.

## To the family holding
I understand your concern about value destruction. I share it.

But value is not preserved by putting plant closures in the center of the negotiation from the outset. That creates conflict, weakens trust, and can destroy more value than it saves.

If you want stewardship, then support a package that protects the company’s German industrial base as far as possible and uses closures only as a last resort, if at all.

## To investors
You want enforceability and margin improvement. That is reasonable.

But I would caution against overestimating what “hard restructuring” means if it destroys labor peace and regional stability. A theoretical cost saving that triggers years of disruption is not a clean win.

A deal that is economically sound and politically survivable is more valuable than a paper solution that is operationally explosive.

## My position, clearly stated
As the regional state government, I will support only a restructuring that:
1. preserves jobs and plants in the state as far as possible,
2. uses closures only as a last resort,
3. rejects compulsory redundancies as a primary tool,
4. includes visible contributions from management and owners,
5. and secures long-term investment in Germany.

What I will not support:
- a closure-driven restructuring,
- a mass-layoff strategy,
- or any attempt to sideline the state’s co-determination and blocking role.

## What would move me
A credible package would need to show:
- real savings,
- internal capacity adjustment,
- fair burden-sharing,
- management and owner contributions,
- and binding commitments to the German sites.

If management can prove that a non-closure package achieves comparable savings, then I will back it and use my role to help stabilize it. If not, then I will not help force through a solution that sacrifices the region for short-term balance-sheet comfort.

That is the line.
