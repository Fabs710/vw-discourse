Let me respond directly.

I appreciate that several parties now acknowledge what we said from the beginning: the status quo is not sustainable, and a purely cosmetic response would fail. On that point, there is common ground.

But I will be equally direct about the line we will not cross.

## What we agree on
- The company needs a future-proof solution, not a temporary patch.
- Savings have to be real, not symbolic.
- The burden cannot fall on one side alone.
- A prolonged conflict would damage the company and the workforce.

## What we do **not** accept
We do **not** accept:
- plant closures in Germany,
- compulsory redundancies,
- or a restructuring that breaks the historic job-security framework and then asks workers to “trust” that the damage will somehow be repaired later.

That is not a bargaining position we can move away from. It is a red line.

## To management
Your argument is still the same one: “overcapacity” therefore closures and job cuts must be possible. But you have not shown that every alternative has been exhausted.

You want to make irreversible decisions first and call that realism. We call that a shortcut.

If there is structural overcapacity, then there are still serious alternatives that must be pursued before closures:
- capacity consolidation without site shutdowns,
- shift and working-time adjustments,
- product and allocation changes,
- temporary internal redeployment,
- productivity measures,
- management and overhead reductions,
- and a negotiated employee contribution tied to binding guarantees.

You keep framing the issue as if the only credible choice is between closure and paralysis. That is false.

## To the family holding
I hear the concern about value destruction and prolonged conflict. That is exactly why forcing a closure-driven confrontation is the wrong path. A strike-heavy escalation is not caused by workers defending their jobs; it is caused by management and owners trying to solve a structural problem by putting entire sites on the chopping block.

If you want value preservation, then back a solution that preserves industrial capability, trust, and implementation capacity. A settlement that destroys social peace and tears up the company’s own commitments will not be “stewardship”; it will be self-inflicted damage.

## To investors
You keep saying you want durable margin improvement. So do we. But durable does not mean socially destructive.

A deal that cuts too deep, breaks guarantees, and leaves the company with a demoralized workforce and a permanent trust deficit is not durable. It is fragile.

You ask for “deal certainty.” Then support a deal that workers can actually live with. Certainty does not come from threats; it comes from binding commitments on both sides.

## Our position on the two scenarios

### 1) Maximal site/job protection with internal savings
This is the only acceptable direction from our side.

Minimum requirements:
- all German plants remain open,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- binding site and job guarantees through 2030,
- co-determination fully involved in the design of the transformation,
- management and owners make visible contributions,
- any employee contribution must be limited, temporary, and reversible.

What we can consider:
- working-time flexibility,
- temporary wage moderation if and only if linked to hard guarantees,
- internal efficiency measures,
- redeployment and qualification,
- capacity adjustments through utilization and shifts, not closures.

What we will not accept:
- disguised layoffs,
- closure threats used as leverage,
- or “guarantees” that can be canceled the moment management finds them inconvenient.

### 2) Deeper structural adjustment including possible closures
This is not acceptable to us.

It fails on principle and on substance:
- it violates the job-security framework,
- it destroys trust,
- it would trigger severe resistance,
- and it risks a spiral of industrial conflict that no one should want.

If management insists on putting closures into the package, then it should be clear: there will be no consent from the workforce side. We will use every co-determination and industrial means available to stop that outcome.

## On burden-sharing
If there is to be any contribution from employees, then it must be part of a broader package where:
- management gives up bonuses, overhead, and strategic vanity projects,
- owners show real responsibility,
- and future investment in German plants is secured.

Workers cannot be asked to pay for strategic errors by losing jobs and protections while everyone else keeps full freedom of action. That is not fairness.

## Bottom line
We are ready to negotiate a **competitive, socially responsible, German-site-preserving** restructuring.

We are **not** ready to negotiate plant closures or compulsory redundancies.

If management wants a settlement, it needs to return with a package built around:
- no closures,
- no forced layoffs,
- binding guarantees to 2030,
- and genuine shared burden.

Anything else is not a compromise. It is an ultimatum dressed up as a plan.
