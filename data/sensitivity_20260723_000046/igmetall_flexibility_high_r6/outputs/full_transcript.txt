[Expert: Independent industrial restructuring ana — Round 4]
As an industrial restructuring matter, the key question is not whether closures are “desirable,” but whether a **non-closure package can credibly deliver closure-level economics**.

## My assessment

### 1) Internal measures can deliver meaningful savings, but usually not all of the savings from footprint reduction
In large automotive plants, the main internal levers are:
- headcount reduction via attrition / voluntary exit,
- reduced overtime and agency labor,
- shift pattern changes,
- working-time flexibility,
- overhead and management-layer cuts,
- procurement and logistics savings,
- product-allocation and utilization improvement,
- postponement or reprioritization of capex.

These can produce substantial savings. However, **they typically do not fully substitute for the fixed-cost removal achieved by closing a plant or a line** if a site is materially underutilized. The reason is structural: plant closure removes a large block of fixed labor, maintenance, utilities, depreciation-related support, and local overhead all at once.

### 2) The biggest determinant is utilization, not just wage level
If a plant is running well below efficient scale, the cost problem is usually:
- under-absorption of overhead,
- too many direct and indirect labor hours per vehicle,
- complex logistics and internal transport,
- legacy capacity built for higher volume,
- and duplicated functions across sites.

In that case, **internal savings are limited by physics of scale**. You can squeeze the workforce and overhead, but you cannot fully fix a low-utilization plant without either:
- reallocating more volume to it, or
- taking capacity out of the system.

### 3) A non-closure path can be credible only if there is enough volume reallocation and labor flexibility
A “no closure” solution is credible if it includes all three:
- **sufficient vehicle allocation to keep key plants near efficient utilization**,
- **material workforce reduction through attrition/voluntary exits/redeployment**,
- **durable changes in working time, shift structure, and overhead layers**.

If those conditions are missing, the package usually becomes a **cost deferral**, not a durable restructuring.

### 4) The implementation risk of closures is real and often underestimated
Even where closures produce better theoretical savings, the realized outcome can be weakened by:
- severance and social-plan costs,
- legal and co-determination delays,
- production transfer disruption,
- supplier disruption,
- quality ramp-up losses at receiving plants,
- and reputational / labor-relations damage.

So the closure case is not simply “more savings = better.” It is **more savings, but with higher execution risk and transition cost**.

### 5) Benchmarking conclusion
Based on plant-level restructuring practice, my neutral conclusion is:

- **Internal restructuring can often capture a large share of the needed savings**, especially if the issue is excess labor cost, overhead, and moderate overcapacity.
- **If the company has deep structural overcapacity, one or more closures or equivalent footprint reductions usually become economically necessary** to achieve full and durable competitiveness.
- The critical question is whether the **gap after internal measures** is small enough that it can be bridged without closures, or whether it remains too large.

## What would make the comparison rigorous
An independent benchmark should compare, for each site and function:
- cost per vehicle at current and post-measure utilization,
- direct labor hours per vehicle,
- indirect labor share,
- logistics cost per unit,
- fixed overhead per unit,
- investment requirements to sustain each option,
- one-time restructuring costs,
- and time-to-benefit.

## My bottom line
A non-closure package is **not impossible**, but it is only credible if the numbers show that:
1. utilization can be restored materially,
2. headcount and overhead reductions are deep enough,
3. and the remaining cost base reaches competitive benchmarks.

If the company’s actual structural gap is large, then **internal measures alone are unlikely to match closure-level savings**. In that case, preserving all sites may be socially attractive but economically insufficient.

If you want, I can next lay out a **neutral comparison template** for “non-closure package vs. closure package” with the exact cost lines and benchmark fields that an independent expert panel should test.

## Core question
Can a **non-closure restructuring package** plausibly deliver savings comparable to a **closure-based restructuring**, once utilization, overhead, logistics, transfer costs, and execution risk are included?

## Expert assessment

### 1) Plant closure usually creates the largest single fixed-cost step-down
If a German automotive plant is materially underutilized, closure or equivalent footprint reduction typically removes:
- direct labor tied to the site,
- indirect labor and supervisory layers,
- maintenance, utilities, security, and site overhead,
- internal logistics complexity,
- and some allocation of central support.

That is why closures often look superior in a pure cost model: they eliminate a large fixed-cost block quickly.

### 2) But closure savings are not equal to net savings
A closure case also brings substantial costs and risks that must be deducted from headline savings:
- severance and social-plan costs,
- transfer and ramp-up costs at receiving plants,
- temporary loss of output during transition,
- supplier disruption,
- quality and launch risk,
- legal and co-determination delay,
- and possible need to invest more heavily in the remaining sites.

So the relevant question is **net, realized savings over time**, not gross site-cost removal.

### 3) Internal measures can be large, but they are constrained by utilization physics
A non-closure package can generate meaningful savings through:
- headcount reduction by attrition/voluntary exit,
- shift and working-time changes,
- elimination of overtime and agency labor,
- redeployment,
- overhead cuts,
- better product allocation,
- logistics simplification,
- and procurement improvements.

However, these measures have a hard ceiling if the underlying issue is **too much installed capacity for the available volume**. If a plant or network runs well below efficient utilization, internal measures can improve the cost base but usually cannot fully replicate the fixed-cost relief of removing a site or major line.

### 4) The decisive variable is utilization
From a benchmarking standpoint, the key test is:
- current utilization,
- achievable utilization after reallocation,
- cost per vehicle at each utilization level,
- and whether the remaining network can operate near efficient scale.

If utilization can be restored materially, a no-closure solution may be economically credible.  
If utilization remains structurally weak, internal savings are usually insufficient for full competitiveness.

### 5) The credible non-closure case requires four conditions
A non-closure package can be benchmark-credible only if it includes all of the following:
1. **Material volume reallocation** to keep remaining plants near efficient loading.
2. **Deep workforce adjustment** through voluntary and natural attrition.
3. **Durable changes** in working time, shift patterns, and overhead structure.
4. **Binding implementation discipline** so savings are not delayed or reversed.

Without these, the package is usually a deferral of the problem, not a solution.

## Benchmark conclusion
My neutral conclusion is:

- **Yes, a non-closure package can produce substantial savings.**
- **No, it is not automatically capable of matching closure-level economics.**
- Whether it can do so depends primarily on **utilization improvement and the depth of capacity removal achieved through internal means**.
- If the structural overcapacity gap is large, then **closure or equivalent footprint reduction is usually economically necessary** to achieve the full cost reset.

- indirect labor share,

- one-time restructuring costs,
- transfer/ramp-up losses,
- capex required to sustain the network,
