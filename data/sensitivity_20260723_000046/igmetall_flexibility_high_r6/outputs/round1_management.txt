I’ll be blunt: I understand the insistence on protecting industrial capability, and I agree that a viable solution cannot be built on chaos, unilateralism, or a purely tactical wage squeeze.

But I do **not** accept the core premise of your position, because it does not address the actual scale of the problem.

### Where I agree
- **No cosmetic restructuring.** A temporary patch that leaves the cost base unchanged is not a solution.
- **We need a negotiated settlement.** We are not pretending we can simply impose lasting change without employee consent.
- **Transformation must be investable.** We do need to protect the company’s ability to fund future products, technology, and the path to 2030.

### Where I disagree
What you are demanding — binding site guarantees across all German plants, job security through 2030, no forced layoffs, no closures — is **not compatible with a genuine structural turnaround** if the current overcapacity and cost levels remain in place.

You cannot simultaneously insist on:
- preserving all sites,
- eliminating compulsory redundancies,
- maintaining full guarantees,
- and expecting management to restore competitiveness and margins,
while the European market shrinks and competitors are operating at materially lower cost.

That is not restructuring. That is freezing the current structure and hoping the market improves. It won’t.

### On plant closures and job cuts
I am not talking about closures as a political gesture. I am talking about **structural capacity correction**. If some plants are inefficient, underutilized, or no longer fit the future production footprint, then we have to be prepared to make hard decisions. And yes, that includes the possibility of closures or consolidation.

I do not say that lightly. But I will not pretend that every site can be preserved regardless of economics. The company cannot carry permanent overcapacity and still compete.

### On labour contributions
I also reject the idea that employee contributions are only acceptable if they come with blanket guarantees first. That sequence is backwards.

If the workforce wants a durable settlement, then a meaningful labour-cost contribution has to be part of it:
- lower labour costs,
- working-time flexibility,
- adjustment of legacy protections,
- and, where necessary, reductions in headcount.

That contribution must be real, not symbolic.

### On fairness
You both keep referring to fairness, and I understand why. But my fiduciary duty is not to preserve every historical promise regardless of economic reality. My duty is to restore the company’s competitiveness so that there is still a strong industrial base here in the future.

That means burden-sharing, yes — but burden-sharing within a framework of **real savings**. Not burden-sharing that avoids the hard choices.

### My position
So let me state it clearly:

1. **We need deep, durable cost and capacity reductions.**
2. **We are prepared to negotiate how those are achieved, including alternatives to outright closures where they deliver equivalent savings.**
3. **We are not prepared to accept a deal that leaves the German cost base structurally uncompetitive.**
4. **We are not prepared to sign up to guarantees that block necessary restructuring through 2030.**

If employee representatives want to avoid closures, then they need to put forward a package that matches the scale of the problem:
- credible capacity reductions,
- substantial labour-cost relief,
- and enforceable measures that actually change the economics.

If that package exists, we can discuss it seriously. If not, then the pressure for plant rationalization will remain on the table.
