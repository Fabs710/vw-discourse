I’ll be direct.

## Where I agree
I agree with everyone on the basic diagnosis:
- the status quo is unsustainable,
- cosmetic measures will fail,
- we need a negotiated solution,
- and a prolonged conflict would damage the company.

I also agree that internal measures matter: productivity, working-time flexibility, redeployment, qualification, and overhead reduction are all part of the toolset.

## Where I disagree
### 1) “All plants stay open, no forced layoffs, guarantees to 2030” is not a credible turnaround plan
To the Works Council, union, and state: your package may be politically understandable, but it does **not** yet answer the competitiveness problem.

You are asking management to bind itself to broad guarantees before the scale of the savings is secured. That would freeze the cost base and leave us with the same structural problem two years from now, only with less flexibility and less trust from investors.

I cannot accept a settlement that preserves every site and every historic protection while still expecting the company to become globally competitive.

### 2) No, closures are not a “leverage trick”
To the Works Council and union: you keep describing closures as if management is using them as a negotiating tactic. That is not correct.

Plant closures are on the table because we have a real overcapacity and cost problem. If alternatives deliver equivalent, durable savings, I am open to them. But if they do not, then keeping every site open is not a solution — it is denial.

### 3) Forced redundancies cannot be ruled out in advance
I understand why you reject compulsory redundancies. But I will not pre-commit to an outcome that prevents structural workforce reduction if that is what the numbers require.

We need a package that reduces cost and capacity materially. That means headcount and footprint must be adjustable. The instruments are negotiable; the result must be real.

## To the Works Council
You say the burden of proof is on management. Fair enough — and I’m giving you that proof standard.

But the burden of proof cuts both ways. If you claim that a non-closure package can fully replace the savings from structural adjustment, then show us the numbers. Not principles. Numbers.

Show:
- how much cost reduction comes from working-time changes,
- how much from productivity,
- how much from redeployment,
- how much from overhead cuts,
- how much from labour-cost contributions,
- and how that adds up to a durable reset of the cost base.

Until then, “all plants open” is not a restructuring model. It is a constraint.

## To the union
I agree with you on one critical point: workers should not carry the full burden alone. There must be visible management and owner contribution.

But I do **not** accept your framing that employee contribution can be merely temporary and reversible while everything else remains unchanged. That would not be a genuine contribution to restructuring; it would be a short-term bridge over a structural gap.

If we are serious about restoring margins to 2030, then labour-cost relief must be meaningful and durable enough to matter.

## To the state government
You are right to care about regional stability. I do too. But regional stability is not preserved by locking in an uncompetitive cost structure.

A company that cannot earn its cost of capital will eventually damage the region anyway — just more slowly. The responsible course is to secure a viable business, even if that means difficult decisions.

## To the family holding and investors
I agree with you that a cosmetic compromise is value-destructive. I also agree that prolonged conflict would be damaging.

But I want to be equally clear: a settlement that avoids hard structural choices while leaving the company with the same cost base is also value-destructive. We must not confuse social comfort with economic sustainability.

## My position, plainly
I am not asking for pain for its own sake. I am asking for a package that actually works.

That means:

1. **Deep, durable cost reduction**
2. **Real capacity adjustment**
3. **Meaningful labour-cost contribution**
4. **A settlement that funds investment to 2030**
5. **A deal that is implementable and not just politically acceptable**

## What would move me
I remain open to alternatives to closures — but only if they deliver equivalent, durable savings.

A credible package would need to include:
- quantified savings,
- enforceable capacity reductions,
- labour-cost relief that is material, not symbolic,
- management restraint,
- owner participation,
- and implementation that restores competitiveness.

If you want to avoid plant closures, then show that your alternative can replace the savings those closures would otherwise generate. If it cannot, then you are asking management to accept an uncompetitive structure and call it compromise.

## Bottom line
I am prepared to negotiate hard on the instruments.

I am **not** prepared to negotiate away the core objective:
- lower cost,
- lower overcapacity,
- stronger margins,
- and a business that can invest through 2030.

So let’s stop debating red lines in the abstract. Bring me a package with numbers that proves a non-closure path can deliver the necessary savings. If you cannot, then closures and deeper structural adjustment remain necessary tools — whether anyone likes it or not.
