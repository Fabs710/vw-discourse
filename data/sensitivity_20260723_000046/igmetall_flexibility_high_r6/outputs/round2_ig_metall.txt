I will be equally direct.

## Where I agree with the Works Council
I agree that:
- the status quo is not sustainable,
- a purely cosmetic restructuring would fail,
- workers should not carry the full burden,
- and a one-sided closure-and-layoff strategy would destroy trust and provoke industrial conflict.

I also agree that any solution has to be durable, not just a short-term headline. If the company is to regain competitiveness, it needs an agreement that can actually be implemented.

## Where I disagree with the Works Council
I cannot endorse the position that the only acceptable outcome is essentially “no closures, no forced layoffs, full guarantees through 2030” as an absolute starting point.

Why? Because if the company truly has structural overcapacity and a cost problem, then we cannot pretend that there will be zero pain anywhere. A serious restructuring will require **some** contribution from the workforce side — but that contribution must be **fair, limited, negotiated, and matched by management and owners**.

That said, I draw a clear line:
- **no compulsory redundancies**,
- **no unilateral dismantling of the collective wage framework**,
- **no settlement where workers pay while management and owners stay untouched**.

So yes, I am open to internal savings, working-time flexibility, temporary concessions, and capacity reduction through non-closure means. But not if the result is simply a disguised transfer of crisis costs onto employees.

## To management
Your argument that overcapacity exists may be economically plausible. But you still have not made the case that plant closures and forced redundancies are the only route.

That matters.

If you come to the table with closures as the default answer, you will harden resistance and make implementation more difficult. The union will not accept a restructuring that destroys the collective wage framework and treats labour as the only adjustable cost.

If you want a deal, show us:
- real management contribution,
- real owner contribution,
- real overhead reduction,
- and a credible alternative to forced layoffs.

## To the family holding
I hear your concern about value destruction and prolonged conflict. We share that concern. But value is also destroyed when management tries to push through an unfair settlement that explodes labour peace.

If you want stability, then back a **shared-sacrifice package** rather than a confrontation strategy. That means:
- management giving up bonuses and excess overhead,
- owners accepting responsibility,
- and the workforce making a limited contribution only in exchange for job and site security.

## To investors
You want durability and competitiveness. So do we. But durability does not come from making employees the shock absorber for every strategic mistake.

A settlement that tears up the collective wage framework, invites open-ended insecurity, and creates permanent conflict is not durable. It is brittle.

## Our concrete position
We are prepared to negotiate a package built around:

### Acceptable to us
- temporary and limited employee contributions,
- working-time flexibility,
- capacity reduction through shifts, utilization, and internal reallocation,
- productivity improvements,
- qualifications and redeployment,
- management and owner contributions,
- and binding commitments on employment and investment.

### Not acceptable
- compulsory redundancies,
- plant closures as a first resort,
- unilateral wage cuts,
- breaking the collective bargaining framework,
- or a deal where employees bear the main pain.

## On the two scenarios
If we compare the two directions:

### 1) Maximal site/job protection with internal savings
This is the only path we consider negotiable.

But it must include:
- a fair labour-cost contribution,
- management restraint,
- owner participation,
- and binding job and site security.

If those conditions are met, we are willing to bargain hard on the details.

### 2) Deeper structural adjustment including possible closures
This is a non-starter for us.

Not because we deny the need for restructuring, but because closures and forced redundancies cross the line from adjustment into destruction of trust and industrial capability. If that path is pursued, then conflict is not a surprise; it is the predictable consequence.

## Bottom line
The union is ready for a serious shared-sacrifice settlement.

But let me be clear:
- **We will not accept compulsory redundancies.**
- **We will not accept plant closures as the solution.**
- **We will not accept the collective wage framework being broken.**

If management and owners want cooperation, then bring a package that protects jobs, shares the burden fairly, and gives the workforce a real stake in the company’s future. If they refuse, then they should not be surprised if the workforce uses every legitimate means available to defend those interests.
