### Expert assessment: can a closure-free package realistically close the structural cost gap?

**Short answer:**  
A **closure-free** restructuring can **reduce** a structural cost gap meaningfully, but in a large German automotive manufacturer it is **usually unlikely** to close a **large, durable** gap **by itself** if the gap is driven by **permanent overcapacity, low utilization, and high fixed manufacturing overhead**.  

So the realistic answer is:

- **Structurally possible:** yes, if the gap is moderate and the company can obtain major concessions through productivity, labor-cost moderation, utilization improvement, and product allocation.
- **Theoretically possible but unlikely:** yes, if the gap is large, because the company would need unusually deep and durable concessions without shrinking the footprint.
- **Not credible under normal industrial constraints:** a package that preserves all German plants, avoids forced redundancies, and still fully offsets a large structural overcapacity problem **without** major volume reallocation, major headcount reduction through attrition, or plant-specific consolidation.

### What matters economically

For a carmaker, the cost gap is not just wages. It typically comes from:
1. **fixed plant costs**
2. **low capacity utilization**
3. **duplicate or underused production lines**
4. **energy and logistics costs**
5. **complexity**
6. **labor cost per unit**
7. **overhead allocation**
8. **insufficient model volume in the right plants**

If management is saying the German footprint is structurally too expensive, the key question is whether savings can be achieved through:
- working-time flexibility,
- wage moderation,
- bonus reduction,
- productivity gains,
- internal redeployment,
- delayed hiring,
- attrition,
- shorter shifts,
- line balancing,
- and better allocation of future models,

**without** needing to remove enough fixed cost to match the overcapacity.

### The central industrial constraint

If a plant is materially underutilized, you cannot fully solve that just by asking the remaining workforce to work differently.  
You can improve economics, but **you cannot make a half-empty factory economically equivalent to a right-sized one** unless one of three things happens:

1. **Volume is shifted in** from elsewhere,  
2. **the plant is repurposed with higher-value products**, or  
3. **headcount and overhead are reduced materially over time**.

If none of those occurs, the savings ceiling of a closure-free package is limited.

### What a closure-free package can realistically deliver

A negotiated package can plausibly deliver:
- **temporary labor-cost relief**
- **meaningful but partial structural savings**
- **better utilization**
- **some fixed-cost reduction through attrition and internal mobility**
- **productivity improvement**
- **bridge time for investment and product transition**

In a large OEM, that can be substantial. But if the required adjustment is, for example, a **double-digit percentage margin rescue** from a genuinely overbuilt domestic footprint, then **labor concessions alone are rarely enough**.

### What would make closure-free restructuring credible

A closure-free solution becomes more credible if management can show at least one of the following:

- **new model allocation** to German plants with sufficient volume
- **major platform consolidation** that reduces complexity
- **extensive attrition-based workforce reduction**
- **significant shift in production mix toward higher-margin vehicles**
- **substantial capex-backed productivity gains**
- **long-duration labor moderation with strong utilization improvement**

In other words: the package must be a **business redesign**, not just a wage compromise.

### What would make it not credible

A closure-free package is **not credible** if it relies mainly on:
- wage cuts without matching footprint rationalization,
- temporary measures to solve a permanent problem,
- guarantees that freeze the footprint while demand continues to fall,
- assuming that “flexibility” alone can absorb structural overcapacity,
- or hoping productivity will close a gap that is mostly capacity-driven.

### My judgment on the specific dispute

Given the facts you provided — shrinking European demand, competition from lower-cost and Chinese rivals, high German factory costs, and a brand missing its return target — it is **plausible that the company has a real structural cost problem**.

But based on normal restructuring practice:

- **A closure-free deal could be enough only if the true gap is moderate and if management is willing to trade plant protection for substantial, durable labor and volume commitments.**
- **If the gap is large and rooted in excess capacity, closure-free measures alone are unlikely to fully close it.**
- **If closures and forced redundancies are both excluded absolutely, then the most likely outcome is partial repair, not full structural reset.**

### Practical conclusion

The employee side is correct that **closures are not automatically proven to be the only solution**.  
Management and capital are correct that **a durable gap cannot be fixed by symbolism or temporary concessions**.

So the realistic verdict is:

- **Equivalent savings without closures are possible in principle, but only if the package includes major, quantified, durable structural changes beyond labor concessions.**
- **Absent those changes, a no-closure, no-forced-layoff package is unlikely to close the full structural gap in a large German carmaker.**
