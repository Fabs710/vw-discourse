I will be direct.

I agree with the diagnosis from management and investors: this company needs a **durable** competitiveness reset, not a cosmetic compromise. The cost base, footprint, and margins cannot simply be left as they are. That would erode long-term value and ultimately damage everyone, including the workforce and the state.

I also agree with the state and labour on one important point: a reckless confrontation would be value-destructive. Prolonged strikes, public escalation, and a broken trust environment are not acceptable outcomes for a core asset of the family holding.

But I do **not** accept the employee side’s attempt to convert the discussion into a categorical ban on closures and structural workforce reductions. That is not a solution; it is a veto on adjustment before the economics have been established.

## To the Works Council
You ask for hard numbers before any closure logic is considered. That is fair. Management should put the quantified case on the table.

But your red line — no closures, no compulsory redundancies, no binding flexibility — goes too far. If the plant network is structurally oversized, then preserving every site in its current form would itself be value-destructive. The company cannot be governed on the basis that every historical footprint must survive unchanged.

What I do agree with is this: if management wants serious negotiations, it must show:
- the exact savings gap,
- the required capacity reduction,
- the timeline to 2030,
- and a plant-by-plant comparison of alternatives.

A vague “all options” stance is not enough.

## To the Union
You are right that labour should not bear the burden alone. Any serious settlement must include management accountability and owner commitment to future investment and product allocation in Germany.

But I do **not** accept that a binding job guarantee through 2030 is the starting point. A guarantee that freezes the structure would prevent necessary realignment and lock in overcapacity. That is not stewardship; it is postponement.

If you want closures avoided, then show a package that delivers **equivalent permanent savings**, not just temporary relief. Time-limited wage moderation, working-time measures, redeployment, and productivity improvements may help, but by themselves they do not prove the cost gap can be closed structurally.

## To the Regional State Government
I respect your role and your concern for the industrial base. I agree that closures are not something to be treated lightly.

But I do not accept the suggestion that plant closures should be pushed so far back that they become functionally impossible. That would make the company less adaptable and less competitive, and eventually weaken the very regional base you want to protect.

A state-backed package can support restructuring, but only if it is economically credible. The burden is not on management alone, but neither can the state demand competitiveness while ruling out the tools that may be required to achieve it.

## To Management
You have support on the need for a serious restructuring, but not a blank cheque.

I want a disciplined, quantified proposal:
- what is the exact structural savings target?
- what footprint change is actually required?
- what measures are truly permanent?
- what would a closure-free alternative need to deliver?
- what investment, product allocation, and execution certainty accompanies the plan?

Without that, no one can judge whether the alternative packages being discussed are genuinely equivalent.

## To Institutional Investors
You are right to insist on durable savings and execution certainty. I share that. But toughness is not the objective in itself. The objective is **long-term value creation**.

If the process creates prolonged conflict, damaged execution, and weak follow-through, it will destroy value even if the headline savings look large. So yes, structural action is needed — but it must be based on a credible plan, not escalation for its own sake.

## My position
The family holding will support a restructuring that:
- restores margins,
- reduces structural cost,
- aligns capacity with demand,
- preserves the ability to invest in future technology,
- and avoids a drawn-out conflict that damages the enterprise.

We will **not** support:
- a cosmetic compromise,
- a settlement that preserves excess capacity,
- or a process that permanently impairs the company’s competitiveness.

## What I expect next
I want two things on the table now:

1. **From management:** a quantified restructuring case with savings target, capacity reduction, and timeline.
2. **From labour:** a quantified alternative package if it wants to avoid closures — one that demonstrates equivalent durable savings, not just temporary concessions.

If the alternative is real, I will examine it seriously. If it is not, then management must retain the option of deeper structural measures, including closures and workforce reductions.

That is not a threat. It is governance.
