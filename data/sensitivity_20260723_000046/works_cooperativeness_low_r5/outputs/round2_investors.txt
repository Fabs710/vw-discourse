We agree with the core diagnosis: this business needs a durable competitiveness reset, not a cosmetic compromise. The current cost base and footprint cannot keep drifting. If the company does not restore margins and investment capacity, shareholders will continue to bear the downside through weak returns and a depressed valuation.

That said, we are **not** prepared to endorse a process that substitutes confrontation for analysis.

## To management
You are right that temporary measures alone are not enough. We agree that the company needs **structural** savings, not just deferred pain.

But your position is still too generic. “All options on the table” is not a strategy; it is leverage. If you want capital markets to support a difficult restructuring, you need to table:
- the exact savings gap,
- the capacity reduction required,
- the timeline,
- and what level of labour-cost improvement is actually needed to get back to competitive margins.

Without that, closures remain a threat rather than a justified measure.

We also do **not** support a settlement that preserves excess capacity and then asks shareholders to absorb weak returns indefinitely. If the economics require plant closures or workforce reductions, then say so clearly and justify them. If you can achieve equivalent durable savings another way, that is preferable — but the burden of proof is on the alternative.

## To the Works Council and Union
We understand your resistance to closures, forced redundancies, and unilateral pay cuts. We also agree that prolonged strikes and industrial conflict destroy value fast.

But your current red lines are too broad to be acceptable **unless** you can demonstrate an equivalent, durable cost solution. A package built mainly on:
- temporary working-time changes,
- short-term flexibility,
- deferred bonuses,
- and general promises of future investment

will not be enough if it leaves the cost base structurally uncompetitive.

A binding job guarantee through 2030 is only acceptable if it does **not** lock in excess cost or prevent necessary realignment. Otherwise it just postpones the problem and weakens the business further.

## To the Regional State Government
We share your concern that a purely coercive process would be value-destructive. Prolonged conflict is not in shareholders’ interests.

But we do not accept the idea that German plant closures should be treated as off-limits in principle. If the company’s footprint is structurally too large, then the market will not reward political discomfort. It will punish weak margins.

That said, closures must be justified by a transparent business case, not used as a bluff. We support your demand for a quantified comparison of options.

## To the Family Holding
We agree that the enterprise needs a disciplined, quantified restructuring and not a vague “deal.” We also agree that trust and execution matter.

Where we differ is on the implicit assumption that toughness itself is the goal. It is not. The goal is **durable value creation**. If a process leads to prolonged conflict, damaged execution, and investor disappointment, it will destroy value even if the paper savings look large.

## Our bottom line
We will support a restructuring that is:
- **deep enough** to restore competitiveness,
- **quantified** and credible,
- **durable** rather than temporary,
- and **executed with certainty**.

We will **not** support:
- a cosmetic settlement,
- an arrangement that leaves structural overcapacity in place,
- or indefinite margin erosion in the name of avoiding hard decisions.

If labour wants to avoid closures, it needs to show an alternative that delivers **equivalent permanent savings** and a credible route back to competitive margins. If not, then management’s case for deeper structural measures becomes stronger.

So our position is simple:  
**we support disciplined cost reduction, not symbolic compromise.**  
The next round needs numbers, not posturing.
