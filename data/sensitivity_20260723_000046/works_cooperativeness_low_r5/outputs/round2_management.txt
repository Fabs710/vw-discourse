We are now at the point where slogans must give way to economics.

I’ll respond directly to what has been said.

## To the Works Council
You keep saying “no closures” and “no compulsory redundancies” as if those were principles that could exist independent of the business reality. They cannot.

I agree with you on one point: a **cosmetic** restructuring would be unacceptable. But that is not what management is proposing. We are saying the cost base and footprint are structurally too large for the market we actually face.

What I **do not** accept is your attempt to pre-empt the outcome by declaring German plant closures illegitimate before the numbers are even discussed. That is not a negotiation position; it is a veto claim.

You ask for the exact structural savings requirement. Fair enough — then let’s be precise:
- We need a **durable** reduction in factory cost and capacity.
- We need a labour-cost contribution that is real, not symbolic.
- We need a footprint that matches demand through 2030, not one that preserves every historical site.

If you want to avoid closures, then show me an alternative that produces **equivalent permanent savings**. Not temporary working-time reductions. Not deferrals. Not internal promises that evaporate after a year. Equivalent structural savings.

And no, a binding job guarantee through 2030 is not something I can accept as a starting point. A guarantee that blocks necessary realignment would lock in the very overcapacity we are trying to remove.

## To the Union
I appreciate that you are at least talking about working-time adjustments, redeployment, qualification, and a time-limited contribution. That is more constructive than a flat refusal.

But you are still drawing a hard line against the measures that may be needed to restore competitiveness. I have to be blunt: **a package that avoids closures in name but leaves the economics unchanged is not a solution.**

You asked for numbers. Here they are in strategic terms:
- The company has a structural cost problem.
- The European market is shrinking.
- German manufacturing costs are above target.
- The brand is missing its return-on-sales objective.
- If we do not reduce overcapacity and labour costs in a durable way, we will not have the investment capacity to fund future products and technology.

That is the business case. It is not vague, and it is not negotiable away by asserting social principles alone.

You also say no forced redundancies. I understand the political value of that line, but I am not prepared to rule out workforce reductions if that is what a serious restructuring requires. If there is a better alternative that creates the same permanent savings, bring it. If not, then we are not solving the problem.

## To the Regional State Government
I agree with you on one point: we cannot ignore the regional and industrial consequences of restructuring. Nor should we.

Where I disagree is your attempt to make plant closures virtually off-limits and to shift the burden of proof entirely onto management as if the burden of adjustment could be avoided by saying “prove it harder.”

The burden is not a legal trick. It is a commercial reality. We are dealing with a business that is structurally too expensive. If alternatives exist that deliver the same savings, I will consider them. But “capacity balancing,” “internal redeployment,” and “working-time measures” are not automatically equivalent to a permanent footprint adjustment.

You say the state will not support a process that destroys regional foundations. Understood. But regional foundations are not preserved by keeping uncompetitive structures alive until the margin base collapses. That is not protection; it is delay.

## To the Family Holding
You are right about one thing: management needs to be explicit on the target and the footprint logic. We will be.

But I do not accept the suggestion that closures are somehow just another abstract possibility to be debated endlessly. The reason they are on the table is simple: if the footprint is too large and too costly, then the business must either reduce it structurally or accept deterioration.

I also want to be clear that I am not asking capital to subsidize inefficiency forever. I am asking for a **durable** package that restores competitiveness. If labour, management, and owners each make a real contribution, then the company can invest through 2030. If the employee side refuses to countenance structural change, then it is not the owners who are blocking a future — it is the refusal to adapt.

## To Institutional Investors
You are right to insist on durable savings and execution certainty. I agree with that. Where I disagree is the idea that the only credible path is to force the workforce into concessions while the company must preserve its historical footprint.

We need a package that actually changes the economics. That may include plant closures. It may include workforce reductions. It may include changes to working time, pay structures, and flexibility. But it has to be **structural**.

What I will not support is a “deal” that:
- preserves excess capacity,
- avoids hard decisions,
- and still expects us to fund future products, technology, and margin recovery from an unchanged cost base.

That is precisely the kind of settlement that would disappoint investors later and damage the company now.

## On the core question: numbers and alternatives
Since everyone wants precision, here is the framework we should use in the next step:

1. **Management will table the required structural savings target**
   - cost reduction,
   - capacity reduction,
   - and timeline to 2030.

2. **Employee representatives will table a credible alternative package**
   - with quantified savings,
   - permanent effect,
   - and clear mechanisms.

3. **We will compare the two on equivalence**
   - not on rhetoric,
   - not on sentiment,
   - but on durable economics.

If the alternative package truly delivers equivalent structural savings without closures, I will examine it seriously. But if it relies mainly on temporary measures, deferred pain, or guarantees that prevent realignment, then it is not equivalent and it will not be enough.

## My bottom line
I am not here to preserve conflict for its own sake. I am here to restore competitiveness.

So let me say this plainly:
- **No**, I will not accept a settlement that leaves the cost base uncompetitive.
- **No**, I will not accept binding guarantees that prevent necessary restructuring.
- **No**, I will not accept a cosmetic deal dressed up as social peace.
- **Yes**, I am open to an alternative that achieves the same durable savings through other structural measures.

The burden now is on the employee side to show that such an alternative is real. If it is not, then the company must retain the option of closures, capacity cuts, and workforce reductions as part of a serious restructuring.
