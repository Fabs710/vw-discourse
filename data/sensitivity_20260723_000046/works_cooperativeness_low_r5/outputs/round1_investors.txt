From the perspective of dispersed capital-market shareholders, the key issue is not preserving any particular plant or protecting any one stakeholder’s comfort zone. The key issue is whether the company gets back to a **credible, durable, competitive cost base** that can support margins, investment, and value creation.

So I will be very direct.

## Where I agree
I agree with management, and partly with the family holding, on the core diagnosis:

- The cost base is too high.
- The current footprint is too expensive relative to the market.
- Temporary fixes alone will not be enough.
- If the company remains structurally uncompetitive, shareholders will keep bearing the downside through weak margins, lower returns, and a deteriorating valuation.

That part is real, and it matters.

I also agree with the point from labour and the state that a purely coercive process would be value-destructive. Prolonged industrial conflict, uncertainty, and an outcome that leaves the workforce hostile will damage execution and margins. That is not in shareholders’ interests either.

## Where I disagree
I do **not** support the employee side’s attempt to pre-emptively rule out the measures that may actually be necessary.

### On plant closures
If management’s analysis is that German overcapacity is structural, then plant closures cannot be taken off the table in advance. A company with this level of pressure cannot promise to preserve every site and still expect the market to believe the restructuring is serious.

If the works council and union want to avoid closures, they need to show a **credible equivalent** in hard numbers:
- permanent capacity reduction,
- measurable productivity gains,
- durable labour-cost improvement,
- and a footprint that is genuinely aligned with demand.

Not temporary deferrals. Not symbolic concessions. Real savings.

### On job guarantees
Binding job guarantees through 2030 may be politically attractive, but from a shareholder perspective they are only acceptable if they do not lock in excess cost or block necessary realignment. A guarantee that preserves jobs at the expense of competitiveness is not protection; it is deferred deterioration.

### On cost burden sharing
I agree that labour should not carry all the burden. But I also do not accept an outcome where capital funds the transition while the operational structure remains unchanged. Shareholders will not support paying for restructuring that does not actually fix the economics.

## What would move us
We would support a negotiated package if it contains all of the following:

1. **A quantified path back to competitive margins**
   - not just broad commitments, but a clear cost and savings target.

2. **Durable structural measures**
   - permanent capacity adjustment,
   - not only short-term working-time cuts or wage deferrals.

3. **A credible labour contribution**
   - something measurable and lasting, not a one-off gesture.

4. **Execution certainty**
   - a deal that reduces the risk of prolonged strikes and board-level deadlock.

5. **Investment discipline**
   - future product and technology commitments only if they are matched by a real restructuring, not as a substitute for it.

## My concern with the current positions
- **Works council / union:** your red lines may be understandable, but if they prevent durable cost correction, they are not compatible with shareholder value.
- **State:** I understand the political need to protect regional employment, but shareholder capital cannot be expected to subsidize an uncompetitive structure indefinitely.
- **Management:** you are right on the need for change, but “all options on the table” is not enough. You need a clear, quantified, and executable plan, not just leverage.
- **Family owners:** I agree with the need for toughness, but the answer is not confrontation for its own sake. If the process destroys trust and drags on, value is lost fast.

## Bottom line
We will support a restructuring that is **deep enough to restore competitiveness**, even if that includes painful measures. We will **not** support a cosmetic compromise that preserves structural inefficiency and calls it a settlement.

If the employee side wants to avoid closures, it must offer a package that delivers **equivalent durable savings** and a credible route to competitive margins. If not, then management’s case for deeper structural cuts becomes stronger, not weaker.

That is the standard capital markets will apply.
