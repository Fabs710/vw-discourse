I will be frank as well.

The discussion has now made one thing clear: everyone agrees the company needs a real restructuring, but management and capital still want to keep **German plant closures** as a standing threat, while labour is rightfully refusing to negotiate under that sword. That is exactly the deadlock we have to break — and I am not prepared to break it by sacrificing sites and jobs in the state.

## To management
I agree that a cosmetic solution would be irresponsible. I also agree that the company needs durable competitiveness, not temporary relief.

But I do **not** accept your repeated implication that closures are the default answer unless labour proves otherwise. That is not a neutral business analysis; it is a unilateral framing choice.

You keep asking for “equivalent permanent savings.” Fine. Then put the numbers on the table in a way that allows a real comparison:
- the exact savings gap,
- the capacity reduction actually required,
- the timeline to 2030,
- and the plant-by-plant economics.

Until then, I will not treat closures as justified. I will treat them as a threat to force concessions.

And let me be equally clear: a binding employment-security commitment is not “locking in overcapacity” by definition. It is the basis on which a negotiated, socially sustainable restructuring becomes possible. If management wants flexibility, it must offer something concrete in return — not just ask labour to absorb all the risk.

## To the Works Council and Union
I agree with you on the core point: the burden cannot fall on labour alone, and no one should be asked to accept plant closures or compulsory redundancies as the first move.

I also agree that temporary measures by themselves are not enough if the company truly needs durable change.

Where I would sharpen your position is this: you should be ready to discuss a **quantified, time-limited contribution package** if — and only if — it is tied to binding guarantees on:
- sites,
- jobs,
- investment,
- future product allocation,
- and co-determination.

That means working-time flexibility, moderated bonuses, redeployment, qualification, productivity agreements, and internal load balancing can be part of a real solution. But they must be part of a package that **preserves the regional industrial base**, not a disguised path to closure.

What I will not support is any package that leaves the state’s plants exposed to later shutdowns after the workforce has already given up concessions.

## To the Controlling Family Holding and Investors
I hear your demand for durable value creation. That is legitimate. I do not dispute the need for structural savings or a credible plan.

What I do dispute is the assumption that the only credible route is to permanently shrink the German footprint in a way that damages the company’s role as a regional anchor and undermines trust in co-determination.

A settlement that destroys industrial confidence, triggers prolonged conflict, and weakens the company’s social license is **not** durable. It may look decisive on paper, but it risks long-term strategic damage.

If you want this restructuring to be credible, you must accept that the company’s long-term health depends on more than short-term cost cuts. It depends on:
- stable industrial relations,
- qualified workers,
- investment in German plants,
- and a footprint strategy that does not hollow out the state’s economic base.

So yes, I support disciplined restructuring. No, I do not support using closures and redundancies as the first or implied solution.

## To the Moderator’s compromise options
Of the three options, only the **two-track package** is acceptable as a starting point — and only if Track B is genuinely independent, time-bound, and not a disguised closure roadmap.

That means:
- immediate non-structural relief can be discussed,
- but the structural review must compare closure and non-closure options transparently,
- and it must include regional and employment consequences, not just margin math.

However, I want to be clear: the purpose of that review cannot be to normalize closures. It must test whether the company can be restored **without** sacrificing German plants and the state’s co-determination role.

## My position
The state will support a tough restructuring if it meets these conditions:
1. **No German plant closures as the default path**
2. **No compulsory redundancies**
3. **A real, quantified comparison of alternatives**
4. **Binding investment and product commitments in Germany**
5. **A negotiated workforce contribution that is time-limited and tied to security**
6. **Preservation of the state’s co-determination and veto role**

That is not sentimentality. It is responsible ownership of a company that is central to the regional economy.

## What I am prepared to support
I am prepared to support:
- working-time adjustments,
- moderated variable pay,
- internal redeployment,
- qualification and upskilling,
- phased capacity adjustments,
- and a negotiated contribution package,

provided it is anchored in real guarantees for sites, jobs, and future production.

## What I will not support
I will not support:
- plant closures presented as inevitable before the analysis is complete,
- forced layoffs,
- unilateral wage cuts,
- or a settlement that strips the state of its co-determination leverage.

The message from this side is simple: **we are ready for restructuring, not for industrial self-destruction.**

## Next step
Management should now publish the quantified restructuring case. Labour should come forward with a quantified closure-free alternative package. And the ownership side should be prepared to commit to the investment and product logic that makes a negotiated settlement credible.

If the numbers show that alternatives truly cannot deliver the needed durable effect, then we will have to examine that honestly. But until then, I will not endorse closures as the price of “credibility.”

That is my line.
