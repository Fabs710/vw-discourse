I will be equally direct.

The Works Council is right on the core point: this crisis cannot be solved by turning German workers into the shock absorber for management’s failed cost structure. And I agree that “all options on the table” is not a plan. If management wants a serious negotiation, it has to put numbers on the table.

But I also want to be clear where I draw the line and where I do not.

## To management
You still have not given a credible, quantified answer to the central question:

- What is the exact structural savings target?
- What capacity reduction is actually required?
- What part of that is truly permanent?
- And why would that require plant closures rather than negotiated alternatives?

Until those numbers are transparent, your closure threats look less like necessity and more like leverage.

If you want the union to consider concessions, then you must first show:
1. the cost gap,
2. the specific savings required,
3. the timeline,
4. and the measures already exhausted before closures are even discussed.

Without that, we are not negotiating facts; we are being asked to concede under pressure.

## To the family holding and institutional investors
I hear your focus on competitiveness and durable savings. That is legitimate. But you are still trying to define “durable” in a way that puts the entire burden on labour while leaving the rest of the governance side largely untouched.

That will not work.

A credible turnaround must include:
- management accountability,
- owner commitment,
- investment in future products and technology in Germany,
- and a fair contribution from capital, not just payroll.

If you insist on plant closures and forced redundancies, you should expect resistance. Not because we are unwilling to change, but because those measures cross a fundamental line.

And let me state this clearly: **we will not accept compulsory redundancies.**
We will not accept a settlement where workers pay twice — first through job losses, then through unilateral wage erosion.

## To the state government
I appreciate your rejection of closures as a first resort. But the union’s position is stronger than “first resort.” We do not accept closures as the answer at all unless every credible alternative has been demonstrated to fail.

That means:
- working-time measures,
- capacity balancing,
- internal redeployment,
- qualification,
- product allocation,
- and time-limited workforce contributions.

Those are the tools that must be used before anyone even starts talking about shutting plants.

## What we are prepared to discuss
We are ready to negotiate a serious package if it protects the industrial base and distributes the burden fairly.

That package can include:
- temporary working-time reductions,
- deferred or reduced bonus components,
- a time-limited employee contribution,
- productivity improvements negotiated with the workforce,
- redeployment and qualification,
- and capacity adjustments that avoid forced layoffs.

We have already shown openness to this path. But it must be tied to binding security, not vague promises.

## What is not acceptable
Let me be explicit:

- No compulsory redundancies.
- No plant closures in Germany as part of a default restructuring path.
- No unilateral pay cuts.
- No dismantling of the collective wage agreement.
- No “restructuring” where labour carries the sacrifice and management/owners carry the narrative.

## On guarantees
You keep calling binding employment security a “lock-in.” For us, it is the minimum condition for trust.

If employees are asked to contribute, then the company must offer:
- site security,
- job security,
- investment commitments,
- and a genuine future-product plan for Germany.

That is how durable settlements are built. Not through threats.

## Where I differ from the Works Council
I support the Works Council’s resistance, but I want to be precise: the union is open to a negotiated contribution from labour if it is fair, limited, and matched by binding commitments from the company side.

So I am not saying “no change, ever.”  
I am saying: **no change at the expense of jobs, wage standards, and industrial security.**

## Bottom line
If management wants a deal, it needs to come back with:
1. hard numbers,
2. a restructuring path that avoids plant closures and forced redundancies,
3. binding site and employment security,
4. and a burden-sharing package that includes management and owners.

If it cannot do that, then the conflict will continue — in bargaining and on the shop floor.

We are not here to endorse a scripted downsizing. We are here to defend the workforce and reach a fair settlement.
