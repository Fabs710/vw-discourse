## To management
I agree with you on one point: the company needs a **real restructuring**, not cosmetic measures. The independent expert has also made clear that a closure-free package is not automatically enough if the structural cost gap is large.

But that does **not** give management a blank cheque to keep **German closures** on the table as the default answer.

You still have not shown, in a way that justifies political and social damage, that closures are necessary rather than merely the easiest way to force concessions. If you want us to consider anything beyond a negotiated alternative, then publish the numbers:

- plant-by-plant fixed costs,
- utilization rates,
- headcount by site and function,
- labor cost per vehicle,
- the exact savings gap,
- and the NPV comparison of closure, hybrid, and closure-free scenarios.

Until then, “closures remain on the table” is leverage, not proof.

And I will say this clearly: if your restructuring plan treats the state’s plants as disposable before the alternatives are properly tested, then you are not acting as a responsible steward of the company’s long-term health.

## To the controlling family holding and institutional investors
I understand your insistence on durable value creation. I share the concern that a weak, temporary compromise would only postpone the problem.

But I do **not** accept your framing that preservation of German sites is somehow sentimentality and that footprint reduction is the only serious form of discipline.

The expert has said exactly the point you keep trying to bypass: a closure-free or hybrid package may well be a **partial** solution, and maybe more than partial if it includes major volume and product reallocation, utilization improvement, and durable structural changes. That means you cannot simply assume closures are superior because they are familiar or cleaner from a cost-accounting perspective.

What I want from capital is not rhetoric about toughness. I want a serious willingness to test whether the company can be fixed **without hollowing out its regional anchor function**. If the answer truly is no, prove it with the data.

## To the Works Council and Union
I agree with you that management must not be allowed to use the crisis to break the workforce’s protections. I also agree that no one should be asked to accept unilateral wage cuts, forced redundancies, or plant closures as the starting point.

Your demand for numbers is correct. Your insistence that closures are **not proven** to be necessary is also correct.

But I need to be just as clear with you: a simple no-closure slogan is not enough either. If the structural gap is real, then the workforce must be prepared to discuss a **serious quantified contribution package** that is durable, not just temporary.

That package can include:
- working-time flexibility,
- moderated bonuses,
- internal redeployment,
- qualification and upskilling,
- productivity measures,
- and time-limited labor contributions.

What I will support is a negotiated restructuring that protects jobs and sites **and** restores competitiveness.

What I will **not** support is:
- compulsory redundancies,
- plant closures as the first move,
- or a deal that shifts the whole burden onto labour while management and owners avoid real commitment.

## To the state government
I appreciate the clear line you have taken: no closures as the default path, and the industrial base matters. I agree.

But I want to sharpen our position. We should not move into a process where closures are treated as a standing threat during bargaining. That is poison to trust and undermines the very social partnership that has long been part of this company’s strength.

I would support a **time-bound independent review** of the alternatives, but only if it comes with a **moratorium on closure decisions during the review period**. Not because closures are off the table forever, but because negotiation under immediate shutdown threat is not a genuine negotiation.

And I would insist that any state-backed support or political legitimacy for a deal be tied to:
- binding investment in German plants,
- future product allocation,
- qualification,
- internal placement,
- and preservation of co-determination and the state’s special statutory role.

## Where I stand on the compromise options
The moderator’s hybrid and two-stage concepts are useful only if they are structured correctly.

I could support:
1. **A quantified hybrid package** that combines labor contributions with product allocation shifts, working-time measures, productivity gains, and targeted site support.
2. **An independent review** that compares closure, hybrid, and closure-free options on equal economic terms.
3. **A closure moratorium during the review**, so the process is not distorted by threat politics.

What I cannot support is a “review” that is really a delayed closure roadmap.

## My bottom line
I am not moving off my core interests:

- protect jobs in the state,
- protect plants in the state,
- preserve the company as a regional economic anchor,
- and preserve the state’s co-determination and veto role.

At the same time, I accept that the company must become more competitive. So I am prepared to back a **negotiated restructuring** if it is credible, quantified, and fair.

But I will not accept:
- plant closures as the starting logic,
- forced redundancies,
- or a settlement that weakens the regional economic base and the company’s social legitimacy.
