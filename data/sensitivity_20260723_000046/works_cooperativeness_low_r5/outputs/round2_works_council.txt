Management is still trying to turn a crisis of strategy and competitiveness into a crisis of employment, and I reject that framing.

Let me respond point by point.

### To management
You say “all options must be on the table.” No. Not if the options include closing German plants and breaking the job-security framework that has underpinned this company’s stability for decades. That is not a neutral restructuring tool; it is a line crossed.

You still have not answered the central question: **what exactly is the structural savings requirement, and why does it supposedly require plant closures?**  
Until you put hard numbers on the table, “all options” is just leverage talk.

If the company needs permanent savings, then show:
- the cost gap you claim,
- the capacity reduction you actually need,
- the time horizon,
- and the alternative measures that have already been ruled out.

Without that, you are asking the workforce to accept irreversible damage on trust alone. That is not serious.

### To the family holding and capital markets
You both talk about “durable competitiveness” as if it automatically justifies closures and job cuts. It does not.

A competitive company is not one that destroys its own industrial base and then congratulates itself on lower costs. If the savings come from gutting sites, forcing redundancies, and weakening co-determination, then you may get a short-term accounting improvement and a long-term strategic wound.

You say a binding job guarantee could “lock in excess cost.” I say a lack of guarantees locks in mistrust, conflict, and industrial instability. That is also a cost — a very real one.

And let me be clear: **the burden cannot be placed on labour alone.**  
If you want a restructuring that is credible, then owners and management must commit to:
- investment in German plants,
- future product allocation,
- qualification and internal redeployment,
- and a genuine industrial plan that makes use of the existing workforce rather than discarding it.

### To the state government
I appreciate your rejection of closures as a first resort. But “first resort” is not enough. The workforce cannot accept closures as a later resort either.

What we need is not a staged path toward shutdowns. We need a binding commitment that German plants remain open, with the work and investment distributed fairly across the footprint. If that requires capacity adjustments, then those must be handled through working-time measures, internal transfers, product allocation, and negotiated load balancing — not through closure decisions.

### On the numbers
The moderator is right: we need numbers. But numbers must not be used to dress up a predetermined closure plan.

So here is the standard we will apply:
- If management claims an “equivalent alternative” is impossible, it must prove that with a transparent calculation.
- If it claims labour contributions are insufficient, it must state exactly what contribution would be sufficient **without** closures or forced layoffs.
- If it wants flexibility, it must explain why that flexibility cannot be achieved through reversible or negotiated measures first.

### What we are prepared to discuss
We are not refusing change. We are refusing **job destruction as the method**.

We are prepared to discuss:
- temporary working-time adjustments,
- internal redeployment across plants,
- qualification and upskilling,
- shifts in product allocation,
- productivity improvements agreed with the workforce,
- delayed or moderated variable pay elements,
- and a fair, time-limited employee contribution **only if** it is tied to hard guarantees.

### What we are not prepared to discuss
- plant closures in Germany,
- compulsory redundancies,
- unilateral pay cuts,
- the dismantling of co-determination,
- or a restructuring that treats German workers as the disposable adjustment variable.

### My position going into this round
If management wants serious engagement, it must stop hiding behind generic restructuring language and put forward:
1. the exact structural savings target,
2. a footprint plan that excludes German closures,
3. binding employment security through 2030,
4. and a real role for co-determination in shaping the industrial plan.

If that is not on the table, then there is nothing to negotiate — only resistance.

And I will say this plainly: if closures or forced layoffs remain part of the plan, the workforce will continue to resist them in the boardroom, in bargaining, and on the shop floor.
