I agree with the Works Council on the core point: this crisis cannot be solved by forcing all the pain onto the workforce.

Let me be just as clear from the union side:

- No compulsory redundancies.
- No plant closures in Germany.
- No dismantling of the collective wage framework under pressure.
- No “restructuring” that means workers pay twice: through job losses and through open-ended wage concessions.

Management says the cost base must come down. We do not deny that the company faces real competitive pressure. But cost reduction is not the same thing as collective punishment. If the company needs a durable solution, then it must be built on shared sacrifice and on preserving the industrial base, not on threatening livelihoods first and negotiating later.

Where we are open:
- temporary or negotiated working-time reductions,
- capacity adjustments,
- deferred or reduced bonus elements,
- employee contributions tied to a binding security package,
- qualification and redeployment,
- productivity measures that do not destroy jobs or sites.

Where we are not open:
- closures dressed up as “strategic consolidation,”
- forced layoffs,
- unilateral pay cuts,
- the erosion of hard-won protections without equivalent commitments from management and owners.

If management genuinely wants a turnaround, it must show three things at the same time:
1. credible investment in future products and technology in Germany,
2. binding guarantees for sites and employment,
3. a fair burden-sharing package that includes management and capital, not just labour.

I also want to address the political and ownership sides clearly. The company’s governance structure gives multiple actors veto power for a reason: to prevent reckless moves that destroy long-term industrial capability. A board majority will not legitimize a plan that tears up the social contract and destabilizes the workforce. If the family owners and state stakeholders want stability, they should be pushing management toward a negotiated solution, not endorsing plant closure threats as leverage.

So our position is this: we are ready to negotiate hard, and we are ready to make contributions if they are part of a serious package. But if the proposal remains built around closures and compulsory redundancies, then we will oppose it with all available means, including industrial action. That is not a threat for effect; it is the necessary response to an unacceptable path.
