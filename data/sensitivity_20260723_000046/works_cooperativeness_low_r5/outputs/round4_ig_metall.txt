## Industrial Union position, round 4

I will be very clear: **we still reject plant closures and compulsory redundancies as restructuring tools**, and we will not bargain as if those outcomes are acceptable defaults. The independent expert has confirmed what the workforce already knows: there is a structural problem, but **closures have not been proven as the only necessary solution**. That matters.

## To management
You keep trying to shift the burden of proof onto the workforce while keeping closure threats alive.

That is not acceptable.

If you want us to consider a serious labour contribution, then you must first publish:
- the exact savings gap,
- plant-by-plant economics,
- the capacity reduction you actually claim is required,
- the timeline to 2030,
- and the cost and effect of each alternative scenario.

Until then, “closures remain on the table” is not a business case. It is leverage.

And let me say this plainly: a binding job guarantee is not a luxury demand. It is the minimum condition for trust after years of promises, and especially now that the peace obligation has ended and our members are being asked to contribute.

## To the controlling family holding and investors
You keep talking about “equivalent permanent savings” as though closures are the obvious benchmark and anything else is somehow soft.

That is not analysis. That is preference.

The expert has already said a closure-free or hybrid package may be economically credible if it:
- restores utilization,
- lowers fixed cost,
- reallocates future volume,
- and produces durable savings through 2030.

So the burden is now on you to prove that these alternatives are insufficient. Not on labour to accept shutdowns because they are convenient to finance or easier to present to markets.

And I need to be blunt: if you force closures, you do **not** get execution certainty. You get conflict, strikes, damaged trust, and a company that is weaker socially and industrially. That is long-term value destruction, not discipline.

## To the regional state government
I agree with you on one central point: the burden cannot fall on labour alone, and a cosmetic compromise would be irresponsible.

But “no closures as the default path” is not enough. If the state truly wants to protect the industrial base, then it should demand:
- a binding no-closure moratorium during the review,
- a genuine no-forced-layoffs framework through 2030,
- binding investment in German plants,
- binding future product allocation,
- qualification and internal placement commitments,
- and formal protection of co-determination in the industrial plan.

Anything less leaves the workforce exposed while management keeps the closure threat alive.

## To the Works Council
I support your refusal of compulsory redundancies and unilateral wage cuts.

Where I want to sharpen the position is this: any workforce contribution must be:
- time-limited,
- reversible where possible,
- and strictly tied to hard guarantees on sites, jobs, investment, and product allocation.

No one should be asked to surrender pay, time, or conditions while plant shutdowns remain possible. That is not partnership. That is extraction.

## On the compromise proposals
The moderator’s two-track or two-stage ideas are only acceptable if they are built on one hard premise:

**No German plant closures. No compulsory redundancies. No closure threat during bargaining.**

If the “review” is just a way to postpone shutdowns until the pressure is lower, then it is not a review. It is a staged closure plan. We will not cooperate with that.

A real process would look like this:
1. Management publishes the full numbers.
2. Labour tables a closure-free package.
3. An independent review tests whether that package can deliver the required savings without closures.
4. During that process, all closure threats are suspended.
5. If a deal is reached, it must include binding site and job security through 2030.

That is the only serious sequence.

## What we are prepared to discuss
We are prepared to discuss a fair, time-limited workforce contribution if, and only if, it is protected by binding guarantees.

That can include:
- working-time adjustments,
- moderated bonus components,
- internal redeployment,
- qualification and upskilling,
- productivity measures,
- and temporary flexibility.

What it cannot include:
- compulsory redundancies,
- plant closures,
- dismantling the job-security framework,
- or a hidden pathway to later shutdowns.

## What we are not moving on
Let me restate the red lines, because this room keeps testing them:

- **No German plant closures.**
- **No compulsory redundancies.**
- **No unilateral wage cuts.**
- **No erosion of co-determination.**
- **No “review” that is really a delayed closure decision.**
- **No bargain that treats labour as disposable.**

## My challenge back to management and capital
If you truly believe the closure-free route cannot work, then prove it with numbers under independent verification.

Until then, I will not accept the premise that shutting plants is necessary. It is one option among many, and it is the one with the highest social damage, the highest conflict risk, and the weakest legitimacy.

If you want a constructive answer from us, bring:
- a real business case,
- a binding no-closure moratorium during the review,
- and a 2030 security framework.

Without those, there is no basis for agreement.

And if you try to force a closure-first outcome, then you already know what follows: resistance in bargaining, in the boardroom, and on the shop floor.
