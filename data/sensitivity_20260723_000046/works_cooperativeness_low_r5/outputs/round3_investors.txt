We agree with the **diagnosis** from management, the family holding, and partly the state: this company has a real structural cost problem, and a cosmetic deal would be value-destructive. If margins stay weak, shareholders continue to absorb the downside through a depressed valuation and poor risk-adjusted returns.

But we also agree with the moderator’s core point: **the issue now is not whether change is needed; it is whether the proposed path is economically credible and executable.**

## To management
You are right that temporary measures alone are not enough. We would not support a settlement that just defers pain while leaving the cost base uncompetitive.

However, your argument is still too generic for the scale of the proposed action. “Durable structural savings” is the right direction, but we need the **numbers** before we can judge whether closures are justified or whether an alternative could truly match them.

Please table, in plain terms:
- the exact savings gap,
- the capacity reduction required,
- the timeline to 2030,
- and the margin impact needed to get back to a competitive return-on-sales level.

Until you do that, plant closures remain a threatened instrument, not a demonstrated necessity.

## To the Works Council and Union
We understand your refusal to accept a cosmetic compromise or a prolonged conflict. We also understand why you resist closures and compulsory redundancies.

But your current position is still too absolute for investors to endorse as a final outcome. A binding no-closure pledge through 2030 is only acceptable if you can show a **closure-free package with equivalent permanent savings**. Temporary working-time changes, bonus moderation, and redeployment are helpful, but they do **not** automatically solve a structural cost problem.

We are open to a negotiated package if it is:
- durable,
- quantified,
- and clearly sufficient to restore competitiveness.

What we cannot support is a deal that preserves excess capacity and then expects shareholders to live with structurally impaired margins.

## To the Regional State Government
We share your concern about avoiding unnecessary industrial damage. Prolonged strikes and broken trust would destroy value quickly.

But “no closures as a default” is not enough. The state should insist on a **real comparison** of:
- closure scenario,
- non-closure scenario,
- savings delivered,
- employment effects,
- and investment effects.

If the non-closure route can genuinely match the savings, that is preferable. If it cannot, then the company must not be forced into preserving an uncompetitive footprint for political reasons.

## To the Controlling Family Holding
We agree with your emphasis on durable value creation and disciplined restructuring. We also agree that a reckless confrontation would be value-destructive.

Where we differ is that we do not think “toughness” itself is the test. The test is **whether the restructuring restores competitive margins with execution certainty**. A drawn-out fight that damages the brand, delays investment, or weakens operations is not a win just because the cost cuts are larger on paper.

## Our position
We will support a restructuring only if it is:
- **deep enough** to restore competitiveness,
- **quantified** and credible,
- **durable**, not temporary,
- and **executable without unnecessary conflict**.

We will **not** support:
- a cosmetic compromise,
- a settlement that leaves structural overcapacity in place,
- or indefinite margin erosion in the name of avoiding hard decisions.

## What we want next
We support the moderator’s two-track approach, but with one condition: Track B must be time-bound and independently verified, not a holding pattern.

So the next step should be:
1. management publishes the quantified restructuring case,
2. the employee side publishes a quantified closure-free alternative,
3. an independent review tests whether the alternative delivers equivalent permanent savings.
