I will respond plainly.

## Where I agree
I agree with the independent expert and with management on the central economic point: this company has a **real structural competitiveness problem**. A cosmetic settlement, or a package built only on temporary relief, would be value-destructive. It would protect appearances and weaken the asset.

I also agree with the state and labour on one practical point: a prolonged public confrontation would destroy value quickly. Strikes, reputational damage, and governance paralysis would hurt the company, the workforce, and the owners alike.

## Where I disagree
I do **not** agree with the employee side’s attempt to convert the current uncertainty into a **categorical ban on closures and structural workforce reductions**.

That position is not credible as a governing answer to a structural cost problem. It may be understandable politically, but it is not enough for a company that must restore margins and fund future investment.

I also do **not** accept the state’s push for a closure moratorium as a starting condition if that moratorium is meant to pre-empt any real structural adjustment. A review period is useful only if it tests serious alternatives, not if it freezes the situation and turns a difficult decision into permanent drift.

## What the expert actually showed
The expert did **not** say that closures are unnecessary. The expert said something narrower and more important:

- the structural problem is plausible,
- closure-free measures can reduce the gap,
- but if the overcapacity is large, closure-free measures are **unlikely to fully close it** unless they include major durable changes beyond labor concessions.

That is consistent with our position. It means:
- yes, alternatives should be tested,
- no, no one gets to assume that they will be sufficient,
- and no, a blanket no-closure pledge cannot be the default outcome.

## To the Works Council and Union
You are right to demand numbers. I support that. Management should publish the plant-by-plant economics, the savings gap, the utilization picture, and the NPV comparison of the scenarios.

But I reject your claim that a binding job guarantee through 2030 is the “minimum condition” for trust. It may be the minimum condition for **your** comfort, but it is not automatically compatible with the company’s need to correct overcapacity. A guarantee of that kind can become a straightjacket.

You also say a closure-free package can work if it restores utilization and lowers fixed cost. Fine. Then show the package. But do not ask the owners to pre-commit to preserving every site before that test is done.

## To the regional state government
I understand your statutory role and your concern for the industrial base. I do not dismiss it.

But I do not accept the idea that preserving industrial identity is the same as preserving every current plant configuration. The company’s long-term value does not come from freezing the footprint. It comes from keeping the enterprise competitive enough to remain a major industrial base at all.

If the state wants to support a negotiated process, that is reasonable. If it wants to veto the possibility of structural realignment, that is not stewardship.

## To management
You have my support on the **need** for durable restructuring. But I will not support an undisciplined or vague process.

You need to publish, clearly and quickly:
- the exact savings gap,
- the required capacity reduction,
- the timeline to 2030,
- plant-by-plant fixed cost and utilization,
- and the NPV comparison of:
  - closure scenario,
  - hybrid scenario,
  - closure-free scenario.

Without that, you do not have the basis for a final decision.

But once those numbers are on the table, I expect the conclusion to be serious. If the closure-free package cannot match the economics, then we should not pretend that it can.

## My position on the moratorium idea
I am **not** willing to agree to a blanket closure moratorium as a governing principle.

I would consider a **time-limited review period** if, and only if:
- it is short,
- it is numerically disciplined,
- it is independently verified,
- and it does not become a device for indefinite delay.

A review is acceptable. A freeze that removes management’s ability to act is not.

## What I will support
I will support a restructuring that:
- restores competitiveness,
- reduces structural cost,
- aligns footprint with demand,
- protects future investment,
- and avoids a reckless conflict.

I am open to a hybrid package if it is genuinely durable and economically equivalent.

## What I will not support
I will not support:
- a cosmetic compromise,
- a settlement that preserves excess capacity,
- a blanket no-closure pledge through 2030,
- a package that rules out workforce reductions regardless of economics,
- or a process that permanently impairs enterprise value.

## What happens next
I support the call for numbers and independent verification. I would add one more thing:

If the employee side wants to avoid plant closures, it must table a **quantified, durable, closure-free alternative** that actually matches the savings target. Not slogans, not general promises, but numbers.

If that package is credible, I will examine it seriously. If it is not, then the company must keep the option of deeper structural measures on the table.

That is not hostility. It is responsible ownership.
