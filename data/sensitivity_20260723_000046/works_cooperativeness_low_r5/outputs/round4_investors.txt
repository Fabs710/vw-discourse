We agree with the expert conclusion and with management on the **core economic point**: this company has a **structural cost and capacity problem**, and a cosmetic settlement would destroy value. We also agree with the family holding that the company cannot drift into prolonged underperformance while pretending to have solved the issue.

But we are **not** prepared to endorse a settlement that preserves an uncompetitive cost base or structurally impaired margins. That remains our line.

## To management
You are right to insist that temporary concessions alone are unlikely to be enough if the cost gap is truly large. We agree.

Where we disagree is on the way you are using that diagnosis. “Closures remain on the table” is not yet a business case. It is leverage until the numbers are published. The expert has made that clear.

So publish the actual case:
- the exact savings gap,
- plant-by-plant fixed cost and utilization,
- the capacity reduction required,
- the NPV comparison of closure, hybrid, and closure-free options,
- and the margin impact needed to restore the return-on-sales target.

If the closure-free package cannot match the economics, fine — then the company needs deeper structural action. But we will not support a process that assumes closures are necessary before the evidence is shown.

## To the Works Council and Union
We agree with you on one point: closures are not yet proven to be the only solution. That matters.

We also agree that a deliberate, quantified alternative should be tested properly before irreversible decisions are made. A binding no-closure moratorium during the review period is not automatically unreasonable **if** it is genuinely time-limited and tied to a serious, independent process.

Where we disagree is with any expectation that shareholders will accept **preserved excess capacity** or a “security-first” outcome that leaves margins structurally weak. A job guarantee through 2030 is only acceptable if it sits inside a package that still delivers durable cost reduction and competitive economics.

In practical terms:
- working-time moderation,
- bonus restraint,
- redeployment,
- qualification,
- productivity gains,
- and product allocation shifts

can be part of a serious package.

But they must be **quantified**, **durable**, and sufficient to restore competitiveness. Temporary pain-sharing without fixed-cost reduction is not enough.

## To the Regional State Government
We understand your concern about avoiding unnecessary industrial damage and protecting the regional base. That is legitimate.

But we do not support a framework that makes closures politically impossible before the economics are tested. The state should insist on hard numbers and verification, not on a preconceived no-closure outcome.

A **short, time-limited moratorium** during the review may be acceptable if it:
- does not delay the turnaround materially,
- does not block credible restructuring options,
- and is paired with real data and deadlines.

What would be unacceptable is a moratorium that becomes a de facto veto on structural adjustment.

## To the Controlling Family Holding
We agree with you that a cosmetic compromise would be value-destructive and that a reckless confrontation would also destroy value.

Where we differ is that “discipline” cannot just mean preserving the freedom to close plants. It must mean choosing the option with the best **risk-adjusted value**. If a closure-free or hybrid package can genuinely close the gap with less disruption, then that may be the better economic outcome.

So: yes to a quantified comparison. Yes to independent verification. No to assuming the answer in advance.

## Our position
We will support a restructuring only if it is:
- **deep enough** to restore competitive margins,
- **durable**, not temporary,
- **quantified** and independently tested,
- and **executable with manageable conflict risk**.

We will **not** support:
- a cosmetic deal,
- a package that leaves structural overcapacity in place,
- or a so-called compromise that merely defers pain while shareholders continue to absorb weak returns.

## What we want next
We support the moderator’s fact-based sequence:
1. management publishes the quantified restructuring case,
2. labour tables a quantified closure-free alternative,
3. an independent review tests whether the alternative delivers equivalent permanent savings.

If the alternative truly matches the economics, we will consider it seriously. If it does not, then management’s case for deeper structural measures becomes stronger.
