I will be equally direct.

I agree with the central premise from management, and I agree with part of what labour and the state have said: this company does need a durable competitiveness reset, not a cosmetic pause. The current cost and capacity structure is not acceptable if we want this brand to remain strong over the long term.

Where I part company with the employee side and, to some extent, the state is on the idea that plant closures and deep structural changes can simply be ruled out in advance.

### To the works council and union
Your insistence on no closures, no compulsory redundancies, and binding guarantees is understandable, but as a governing owner I cannot endorse red lines that may lock in an uncompetitive structure. If the company’s economics do not improve, then the result will not be “protection” of jobs; it will be slower erosion, weaker investment, and ultimately a worse outcome for everyone, including the workforce.

That said, I do **not** support reckless or symbolic closures. If closures or major footprint changes are proposed, they must be justified by a credible, quantified business case and accompanied by a serious plan for redeployment, qualification, and transition. I am not interested in breaking the company’s social fabric for its own sake.

### To management
I support the need for structural change, but I want to be clear: a strategy that simply announces closures and expects the rest of the system to fall into line is too crude. This company’s governance structure does not allow that, and rightly so. If management wants a durable outcome, it must bring forward a plan that is not only hard on costs, but also politically and industrially sustainable.

I also want management to be explicit on one point: what exactly is the structural savings target, what footprint changes are truly necessary, and what would count as an equivalent alternative if closures are avoided? “All options on the table” is not yet a solution.

### To the state
I agree that the company’s industrial base matters and that a default path to closures would be damaging. But I do not agree that the answer is to treat plant preservation as the starting assumption regardless of economics. The special statute and co-determination are meant to force serious governance, not to preserve every historical arrangement forever.

### Our position
From the family holding’s perspective, the non-negotiable point is this: we need a package that restores margins and competitiveness in a durable way, and we need labour peace and execution stability. We will not support anything that permanently weakens the enterprise by leaving excess capacity, structurally high labour costs, or an inability to realign the footprint.

At the same time, we are open to a negotiated solution if it is real and measurable. That means:

- structural savings, not just deferred pain;
- a fair workforce contribution;
- management accountability on investment and product allocation;
- and if plant closures can be avoided through equivalent measures, that is preferable — but only if the numbers genuinely add up.

So my message is simple:  
**I am not prepared to rule out closures in principle, and I am not prepared to accept a purely defensive “no-change” posture.** But I am equally not prepared to endorse a confrontation that destroys value through prolonged conflict. Bring forward a credible, quantified alternative that achieves the required savings and I will look at it seriously. If not, then the pressure for deeper restructuring will remain justified.
