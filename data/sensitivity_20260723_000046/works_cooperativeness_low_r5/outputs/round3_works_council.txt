Management still has not answered the real question: **why must this be solved by threatening German plants and jobs?**

I’ll be very clear.

## To management
You keep saying “durable structural savings,” but you still have not published the hard numbers needed to justify any closure logic.

So here is what I want on the table now:
- the exact savings gap,
- the exact capacity reduction you claim is necessary,
- the timeline to 2030,
- and a plant-by-plant comparison of why alternatives are supposedly insufficient.

Without that, “closures may be needed” is not a business case. It is pressure.

And I reject the idea that a binding job guarantee “locks in excess cost” by definition. A job guarantee is not a vanity demand. It is the minimum condition for trust after management has already broken the historic security pledge. If you want workforce contributions, then you must first prove you are not simply using the crisis to force through irreversible cuts.

## To the controlling family holding and investors
You both keep repeating “equivalent permanent savings” as if the only serious route is to shrink the workforce and footprint.

That is too narrow, and you know it.

A closure-free package can be structurally meaningful if you are willing to accept:
- working-time adjustments,
- internal redeployment across sites,
- product allocation shifts,
- qualification and upskilling,
- productivity measures agreed with labour,
- moderated variable pay,
- and temporary employee contributions tied to hard guarantees.

What you cannot demand is “equivalent savings” on paper while dismissing every tool except closures and forced redundancies. That is not neutral analysis. It is a preferred outcome.

And let me say this plainly: if you force through closures, you will not get “execution certainty.” You will get conflict, strikes, damaged trust, and a company that is weaker socially and industrially.

## To the state government
I appreciate your insistence that closures are not the starting point. But I need more than that.

If the state is serious about protecting the industrial base, then it should demand:
- binding investment in German plants,
- binding future-product allocation,
- binding qualification and internal placement commitments,
- and a formal no-closure commitment for the German footprint.

Not “first resort,” not “default path” — **no closures**.

The workforce cannot be asked to contribute under the shadow of plant shutdowns. That is not a negotiation; it is blackmail with a timetable.

## To the union
I support your rejection of compulsory redundancies. That remains non-negotiable.

But I want to sharpen the position: if there is to be any labour contribution at all, it must be:
- time-limited,
- reversible where possible,
- matched by binding site and job security,
- and protected by co-determination.

No unilateral pay cuts. No dismantling of wage standards. No “temporary” sacrifice that later becomes permanent.

## On the compromise options
The moderator’s “two-track” and “site-specific rescue” ideas are only acceptable if they start from one premise: **no German plant closures and no forced layoffs.**

If Track B is used to smuggle in shutdown decisions later, then it is unacceptable. A “review” that is really a delayed closure plan is not a compromise. It is a tactic.

If there is to be a structural review, then it must be used to:
- secure work for all German plants,
- distribute future production fairly,
- and identify how to use the workforce, not discard it.

## What we are prepared to discuss
We are prepared to discuss a serious package that protects jobs and sites:
- temporary working-time measures,
- bonus moderation,
- internal redeployment,
- qualification,
- product allocation changes,
- productivity measures,
- and a time-limited employee contribution.

But only if it is anchored by:
1. a binding no-closure commitment,
2. no compulsory redundancies,
3. site and job security through 2030,
4. and genuine co-determination in the industrial plan.

## What we are not prepared to accept
Let me restate the red lines, since management still seems to be testing them:

- no German plant closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- no unilateral wage cuts,
- no erosion of co-determination,
- no “structural” deal that treats labour as disposable,
- no blackmail by threat of shutdown.

## Bottom line
If management wants a constructive process, it must stop talking in generalities and put forward a **closure-free** industrial plan with hard numbers, investment commitments, and guaranteed employment security.

If it cannot do that, then I will say it again: there is nothing to agree to. There is only resistance.

And I mean resistance in the boardroom, in bargaining, and on the shop floor.
