I hear two things clearly from both labour sides, and I agree with part of each of them.

First: this company cannot be restructured by simply shifting the burden onto the workforce and then expecting social peace. That would be politically and economically unsound, and it would damage the trust that has held this group together for decades.

Second: management is right that there is a real competitiveness problem and that temporary measures alone will not be enough. We cannot pretend that the market situation, cost gap, and margin pressure will disappear on their own.

Where I differ is on the remedy management is trying to force onto the table.

### On plant closures
I do **not** accept plant closures in Germany as the starting point of this process. That is not a neutral “option among others” in a company like this; it is a decision with long-term consequences for regional economies, industrial capability, and trust in co-determination.

If management wants to argue that a particular footprint adjustment is unavoidable, then it must first prove that all alternatives have been exhausted and that no comparable restructuring can be achieved through capacity reductions, working-time measures, internal redeployment, qualification, product allocation, and negotiated contributions. That burden of proof is on management, not on the workforce.

### On job cuts and pay cuts
I am also not prepared to support a restructuring that treats large-scale job destruction as a default lever. A durable solution must protect as much employment as possible and avoid forced redundancies wherever feasible. The state cannot endorse an outcome that guts regional employment in order to fix a short-term margin problem.

At the same time, I do not dismiss the need for a contribution from labour. But it has to be:
- negotiated,
- time-limited where possible,
- tied to concrete investment and future-product commitments,
- and part of a package that preserves sites and jobs.

That is very different from permanent unilateral cuts.

### On guarantees
The demand for binding site and employment security is not unreasonable. In fact, without some form of credible guarantee, no workforce will accept sacrifices. If you want flexibility from employees, you need reliability from management and owners.

What I would consider constructive is a package with:
- binding commitments to key German sites,
- protection against forced redundancies,
- clear investment commitments for future technologies in Germany,
- and a restructuring path that uses internal adjustment before external closure.

### On governance and co-determination
Let me be explicit: the state will not accept being sidelined, and it will not accept a process that seeks to bypass the company’s governance architecture to impose irreversible decisions through pressure alone. The special statute and board structure exist for a reason. They are there precisely to prevent reckless decisions that damage the regional base of this company.

### On the way forward
I am open to a tough restructuring. I am **not** open to a restructuring that destroys the company’s social and regional foundations.

So my position is this:
- No German plant closures as a first resort.
- No forced layoffs.
- No unilateral dismantling of protections.
- Yes to a negotiated, credible competitiveness package.
- Yes to a measurable workforce contribution if it is matched by management and ownership.
- Yes to a solution that protects sites, secures future investment, and preserves the company as a regional anchor.

If management can bring forward a serious alternative that achieves structural savings **without** breaking those conditions, then we have a basis for further discussion. If not, I will not support it.
