We are at the point where the real question is no longer whether the company needs restructuring. It does. The question is whether management, owners, and investors are prepared to accept a **fair, quantified, shared-sacrifice solution** instead of trying to force the workforce to absorb the whole adjustment.

## To the works council
I agree with you on the core red lines:

- no plant closures as a starting point,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- no dismantling of the collective job-security framework.

You are right that “structural necessity” is not a magic word that makes those outcomes acceptable. If management wants to claim that closures are unavoidable, then it has to prove that with numbers, not with pressure.

Where I want to sharpen your position is this: we should be careful not to let the debate get trapped in slogans on either side. The expert was clear that the issue is **quantified savings and durability**. So yes, we should insist that management discloses the real gap, and yes, we should insist that all non-closure tools are exhausted first. That is the correct burden of proof.

## To management
You still have not shown why the company must start from closure and redundancy threats instead of from a serious non-closure package.

If you say the gap is structural, then show it:

- What is the annual cost gap?
- What savings come from attrition, retirement, internal redeployment, working-time measures, and labor-cost contributions?
- How much fixed cost do you actually remove without closures?
- What is the time profile?
- Why is that insufficient?

Until those numbers are on the table, your closure and forced-layoff language remains a threat, not a plan.

And let me be explicit: I do **not** accept a restructuring logic in which workers are told to give up wages, protections, and guarantees while management retains all strategic freedom and owners keep full control. That is one-sided, and it is exactly what will provoke escalation.

## To the family owners
If you truly care about long-term value, then you should stop treating closures as a convenient bargaining lever.

Long-term stewardship means:
- preserving trust,
- protecting industrial capability,
- and avoiding a rupture with the workforce that would poison the company for years.

I can support a serious contribution from labor if it is matched by real commitments from management and owners. But if the owners back a course that effectively says “accept cuts or we close sites,” then they are undermining the very industrial base they claim to protect.

## To the regional state government
Your role matters, and it should not be reduced to political commentary.

If the state accepts a permanent weakening of co-determination, job security, and regional employment as the price of “competitiveness,” then it will have given up its responsibility too easily. I support your insistence that alternatives must be exhausted first.

But I also want to be clear: I do not want a symbolic veto politics game. I want the state to back a **real alternative package** that protects sites, jobs, and future investment. That means standing firm against plant closures and forced redundancies unless and until it is proven that no other route can deliver the needed savings.

## To investors
You are right about one thing: a cosmetic compromise would be worthless.

But here is the problem with your current line: you keep implying that if labor does not accept closures and redundancies up front, then the company is not being serious. That is not analysis; that is a preference for maximum management leverage.

The expert did **not** say closures are inevitable. The expert said a non-closure solution is possible in principle and could be credible if the gap is not excessive and if the measures are durable and quantified. That means you do not get to assume the harshest tool is necessary before the numbers have been tested.

If you want credibility, then demand transparency and verification. Do not demand surrender first.

## To management, again, on the so-called conditional pact
I reject any “conditional no-closure pact” that is really just a delayed closure plan with better branding.

A genuine settlement would require:

- a binding no-closure commitment,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- a fair and time-limited employee contribution,
- capacity reduction through non-closure measures,
- and binding investment and product allocation for German sites.

If your idea of “conditional” means, “we get labor concessions now and then reopen the closure threat later,” then that is not a settlement. It is a trap.

## Our union position, plainly
We remain prepared to negotiate a **shared-sacrifice package**, but only if the burden is genuinely shared.

That can include:
- temporary wage restraint or bonus moderation,
- working-time flexibility,
- shift and capacity adjustments,
- natural attrition and retirement pathways,
- redeployment and qualification,
- and a limited employee financial contribution.

But only if these are matched by:
- **no closures**,
- **no compulsory redundancies**,
- **binding job and site security**,
- **real future industrial commitments**,
- and contributions from management and owners as well.

## Where we stand after the expert’s intervention
The expert confirmed what we have been saying all along: this is a **numbers question**, not a slogan question.

Good. Then let management show the numbers. Let them quantify:
1. the real structural gap,
2. the savings from each non-closure measure,
3. the durability of each measure,
4. and why they say that still falls short.

Until then, there is no justification for threatening German plant closures or forced redundancies.

## Final point
We will not retreat from our red lines because others repeat them often enough.

- **No plant closures**
- **No compulsory redundancies**
- **No unilateral destruction of the collective wage and job-security framework**

If management wants a durable settlement, it has a path:
shared sacrifice, binding future work, and a real industrial plan.

If it refuses that path and insists on coercion, then it should expect resistance, not consent.
