We are listening, and our position remains clear:

**We support a restructuring only if it credibly restores competitive margins and fixes the structural cost problem.**  
We do **not** support a settlement that leaves the German cost base uncompetitive, even if that settlement is politically easier or socially more palatable.

## Where we agree

### With management
You are right on the core issue: this cannot be cosmetic.  
If the company’s margins are below target and the cost base is structurally too high, then **durable** action is required. We also agree that the burden must be quantified, not argued in slogans.

### With the expert
The finance framing is the right one. The question is not ideology; it is whether the **non-closure package is large enough, durable enough, and enforceable enough** to close the gap. That is the test we care about.

### With the family
We agree that a prolonged conflict would destroy value. We also agree that the company needs a durable industrial solution, not a temporary truce that leaves the economics broken.

## Where we disagree

### With the works council and union
Your red lines are understandable, but they are **not finance-credible as absolutes** unless you can show equivalent savings.

- **No closures**
- **No compulsory redundancies**
- **Job guarantees to 2030**

Those may be good bargaining positions, but they are not enough on their own to convince capital markets that the company will return to competitive returns on sales.

If the alternative package truly closes the gap, fine. But we have not yet seen proof that it does. Without that proof, an upfront categorical exclusion of closures and forced redundancies simply shifts the burden away from the structural problem and onto future margins.

### With the state
We respect the regional interest and the blocking role, but the state cannot use that power to preserve an uncompetitive structure indefinitely. Protecting the region means protecting a viable company, not freezing a footprint that remains too expensive.

## Our financial test
For us to support a no-closure or hybrid solution, it must demonstrate:

1. **Annual recurring savings** large enough to close the structural gap  
2. **Durability** over multiple years  
3. **Real fixed-cost reduction**, not just deferral  
4. **Implementation certainty** with measurable milestones  
5. **A credible path to margin recovery and return-on-sales improvement**

That is the standard. Anything less is a compromise that looks good today and fails tomorrow.

## On the current debate
We do **not** fetishize closures. We do not want disruption for its own sake. But we also do not accept a deal that preserves every site and still leaves the company structurally impaired.

If a non-closure package can genuinely and verifiably close the gap, then it is supportable.  
If it cannot, then management must retain the tools needed to restore competitiveness.

## What we would need next
We need a **numbers-based bridge**, not another round of principle statements.

Specifically:
- What is the **annual structural gap**?
- What savings come from attrition, retirement, redeployment, working-time changes, and labor-cost moderation?
- What fixed cost is actually removed?
- What remains uncovered?
- What return-on-sales improvement follows?

If those figures show that a non-closure package truly restores competitiveness, we can support it. If not, then it is not enough.

## Our bottom line
- **Support a deal that credibly restores margins.**
- **Reject a deal that merely postpones the problem.**
- **Reject any outcome that leaves the cost base uncompetitive.**

So we are open to a negotiated compromise, but only if it is financially equivalent to the restructuring the business actually needs.
