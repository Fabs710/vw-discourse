I will be equally direct.

## My position remains firm
As the state shareholder, I will not support a restructuring that:
- **guts employment in this state,**
- **normalizes plant closures as a first resort,**
- or **sidelines our co-determination and blocking role**.

I do accept that the company needs durable restructuring. I do accept that the current cost base cannot simply continue. But I do **not** accept the leap from “there is a gap” to “therefore closures and forced redundancies must remain available from day one.”

That is not prudence. That is a pressure strategy.

---

## Where I agree

### With management
You are right on one point: this cannot be cosmetic. A deal that merely delays the problem would be irresponsible.

I also agree that the discussion must be quantified. The company needs:
- an annual structural gap,
- a savings bridge,
- and a durable industrial plan.

### With the expert
The expert’s core benchmark is useful:
- non-closure measures can work for **moderate gaps**,
- they become harder if the gap is very large and persistent,
- partial consolidation is often the best midpoint.

That is the correct analytical frame.

### With labor on one point
You are right that **management must prove necessity**, not simply assert it.  
If the company wants major sacrifices, it must show the numbers transparently.

---

## Where I disagree

### 1) I do not accept closures as a default bargaining tool
Management, family, and investors keep saying closures must stay available “just in case.” From the state’s perspective, that is the wrong starting point.

A plant closure in this state is not a line item. It means:
- permanent job losses,
- damage to supplier networks,
- loss of skills,
- regional economic harm,
- and a serious blow to trust in the company.

So I will not support a process that keeps closures openly on the table as an early bargaining lever.

### 2) I do not accept compulsory redundancies as a starting assumption
Forced exits cannot be the first answer. They may be discussed only after voluntary and negotiated tools have truly been exhausted.

### 3) I do not accept any outcome that weakens the state’s veto role
Our special statute is not ornamental. It exists to protect the regional and public interest. I will not concede that role in the name of “flexibility.”

---

## To management
You say the gap is structural and the tools must remain available. Fine — but you still have not shown the actual bridge.

So I ask again:
1. What is the annual structural gap?
2. What savings come from attrition, retirement, redeployment, working-time changes, and labor contributions?
3. What fixed cost is truly removed by partial consolidation?
4. What remains uncovered?
5. Why exactly is that remainder impossible to close without closures?

Without that, your insistence on keeping closures available is not analysis. It is leverage.

And I do **not** accept the framing that worker protections are merely obstacles to competitiveness. Long-term competitiveness also depends on:
- labor peace,
- skill retention,
- stable governance,
- and social legitimacy.

---

## To the works council and union
I support your insistence that workers should not pay the entire bill. That is correct.

But I also want to be clear: a pure “nothing changes” position would not be responsible if it leaves the company structurally uncompetitive. We do need real restructuring.

So I can support:
- attrition,
- retirement pathways,
- redeployment,
- qualification,
- working-time flexibility,
- limited and time-bound employee contributions,
- and capacity consolidation,

**if and only if** they are matched by:
- binding investment commitments,
- product allocation to German sites,
- and real protections for jobs and sites.

What I cannot support is a “conditional” process that is really just a delayed closure strategy.

---

## To the family owners
You say you care about stewardship and enterprise value. I believe that can be sincere. But stewardship is not served by using closures as a bargaining club.

Long-term value is damaged by:
- destroying trust,
- provoking prolonged conflict,
- undermining co-determination,
- and weakening the company’s industrial base in Germany.

If you want a durable company, then back a solution that preserves sites and strengthens the regional anchor role, not one that threatens that role away.

---

## To the investors
You are right that a weak compromise would be value-destructive. I agree.

But I reject the implication that financial credibility automatically requires keeping closures and forced redundancies on the table from the outset. The expert did **not** say that. The expert said a non-closure solution may work if the gap is moderate and the measures are large and durable enough.

So the burden is still on management to show that a non-closure or hybrid package cannot work. Until then, it is premature to insist on keeping closures available as if they were already justified.

---

## What I would support
I am prepared to support a **genuine two-stage process** only if it is not a trap:

### Stage 1
A binding package with:
- attrition and retirement pathways,
- redeployment and retraining,
- working-time changes,
- a fair but time-limited labor contribution,
- partial consolidation where it truly reduces fixed cost,
- and binding product/investment commitments for German sites.

### Stage 2
Only if an **independent review** proves that a significant structural gap still remains after Stage 1, the parties can discuss further steps.

That is a last-resort framework.  
What I will not support is a “two-stage” label that simply gives management concessions now and closure rights later.

---

## What I will not accept
Let me state the limits clearly:

- **No plant closures as a default option**
- **No compulsory redundancies as a starting point**
- **No weakening of co-determination**
- **No outcome that guts jobs in this state**
- **No restructuring that treats the region as disposable**

If the company wants my support, it must show that it can restore competitiveness **without** dismantling its regional base.

---

## What I want next
I support the call for a **site-by-site restructuring model**.  
[SUMMON: Independent site-by-site restructuring model to quantify savings from attrition, working-time changes, partial consolidation, and full closures, including fixed-cost removal and implementation risk.]

That model must answer, in hard numbers:
- what each plant contributes,
- what each non-closure measure saves,
- what remains uncovered,
- and whether the gap can be closed without plant closures.

Until then, I will continue to treat closure pressure as unjustified.

## Bottom line
We are open to a serious restructuring. We are **not** open to a restructuring that sacrifices German sites and jobs first and asks for political approval later.

If the numbers show that a non-closure or hybrid solution can work, I will support it.  
If they do not, then management must still prove that any harder step is truly unavoidable — and even then, my red lines on regional employment and governance are very high.

That is where I stand.
