From a restructuring-finance standpoint, the key issue now is not rhetoric but **whether the non-closure package can mathematically and operationally close the structural gap**.

## My answer in one line
A **credible no-closure deal is possible only if the annual recurring savings requirement is moderate**; if the gap is large and persistent, then **capacity rationalization and possibly site closures remain financially necessary as fallback tools**.

## Benchmark view: what non-closure tools can usually deliver
Using German automotive restructuring benchmarks, a package built from:

- natural attrition / retirements,
- internal redeployment,
- working-time reduction,
- wage moderation / bonus restraint,
- capacity consolidation without full closure,

typically yields:

- **Attrition / non-replacement:** slow but durable; often **1–3% headcount per year** in a managed freeze environment.
- **Working-time changes:** often worth **low single-digit % of direct labor cost**; helpful, but not enough if fixed plant overhead is the main issue.
- **Wage moderation / bonus restraint:** often **1–5% of relevant payroll cost annually** if time-bound and negotiated.
- **Partial capacity consolidation:** can be meaningful if it truly removes fixed cost, but if the footprint stays intact, savings are much smaller than management hopes.

## Financial test
A non-closure package is usually finance-credible if the gap is roughly:

- **low to mid three-digit millions annually** for a large German operation, or
- a **moderate share of domestic fixed costs**.

If the required recurring correction is more like:

- **high three-digit millions to low billions annually**, and
- the company has **persistent overcapacity across multiple plants**,

then non-closure measures alone are often **not enough**, unless labor accepts unusually deep and multi-year concessions.

## What matters most
The deciding factors are:

1. **Annual recurring savings**, not just one-off relief  
2. **Durability** over several years  
3. **Ability to remove fixed cost**, not just defer it  
4. **Execution certainty**  
5. **Whether the package actually restores return on sales**

## Practical conclusion
- If management’s gap is **moderate**, a **no-closure solution can work**.
- If the gap is **large and structural**, then **site rationalization must remain available** because otherwise the company risks preserving excess fixed cost and losing margin restoration.
- The best compromise is often **capacity consolidation without full closure**, but only if it truly simplifies the footprint and lowers fixed overhead.

## What I would ask management for next
To judge this properly, I would need:

- domestic headcount by site,
- direct vs indirect labor split,
- fixed site cost by plant,
- target margin bridge,
- volume outlook,
- and the savings assumed from each measure.

Without that, nobody can responsibly say that closures are definitely required — or definitely avoidable.
