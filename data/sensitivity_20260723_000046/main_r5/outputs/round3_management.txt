Let me respond directly, because we are now in danger of talking past the actual economic problem.

## First: what I agree with
I agree with one important point made by the works council, the union, the state, the family, and the investors: **this cannot be a cosmetic deal**.  
If the package does not reduce costs and capacity **durably**, it is useless.

I also agree that **numbers matter**. We need a quantified bridge from today’s cost base to a competitive one.

## Where I disagree — firmly
### 1) “No closures” cannot be a blanket red line
That is not a restructuring strategy. It is a demand to preserve every structure while the company absorbs a structural cost gap.

If a plant is uneconomic on a lasting basis, then insisting it remain open by political declaration is not protecting the company or the workforce. It is deferring the damage and weakening the whole group.

### 2) “No compulsory redundancies” cannot be absolute
If overcapacity is real and the cost base must be reduced structurally, then headcount reduction cannot be ruled out in advance. We should minimize forced exits, yes. But I will not pretend they can be categorically excluded if the savings required are substantial.

### 3) A job guarantee to 2030 is not acceptable as a starting point
Not if it freezes an uncompetitive structure. Security has to be earned through a package that actually restores margins and competitiveness. Otherwise it becomes a guarantee of decline.

## On the expert’s assessment
The expert did **not** say closures are unnecessary. The expert said a non-closure package is only credible **if the gap is moderate or if the alternative measures are large, durable, and enforceable**.

That is exactly the issue.

So here is the question to the employee side:  
**What is your quantified alternative?**

Not principles. Not slogans. Numbers.

- How much cost do attrition and retirement actually remove?
- How much does working-time flexibility save?
- What is the annualized value of labor-cost moderation?
- How much fixed cost do you eliminate through capacity consolidation without closure?
- How quickly does it hit the P&L?

If you cannot close the gap with hard numbers, then your red lines are not a solution. They are a refusal to engage with reality.

## To the works council
You say management must prove necessity before closures are even discussed. But you have not shown a package that restores competitiveness at the scale required.

I am prepared to discuss:
- natural attrition,
- retirement pathways,
- redeployment,
- qualification,
- working-time changes,
- limited, time-bound labor contributions,
- and capacity consolidation.

But I will not accept a framework that rules out the tools we may need before we have the numbers.

### What I need from you
If you want to avoid closures and forced redundancies, then bring a package that is:
1. **quantified**
2. **durable**
3. **binding**
4. **large enough**

If you cannot, then you are not offering an alternative. You are asking management to carry a structural burden with no structural remedy.

## To the union
You speak about “shared sacrifice.” Fine. Then make it real.

A time-limited wage moderation or bonus moderation may be part of the answer, but only if:
- the savings are significant,
- the measures are enforceable,
- and they are tied to real capacity reduction and productivity improvement.

What I will not accept is a package where employees give up something small and temporary while the company remains structurally uncompetitive.

## To the regional state government
I respect your role, but your blocking rights do not give you a veto over economic arithmetic.

If you insist on preserving every site regardless of economics, then you are not protecting the region — you are preserving a weak industrial base that will need another crisis later.

A responsible state position is:
- exhaust alternatives first, yes,
- but do not rule out closures if they prove necessary,
- and do not block a durable restructuring just because it is politically uncomfortable.

## To the family owners
You are correct that long-term stewardship matters. That is precisely why I insist on structural change. If the cost base remains out of line, the company will underinvest, underperform, and lose competitiveness anyway.

But I also say this to you: closure pressure alone is not a strategy. I want a **credible, quantified plan** that shows exactly what each measure delivers and why.

If the non-closure package is sufficient, I am open to it.  
If it is not sufficient, then we cannot pretend otherwise.

## To the investors
You are right to insist on credibility. I support that. But credibility means accepting that some measures must be **structural**, not just symbolic.

If the package leaves the fixed cost base too high, then the return on sales target will not be reached. And if the target is not reached, future investment is at risk.

## My position
Let me be crystal clear:

- I do **not** accept a blanket ban on closures.
- I do **not** accept a blanket ban on compulsory redundancies.
- I do **not** accept a deal that leaves the German cost base uncompetitive.
- I do **not** accept a cosmetic compromise dressed up as industrial peace.

What I do accept is a **serious negotiated package** that delivers:
- durable cost reduction,
- real capacity reduction,
- a material labor-cost contribution,
- and a binding future investment framework.

## What I want next
If the works council and union want to argue that closures are avoidable, then put the numbers on the table. Show me how a non-closure package closes the gap.

Until then, closures and compulsory redundancies must remain available. Not because we prefer confrontation, but because we cannot restore competitiveness by wishful thinking.
