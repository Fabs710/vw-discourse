We are listening closely, and our position is unchanged in substance:

**We support a restructuring only if it produces a credible, durable return to competitive margins.**  
We do **not** support a settlement that preserves an uncompetitive cost base, delays the adjustment, or converts structural problems into recurring conflict.

## Where we agree

### With management
You are right that this cannot be a cosmetic compromise. The company needs **material, permanent savings** and **capacity alignment**. Temporary optics are not enough, and a weak deal would destroy shareholder value.

### With the family
You are right that long-term value matters and that an open-ended strike or prolonged instability would be value-destructive. We share the view that the company needs a durable industrial solution, not a political gesture.

### With the expert
The finance framing is correct: the real question is whether the **non-closure package is large enough, durable enough, and enforceable enough** to close the structural gap. That is the right test.

## Where we disagree

### With the works council and union
Your position is still too absolute.

- **“No closures”**
- **“No compulsory redundancies”**
- **“Job guarantees to 2030”**

These are not acceptable as starting red lines if you cannot show equivalent savings. They may be politically understandable, but they are not financially sufficient on their own.

If the company’s structural gap is meaningful — and the evidence suggests it is — then simply shifting the burden to working-time changes, attrition, and temporary concessions is unlikely to restore margins at the required scale. That is not turnaround; that is postponement.

### With the state
We respect the regional responsibility, but the state cannot use its blocking power to freeze an uncompetitive footprint indefinitely. Protecting the region means protecting a viable company, not preserving every site regardless of economics.

## Our investor test

We are not asking for maximum pain on labor. We are asking for a package that **actually fixes the economics**.

For us to support a non-closure solution, it must demonstrate:

1. **Annual recurring savings** large enough to close the gap  
2. **Durability** over multiple years  
3. **Real fixed-cost reduction**, not just delay  
4. **Implementation certainty** with measurable milestones  
5. **A credible path to margin recovery and return-on-sales improvement**

If the package does not meet those criteria, it is not credible.

## On the current debate

What concerns us is that several parties are treating the preferred outcome as if it were automatically finance-feasible. It is not.

### If the non-closure package can close the gap:
We would support it.

### If it cannot:
Then management must retain the ability to use the necessary structural tools, including site rationalization, to restore competitiveness.

We do not fetishize closures. But we will not back a deal that leaves the company structurally impaired and then calls that social peace.

## What we want next

We need a **numbers-based bridge**, not another round of general statements.

Specifically:

- What is the **annual structural gap**?
- What savings come from each non-closure measure?
- What is **permanent** versus **temporary**?
- What capacity is actually removed?
- What is the timing of cash and P&L impact?
- What return-on-sales improvement results?

If those figures show that a non-closure package truly restores competitiveness, fine. If not, then the hard measures must remain available.

## Bottom line

Our threshold is simple:

- **Support a deal that credibly restores margins.**
- **Reject a deal that merely postpones the problem.**
- **Reject any outcome that leaves the cost base uncompetitive.**

So we are open to a negotiated compromise, but only if it is financially equivalent to the structural correction the business needs. Otherwise, the market will do what governance will not: it will punish the equity.
