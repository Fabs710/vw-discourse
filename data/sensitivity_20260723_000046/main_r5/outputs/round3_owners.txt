I will be direct.

## Where I agree
I agree with the central diagnosis from management, investors, and the expert:
- the cost base is too high,
- capacity is misaligned,
- and a cosmetic settlement would destroy value.

I also agree with the state and the labor side on one important point:
- a prolonged conflict would be damaging,
- and any solution must be durable, not symbolic.

## Where I disagree
### 1) “No closures” cannot be a blanket red line
I do **not** accept a categorical exclusion of plant closures if the economics do not work otherwise.

If a site is structurally uneconomic and no alternative closes the gap, then refusing closure in advance is not stewardship. It is preserving losses and passing the bill to the company and its shareholders.

### 2) “No compulsory redundancies” cannot be absolute
I do **not** accept an absolute ban on forced reductions if the overcapacity problem is real and large.

We should minimize compulsory exits. We should exhaust voluntary instruments first. But I will not accept a position that rules them out regardless of scale or timing while still expecting a structural turnaround.

### 3) The state cannot use its blocking power to freeze the structure
I respect the state’s role, but I do **not** accept the idea that it can prevent economically necessary correction indefinitely.

Regional responsibility matters. So does the company’s competitiveness. If the state blocks every hard decision, the company will weaken, and that will ultimately hurt the region as well.

## On the works council and union
You are right that worker concessions cannot be the only lever. I agree with:
- attrition,
- retirement pathways,
- internal redeployment,
- qualification,
- working-time flexibility,
- and time-limited labor contributions.

But I do **not** accept your red lines as starting assumptions:
- no closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- job guarantees to 2030 regardless of economics.

That is not a restructuring framework. It is an attempt to preserve the current structure and call it reform.

## On management
I agree with management on the need for structural change, but not on using closure pressure as a substitute for a quantified plan.

Management must now show:
1. the real annual structural gap,
2. the savings from each measure,
3. the time profile,
4. and the operational impact.

Without that, closure threats are leverage, not strategy.

## On the expert’s assessment
The expert was helpful. The key takeaway is simple:
- a non-closure package can work if the gap is moderate,
- but if the gap is severe, it is unlikely to be enough.

That is exactly why I will not support a blanket no-closure commitment. The numbers must decide, not slogans.

## My position
The family holding’s position remains:

1. **We support a real restructuring, not a cosmetic compromise.**
2. **We do not support blanket prohibitions on closures or compulsory redundancies.**
3. **We prefer negotiated, reversible, and socially stable measures first.**
4. **We require a quantified, durable bridge to restored competitiveness.**
5. **We will not accept any outcome that permanently impairs enterprise value or governance control.**

If the non-closure package truly closes the gap, fine — prove it.  
If it does not, then the company must retain the tools needed to act.

## What I want next
I want management to table a **numbers-based restructuring bridge**:
- what must be saved,
- what each measure saves,
- what is temporary and what is permanent,
- and what gap remains.

If labor wants to avoid harsher measures, it must present an alternative that is **material, durable, and quantified**. If it cannot, then I will not back a deal that protects every current structure while undermining the company’s future.
