From a restructuring-finance and industrial-turnaround perspective, the key question is not whether the company **wants** to avoid plant closures and forced redundancies, but whether a **non-closure package can credibly deliver the same durable savings** at the scale needed to restore margin.

## Bottom line

My assessment is:

- **A non-closure solution is possible in principle only if the cost gap is moderate.**
- If the company’s German operations are structurally overcosted by a **high three-digit million or low single-digit billion euro amount per year**, then **attrition + working-time measures + wage concessions alone are usually not enough**.
- To replace closures, the package would need to be **large, binding, and durable**, likely combining:
  - headcount reduction through attrition and retirements,
  - working-time and shift-system changes,
  - labor-cost concessions,
  - consolidation of production across fewer lines/units,
  - and possibly substantial non-labor overhead reduction.
- If the required annual savings are in the **€1.5bn–€2.5bn+ range**, then **at least some plant/site rationalization or equivalent permanent capacity removal is usually needed** to make the economics believable.

So: **if management’s gap is truly structural and large, the union/works council’s “no closures, no compulsory redundancies” position is not obviously finance-feasible unless they can quantify a substitute package of similar magnitude.**

## How I would frame the economics

In a large automotive manufacturer, the annual savings from different measures typically look roughly like this:

### 1) Natural attrition / retirements
- Useful, politically easier, but slow.
- Typical annualized benefit: **low tens of millions to perhaps low hundreds of millions** over time, depending on workforce age profile and hiring freeze.
- Good for smoothing workforce reduction, **not sufficient alone** for major structural turnaround.

### 2) Working-time reduction / shift changes / temporary wage moderation
- Can reduce variable labor cost and some overhead absorption issues.
- Typical annualized impact: **mid tens of millions to low hundreds of millions**, depending on breadth and duration.
- Strongest when production volumes are falling and utilization is weak.
- But these are often **partly reversible** and do not fully fix structural overcapacity.

### 3) Permanent labor-cost concessions
Examples:
- lower base pay or supplements,
- removal of guarantees,
- bonus moderation,
- longer working time for same pay,
- redesign of allowances and premium systems.

Potential annualized value: **high tens of millions to several hundred million euros**, depending on workforce size and concession depth.

This can be material, but usually **still does not fully replace a site closure if one site is chronically uneconomic**.

### 4) Capacity reduction without formal closure
This can be done by:
- consolidating models,
- removing lines,
- cutting shifts,
- changing product mix,
- relocating volumes to better-utilized plants,
- subleasing, mothballing, or partial conversion.

This is often the **best non-closure substitute** if politically achievable, because it removes fixed cost and improves utilization without necessarily announcing a full closure.

Savings can be substantial:
- **hundreds of millions annually** if a meaningful amount of fixed cost, logistics, indirect labor, and capex is removed.
- But the savings only materialize if capacity is genuinely eliminated, not merely “paused.”

### 5) Full plant closure
This usually produces the largest and most durable savings because it removes:
- fixed labor,
- maintenance,
- site overhead,
- utilities,
- logistics complexity,
- and future capex commitments.

For a major auto plant, annualized benefit can plausibly be **€150m–€400m+ per site** depending on scale, product complexity, and what production is truly removed versus relocated.

That is why management keeps it on the table: from a finance standpoint, closure is often the cleanest way to remove a persistent cost disadvantage.

## What makes a non-closure package credible?

For the union/works council alternative to be finance-credible, it must satisfy all four of these tests:

### 1) It must be large enough
If management needs, say, **€1.0bn–€2.0bn** in durable annual improvement, then a package that only delivers **€200m–€400m** is not a substitute.

### 2) It must be durable
Temporary wage moderation or one-off cuts do not restore margin for long. Markets, EV transition costs, and competitive pressure will reassert themselves.

### 3) It must reduce fixed cost, not just postpone pain
Working-time reductions help only if they are paired with real volume/capacity removal. Otherwise the company still carries the same industrial footprint.

### 4) It must be operationally enforceable
A paper agreement with broad promises but no hard mechanisms is not enough. The company needs:
- headcount targets,
- site-by-site capacity targets,
- product allocation commitments,
- timing,
- and trigger-based execution.

## My scenario assessment

### Scenario A: Moderate gap, roughly €300m–€700m/year
In this case, a non-closure package is **quite plausible**.

A combination of:
- attrition,
- retirement,
- working-time flexibility,
- limited wage/bonus moderation,
- internal transfers,
- procurement and overhead cuts,

could realistically cover most or all of the gap.

**Result:** closures may not be necessary.

### Scenario B: Material structural gap, roughly €700m–€1.5bn/year
This is the gray zone.

A non-closure deal **might** work only if:
- labor gives a meaningful permanent contribution,
- capacity is cut significantly,
- at least some plants or lines are reconfigured sharply,
- and the company gets strong product/investment commitments to raise utilization.

**Result:** possible, but only with a very tough agreement and major operational redesign.

### Scenario C: Severe gap, above roughly €1.5bn/year
Here, I think a no-closure/no-forced-redundancy package becomes **hard to believe** unless the company is hiding very large non-labor savings or major product upside.

At that scale, the economics usually point to:
- at least one site closure or
- equivalent permanent removal of capacity and cost
- plus workforce reduction beyond what attrition alone can deliver.

**Result:** the non-closure position becomes financially weak unless replaced by a very large and enforceable alternative.

## Important implementation risks

Even if a negotiated package is signed, these are the main risks:

- **Volume risk:** EV demand or European demand stays weak, so the company still lacks absorption.
- **Execution risk:** savings are delayed by works-council complexity, legal process, or operational disruption.
- **Substitution risk:** savings from one measure get offset by inflation, supplier costs, or new capex.
- **Time lag:** many labor measures save cash slowly, while the company may need margin relief now.
- **Credibility risk:** investors discount savings that are not fully structural or independently measurable.
- **Industrial relations risk:** a weak compromise can produce recurring conflict, which destroys productivity and output.

## My judgment on the positions in the room

### Works council / union
Their position is understandable politically, but from a turnaround-finance standpoint it is only credible if they can propose **quantified equivalent savings**. “No closures” is not enough by itself.

### Management
Management is correct that **structural cost removal** is needed if the gap is large. But closures should be treated as the **last-resort economic tool**, not merely a bargaining threat. They need to show the numbers.

### State / family
Both are right to demand a durable solution. The family is also right that mere closure pressure is not a strategy. The state is right that closures have high regional cost. But neither side can escape the underlying arithmetic.
