[Works Council (Betriebsrat) (Plant-level employee representation under co-determination) — Round 1]
As Works Council, I will be very clear from the outset:

We will **not accept plant closures in Germany** and we will **not accept compulsory redundancies**. That is our red line.

Management cannot ask the workforce to pay for strategic failures by destroying jobs, breaking the historic job-security framework, and turning German sites into sacrificial assets. The company’s future must be secured **with the sites, with the people, and with co-determination intact**.

What we are prepared to discuss is a **credible restructuring without closures and without forced layoffs**:
- binding **site guarantees** for all German plants,
- a **job guarantee through 2030**,
- use of **natural attrition, retirement, internal transfers, qualification, and working-time adjustments**,
- negotiated contributions only if they are **fairly shared** and tied to a real long-term industrial plan,
- and a **genuine role for employee representatives** in shaping the transformation.

If management wants a durable solution, it must come with:
1. **No closures**
2. **No compulsory redundancies**
3. **Respect for co-determination**
4. **A binding commitment to investment, product allocation, and future work for German sites**

Anything else is not a restructuring plan — it is an attack on the workforce and on the industrial base of the company. We are ready to fight that with all the means available to us.

## How I would frame the economics

In a large automotive manufacturer, the annual savings from different measures typically look roughly like this:

### 1) Natural attrition / retirements
- Useful, politically easier, but slow.
- Typical annualized benefit: **low tens of millions to perhaps low hundreds of millions** over time, depending on workforce age profile and hiring freeze.
- Good for smoothing workforce reduction, **not sufficient alone** for major structural turnaround.

### 2) Working-time reduction / shift changes / temporary wage moderation
- Can reduce variable labor cost and some overhead absorption issues.
- Typical annualized impact: **mid tens of millions to low hundreds of millions**, depending on breadth and duration.
- Strongest when production volumes are falling and utilization is weak.
- But these are often **partly reversible** and do not fully fix structural overcapacity.

### 3) Permanent labor-cost concessions
Examples:
- lower base pay or supplements,
- removal of guarantees,
- bonus moderation,
- longer working time for same pay,
- redesign of allowances and premium systems.

Potential annualized value: **high tens of millions to several hundred million euros**, depending on workforce size and concession depth.

This can be material, but usually **still does not fully replace a site closure if one site is chronically uneconomic**.

### 4) Capacity reduction without formal closure
This can be done by:
- consolidating models,
- removing lines,
- cutting shifts,
- changing product mix,
- relocating volumes to better-utilized plants,
- subleasing, mothballing, or partial conversion.

This is often the **best non-closure substitute** if politically achievable, because it removes fixed cost and improves utilization without necessarily announcing a full closure.

Savings can be substantial:
- **hundreds of millions annually** if a meaningful amount of fixed cost, logistics, indirect labor, and capex is removed.
- But the savings only materialize if capacity is genuinely eliminated, not merely “paused.”

### 5) Full plant closure
This usually produces the largest and most durable savings because it removes:
- fixed labor,
- maintenance,
- site overhead,
- utilities,
- logistics complexity,
- and future capex commitments.

For a major auto plant, annualized benefit can plausibly be **€150m–€400m+ per site** depending on scale, product complexity, and what production is truly removed versus relocated.

That is why management keeps it on the table: from a finance standpoint, closure is often the cleanest way to remove a persistent cost disadvantage.

## What makes a non-closure package credible?

For the union/works council alternative to be finance-credible, it must satisfy all four of these tests:

### 1) It must be large enough
If management needs, say, **€1.0bn–€2.0bn** in durable annual improvement, then a package that only delivers **€200m–€400m** is not a substitute.

### 2) It must be durable
Temporary wage moderation or one-off cuts do not restore margin for long. Markets, EV transition costs, and competitive pressure will reassert themselves.

### 3) It must reduce fixed cost, not just postpone pain
Working-time reductions help only if they are paired with real volume/capacity removal. Otherwise the company still carries the same industrial footprint.

### 4) It must be operationally enforceable
A paper agreement with broad promises but no hard mechanisms is not enough. The company needs:
- headcount targets,
- site-by-site capacity targets,
- product allocation commitments,
- timing,
- and trigger-based execution.

[Expert: Independent restructuring finance adviso — Round 3]
Here is the finance and benchmarking assessment, focused on the specific question: **can a non-closure package realistically close the cost gap, or are plant closures / forced redundancies financially necessary?**

## Bottom line

On a restructuring-finance basis, the answer depends on the **size of the annual fixed-cost gap** that management must close. In large German automotive operations, a non-closure package built from:

- attrition / early retirement,
- working-time changes,
- wage moderation / bonus restraint,
- internal redeployment and qualification,
- capacity consolidation without full closure,

can usually deliver **meaningful savings**, but it is often **slower and less certain** than a site-rationalization package.

### Practical benchmark
A non-closure package is typically credible if the required gap is roughly in the range of:

- **low-to-mid three-digit millions annually** for a single large operation, or
- a **moderate percentage of domestic fixed costs**.

If the required durable gap is more like:

- **high three-digit millions to low billions annually**, and
- the business has **persistent overcapacity across multiple plants**,

then non-closure measures alone often become insufficient unless the workforce contribution is unusually large and sustained for many years.

That is the finance reality: **non-closure solutions can bridge moderate gaps; they struggle with very large structural gaps.**

---

## 1) What the available measures usually deliver

Below are benchmark ranges, not company-specific facts.

### A. Natural attrition / retirement / non-replacement
This is usually the least disruptive and most finance-efficient tool.

**Typical effect:**
- annual gross savings depend on workforce size and vacancy rate,
- net savings are limited by backfilling needs, qualification lags, and mix of labor grades.

**Benchmark range:**
- For a large industrial workforce, attrition can often remove **1–3% of headcount per year** without forced redundancies, sometimes more if hiring is frozen.
- On a fully loaded basis, that can translate into **single-digit to low double-digit millions per 1,000 employees per year**, depending on wage level and benefits.

**Constraint:**
- It is **slow**. If the cost gap needs to be closed quickly, attrition alone is rarely enough.

### B. Working-time changes
Examples: lower overtime, reduced shifts, more flexible schedules, temporary short-time style arrangements, or permanent reduction in hours with partial compensation adjustment.

**Typical effect:**
- good for reducing variable labor cost and aligning labor with lower volume,
- less effective if the problem is primarily fixed overhead and plant underutilization.

**Benchmark range:**
- Often worth **low single-digit % of direct labor cost** in a negotiated package, sometimes more if overtime and shift premiums are material.
- In an overcapacity setting, the value can be larger if working-time changes allow permanent line reduction.

**Constraint:**
- Does not fully solve **fixed plant-cost absorption** if the site remains structurally underutilized.

### C. Wage moderation / bonus restraint / employee contribution
This is the fastest-to-cash measure if negotiated.

**Typical effect:**
- immediate P&L relief,
- durable only if agreed for multiple years and not fully offset by inflation or catch-up clauses.

**Benchmark range:**
- A time-bound concession package may yield about **1–5% of relevant domestic payroll cost annually**, depending on scope.
- If it includes bonus waivers, special payments, and shift-premium reductions, the impact can be material, but still usually not enough to fix a major fixed-cost problem on its own.

**Constraint:**
- If used alone, it is often viewed as a bridge, not a structural solution.

### D. Capacity consolidation without full closure
This includes line removal, shift elimination, product transfer, footprint simplification, and partial site downsizing.

**Typical effect:**
- this is often the **best compromise** financially,
- because it can remove fixed cost while avoiding the political and labor-relations cost of a full closure.

**Benchmark range:**
- Savings can be significant if it truly reduces:
  - maintenance,
  - supervision,
  - logistics,
  - utilities,
  - indirect labor,
  - and depreciation/overhead burden,
  rather than just moving volume around.

**Constraint:**
- The savings depend on whether the footprint is actually simplified. If the company keeps most fixed infrastructure in place, savings are modest.

### E. Full plant closure / site rationalization
This is the most decisive lever for fixed-cost elimination.

**Typical effect:**
- strongest reduction in overhead, site administration, maintenance, logistics, and often indirect labor,
- also the clearest way to remove persistent underutilized capacity.

**Benchmark range:**
- Can deliver **very large annual fixed-cost savings** once fully implemented.
- But it has higher one-off costs: severance, transfers, asset write-downs, supply-chain disruption, and transition costs.

**Constraint:**
- The financial advantage depends on implementation timing and the size of one-off charges.
- If labor peace fails or execution is delayed, the NPV can deteriorate quickly.

## 3) Comparative benchmark: non-closure versus closure economics

### Non-closure package
**Strengths**
- lower one-off cost,
- lower execution risk,
- better chance of labor agreement,
- preserves optionality.

**Weaknesses**
- savings are usually spread over time,
- may not remove enough fixed cost,
- can be reversible and therefore less credible to markets if too small.

### Closure / rationalization package
**Strengths**
- deeper and more durable fixed-cost removal,
- clearer signaling to investors and competitors,
- better for chronic overcapacity.

**Weaknesses**
- high one-off cost,
- higher labor conflict risk,
- slower implementation if blocked,
- can damage future flexibility if done too aggressively.

### Finance conclusion
If the company’s problem is mainly **cost moderation**, a non-closure package may work.  
If the problem is **structural overcapacity plus an oversized German fixed-cost base**, then the non-closure package must be unusually large and enforced over several years to substitute for closures.

## Indicative savings ranges by measure

These are rough order-of-magnitude ranges, not verified company figures.

### 1) Attrition / retirement / voluntary exit
Usually useful, but limited unless the workforce is already aging and the plant structure allows headcount to fall without destroying output.

- **Typical recurring saving:** low to moderate
- **Magnitude:** maybe **€20k–€70k per departing employee per year** in fully loaded cost terms, depending on grade mix
- **Problem:** slow and uncertain; does not remove enough fixed cost quickly if overcapacity is material

### 2) Working-time changes / shift redesign / short-time style flexibility
Can reduce labor cost and some overhead, but often only part of the answer.

- **Typical recurring saving:** moderate
- **Magnitude:** often **single-digit to low-double-digit millions per plant per year** unless shifts are cut materially
- **Problem:** if volume later rebounds, some of the benefit reverses

### 3) Wage moderation / bonus restraint / temporary labor contribution
This can buy time and lower cost, but it is the least durable element unless repeated.

- **Typical recurring saving:** moderate in the first years
- **Magnitude:** often **1–3% of wage bill** in a serious package; more if combined with benefit changes
- **Problem:** if it expires, the savings expire

### 4) Redeployment / internal transfer / qualification
Good for avoiding severance and preserving know-how, but not a direct margin fix by itself.

- **Typical financial benefit:** indirect
- **Problem:** helps with headcount reduction, but doesn’t automatically remove capacity cost unless volumes are actually consolidated

### 5) Partial consolidation / line removal / shift elimination
This is usually the **most important non-closure lever**.

- **Typical recurring saving:** high
- **Magnitude:** can be **tens of millions annually per site**, potentially more if whole lines or shifts are removed
- **Why it matters:** it reduces fixed cost without a full legal closure
- **Risk:** if product allocation is not changed decisively, the savings leak away

5. **The burden-of-proof conflict is itself part of the deadlock.**  
   Each side demands the other prove its case first, which means the process stalls unless a common model and trigger rule are introduced.

6. **Closures function as both economic tool and bargaining weapon.**  
   Labor’s strongest objection is not only the closure outcome itself, but the use of closure threats as leverage before alternatives are fully tested.

7. **Investor and family positions are not identical.**  
   Both favor credibility and durable savings, but the family is more sensitive to long-term stewardship and industrial legitimacy, while investors focus more narrowly on return-on-sales recovery.

8. **The expert framing was accepted, but only selectively.**  
   Everyone converged on “moderate gap vs. large gap,” yet each stakeholder used that frame to justify its own red lines.
