We are now past the point where repeating “competitiveness” is enough to justify forcing the workforce to pay for the entire restructuring.

## To management
You still have not supplied the one thing that would make your position testable: a **transparent, quantified bridge**.

So let me be explicit:

- **What is the annual structural gap?**
- **What does each non-closure measure contribute?**
- **How much of the gap remains after attrition, retirement pathways, redeployment, working-time changes, bonus restraint, and capacity consolidation?**
- **Why exactly is that remainder impossible to close without closures or forced redundancies?**

Until you answer those questions with numbers, your closure and redundancy threat is not a solution. It is leverage.

And I reject the framing that labor must first accept plant closures and compulsory redundancies just to be taken seriously. That is not a fair bargaining posture.

## Where I agree with others
I agree with the expert and, in part, with management and the investors on one point: this must be a **durable** restructuring, not a temporary patch.

I also agree that:
- attrition alone will not solve everything,
- working-time changes must be meaningful,
- a labor contribution can be part of the package,
- and capacity must be reduced in a real way.

But that does **not** mean closures or forced layoffs must remain the default tool.

## Where I disagree
### With management
You keep presenting closures as the logical fallback before the alternatives have been fully quantified. That is backwards.

A serious restructuring sequence is:
1. quantify the gap,
2. exhaust non-closure tools,
3. verify the remaining gap independently,
4. only then discuss whether stronger measures are genuinely unavoidable.

Not the other way around.

### With the family and investors
You speak as if “value” only means maximizing flexibility for management and keeping every hard tool on the table. I do not accept that definition.

Long-term value also depends on:
- labor peace,
- trust,
- productivity,
- know-how retention,
- and the company’s reputation as a reliable employer.

A deal that destroys those assets in pursuit of short-term cost discipline is not stewardship. It is self-harm.

### With the state
I appreciate the insistence that closures should be a last resort. That is correct. But I want to be clear: “last resort” cannot mean “kept openly available as a bargaining club from day one.”

If the state wants to protect the industrial base, then it should back a process that begins with **site and job security**, not with threats that destabilize the workforce before the numbers are tested.

## What we are prepared to discuss
We remain open to a **shared-sacrifice package** if, and only if, it is genuinely shared.

That can include:
- temporary wage moderation,
- bonus restraint,
- working-time flexibility,
- shift adjustments,
- natural attrition,
- retirement windows,
- redeployment,
- qualification and retraining,
- and a limited employee contribution.

But only if these are matched by:
- **no plant closures**
- **no compulsory redundancies**
- **binding site and job security**
- **future product and investment commitments**
- **management and owner contributions as well**

That is the only basis on which we will negotiate seriously.

## On the “two-stage” and “hybrid” ideas
I do not reject them outright, but I will be very clear:

- A **two-stage process** is acceptable only if stage one contains a **real no-closure commitment**, not a trapdoor.
- A **hybrid consolidation** is acceptable only if it is **not closure by another name**.

If “consolidation” means hidden shutdowns, permanent job losses, and a weakened collective framework, then we will oppose it just as firmly as outright closure.

## What would move us
A proposal could move us if it contains all of the following:

1. **A quantified annual savings target**
2. **A serious non-closure savings package**
3. **A fair employee contribution that is time-limited**
4. **Binding investment and product commitments for German sites**
5. **No compulsory redundancies**
6. **No plant closures**
7. **A credible governance mechanism with independent verification**

That is the kind of package we can discuss. Anything short of that asks workers to carry the risk while others keep the decisive power.

## What we will not accept
Let me state our limits plainly:

- No German plant closures
- No compulsory redundancies
- No bargaining away the collective wage framework
- No “temporary” concessions that become permanent one-way sacrifices
- No conditional moratorium that is really just a delayed closure plan

If the company wants savings, it has tools. Use them fairly, and use them first.

## Closing point
The burden is still on management to prove that non-closure alternatives are insufficient. Until then, closures and forced redundancies are not justified.

If management is serious, table the numbers. If not, then it should not be surprised when the workforce treats its threats as exactly that: threats.
