We agree with the expert’s core point: **the company needs durable, quantified savings**, not slogans. And we agree with management, the family, and the state that a cosmetic compromise would be value-destructive.

But from an investor perspective, the key issue is not ideological preference for closures. It is **whether the package is credible enough to restore margins on a durable basis**.

## Where we agree

### With management
- The current cost base is too high.
- Capacity misalignment cannot be left unresolved.
- A permanent improvement in return on sales is necessary.
- Temporary, symbolic measures are not enough.

### With the family
- Long-term value matters.
- A strike-heavy or permanently unstable outcome would destroy equity value.
- A negotiated, durable solution is preferable if it truly fixes the economics.

### With the state
- Political stability matters.
- A prolonged industrial conflict would damage the company, the region, and shareholder value.
- A solution that preserves future investment is better than a purely punitive one.

### With the expert
- A package must be quantified by annual savings, permanence, timing, and execution certainty.
- If the gap is severe, non-closure measures alone may not be enough.

## Where we disagree

### With the works council and union
Your repeated “no closures / no compulsory redundancies” line is **not acceptable by itself** if you cannot show equivalent savings. Investors cannot fund structurally impaired margins indefinitely.

A job-protective package is only supportable if it delivers:
1. **durable cost reduction,**
2. **capacity reduction in fact, not just on paper,**
3. **a clear path back to target margins,**
4. **deal certainty and implementation credibility.**

If your alternative amounts to:
- attrition,
- retirement,
- working-time tweaks,
- and temporary concessions,

but does not remove enough fixed cost, then it is not a turnaround. It is postponement.

## Our position on the options

### 1) Full closure-first strategy
We are **not automatically in favor** of closures. They can create execution risk, operational disruption, and strike escalation. That can destroy value quickly.

But closures are not off the table if they are the only credible way to restore economics. If management can demonstrate that a site is structurally uneconomic and that equivalent savings cannot be achieved otherwise, then we will not oppose necessary rationalization in principle.

### 2) No-closure settlement
We would support this **only if it is financially equivalent**. That means the package must be:
- large enough,
- durable,
- enforceable,
- and clearly tied to measurable margin improvement.

### 3) Hybrid compromise
This is the most plausible route **if** it is real. For us, a viable compromise would likely need:
- significant labor-cost contribution,
- meaningful capacity consolidation,
- strict headcount reduction targets,
- site-specific investment commitments,
- and a timetable that shows savings arriving quickly enough to matter.

If the compromise protects every current site and merely softens the pain, then it fails our test.

## Our threshold

We are not asking for maximum pain on labor. We are asking for **maximum credibility in restoring value**.

So our threshold is simple:

- If the package **materially closes the structural cost gap**, we support it.
- If it does **not** materially close the gap, we do not support it.
- If it preserves the present structure while claiming future competitiveness, we regard that as value destruction.

## What we want next
Before any party asks for support, put the numbers on the table:

1. What is the **annual structural gap**?
2. What savings come from each measure?
3. What is **permanent** versus **temporary**?
4. What is the cash impact in **year 1, year 2, year 3**?
5. What is the expected effect on **return on sales**?

Without that, nobody can credibly claim that the restructuring will restore competitiveness.

## Bottom line
We are open to a negotiated solution. We are not open to a **cosmetic settlement** or an outcome that leaves the company with an uncompetitive cost base.

If the non-closure alternative truly matches the economics, we will back it.  
If it does not, then management must use the necessary structural tools — including plant rationalization if required — to restore shareholder value.
