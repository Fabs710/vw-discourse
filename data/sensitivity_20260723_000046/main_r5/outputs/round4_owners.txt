I will be direct.

## Where I agree
I agree with management, investors, and the expert on the central economic point: **this is not a cosmetic problem**. The company needs a **durable** restructuring that actually restores margins, reduces fixed cost, and improves competitiveness.

I also agree with the state, the works council, and the union on one important issue: **a prolonged industrial conflict would destroy value**. It would damage execution, reputation, and the company’s ability to invest in the product and technology transition.

So yes: the goal must be a **settlement that is economically real and socially stable**.

---

## Where I disagree, clearly
### 1) I do **not** accept a blanket “no closures, no forced redundancies” position
That is not a restructuring strategy. It is a red line.

If the non-closure package truly closes the gap, then good — closures may be unnecessary. But I will **not** accept an upfront categorical ban on closures or compulsory redundancies when the company still has an unresolved structural cost problem.

Why? Because that would hand the workforce a veto over the very tools needed if the economics prove worse than hoped. As controlling owners, we cannot accept a framework that preserves every structure regardless of competitiveness.

### 2) I do **not** accept a state-led freeze on economically necessary correction
I respect the regional role and the statute, but the blocking power does not entitle anyone to preserve an uncompetitive footprint indefinitely.

A state position of “last resort only” is understandable. A position of “never, unless impossible to avoid” without rigorous proof is not enough. Long-term regional interests are not served by protecting an industrial structure that continues to lose money.

### 3) I do **not** accept management’s position without a quantified bridge
Management is right that structural action is needed, but I agree with labor on one procedural point: **the numbers must be made explicit**.

Management cannot just say “overcapacity” and assume that makes closures necessary. It must show:
- the annual structural gap,
- the savings from each non-closure measure,
- what remains uncovered,
- and why that remainder cannot be fixed without stronger tools.

That is the burden management must meet.

---

## On the union and works council
You both argue that non-closure tools should be exhausted first:
- attrition,
- retirement,
- redeployment,
- qualification,
- working-time changes,
- wage moderation,
- partial consolidation.

I am open to all of that. In fact, I prefer it if it is **material, binding, and durable**.

But I do **not** accept the assertion that these measures are automatically sufficient. They may be, or they may not be. That is exactly what the numbers test must determine.

What I will not support is a deal where labor gives a limited concession and then claims the restructuring problem is solved, while the fixed-cost base remains structurally out of line.

That would be postponement, not turnaround.

---

## On the state and co-determination
I respect co-determination. I also respect the state’s interest in industrial stability. But governance rights exist to protect the enterprise, not to fossilize it.

If closures and redundancies remain unavailable in principle, then management loses credibility in the restructuring process and investors will assume the company cannot act when needed. That is value-destructive.

So my position is:
- yes to a serious negotiated restructuring,
- yes to using non-closure measures first where they work,
- no to an absolute ban on the tools that may be required if the gap proves large.

---

## On the expert’s assessment
The expert’s analysis was useful. The key point is this:

- A **moderate** gap may be bridged with non-closure measures.
- A **large and persistent** gap usually cannot be closed without deeper capacity rationalization.

That does **not** mean closures are the first choice.  
It does mean they must remain available as a fallback if the numbers justify them.

So I do **not** accept the argument that because non-closure tools exist, closures should be ruled out. That is not finance. That is wishful thinking.

---

## Where I stand on a possible compromise
I am open to a **hybrid, quantified, performance-based package** if it is serious.

That would need to include:
- a transparent savings target,
- a real non-closure package,
- binding employee contributions for a fixed period,
- capacity consolidation where it truly removes fixed cost,
- independent verification,
- and a clear threshold at which harder measures return to the table.

What I will **not** accept is a “conditional no-closure pact” that is really just a trapdoor in disguise — meaning labor gives up leverage now, but management remains permanently constrained later. Equally, I will not accept the reverse: labor gets guarantees now while the company is left with no credible fallback if the economics do not work.

The arrangement must be balanced and economically credible.

---

## What would move me
I would support a non-closure solution if it meets these conditions:
1. **Material recurring savings**
2. **Durable execution over multiple years**
3. **Real fixed-cost reduction**
4. **Verified gap closure**
5. **Investment and product commitments that preserve future competitiveness**

If those conditions are met, then yes, there is no need to insist on closures.

If they are not met, then closures and compulsory redundancies must remain available.

---

## My position, plainly
- I support restructuring.
- I support negotiated, non-disruptive measures first where feasible.
- I do **not** support blanket prohibitions on plant closures or compulsory redundancies.
- I do **not** support a solution that preserves an uncompetitive German cost base.
- I do **not** support a cosmetic compromise that merely delays the problem.

The company needs a package that restores margins and safeguards long-term value. If the non-closure route can truly do that, I am prepared to back it. If it cannot, then the harder tools must remain available. That is the responsible position for a controlling shareholder.
