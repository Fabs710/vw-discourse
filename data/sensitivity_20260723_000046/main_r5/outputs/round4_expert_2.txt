From a restructuring-finance perspective, the key issue is now clear:

## 1) What can be said with confidence
A **credible non-closure solution** must do more than delay the problem. It must permanently remove enough cost and capacity to close most of the structural gap.

In practical terms, that usually requires a combination of:
- attrition / retirement pathways,
- working-time changes,
- wage moderation or time-limited labor contribution,
- redeployment / qualification,
- **and** real capacity consolidation:
  - shift cuts,
  - line removals,
  - overhead centralization,
  - product reassignment.

If the package does **not** include meaningful capacity subtraction, it will usually be too weak for a large German automotive footprint with excess fixed cost.

## 2) What is likely true on the economics
Based on the fact pattern you gave:
- a **pure no-closure / no-forced-redundancy** package is **possible only if the cost gap is moderate**;
- if the gap is **large**, the financially preferred fallback is usually **hybrid consolidation**;
- if the gap is **very large**, then **plant closures or closure-equivalent actions** become the most finance-efficient route.

So the dispute is not really about principles. It is about whether the gap is:
- moderate enough for non-closure measures,
- or too large, requiring deeper footprint reduction.

## 3) What the parties still need
To decide this properly, management should table a **site-by-site bridge** showing:
1. current annual structural gap,
2. savings from each measure,
3. one-off restructuring charges,
4. timing of savings,
5. residual gap after non-closure measures,
6. comparison of:
   - no-closure,
   - hybrid consolidation,
   - full rationalization.

Without that, nobody can objectively say whether closures are necessary or not.

## 4) My finance view on the compromise options

### Option A: Full no-closure pact
Finance-credible only if:
- recurring savings are large enough,
- capacity is genuinely reduced,
- and the residual gap is small.

Otherwise it is too weak.

### Option B: Hybrid consolidation
This is usually the **best midpoint** financially:
- preserves more industrial base,
- reduces fixed cost materially,
- lower one-off cost than full closure,
- better chance of political and labor acceptability.

### Option C: Full rationalization / closure
Financially strongest on fixed-cost removal, but:
- highest one-off costs,
- highest execution risk,
- highest conflict risk.

It becomes rational mainly when the gap cannot otherwise be closed.

## 5) Bottom line
The finance answer is:

- **Non-closure can work if the gap is moderate.**
- **Hybrid consolidation is the most likely workable compromise if the gap is material.**
- **Closures become the preferred fallback if the remaining gap after non-closure measures is still large.**
