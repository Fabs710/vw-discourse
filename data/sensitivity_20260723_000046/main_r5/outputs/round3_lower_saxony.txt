I will be equally direct.

I agree with the repeated diagnosis that this company needs a **durable restructuring** and that a cosmetic compromise would be irresponsible. I also accept the finance expert’s core point: this is fundamentally a **numbers question**. But I do **not** accept the leap that therefore closures and compulsory redundancies must stay broadly available as a first-line option.

From the state’s perspective, that is the wrong starting point.

## Where I agree
- The current cost base cannot remain unchanged.
- The company needs a **quantified**, **binding**, and **durable** solution.
- Reversible measures should be used first wherever they genuinely save money:
  - attrition,
  - retirements,
  - redeployment,
  - qualification,
  - working-time flexibility,
  - and a fair employee contribution.
- Future investment and product commitments for German sites are essential.
- A prolonged industrial conflict would damage the company, workers, and the region.

## Where I disagree
### 1) I do not accept closures as a default bargaining tool
A plant closure in this state is not an abstract cost item. It is a **permanent regional loss**:
- jobs disappear,
- suppliers are hit,
- skills are lost,
- trust in the company and in co-determination is damaged for years.

So I reject the idea that management may simply keep closures on the table from the outset as leverage. If closures are ever to be discussed, they must be the **last resort**, after alternatives have been properly quantified and genuinely exhausted.

### 2) I do not accept compulsory redundancies as a starting point
Forced job cuts cannot be the first answer to a restructuring problem. If overcapacity exists, then the company must first use every socially responsible instrument available before resorting to dismissals. That is not sentimentality; it is prudent industrial and regional policy.

### 3) I do not accept any outcome that weakens the state’s co-determination and veto role
The special statute and our board position are not decorative. They exist precisely so that long-term corporate decisions cannot ignore the regional and public interest. I will not support any path that sidelines that role.

## On the expert’s assessment
The expert’s analysis is useful, and I take it seriously. It confirms:
- non-closure packages can be credible in **moderate-gap** scenarios,
- they become harder only when the structural gap is very large,
- and the real issue is whether the company has actually quantified the gap and the savings.

That means the burden is on management to prove necessity with numbers, not on labor or the state to accept closures in advance.

What I would like to see next is not more rhetoric about “structural change,” but a **clear bridge**:
1. What is the annual structural gap?
2. What savings come from each non-closure measure?
3. What capacity is actually removed without closures?
4. What is the time profile?
5. Why exactly is that insufficient?

Until those questions are answered, closure pressure is not proof of necessity.

## On the works council and union
I support your insistence that workers should not be made the sole shock absorber. You are right to reject a one-sided restructuring.

But I also want to be clear: a purely categorical “no change at all” position would not be responsible if it leaves the company structurally uncompetitive. The path forward has to be a **real** restructuring, not a freeze.

So I can support:
- temporary wage moderation,
- working-time adjustments,
- shift and capacity changes,
- attrition and retirement,
- redeployment and retraining,
- and a limited, time-bound employee contribution,

**if and only if** they are matched by:
- binding investment commitments,
- product allocation to German plants,
- and serious protections for sites and jobs.

That is a negotiable basis. What is not negotiable for me is turning worker protection into a pretext for closure-first policy.

## On the family and investors
I respect the family’s focus on long-term stewardship and the investors’ demand for credibility. But I will say this plainly: long-term value is not protected by breaking trust with the workforce and hollowing out the industrial base in Germany.

A solution that destroys labor peace and regional confidence may satisfy a narrow cost logic in the short term, but it creates new risks:
- conflict,
- political backlash,
- lower productivity,
- implementation delays,
- and reputational damage.

That is not a durable solution.

## My position
So the state’s position is:

1. **No blanket acceptance of plant closures.**
2. **No acceptance of compulsory redundancies as the starting point.**
3. **Yes to a serious, quantified restructuring built first on reversible and negotiated measures.**
4. **Yes to a fair employee contribution if it is time-limited and matched by binding future commitments.**
5. **No outcome that guts employment in this state or sidelines our co-determination role.**

## What I want next
I support a **two-stage approach** only if it is genuine and not a trap:
- Stage one: quantified non-closure measures with binding milestones.
- Stage two: only if the verified gap remains truly severe, then a harder discussion about further structural measures.

But I will not sign up to a “conditional” process that is simply a delayed closure strategy. If the numbers show that non-closure options can close the gap, then closures are off the table. If they do not, then the company must still prove that any further step is genuinely unavoidable and proportionate.

If management wants my support, it should stop speaking in generalities and table the actual figures. Until then, I will remain opposed to closures and forced redundancies as default instruments, and I reserve the state’s blocking rights accordingly.
