Let me be very clear: we are not moving from our red lines because management, investors, and even the family keep repeating “structural necessity” as if that phrase alone makes closures and forced redundancies acceptable.

It does not.

## To management
You still have not answered the central question: **why must plant closures and compulsory redundancies remain available if the company already has other tools?**

You heard the expert: a non-closure package can be credible if the gap is not exaggerated and if the company is prepared to use real measures:
- attrition,
- retirement pathways,
- internal redeployment,
- working-time changes,
- labor-cost contributions,
- consolidation of capacity without closure.

That is exactly where the negotiation must start. Not with threats. Not with a fait accompli. Not with “accept our logic or we close plants.”

And I reject your attempt to turn “no closures, no forced redundancies” into a refusal to restructure. It is not. It is a refusal to let workers pay for management’s failure to plan ahead.

If you want our support, then put a **quantified bridge** on the table:
- how much savings are needed,
- how much each non-closure measure delivers,
- and why you claim that is still insufficient.

Without that, your closure threat is not a plan. It is pressure.

## To the controlling family
You say you want long-term value and durable savings. Fine. Then prove it by supporting a solution that preserves the company’s industrial base and trust with the workforce.

What destroys long-term value is not a protected workforce. What destroys long-term value is:
- breaking the job-security framework,
- undermining co-determination,
- creating permanent conflict,
- and making German plants disposable.

You cannot claim stewardship while backing a process that treats closures as a bargaining club. If you truly want long-term value, then back a restructuring that uses the company’s remaining strength to invest in future work here, not to threaten whole sites into submission.

## To the regional state government
Your role is not to stand aside while management normalizes closure as a first resort.

You have a blocking position for a reason. Use it. Not as theater, but as a real defense of:
- industrial capacity,
- regional employment,
- supplier networks,
- and the social fabric.

I do hear your point that “preserve everything at any cost” is not viable. But that is not what we are saying. We are saying: **exhaust the alternatives first, and do not start by sacrificing sites and jobs.**

If you support a review process, then it must be a review of alternatives **without** closures and **without** forced redundancies as the starting assumption.

## To the union
We are aligned on the core red lines, and I support that. But let me sharpen it: a “conditional no-closure pact” only has value if it is a real guarantee, not a trapdoor.

We can discuss:
- temporary working-time adjustments,
- limited, time-bound employee contributions,
- internal flexibility,
- qualification,
- redeployment.

But only if they are matched by:
- **binding no-closure commitments**,
- **no compulsory redundancies**,
- **job security through 2030**,
- and **real product and investment commitments**.

If there is an “independent review” mechanism, it must not be a disguised route to reopen closure demands later. I will not accept a staged process that simply delays the attack on jobs.

## To the investors
You keep saying “if the numbers work, we’ll back it.” Good. Then stop acting as if the only serious numbers are the ones management prefers.

The expert’s answer was not “closures are inevitable.” The expert said a non-closure solution is possible in principle and may be finance-credible depending on the actual gap. That means the burden is on management to **prove necessity**, not on workers to surrender first and ask questions later.

If you want credibility, then demand:
- transparent savings math,
- independent verification,
- and proof that all non-closure measures have been properly exhausted.

What you should not do is treat worker protections as a problem to be overridden because they are inconvenient for margins.

## On the expert’s assessment
The expert was useful in one respect: it exposed that this is a **numbers fight**, not a slogan fight. Good. Then let’s have the numbers.

But I will also say this: the expert did **not** conclude that closures are required. The expert said a non-closure package is possible and that closures become more likely only if the gap is truly severe.

So management does not get to hide behind that analysis. It must now show:
1. the real gap,
2. the real savings from non-closure measures,
3. and why it claims those measures are inadequate.

Until then, closures are not justified.

## Our position in round 3
We remain firm:

- **No German plant closures**
- **No compulsory redundancies**
- **No tearing up of the job-security framework**
- **No weakening of co-determination**
- **Yes to restructuring only through negotiated, reversible, and fair measures**

If the company wants savings, it must start with:
- working-time and flexibility measures,
- natural attrition and retirement,
- redeployment and qualification,
- limited, fair, time-bound employee contributions,
- and a binding future industrial plan.

What we will **not** do is sign up to a “conditional” no-closure pact that merely postpones the closure agenda. If the word “conditional” means labor guarantees now and management freedom later, then it is not a guarantee. It is a trap.

## What I expect next
Management must table a quantified proposal that shows:
- how much of the gap is closed without site closures,
- what capacity reduction is achieved by non-closure means,
- and what future investment is committed to German plants.

If they cannot do that, then the issue is not our refusal. The issue is their unwillingness to build a fair solution.

And let me end where I began:  
**No closures. No compulsory redundancies. No bargaining away the workforce to cover management’s structural mistakes.**
