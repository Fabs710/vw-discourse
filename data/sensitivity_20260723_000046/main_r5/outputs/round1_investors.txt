From the perspective of dispersed institutional investors, the key question is not whether the restructuring is painful, but whether it is **credible enough to restore competitive margins and protect long-term equity value**.

I agree with management, the family, and the state on one essential point: **the current cost base and capacity cannot be left as they are**. If this ends in a cosmetic deal, the market will treat it as delay, not restructuring. That would be unacceptable. We have already seen pressure on margins and the share price because investors do not believe the status quo is sustainable.

Where I differ is on the idea that the only credible route is plant closure. We do **not** have a strategic attachment to closures. We have an attachment to **results**. If alternative measures can deliver the same durable savings, then that is preferable because it reduces execution risk and the chance of a prolonged industrial conflict that destroys value in the near term. A strike-heavy confrontation would be value-destructive.

So our position is:

- **No support for a cosmetic settlement.**
- **No support for preserving an uncompetitive cost base.**
- **No support for a prolonged conflict that erodes margins further.**
- **Support for whichever package credibly delivers durable cost reduction, capacity alignment, and a clear return to target profitability.**

To the works council and union: your insistence on no closures and no compulsory redundancies is understandable, but it is not enough on its own. If that stance blocks equivalent savings, then it becomes a demand that investors continue funding structural underperformance. That is not acceptable. If you want investor support, you need to show how the same economic outcome is achieved through other means — and it must be durable, not temporary.

To management: you need to be careful not to confuse toughness with credibility. If you push closures without a well-evidenced case that they are necessary, you risk triggering a destructive fight and losing market confidence. We need a plan that is concrete, quantified, and deliverable. Tell us:
1. how much cost reduction is achieved,
2. by when,
3. how durable it is,
4. what operational risks it creates,
5. and how it restores return on sales.

To the state and family: we respect governance reality, but we will judge this on economic outcome, not symbolism. If political constraints or ownership preferences produce a weak compromise, capital will reallocate elsewhere. If, however, you back a disciplined package that actually fixes margins, we will support it.

So the investor line is straightforward:

- **We need structural savings, not announcements.**
- **We need deal certainty, not open-ended conflict.**
- **We will back a negotiated alternative only if it is provably equivalent in financial effect.**
- **If it is not equivalent, we will regard it as value destruction.**

In short: restore competitiveness, or do not expect capital markets support.
