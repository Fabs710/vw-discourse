Let me be equally direct.

I appreciate that the works council and union are consistent in defending job security and the industrial base. I also appreciate that the state and the family are both saying the company needs a durable solution, not a cosmetic one. On that point, we are aligned.

But I must correct a central illusion in what I’m hearing:

**A guarantee of “no closures, no compulsory redundancies, no structural break” is not a restructuring plan.**  
It is a demand that management solve a hard competitiveness problem while being denied the tools needed to fix it.

That is not acceptable.

### Where I agree
- Yes, we need a **negotiated** solution, not open-ended conflict.
- Yes, we should use **natural attrition, retirement, internal mobility, qualification, and working-time flexibility** wherever they create real savings.
- Yes, we need **future investment and product commitments** for German sites.
- Yes, the solution must be **durable** and not a short-term postponement.

### Where I disagree — firmly
#### 1) “No closures” cannot be a blanket red line
If a site cannot be made competitive on a sustainable basis, then keeping it open by political declaration is not protecting the company — it is locking in structural losses.

I am not saying closures are the first preference. They are not.  
But I am saying they must remain **available** if they are necessary to restore competitiveness.

#### 2) “No compulsory redundancies” cannot be absolute
If overcapacity exists and cost reduction must be structural, then job reductions cannot be ruled out in advance. We can and should minimize forced exits, but I will not accept a position that forbids them categorically while still expecting the necessary savings.

#### 3) Site and job guarantees through 2030 cannot be unconditional
I will not sign up to binding guarantees that freeze the current structure regardless of economics. If you want security, it has to be earned through a package that actually delivers margin improvement and capacity alignment.

### To the works council and union
You keep saying: “Show us an alternative.” Fair enough.

But the alternative must do **more than shift pain around**. It must produce:
- lower fixed costs,
- lower labour costs,
- reduced overcapacity,
- and a materially better return on sales.

If your proposal is only:
- attrition,
- retirement,
- working-time adjustments,
- and temporary wage moderation,

then I say plainly: **that is unlikely to be enough** unless it delivers equivalent quantified savings at the scale required. We are not talking about marginal improvement. We are talking about a structural cost gap.

So if you want to rule out closures and forced redundancies, then bring forward a package that closes the gap in a way that is:
1. **quantified**,
2. **binding**,
3. **durable**,
4. and **large enough**.

If you cannot, then your red lines are simply a refusal to restructure.

### To the regional state government
I respect your role and your concerns. But your blocking rights cannot become a veto on necessary economic correction.

If the state insists on preserving every site regardless of economics, then the company will carry excess cost for years — and that means less investment, less product competitiveness, and eventually weaker employment across the network, including in the region you want to protect.

Protecting the region does **not** mean preserving every current structure. It means securing a company that can still invest, compete, and employ people in 2030 and beyond.

### To the controlling family
You are right that long-term owners should care about industrial fabric and trust. I agree. But long-term stewardship also means refusing to let sentiment substitute for economics.

I welcome your focus on durable value. That is exactly why I insist on structural change. If we do not fix the cost base now, the company will be weaker later, and then the damage will be even greater.

### To the investors
You are asking for credibility and quantification. That is reasonable.

Here is the reality: we do not have the luxury of an indefinite negotiation while costs continue to run above target. Markets, competitors, and the product cycle will not wait. I will not support a package that merely promises savings sometime in the future without hard mechanisms.

### What management is prepared to discuss
I am not closing the door on alternatives. But the alternative must meet these conditions:

- **material and durable cost reduction**
- **genuine labour-cost contribution**
- **real capacity reduction**
- **a binding future investment framework**
- **clear governance and implementation certainty**

That can be achieved through a combination of measures, but it must be enough to restore competitiveness. If it is not, then we have not solved the problem.

### My bottom line
I am prepared to negotiate hard on the *how*.  
I am **not** prepared to negotiate on the *need* for deep structural change.

So let me be explicit:

- I do **not** accept a blanket ban on closures.
- I do **not** accept a blanket ban on compulsory redundancies.
- I do **not** accept a deal that preserves an uncompetitive cost base.
- I do **not** accept a cosmetic compromise dressed up as industrial peace.

If the workforce side wants to avoid closures and forced job cuts, then it must help deliver savings of sufficient scale and permanence. Otherwise, management will continue to pursue the structural measures required to restore the company’s competitiveness.
