Below is the restructuring-finance view of the impasse, staying strictly on the economics.

## Bottom line

A **non-closure package can be finance-credible only if it removes enough recurring fixed cost to close most of the structural gap**. In a large German automotive operation with this kind of cost pressure, that usually means:

- **moderate gap**: non-closure package is plausible;
- **large gap**: partial consolidation may work, but full no-closure becomes hard;
- **very large gap**: closures or equivalent site rationalization usually become the financially preferred fallback.

Without the actual site data, I can only estimate. But the key point is that **attrition, working-time flexibility, and temporary concessions rarely solve a genuine structural overcapacity problem by themselves**. They help, but they do not usually get you all the way.

---

## What a “finance-credible” no-closure package needs to achieve

For a carmaker of this scale, the restructuring must usually deliver:

1. **Recurring annual savings**
   - not just one-off labor concessions;
   - not just a temporary wage freeze;
   - but a lower steady-state cost base.

2. **Real fixed-cost reduction**
   - fewer shifts,
   - lower plant overhead,
   - less duplicated capacity,
   - lower indirect labor,
   - less logistics and complexity.

3. **Durability**
   - savings must last beyond the agreement term,
   - ideally through product cycle volatility.

4. **Operational enforceability**
   - if the plan depends on goodwill or overtime restraint, it is weaker;
   - if it is embedded in work design, footprint consolidation, and product allocation, it is stronger.

---

## Indicative savings ranges by measure

These are rough order-of-magnitude ranges, not verified company figures.

### 1) Attrition / retirement / voluntary exit
Usually useful, but limited unless the workforce is already aging and the plant structure allows headcount to fall without destroying output.

- **Typical recurring saving:** low to moderate
- **Magnitude:** maybe **€20k–€70k per departing employee per year** in fully loaded cost terms, depending on grade mix
- **Problem:** slow and uncertain; does not remove enough fixed cost quickly if overcapacity is material

### 2) Working-time changes / shift redesign / short-time style flexibility
Can reduce labor cost and some overhead, but often only part of the answer.

- **Typical recurring saving:** moderate
- **Magnitude:** often **single-digit to low-double-digit millions per plant per year** unless shifts are cut materially
- **Problem:** if volume later rebounds, some of the benefit reverses

### 3) Wage moderation / bonus restraint / temporary labor contribution
This can buy time and lower cost, but it is the least durable element unless repeated.

- **Typical recurring saving:** moderate in the first years
- **Magnitude:** often **1–3% of wage bill** in a serious package; more if combined with benefit changes
- **Problem:** if it expires, the savings expire

### 4) Redeployment / internal transfer / qualification
Good for avoiding severance and preserving know-how, but not a direct margin fix by itself.

- **Typical financial benefit:** indirect
- **Problem:** helps with headcount reduction, but doesn’t automatically remove capacity cost unless volumes are actually consolidated

### 5) Partial consolidation / line removal / shift elimination
This is usually the **most important non-closure lever**.

- **Typical recurring saving:** high
- **Magnitude:** can be **tens of millions annually per site**, potentially more if whole lines or shifts are removed
- **Why it matters:** it reduces fixed cost without a full legal closure
- **Risk:** if product allocation is not changed decisively, the savings leak away

---

## The key question: how big is the structural gap?

That is the decisive variable.

### If the gap is “moderate”
Meaning roughly:
- one or two underutilized sites,
- some excess shifts,
- labor costs above benchmark but not wildly so,
- and the brand can still support German production with better efficiency,

then a **non-closure package plus partial consolidation** can plausibly close the gap.

### If the gap is “large”
Meaning:
- several plants are materially underutilized,
- overhead duplication is significant,
- German unit cost is far above target,
- and EV transition volumes are insufficient,

then **non-closure alone usually struggles**. You can still reduce cost without total closure, but you need **real capacity subtraction**, not just labor moderation.

### If the gap is “very large”
Then a no-closure solution is generally **not finance-credible** unless accompanied by:
- major product reassignment,
- deep shift cuts,
- large headcount reduction,
- and perhaps one or more plant shutdowns or quasi-shutdowns.

---

## What would make the non-closure route credible

A credible package would likely need all of the following:

- **permanent shift reductions**
- **significant headcount reduction through attrition and voluntary exits**
- **working-time redesign**
- **multi-year wage moderation**
- **centralization of support functions**
- **material product consolidation**
- **binding volume allocation to fewer lines**
- **investment commitments that justify remaining sites**

If those measures are implemented seriously, a no-closure plan can work. But if “no closure” just means “keep all sites open and ask labor to contribute,” then the financial effect is usually too weak.

---

## When partial consolidation is better than full closure

This is often the best midpoint.

Partial consolidation can deliver:
- lower fixed cost,
- fewer underused assets,
- fewer indirect roles,
- better utilization,
- less political and legal friction than outright closure.

From a finance standpoint, it is often superior if it captures **most of the savings** at lower disruption and lower one-off charges.

But it still needs:
- binding volume shifts,
- decommissioning of redundant equipment,
- clear plant role definitions.

Without those, “consolidation” becomes a label, not savings.

---

## Closure vs. no-closure: financial logic

### No-closure path
Pros:
- lower one-off charges
- lower conflict risk
- preserve know-how and labor relations
- less political blowback

Cons:
- savings may be insufficient
- slower implementation
- risk of compromise diluting the fixed-cost reduction

### Closure path
Pros:
- strongest fixed-cost removal
- cleaner footprint
- more certain long-term savings

Cons:
- high one-off costs
- execution risk
- labor conflict
- governance and political friction
- possible operational disruption

So financially, **closures are not automatically the best answer**. They are the strongest answer if the gap is too large for alternatives. If not, they may destroy more value than they save.

---

## My estimate: what is the minimum plausible non-closure package?

For a company this size, a serious no-closure restructuring would likely need to deliver:

- **roughly high tens of millions to low hundreds of millions of euros annually**, depending on how large the underlying gap is,
- with a substantial share coming from **permanent capacity and overhead reduction**, not just labor concessions.

If the required gap is above that range, then no-closure becomes increasingly difficult unless product volumes and plant utilization improve materially.

---

## The real test the parties should use

They should compare three scenarios:

### Scenario A: No-closure package
- attrition
- retirement pathways
- working-time changes
- labor-cost contribution
- redeployment
- selective shift cuts
- product consolidation

### Scenario B: Hybrid consolidation
- all of the above
- plus line removal
- deeper shift cuts
- some site downsizing
- stronger overhead centralization

### Scenario C: Full rationalization
- closures or shutdown-equivalent actions
- maximum fixed-cost removal
- highest one-off cost
- strongest margin reset

Then compare:
- annual recurring savings
- one-time restructuring charges
- implementation risk
- labor conflict risk
- timeline to margin recovery

---

## Likely conclusion from a neutral finance lens

If the company’s cost gap is truly structural and significant, then:

- **pure no-closure is often too weak**
- **hybrid consolidation is often the best compromise**
- **full closures become necessary only if hybrid measures still leave a large residual gap**

That is the finance answer, independent of the politics.
