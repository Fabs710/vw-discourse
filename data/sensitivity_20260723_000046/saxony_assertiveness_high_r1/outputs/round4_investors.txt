We agree on the destination: a durable restructuring, not a cosmetic fix. But from a capital-markets perspective, the test is not whether the package feels fair or politically comfortable — it is whether it **credibly restores competitive margins** and **actually lowers the structurally too-high cost base**.

On that point, we are not persuaded by the repeated insistence that all German plants must remain open and compulsory redundancies must be off the table **as fixed preconditions**. That may be understandable as a negotiating position, but it is not yet a credible answer to the company’s cost problem.

## Where we agree
We agree with nearly everyone on some basics:
- voluntary instruments should come first;
- management, owners, and capital must contribute too;
- a cosmetic settlement would destroy value;
- investment in future products and technology in Germany is necessary;
- prolonged industrial conflict would be value-destructive;
- co-determination is real and must be factored into execution.

Those are all correct.

## Where we disagree
### 1) “No closures” is not a strategy by itself
Works council, union, and state keep treating site preservation as the starting point. We do not accept that as sufficient.

If all plants remain open, then we need a **quantified, credible replacement** for the savings that plant rationalization would otherwise deliver. So far, we have heard assertions that internal measures might work, but not a convincing model showing that they will close the gap at scale.

### 2) “No compulsory redundancies” is only acceptable if the numbers work
We are open to voluntary exits, early retirement, redeployment, retraining, and working-time tools. But if the required workforce adjustment cannot be reached that way, then an unconditional no-layoff promise simply preserves overcapacity and defers the pain into future margin erosion.

That is not acceptable from a shareholder value standpoint.

### 3) Temporary concessions are not enough if the structural gap remains
The union’s position that employee contributions must be temporary and limited is understandable, but investors cannot back a package that merely pauses the problem.

If the company needs durable margin repair, then the solution must be durable too.

## What would move us
We could support a deal if it contains all of the following:
1. **Quantified savings targets**
2. **Durable cost reduction**
3. **Meaningful workforce adjustment**
4. **Visible management and owner restraint**
5. **Binding investment commitments in Germany**
6. **Execution certainty under co-determination**

That means we need hard numbers, not slogans:
- what is the exact margin gap?
- how much comes from labor cost?
- how much from overhead?
- how much from capacity reduction?
- how much from productivity and procurement?
- what is the time path to get there?

Without that, “site security” is not a solution; it is a wish.

## What would not move us
We will not support:
- a purely political compromise,
- a deal that leaves the cost base structurally uncompetitive,
- symbolic savings that do not materially change the economics,
- or a settlement that protects every existing structure while returns remain impaired.

## Direct reply to the other parties
### To management
You are right that the business needs a real reset. But credibility depends on precision. Bring the quantified package, not just open-ended pressure with closure options kept alive as leverage.

### To the works council and union
If you want investors to back a site-preserving alternative, then show us the numbers. We are not prepared to finance a compromise that simply maintains the current footprint and hopes margins recover on their own.

### To the state government
Your role matters, but a veto culture cannot replace economics. Regional stability is important only if the company remains viable and investable.

### To the family holding
We agree that long-term value matters more than optics. But long-term value is not preserved by permanently sheltering an overcost structure.

## My position, clearly
We are prepared to back any package that **credibly restores competitiveness**. That includes a site-preserving package — **if and only if** it can be shown, in hard numbers, to achieve the required savings and margin recovery.

Until then, we cannot support unconditional guarantees that lock in overcapacity and leave the cost base uncompetitive.

If the parties want a constructive next step, we support the moderator’s request for a **quantified comparison** of:
- a no-closure / no-forced-layoff package, and
- a structural-adjustment package,

with explicit assumptions on:
- annual savings,
- headcount impact,
- site impact,
- one-time restructuring charges,
- and margin trajectory through 2030.
