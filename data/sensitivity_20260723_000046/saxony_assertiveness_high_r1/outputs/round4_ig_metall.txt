I’ll respond directly.

## Where I agree
I agree with the works council, the state government, and the expert on the essentials:

- A cosmetic compromise is useless.
- Any agreement must be durable and executable.
- Voluntary instruments should come first: redeployment, retraining, early retirement, working-time tools, internal transfers.
- Management, owners, and capital must contribute visibly.
- We need hard numbers, not slogans.
- Industrial peace only exists if the workforce sees fairness and a real future.

That is all correct.

## Where I disagree
I disagree with the continued attempt to make **closures** and **forced layoffs** the default “serious” option.

The expert did **not** say closures are necessary. The expert said a no-closure, no-forced-layoff package can be plausible if the gap is moderate and the measures are durable. That is exactly why I reject the idea that plant closures are the natural baseline.

Management keeps speaking as if the burden of proof lies with labour to justify keeping sites open. I do not accept that. If you want to tear up job security, you need to prove that every less destructive option has been exhausted. So far, I have heard assertions, not proof.

## To management
You say you need a structural reset and durable savings. Fine. But you still have not shown why that must mean plant closures or compulsory redundancies.

You keep saying “the numbers” justify it, but you have not put the plant-by-plant, line-by-line case on the table in a way that demonstrates:
- which site is genuinely non-viable,
- what internal measures were tested,
- what savings are required,
- and why those savings cannot be reached through negotiated internal adjustments.

Until you do that, closure talk remains a pressure tactic, not a necessity.

And let me be blunt: a deal that breaks the collective wage framework and destroys trust in the workforce will not be durable either. It will produce conflict, not competitiveness.

## To the works council
I largely share your position:
- no plant closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- no weakening of co-determination,
- and no settlement that makes job security discretionary.

Where I want to sharpen the union line is this: if the company wants a temporary employee contribution, it must be **strictly limited, fair, and matched by binding commitments**. We should not allow a “temporary” contribution to become a permanent wage cut by another name.

So yes, I support your site-security position — but only if the package also protects the collective wage framework and includes real burden-sharing from management and owners.

## To the state government
I appreciate your support for site security and your willingness to back a fair package. But I want to be equally clear: the state should not merely ask for “credibility” while leaving closure logic on the table as if it were neutral.

Use your leverage to secure:
- all German plants safeguarded,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- binding investment in Germany,
- and a settlement that preserves industrial stability.

Do not let “competitiveness” become a euphemism for shifting structural mistakes onto workers.

## To the controlling family holding and investors
I understand your focus on margins, execution certainty, and long-term value. I am not dismissing that.

But I do reject the notion that a site-preserving package is automatically unserious unless it starts with closures. That is a false binary. A credible package can be built around:
- meaningful labour-cost contribution,
- voluntary workforce reduction,
- working-time flexibility,
- overhead restraint,
- management discipline,
- and investment in future products.

What would be reckless is a deal that creates permanent insecurity and then expects the workforce to carry the risk alone.

## What would move us
We can support a settlement if it contains all of the following:

1. **No compulsory redundancies**
2. **No plant closures**
3. **Binding job security through 2030**
4. **Temporary and limited employee contribution**
5. **Material management and owner contribution**
6. **Binding investment in Germany**
7. **Internal, reversible capacity adjustments**
8. **Transparent figures and joint monitoring**

That is the basis for a fair and durable agreement.

## What we are prepared to discuss
We remain open to:
- a temporary wage-related contribution,
- shift and working-time flexibility,
- productivity measures,
- voluntary exits,
- phased retirement,
- redeployment,
- retraining,
- and internal transfers.

But we are **not** open to:
- closures,
- forced layoffs,
- or any settlement that makes jobs contingent on future management discretion.

## A direct answer to the “two-package” idea
Yes, compare packages — but the comparison must be honest.

### Acceptable path:
A **no-closure / no-forced-layoff** package with quantified savings, management restraint, owner contribution, and binding investment.

### Not acceptable path:
A “structural adjustment” package that assumes closures and dismissals from the start and asks workers to accept that as realism.

That is not a neutral comparison. That is a loaded one.

## Bottom line
I remain ready for a serious settlement. But the settlement must be built on:

- **site security**
- **job security**
- **fair burden-sharing**
- **binding investment**
- **collective-bargaining integrity**

If management and capital want cooperation, they need to come forward with a genuine site-preserving restructuring in hard numbers.

If they cannot, then they should not expect labour to finance the restructuring by surrendering jobs and bargaining standards.
