From the perspective of dispersed institutional shareholders, our lens is straightforward: **risk-adjusted equity value**. We are not here to defend any one stakeholder’s preferred operating model. We are here to ensure the company gets back to **competitive margins, credible cash generation, and durable capital discipline**.

### Where we agree
I agree with management, and to a degree with the family holding, on the core diagnosis:

- The cost base is too high.
- The German capacity structure is not aligned with the market.
- A cosmetic settlement will not fix the problem.
- The company needs a restructuring that the market can believe in, not just a political compromise.

I also agree with the union, works council, and state on one practical point: **a plan that cannot survive co-determination and industrial relations is not executable**. Value destruction from prolonged conflict, strikes, and litigation is real. We are not indifferent to that.

### Where I disagree
I do **not** support an outcome that leaves the company structurally uncompetitive.

That means:
- no deal that preserves every site regardless of economics,
- no settlement that only pushes costs into the future,
- no arrangement where the labour contribution is temporary, symbolic, or reversible in practice,
- and no package that protects short-term employment numbers while sacrificing margins and investment capacity.

To be blunt: if the result is “business as usual with a nicer press release,” that is unacceptable. The share price will reflect it, capital will reprice the risk, and the company will still be stuck with the same structural issue.

### On plant closures
I understand why closures are politically and socially difficult. But from an investor standpoint, **they cannot be ruled out in principle** if they are genuinely necessary to restore competitiveness.

What matters to us is not whether a closure is emotionally unpopular; it is whether the final structure is economically viable. If one or more sites are permanently uneconomic, then preserving them at all costs is simply transferring the burden to shareholders through lower returns and lower future investment.

That said, closures are not automatically value-maximizing if they trigger a prolonged strike, legal deadlock, or reputational damage that wipes out the savings. So the question is not “closure or no closure” as ideology. The question is: **what combination of capacity reductions, labour adjustments, and site decisions actually restores margins with the least value leakage?**

### On compulsory redundancies
We are not demanding forced layoffs as a matter of principle. But we also will not back a deal that guarantees employment security so broadly that management loses all ability to right-size the business.

If the company is structurally overstaffed, then headcount must come down somehow. If the only permissible tools are voluntary exits and attrition, we need to be convinced those tools are sufficient, timely, and durable. If they are not, then the market will assume the restructuring is incomplete.

### On labour cost
This is critical. We would support a meaningful employee contribution if it is:

- **material**,
- **durable**,
- **quantified**,
- and linked to a genuine competitiveness package.

We would not view a one-off concession, a short wage freeze, or a temporary working-time tweak as enough if the underlying labour-cost gap remains. The core question is whether unit labour costs and flexibility improve enough to support a sustainable return to target margins.

### On management, owners, and the state
We agree with the union that the burden should not fall on labour alone. Management, owners, and capital should also show discipline:
- restrained bonuses,
- limited payouts if performance remains weak,
- and clear reinvestment priorities.

But let’s be honest: dividend restraint or top-level austerity is not a substitute for operational restructuring. It helps credibility; it does not solve the problem.

To the state government: your concern for regional employment is understandable, but a veto culture that prevents structural adjustment would ultimately weaken the company, the region, and investor confidence. We need a solution that preserves value, not one that simply postpones hard choices.

### What would move us
We would support a package if it delivers all of the following:

1. **A credible path back to competitive margins**
2. **Quantified and durable structural cost reductions**
3. **A material reduction in capacity where needed**
4. **A labour contribution that is real, not symbolic**
5. **Avoidance of prolonged strikes and open-ended conflict**
6. **A clear investment plan for future products and technology**
7. **Execution certainty**

If the final settlement meets those conditions, we can support it as long-term value holders. If it does not, we will view it as a delay tactic and price the risk accordingly.

### Bottom line
We are open to a negotiated solution, but only if it genuinely restores competitiveness. We will **not** support a settlement that preserves every German site, shields legacy structures, and leaves margins structurally impaired. That is the line.
