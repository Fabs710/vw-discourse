I will be direct.

## Where I agree
I agree with the repeated point that a cosmetic deal is worthless. If this restructuring does not restore competitiveness, margins, and investment capacity, then it will simply postpone a larger problem.

I also agree that:
- co-determination is a real governing constraint, not a formality;
- management, owners, and capital must contribute;
- voluntary exits, redeployment, retraining, working-time tools, and phased measures should be used first;
- and industrial peace matters because a prolonged conflict would destroy value.

On those points, we are aligned.

## Where I disagree
I do **not** accept the labour and state position that “all German plants remain open” and “no compulsory redundancies” can be treated as fixed preconditions.

That is not a restructuring solution. It is a demand to preserve the current footprint regardless of whether the economics support it.

I also do **not** accept the implication that closures or workforce adjustment are somehow illegitimate by definition. They are not desirable, but they are not taboo if the company truly needs a structural reset. As owners, we cannot sign up to a settlement that protects every existing structure while leaving the business overcosted and under-margined.

Equally, I do **not** accept management’s tendency to present closures and forced reductions as if they were the only serious route. That is too blunt and too risky in this governance setting. If there is a way to achieve the required savings through negotiated, reversible, and voluntary means, that is preferable and more value-preserving.

## The controlling shareholder’s position
Our position remains:

- We support a restructuring that **restores competitiveness and margins**.
- We will not support a settlement that leaves the company structurally uncompetitive.
- We will not support a prolonged, value-destroying conflict.
- We are open to a negotiated package that achieves **durable, quantified** savings with industrial peace.

That means we need to see:
1. a credible savings target,
2. a credible path to reach it,
3. burden-sharing from all sides,
4. and binding investment commitments in Germany.

## To management
You are right to insist that the package must be real, not symbolic. But you need to be more precise and more disciplined in what you ask for.

“Material, durable labour-cost contribution” is too vague on its own. Show:
- what exact competitiveness gap must be closed,
- what part comes from productivity,
- what part comes from capacity reduction,
- what part comes from labour cost,
- and what part comes from management/owner restraint.

If you want governance support, bring a quantified plan. Not just pressure, not just closure options kept alive as leverage. We need an executable package, not brinkmanship.

## To the works council and union
I hear your insistence on site and job security. I understand why you are drawing a hard line.

But you are asking us to commit to a level of protection that may remove the very flexibility needed to save the business. A blanket promise of no closures and no forced reductions is only acceptable if you can show — with numbers, not slogans — that the same structural savings can be achieved through other means.

So far, I have not seen that proof.

That said, I do **not** want to turn this into a binary threat exercise. If the workforce side can help produce:
- voluntary exits,
- working-time reductions,
- redeployment,
- qualification,
- phased capacity changes,
- and a meaningful temporary contribution,

then that is the kind of solution we can support. But it must be **durable enough** to matter and not just reverse in two years.

## To the state government
I respect your responsibility for the regional base. But you cannot simply elevate plant preservation above economic reality and then call that governance.

Your leverage is real, but so is the company’s need for a viable cost base. If you want to defend German industrial capacity, then support a package that actually keeps the company investable.

A preserved but declining footprint is not a victory. A competitive, investable German base is.

## What would move us
We would support a settlement if it contains all of the following:

1. **Quantified margin and cost improvement targets**
2. **A material, durable labour contribution**
3. **Meaningful workforce adjustment**
4. **Management and owner restraint**
5. **Binding investment in future products and technology in Germany**
6. **A path that is executable under co-determination and avoids prolonged conflict**

What would **not** move us:
- a purely political compromise,
- a blanket promise to preserve every site regardless of economics,
- temporary optics with no structural effect,
- or a deal that leaves the company unable to compete.

## Bottom line
I am not retreating from the requirement for structural improvement. That is non-negotiable from an ownership perspective.

But I am also not endorsing a closure-first approach if a negotiated, site-preserving package can achieve the same economic result. If such a package exists, bring it forward in numbers. If it does not, then the company must not be trapped by slogans about absolute site security.

So my position is simple:
- **Yes** to a real restructuring.
- **Yes** to burden-sharing.
- **Yes** to investment and industrial peace.
- **No** to cosmetic compromise.
- **No** to permanent value destruction.
- **No** to pretending that competitiveness can be restored without serious structural change.
