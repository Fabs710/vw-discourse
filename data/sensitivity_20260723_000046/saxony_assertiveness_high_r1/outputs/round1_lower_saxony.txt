I will be equally direct.

The Regional State Government is not here to defend the status quo at any price, but we are also not here to sign off on a restructuring that destroys the company’s regional base, weakens co-determination, and treats plant closures as the default solution.

### Where I agree
I agree with management on one point: the company does need a **credible, durable restructuring**. A cosmetic package will not solve the competitive gap, and nobody benefits if the company keeps drifting.

I also agree that we should not rule out every operational adjustment in advance. **Working-time measures, qualification, redeployment, voluntary exits, and capacity adjustments** can all be part of a serious settlement.

And I agree with the union and works council that any solution must be **negotiated**, not imposed. In this company, that is not just a political preference — it is a governance reality.

### Where I disagree sharply
I do **not** accept management’s framing that plant closures are simply the necessary starting point. That may be your preferred route to the fastest cost reduction, but from the standpoint of the region, this is not a neutral technical choice. A closure is a lasting industrial and social rupture. It is also a political line I will not cross lightly, especially where there are alternatives that preserve sites and jobs.

I also do **not** accept the implication that workers should carry the main burden while management treats structural downsizing as unavoidable. If there is to be sacrifice, then it must be **balanced**:
- from owners,
- from management,
- from capital,
- and from the workforce,
with each side contributing in proportion to its role and capacity.

To the union and works council: I support your insistence that **no compulsory redundancies and no plant closures** are the right baseline for discussion. But I will be candid: a blanket refusal to discuss any meaningful productivity or cost adjustment will not get us to a settlement either. If the company’s economics are genuinely under pressure, then we need a package that protects the core sites **and** restores competitiveness.

### What I will support
A package is conceivable if it contains all of the following:
1. **Binding guarantees for German sites and regional employment**
2. **No compulsory redundancies**
3. **Preference for voluntary, internally managed workforce adjustment**
4. **A negotiated employee contribution that is temporary, fair, and linked to clear safeguards**
5. **Tangible commitments from management and owners** — including restraint on payouts at the top where appropriate
6. **Real investment commitments** in future products and technology at the German sites
7. **Preservation of the state’s co-determination and veto role** in any restructuring that affects plants

That is the minimum basis for a legitimate settlement.

### What I will not accept
I will not accept:
- closures presented as the only serious option,
- mass layoffs as a blunt instrument,
- or a restructuring that weakens the regional industrial base while preserving decision-making power at the top and shifting the damage onto the workforce and the state.

If management believes it has a case for deeper restructuring, then it must prove that **site-preserving alternatives are insufficient**, not merely less convenient. And if it wants public and political support, it must show that it is not asking the region to absorb the damage while the company’s future value is protected elsewhere.

So my message is simple:
- We are open to restructuring.
- We are not open to industrial dismantling.
- We are prepared to broker a socially responsible compromise.
- But any solution must protect jobs, preserve German sites, and maintain the state’s legitimate co-determination role.

If management is prepared to move on that basis, then we can talk seriously. If not, then it should expect resistance — including at board level.
