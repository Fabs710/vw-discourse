## Expert assessment: can a no-closure, no-forced-layoff package realistically close the competitiveness gap?

### Bottom line
**Yes, in principle—but only if the gap is moderate and the package includes a large, quantified, multi-year cost reset.**  
**No, if management’s required improvement is a deep structural reset requiring permanent, immediate capacity removal beyond what voluntary internal measures can deliver.**

Without the company’s actual figures, I cannot confirm which case applies. But economically, a **no-closure / no-forced-layoff** solution is only plausible if it can generate savings on the order of **several hundred million to low single-digit billions of euros annually**, using a combination of:
- permanent labor-cost concessions,
- voluntary headcount reduction,
- working-time and shift redesign,
- procurement and overhead reduction,
- productivity improvement,
- and deferred or rephased capex.

If the required gap is materially larger than that, or if it is mainly a **capacity overhang** problem, then a no-closure package becomes much less plausible.

## 2) Economic logic: what can and cannot be done without closures

A no-closure, no-forced-layoff package can reduce cost through four main channels:

### A. Labor cost per vehicle
Potential levers:
- wage moderation or temporary wage concession,
- removal or suspension of allowances/benefits,
- longer working hours without proportional wage increase,
- shift premiums redesign,
- temporary employee contribution,
- lower variable pay.

**Typical magnitude:**  
- Temporary concessions: useful, but often only **low hundreds of millions annually** at large scale.
- More durable labor-cost reset: can be larger, but only if employees accept substantial multi-year changes.

### B. Headcount reduction without forced layoffs
Potential levers:
- voluntary exits,
- early retirement,
- natural attrition,
- hiring freeze,
- internal redeployment,
- nonreplacement of vacancies,
- temporary agency labor reduction.

**Typical magnitude:**  
This can be meaningful, but it is **slow**.  
In a large industrial workforce, voluntary/attrition-based reduction can usually deliver perhaps **1–3% of headcount per year** without coercion if designed well. More is possible, but costly and not always reliable.

### C. Capacity and operating model changes without closures
Potential levers:
- shift reduction,
- line balancing,
- longer cycles,
- product mix changes,
- temporary idling,
- insourcing/outsourcing mix,
- platform consolidation inside existing plants,
- lower overtime and premium hours.

**Typical magnitude:**  
This can reduce fixed cost and overhead absorption, but it rarely replaces the economics of closing a structurally redundant site.

### D. Non-labor savings
Potential levers:
- procurement,
- logistics,
- indirect overhead,
- SG&A reduction,
- capex deferral,
- administrative simplification,
- digital productivity.

**Typical magnitude:**  
These can contribute significantly, but they usually do **not** fully offset a high-cost manufacturing footprint.

## 3) Rough savings scenarios

Because actual figures are missing, I’ll give bounded estimates using plausible industrial ranges.

### Scenario 1: Mild competitiveness gap
If the company needs roughly **€300m–€700m annual improvement**, then a no-closure, no-forced-layoff package is **very plausible**.

Possible mix:
- labor concessions: €100m–€250m
- voluntary exits / attrition savings: €100m–€200m
- overhead/procurement/logistics: €100m–€250m
- productivity and shift redesign: €50m–€150m

This kind of gap can usually be closed without plant closures if the organization is disciplined.

### Scenario 2: Moderate gap
If the required improvement is roughly **€700m–€1.5bn annually**, a no-closure package is **possible but difficult**.

This would likely require:
- a **multi-year labor contribution**,
- sizable voluntary exit volumes,
- aggressive overhead reduction,
- deeper capacity utilization measures,
- and possibly some permanent cost concessions.

This is the range where negotiations become fragile: it may work if the workforce accepts a real structural contribution, not just a short-term freeze.

### Scenario 3: Large structural gap
If the company needs **€1.5bn–€3bn+ annual improvement**, then a no-closure, no-forced-layoff package is **unlikely to be sufficient** unless there are huge offsetting gains elsewhere.

Why?
- Voluntary exits alone are too slow and too expensive.
- Productivity gains of that magnitude are hard to sustain.
- Labor concessions have limits.
- Non-labor savings rarely bridge such a gap in a mature auto manufacturer.

In that case, management’s closure argument becomes economically more credible.

## 4) The key question: is the problem fixed-cost overhang or variable-cost excess?

This is the central test.

### If the issue is mainly variable cost
Then a labor deal can help a lot:
- lower unit labor cost,
- higher flexibility,
- shift redesign,
- overtime discipline,
- internal redeployment.

### If the issue is mainly fixed-cost overhang / capacity duplication
Then a no-closure package is much weaker:
- fixed costs remain embedded,
- underutilized plants stay expensive,
- margin recovery is limited by volume absorption,
- savings are often temporary or accounting-based.

In plain terms:  
**You can negotiate labor cost down more easily than you can make a structurally redundant plant disappear without closing it.**

---

## 5) What a credible no-closure package would need to include

If the goal is truly “no closures, no forced layoffs,” then to be credible it should likely include all of the following:

### Labor-side measures
- 2–5 year wage restraint or contribution
- suspension or reduction of bonuses/variable pay
- reduction in premiums and allowances
- longer working-time flexibility
- temporary reduction in paid hours or conversion to working-time accounts
- redeployment across plants
- voluntary exits and early retirement

### Capacity-side measures
- shift reduction at selected lines
- elimination of idle capacity through product reallocation
- platform consolidation within existing sites
- nonreplacement of vacancies
- limits on external hiring
- temporary production balancing among plants

### Management/owner contribution
- bonus restraint at top levels
- dividend/payout discipline if financially defensible
- capex prioritization
- fixed-cost reduction in corporate overhead
- stronger product and investment commitment to Germany

### Governance protections
- binding review milestones
- triggers if savings are missed
- monitoring by a joint committee
- quantified metrics, not vague promises

Without quantified triggers, a no-closure package often becomes a delay mechanism rather than a restructuring.

---

## 6) Why “no forced layoffs” is not automatically incompatible with restructuring
Economically, **no forced layoffs** is feasible if:
- voluntary exits are generously incentivized,
- early retirement channels are available,
- internal mobility is real,
- and the headcount reduction target is modest enough.

It becomes difficult if:
- the required headcount reduction is large,
- the workforce is older but not retirement-ready,
- many skills are site-specific,
- or the company needs rapid savings.

So the feasibility depends heavily on:
- age profile,
- skill transferability,
- pension obligations,
- and the time allowed.

---

## 7) The main financial risk of a no-closure compromise
The biggest risk is **insufficient durability**.

A package that relies on:
- one-time labor concessions,
- voluntary exits that plateau after 12–24 months,
- and temporary accounting savings

may improve cash flow briefly, but not necessarily fix the underlying competitiveness gap.

If management’s target is to restore a **durable return on sales improvement**, then the package must create recurring savings, not just delay losses.
