I will respond directly.

## Where I agree
I agree with the expert and with several other parties that:

- a cosmetic compromise is worthless,
- the package must be durable and executable,
- voluntary instruments should come first,
- management, owners, and capital must contribute,
- and the solution must survive co-determination and industrial peace reality.

I also agree that we should stop talking in slogans and test actual packages against actual numbers.

## Where I disagree
I do **not** accept the repeated attempt to make **plant closures** and **compulsory redundancies** the default benchmark for a “serious” restructuring.

That is not a neutral economic truth. It is a management choice, and one that has not been shown to be unavoidable under the conditions that matter here: regional employment, social partnership, and the state’s co-determination role.

The expert’s assessment was useful, but it does **not** give management a blank check. It says a no-closure, no-forced-layoff package is plausible if the gap is moderate and the measures are durable. That is exactly the point: you have not yet shown that the gap is so extreme that every site-preserving option must be discarded.

## My position on the two-package test

### 1) No-closure / no-forced-layoff package
This is the only package I can support as a starting point.

It must include:
- all German plants safeguarded,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- no operational dismissals,
- a temporary and quantified employee contribution,
- visible management and owner restraint,
- binding investment in Germany,
- and internal, reversible capacity adjustment.

If that package can close the gap, then it is the responsible path.

### 2) Structural-adjustment package
If this means plant closures or forced layoffs, then it is **not acceptable** from my side.

Not because I refuse to face reality, but because the burden of proof for destroying regional jobs has not been met. If the company can be restructured without that damage, then it must be.

## Where I stand on the arguments from others

### To management
You keep saying the footprint must shrink and the cost base must come down durably. I am not denying the need for cost reduction.

What I am denying is that closures and forced layoffs are the only serious route. You have still not put forward a plant-by-plant, line-by-line case showing why internal reconfiguration, voluntary exits, and working-time measures cannot do the job first.

So far, I hear pressure. I do not yet see proof.

### To the works council and union
I agree with your core red lines:
- no plant closures as a starting point,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- no weakening of co-determination,
- and no hidden layoff program through pressure or attrition.

I support your insistence that any employee contribution must be temporary, limited, and tied to binding guarantees.

But I also want to be clear: those guarantees must be matched by a credible savings package, not just a political statement. If the numbers do not work, we need to know that honestly.

### To the controlling family holding
I understand your insistence on competitiveness and durability. But you should not act as though “structural change” automatically means closures. It does not.

A site-preserving restructuring can be serious if it is quantified, disciplined, and durable. If you want the workforce and the state to accept contributions, then stop treating plant closures as the natural reference case.

### To institutional investors
You are right to demand quantified improvement and execution certainty. I support that.

Where I do not agree is your assumption that preserving all German sites is unserious unless disproven first. The burden is not on labour and the state to prove that every site must stay open. The burden is on management and capital to prove that closures are unavoidable.

## What moves me
What would move the state shareholder is a package that shows, concretely:

1. how the savings are achieved **without closures**,
2. how workforce reduction, if needed, happens **without compulsory redundancies**,
3. how management and owners share the burden visibly,
4. and how binding investment in Germany secures the future of the company.

That is the test.

## What I will not accept
I will not support:
- plant closures in the state as the default solution,
- compulsory redundancies,
- or any settlement that removes or weakens the state’s co-determination and veto role.

Those are not negotiable for me.

## My practical position
I am prepared to broker and defend a serious restructuring if it contains:

- all German plants safeguarded,
- no forced layoffs,
- temporary employee contribution only,
- management/owner restraint,
- binding investment commitments,
- internal capacity adjustment,
- transparent figures and joint monitoring,
- and a genuine role for co-determination in implementation.

If that package is real, I will work for it.

If the response is still that closures must remain the baseline threat, then I will use the leverage the statute gives me. Because protecting regional jobs, the industrial base, and the state’s role in governance is not a side issue. It is my mandate.
