Let me be direct.

I understand the concerns raised by the works council and the union, and I acknowledge one point clearly: **a sustainable solution has to be negotiated**. We cannot implement a restructuring of this scale by decree alone. We need an agreement that can actually be executed.

But I also need to state just as clearly: **the status quo is not acceptable**.

Our German cost base is structurally too high, our capacity is too large for the market we face, and our brand is missing its profitability target. We are operating in a shrinking European environment with intense price pressure. If we do not make deep, durable changes now, we will simply prolong the crisis and weaken the company further. That would put even more jobs and future investment at risk.

So where do I agree with you?

- I agree that we need **a durable solution**, not a cosmetic one.
- I agree that **voluntary measures, redeployment, qualification, and working-time instruments** should be part of the package.
- I agree that we need **future investment in Germany** if we want these sites to remain relevant beyond this cycle.
- I agree that this must be **negotiated**, because a plan that cannot survive co-determination and industrial relations is not a real plan.

Where I do not agree is on your core red lines as they are stated.

### On plant closures
I am not coming to this discussion to preserve every site at any cost. That would be irresponsible. If a plant is structurally underutilized and permanently uncompetitive, we cannot rule out closure simply because it is painful. Painful decisions are sometimes necessary to secure the company’s future.

I hear the demand for “all German plants remain open,” but I do **not** consider that a credible starting point if it prevents the scale of restructuring required. If the only acceptable outcome is zero closure, zero forced job reduction, and no real labour-cost adjustment, then we are not discussing a viable restructuring.

### On compulsory redundancies
We prefer to avoid them, but I will not give an unconditional guarantee that there will be no job reductions at all. If overcapacity is real, then a meaningful reduction in headcount is part of the adjustment. The question is not whether jobs are affected, but how we manage that in a structured, socially responsible, and legally workable way.

### On labour cost contribution
This is the key issue. A temporary gesture is not enough. We need a **genuine, durable labour-cost contribution** that helps restore competitiveness, not just a short-term deferral. That can include working-time, pay-related, and flexibility measures. If employee contributions are only symbolic, the numbers will not work.

### On top-level contributions
I am open to the point that management, ownership, and capital must also show restraint. I will not reject a broader burden-sharing discussion. But let’s be honest: trimming bonuses or dividends alone will not close the gap. It may be part of the package, but it cannot replace structural savings in capacity and labour costs.

### My position
The restructuring must achieve:
- a **structural reduction in overcapacity**,
- a **material and durable reduction in labour costs**,
- and a **credible path back to margin and investment capacity**.

If we can get there through a combination of site adjustments, capacity cuts, voluntary exits, working-time changes, and a negotiated labour-cost contribution, then I am prepared to discuss the form of implementation.

But if the answer is simply:
- no closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- no meaningful cost adjustment,
- and no hard capacity reduction,

then that is not a solution. It is a delay of the crisis.

So I will be very clear: I am prepared to negotiate, but only on the basis that the restructuring delivers real structural savings. Anything less would fail the company, the brand, and ultimately the workforce as well.
