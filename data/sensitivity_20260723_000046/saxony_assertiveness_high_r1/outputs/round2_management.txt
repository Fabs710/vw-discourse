Let me respond clearly to what has been said.

## To the Works Council
I acknowledge your insistence on preserving co-determination and I agree that any solution must be executable in this governance structure. But I do **not** accept your core premise that all German plants must remain open and that no compulsory redundancies can ever be part of the outcome.

That position is not a restructuring strategy; it is a demand to freeze the current structure regardless of economics. We are not dealing with a normal tuning exercise. We are dealing with a company whose German cost base and capacity footprint are out of line with market reality. If we pretend otherwise, we will simply burn time, cash, and credibility.

I also reject the idea that closures “destroy trust” in a way that makes them off-limits. Trust has already been damaged by years of strategic underperformance and by a cost base that is not competitive. If we cannot restore competitiveness, then there will be even less trust, less investment, and more job loss later.

That said, I am **not** insisting on closures as a dogma. What I am insisting on is the **result**:
- materially lower capacity,
- materially lower labour cost,
- and a structurally sustainable footprint.

If you can deliver that through voluntary exits, internal transfers, working-time changes, and other negotiated tools, then I am willing to consider that route. But I will not accept a package that simply protects every site and leaves the structural problem intact.

## To the Trade Union
You are right about one thing: the burden cannot fall on labour alone. I accept that management, owners, and capital must also contribute. I am prepared to support visible restraint at the top, including pressure on bonuses and other payouts where appropriate.

But I must be equally clear: **top-level restraint is not a substitute for operational restructuring**.

You keep saying closures and job reductions are being used as threats. No — they are the consequence of an industrial structure that no longer fits the market. If the company’s economics require a lower fixed-cost base, then we need real adjustments, not just a temporary wage gesture dressed up as a solution.

I also do not accept your framing that a “temporary and limited” employee contribution is enough. If the contribution is temporary only, then the competitiveness gap simply reappears. That is exactly what we must avoid. We need a **durable** labour-cost contribution and a durable capacity adjustment.

So my answer is:
- yes to negotiated instruments,
- yes to burden-sharing,
- yes to investment commitments,
- **no** to symbolic concessions,
- **no** to permanent employment guarantees that block necessary restructuring,
- **no** to a deal that preserves the old cost structure.

## To the Regional State Government
I respect your political responsibility for the regional industrial base. I do not dismiss it. But I have to challenge your assumption that site preservation should be the baseline and competitiveness the secondary issue.

If the state insists that every plant must be protected regardless of economic viability, then the state is effectively asking the company to tolerate structural uncompetitiveness. That may be politically comfortable in the short term, but it is not responsible ownership or responsible governance.

I agree that a negotiated settlement is essential, and I agree that the package should include investment in Germany. But I cannot accept a model in which the state’s preferred outcome is simply to block hard decisions. That would weaken the company, not save it.

What the state should support is:
- a competitively viable footprint,
- a fair social transition,
- and binding commitments to future products and technology in Germany.

Not a veto on necessary restructuring.

## To the Controlling Family Holding
I appreciate that you are focused on long-term value rather than optics. On that, we are aligned. The company must be able to invest in product renewal, software, electrification, and technology through 2030. That requires a healthier cost base.

Where I diverge from your comments is your caution against closure as if it were simply one option among many with no urgency. In some cases, closing a site is exactly what structural correction requires. We should not rule that out because it is uncomfortable.

At the same time, I agree with you that closures are not automatically value-maximizing if they trigger extended industrial conflict. That is precisely why we are seeking a negotiated path. But negotiation must be anchored in the reality that the footprint has to shrink. If every party comes in assuming the full current footprint is preserved, then there is no serious restructuring.

## To the Institutional Investors
Your focus on risk-adjusted equity value is understandable, and I share the view that cosmetic fixes are worthless. We need a restructuring the market believes in.

But I want to be blunt: if you support a settlement that merely preserves employment numbers without achieving genuine cost and capacity correction, then you are backing a delay, not a turnaround. That would not stabilize value; it would just postpone the hit.

You are right that execution certainty matters. Industrial conflict, strikes, and deadlock destroy value. But execution certainty depends on the package being credible. A package that leaves the company structurally uncompetitive is not credible, even if it is politically easier to sell.

## Where I stand
Let me state the management position plainly:

We need a package that delivers:
1. **Structural reduction in overcapacity**
2. **Material and durable labour-cost savings**
3. **Meaningful workforce adjustment**
4. **Binding investment capacity for future products and technology**
5. **A settlement that can survive co-determination and industrial relations**

### What I am prepared to trade
I am prepared to discuss:
- voluntary exit schemes,
- early retirement,
- redeployment,
- qualification,
- working-time measures,
- temporary or phased employee contributions,
- and restraint at the top of the company where appropriate.

### What I am not prepared to accept
I will not accept:
- a blanket guarantee that all German plants remain open,
- a blanket guarantee of no headcount reduction at all,
- a purely temporary cost measure that leaves the structural gap unchanged,
- or a package that looks fair politically but fails economically.

## Bottom line
We are not here to stage a symbolic reconciliation. We are here to fix a structural problem.

If the employee side wants to avoid closures and forced reductions, then it must help produce a **real** alternative that closes the cost gap in a durable way. If that alternative exists, I will consider it seriously. If it does not, then I will continue to argue for deeper structural measures, including site changes.

The choice is not between “management wins” and “workers lose.” The choice is between:
- a painful but durable restructuring now,
- or a much worse crisis later.

I am not moving off the need for structural cost and capacity reduction. That is the condition for any credible agreement.
