We agree with management and the family holding on the **diagnosis**: this company cannot preserve shareholder value with an uncompetitive cost base, structurally excessive capacity, and margins that remain below target. A cosmetic settlement would be value-destructive.

We also agree with labour and the state on one practical point: **a deal that cannot survive co-determination and industrial relations is not executable**. A prolonged strike, legal deadlock, or governance stalemate would destroy the very value that restructuring is meant to restore.

But we need to be very clear about the **non-negotiable end state** from our perspective:

## What we support
We would support a package that delivers:
- **material, durable, quantified cost reduction**
- **credible capacity reduction**
- **a clear path back to competitive margins**
- **execution certainty**
- **binding investment in future products and technology**
- **burden-sharing across labour, management, and capital**

That is the only basis on which we can back the restructuring as long-term holders.

## Where we agree with labour
We agree that:
- management, owners, and capital should also contribute;
- bonuses, payouts, and top-level compensation should be restrained if performance stays weak;
- voluntary exits, redeployment, retraining, and working-time tools should be used first where feasible;
- a negotiated solution is better than a destructive confrontation.

We also agree that employment destruction for its own sake would be poor value creation. If closures or layoffs trigger prolonged conflict that wipes out the savings, then they are not good outcomes.

## Where we disagree sharply
We do **not** accept a deal that preserves every German site regardless of economics.

A blanket “all plants open” position is not financially credible unless it is paired with another mechanism that fully closes the competitiveness gap. So far, we have not heard such a mechanism. Without real structural change, this becomes a delayed loss, not a restructuring.

We also do **not** accept a solution built on:
- temporary gestures only,
- symbolic wage restraint,
- reversible measures that do not materially change the cost base,
- or assurances that preserve headcount while leaving margins structurally impaired.

That would simply defer the problem and likely force a worse correction later.

## On plant closures and headcount
We are not ideologically attached to plant closures or forced redundancies. They are tools, not goals.

But we are equally clear that **some combination of capacity reduction and workforce adjustment must happen** if the economics require it. If voluntary exits, internal transfers, and working-time tools are sufficient, excellent. If they are not sufficient, then management cannot be forced into a fake solution that keeps all the costs and none of the savings.

From an investor standpoint, the question is not “can every site be preserved?” The question is: **what package actually restores sustainable margins without destroying more value through conflict than it saves through restructuring?**

## On labour’s demand for guarantees
We cannot support unconditional guarantees that permanently remove management flexibility. A promise of no compulsory redundancies and no closures, without clear evidence that the required savings can be achieved otherwise, is too risky.

However, we would support:
- preference for voluntary instruments,
- meaningful protection against unnecessary layoffs,
- and a time-limited settlement with clear review points.

If labour wants stronger site security, that must be matched by **hard, quantified, durable cost contributions** and not just promises.

## On management and ownership
We agree with labour that the burden cannot fall on workers alone. There should be visible restraint from management and owners:
- bonus discipline,
- payout restraint if performance weakens,
- and a clear reinvestment priority over short-term extraction.

But we need to be frank: **restraint at the top is not a substitute for structural restructuring**. It helps legitimacy and alignment, but it will not solve the cost problem by itself.

## Our position on the state
We respect the state’s regional responsibility. But a veto culture that makes structural correction impossible would ultimately harm the company, the region, and shareholder value. The state should be pressing for a **credible, negotiated, economically defensible settlement**, not for preserving the current footprint as a principle.

## What would move us
A package would need to include:
1. **Quantified margin improvement targets**
2. **Structural cost reduction, not temporary optics**
3. **Capacity reduction aligned with demand**
4. **A real labour contribution**
5. **Management/owner restraint**
6. **Investment commitments in future products and technologies**
7. **Execution certainty with limited risk of prolonged conflict**

## What would not move us
We will not support:
- a cosmetic compromise,
- a settlement that preserves every site and every guarantee while leaving the company overcosted,
- or a deal that substitutes political comfort for economic reality.

So our message is simple: **we are open to compromise, but only if it genuinely restores competitiveness**. If the final package does not materially improve margins and cost discipline, then it is not a compromise we can support — it is value erosion with better PR.
