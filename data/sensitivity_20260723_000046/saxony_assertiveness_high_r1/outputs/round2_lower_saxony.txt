Let me be equally direct from the state shareholder’s perspective.

I recognize the pressure management is under. I also recognize that a purely cosmetic solution would be irresponsible. But I do **not** accept the idea that the only serious way to restore competitiveness is by threatening closures and forcing the workforce to absorb the main burden.

That is not a neutral technical judgment; it is a choice with major regional and political consequences. And for us, the consequences are decisive.

## To management
I agree with you on one point: the company needs a **durable restructuring**. But I disagree with the way you are framing the alternatives.

You say a structural reduction in overcapacity is required, and that closures must remain on the table. I say: prove that you can’t achieve the necessary savings through a **site-preserving package** first.

You have not yet shown that closures are unavoidable. What you have shown is that closures are the quickest route to a cleaner cost picture. That is not the same thing as the best long-term solution for the company, the region, or the governance system.

I also reject the idea that “meaningful workforce adjustment” has to mean headcount cuts through dismissal or site shutdown. If there is overcapacity, then use the instruments that preserve industrial capability:
- shift and working-time adjustments,
- temporary capacity reductions,
- internal transfers,
- qualification and redeployment,
- voluntary exits and phased retirement.

If those tools are not enough, then we should examine why the restructuring case has not been made convincingly enough. The burden of proof is on those proposing lasting damage to sites and jobs.

## To the works council and union
I agree with you on the core red line: **no compulsory redundancies, no plant closures as the starting point, and no stripping of co-determination**.

That is not just a political slogan. It is a legitimate governance line in this company, and I will defend it.

But I also want to be clear: if we want to preserve sites and jobs, then we must present a package that is economically credible. A blanket refusal to discuss any durable cost contribution from labour would not help the negotiation. So I support your willingness to discuss:
- temporary employee contributions,
- working-time flexibility,
- productivity measures,
- voluntary exits,
- and internal adjustment.

What I will **not** support is a model where workers are asked to pay indefinitely while the company refuses to make equivalent contributions at the top or commit to investment in Germany.

## To the family holding and investors
I understand the insistence on long-term value. That concern is legitimate. But long-term value is not only about cost cutting. It is also about preserving industrial capability, social stability, and the company’s embeddedness in its home region.

A restructuring that tears up trust, triggers prolonged conflict, and weakens the company’s social legitimacy will damage value too. So I do not accept the premise that closures and hard layoffs are simply “value discipline.”

If the final package preserves the regional base, protects the company’s manufacturing footprint, and restores competitiveness through negotiated measures, that is also value-preserving. In fact, in this governance setting, that is the more durable form of value preservation.

## What I can support
A settlement is conceivable if it contains all of the following:

1. **Binding guarantees for all German plants**
2. **No compulsory redundancies**
3. **A negotiated, time-limited employee contribution**
4. **Visible restraint from management and owners**, including bonuses and payouts
5. **Real investment commitments** in future products and technologies in Germany
6. **Capacity reductions through reversible or internal measures**, not closures
7. **A genuine co-determination role** in shaping implementation

That is the minimum basis for my support.

## What I cannot accept
I will not support:
- a closure-first strategy,
- a package that weakens the state’s co-determination and veto role,
- a restructuring that shifts the pain almost entirely onto labour,
- or any “deal” that preserves financial flexibility at the cost of regional industrial destruction.

If management wants my support, it must come back with a concrete alternative that keeps sites open and protects the company’s regional anchor function while still improving competitiveness. Otherwise, I will use the leverage that the statute gives us.

## Bottom line
I am prepared to help broker a real compromise. But that compromise must be built on:
- **site security**,
- **job security**,
- **shared sacrifice**,
- and **binding investment**.

Not on plant closures as a default, and not on asking the region to pay for a restructuring that others design from a distance.

If management wants to discuss a serious package on that basis, I am ready. If not, then it should be clear that I will not be sidelined, and I will not accept an outcome that guts the state’s industrial base.
