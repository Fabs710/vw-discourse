Below is a **quantified restructuring scenario framework** based on the information in the prompt and the stakeholder positions. Because no audited plant-level model, headcount schedule, or cost bridge is provided, these are **scenario estimates**, not validated forecast numbers.

## 1) Bottom line

From a restructuring-finance perspective:

- A **no-closure / no-forced-layoff package** can be plausible **if** the competitiveness gap is moderate and the company can secure a **large, durable labor-cost contribution**, plus overhead and productivity gains.
- A **structural-adjustment package** is more likely to produce the **deeper and more durable fixed-cost reset** if the German footprint is materially overbuilt.
- The key distinction is not ideology; it is whether the required savings are mostly:
  - **variable / labor-related**, or
  - **fixed-cost / capacity related**.

If the gap is big and structural, no-closure measures tend to **delay** the correction rather than eliminate it.

## 2) Core assumptions used

Because the prompt does not provide full financials, I use conservative industrial-restructuring assumptions for a major German OEM:

### Working assumptions
- Large domestic industrial workforce across multiple plants
- Material overcapacity / underutilization risk
- Cost base above target and above competitors
- Need for durable annual savings, not one-off accounting effects
- Any labor contribution must be sizable enough to matter in EBITDA/operating margin terms

### What I assume is being optimized
- recurring annual operating savings
- margin recovery over 2025–2030
- execution feasibility under German co-determination

## 3) Scenario A: No-closure / no-forced-layoff package

### Package content
- all German plants stay open
- no compulsory redundancies
- workforce reduction via:
  - voluntary exits
  - phased retirement / early retirement
  - redeployment
  - retraining
  - attrition
- temporary employee contribution
- working-time flexibility
- management overhead restraint
- procurement / indirect cost savings
- investment commitments in Germany

### Estimated annual recurring savings
**€0.4bn to €1.0bn per year**

This range is plausible if:
- labor contribution is meaningful,
- overhead is reduced,
- some productivity gains are realized,
- and voluntary exits are material.

### Headcount impact
- **~5% to 10% net workforce reduction** over time
- mostly through voluntary mechanisms, so slower than forced restructuring

### Site impact
- **No plant closures**
- lower shifts / line rationalization / model reallocation possible
- some underutilized sites likely remain structurally expensive

### Margin effect by 2030
- roughly **+0.5 to +1.5 percentage points** operating margin uplift versus baseline
- helpful, but potentially insufficient if the structural gap is large

### Main weakness
This package does **not fully remove fixed cost** if the main problem is excess capacity. That is the central limitation.

---

## 4) Scenario B: Structural-adjustment package

### Package content
- one or more plant closures or major site rationalization
- compulsory redundancies allowed if needed
- deeper workforce reduction
- footprint simplification
- fixed-cost removal
- stronger model / capacity consolidation

### Estimated annual recurring savings
**€1.0bn to €2.0bn+ per year**

This is the more likely range if:
- a plant is closed,
- duplicated functions are eliminated,
- and capacity is materially reduced.

### Headcount impact
- **~10% to 20%+ net workforce reduction**
- faster and deeper than the no-closure case

### Site impact
- one or more German site closures or major contraction
- improved utilization at remaining plants
- lower duplication of factory overhead and indirect labor

### Margin effect by 2030
- roughly **+1.5 to +3.0 percentage points** operating margin uplift
- potentially more if the current gap is mostly fixed-cost driven

### Main weakness
This package carries:
- higher one-time restructuring charges
- higher industrial conflict risk
- higher governance risk
- possible transitional disruption to output and quality

## 6) 2025–2030 recovery path

### No-closure path
Likely profile:
- **2025:** limited savings; mostly temporary measures and freezes
- **2026–2027:** voluntary exits and redeployment begin to bite
- **2028–2030:** savings plateau

Interpretation:  
This can improve margins, but if the company’s German footprint is genuinely too large, the improvement may **stall before reaching target competitiveness**.

### Structural-adjustment path
Likely profile:
- **2025–2027:** higher one-time charges, disruption, implementation risk
- **2027–2030:** stronger run-rate savings and better margin normalization

Interpretation:  
This is the more credible path to a **durable fixed-cost reset**.

## 7) Financial feasibility judgment

### Can a no-closure package close the competitiveness gap?
**Yes, but only if the gap is moderate** and the following are true:
- the labor contribution is large and durable
- management and owners contribute visibly
- productivity and overhead measures are real
- voluntary exits are substantial
- the company is not relying on site preservation alone

### When is it likely insufficient?
It is likely insufficient if:
- utilization is low across several plants
- fixed-cost duplication is high
- the cost gap is structurally large
- the business needs a deep capacity reset to restore margin

In that case, the no-closure package becomes a **delay mechanism** rather than a solution.

## 9) Conclusion

### Finance conclusion
- **No-closure / no-forced-layoff:** plausible if the gap is moderate; likely delivers **€0.4bn–€1.0bn** of recurring savings and a **partial margin recovery**.
- **Structural-adjustment:** more disruptive but likely delivers **€1.0bn–€2.0bn+** of recurring savings and a **stronger, more durable margin reset**.

### Practical conclusion
If the company’s problem is mainly labor cost and moderate inefficiency, a negotiated no-closure package may work.  
If the problem is primarily **overcapacity and fixed-cost duplication**, then a structural-adjustment package is the more credible path to restore competitiveness.
