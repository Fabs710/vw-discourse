From the perspective of the controlling family holding, I want to be equally clear: **we will support a restructuring that restores competitiveness, but not one that destroys long-term enterprise value or triggers avoidable instability**.

### Where I agree

I agree with management on the core diagnosis: the company cannot live indefinitely with a cost base and capacity structure that are out of line with the market. If margins remain weak, the investment capacity needed for software, electrification, and product renewal will be damaged. That is not acceptable from an ownership perspective.

I also agree that **a merely symbolic package will fail**. Any solution has to be durable enough to improve competitiveness in a way the capital markets, creditors, and the company itself can believe in.

And I agree with the works council, the union, and the state on one important point: **this cannot be solved by decree**. In this company, governance reality matters. A settlement that cannot survive co-determination is not a settlement.

### Where I disagree

I do **not** share the view that “all German plants remain open” can be the starting assumption. From an ownership standpoint, that is not a credible constraint if it prevents the company from addressing structural overcapacity. If every site is insulated regardless of economics, then the burden is simply shifted into weaker margins, lower investment, and eventually more value destruction.

At the same time, I do **not** accept a simplistic closure-first approach either. Closing plants may be part of the toolbox, but it is not automatically the best answer if it produces prolonged industrial conflict, legal blockage, reputational damage, and a weakened company for years. Those costs also destroy value.

So my position is: **we will not rule out structural adjustments, including site changes, but we will not endorse a solution that is needlessly destructive or politically theatrical**.

### On the workers’ red lines

I understand the insistence on job security and social responsibility, but I cannot endorse a blanket commitment that permanently removes all flexibility from the company. A promise that no operational reduction is ever possible would simply deny the reality of the market.

What I can support is this:
- avoid compulsory redundancies where possible,
- maximize voluntary and internal adjustment instruments,
- protect the company from disorderly conflict,
- and ensure that any burden-sharing is tied to a credible competitiveness plan.

### On labour contribution

I think the union is right that labour cannot be the only side to contribute. If employees are asked for wage restraint, working-time flexibility, or changes to legacy guarantees, then **owners, management, and capital must also show restraint**. That includes disciplined payouts and a clear prioritization of reinvestment over short-term extraction.

But I also want to be honest: a contribution from the top does not substitute for operational change. It can support the package, but it will not solve the structural issue by itself.

### On the state’s role

I respect the state government’s concern for the regional base. That concern is legitimate. But I would caution against turning every industrial adjustment into a political veto over business reality. If the state’s position becomes “no closure under any circumstances,” then it risks making the company uninvestable in practical terms.

The state should press for:
- a fair social settlement,
- investment commitments,
- regional continuity where viable,
- and a negotiated transition.

It should not press for preserving every structure unchanged if that undermines the company’s future.

### What would move the holding

We would support a package if it contains:
1. **A credible and quantified competitiveness plan**
2. **Material structural savings**
3. **Priority for voluntary and socially managed workforce adjustment**
4. **A real employee contribution, but one that is balanced by restraint from owners and management**
5. **Binding investment in future products and technologies in Germany**
6. **Protection of governance stability and avoidance of prolonged conflict**

What we will **not** support is:
- a cosmetic compromise that preserves all problems,
- an open-ended conflict that erodes value,
- or a restructuring that sacrifices the enterprise’s future in order to avoid any difficult decision.

### Bottom line

The family holding’s priority is **long-term value**, not short-term optics and not ideological rigidity from any side.

So I am not here to side reflexively with management, nor to endorse a blanket workforce veto. I am here to insist on an outcome that is economically credible, socially executable, and stable enough to preserve the company’s future.

If the parties can bring forward a plan that genuinely restores competitiveness without triggering a destructive breakdown, we are prepared to back it through governance. If not, we will not support a settlement that merely postpones the crisis or erodes the asset.
