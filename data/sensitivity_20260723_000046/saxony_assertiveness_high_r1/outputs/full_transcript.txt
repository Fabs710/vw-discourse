[Works Council (Betriebsrat) (Plant-level employee representation under co-determination) — Round 1]
As Works Council, I will be very clear from the outset:

We will not accept plant closures in Germany and we will not accept compulsory redundancies. That is the line.

The workforce has already made major sacrifices over many years, and the historic job-security promise cannot simply be torn up because management now wants a faster, easier balance-sheet solution. If the company has structural problems, then the answer must be a negotiated restructuring that protects all German sites, preserves co-determination, and gives the workforce a credible future.

What we are prepared to discuss:
- temporary or reversible capacity reductions,
- working-time adjustments,
- internal redeployment,
- qualified retirement and voluntary measures,
- targeted productivity improvements,
- and a fair employee contribution, if and only if it is matched by binding guarantees.

What must be on the table for any agreement:
- a binding guarantee that all German plants remain open,
- no operational layoffs,
- site and job security through 2030,
- real investment commitments for future products and technologies at the German sites,
- and a genuine co-determination role in shaping the restructuring, not a fait accompli.

If management comes here with closure plans disguised as “necessary realism,” or with demands that workers pay for strategic mistakes while losing their jobs, then I will oppose that with every means available: in the supervisory board, at the bargaining table, and, if necessary, through industrial action.

We are prepared to help secure the company’s future. But not by sacrificing the workforce and dismantling the German industrial base.

- a **structural reduction in overcapacity**,
- a **material and durable reduction in labour costs**,
- and a **credible path back to margin and investment capacity**.

If we can get there through a combination of site adjustments, capacity cuts, voluntary exits, working-time changes, and a negotiated labour-cost contribution, then I am prepared to discuss the form of implementation.

But if the answer is simply:
- no closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- no meaningful cost adjustment,
- and no hard capacity reduction,

then that is not a solution. It is a delay of the crisis.

So I will be very clear: I am prepared to negotiate, but only on the basis that the restructuring delivers real structural savings. Anything less would fail the company, the brand, and ultimately the workforce as well.

## 2) Economic logic: what can and cannot be done without closures

A no-closure, no-forced-layoff package can reduce cost through four main channels:

### A. Labor cost per vehicle
Potential levers:
- wage moderation or temporary wage concession,
- removal or suspension of allowances/benefits,
- longer working hours without proportional wage increase,
- shift premiums redesign,
- temporary employee contribution,
- lower variable pay.

**Typical magnitude:**  
- Temporary concessions: useful, but often only **low hundreds of millions annually** at large scale.
- More durable labor-cost reset: can be larger, but only if employees accept substantial multi-year changes.

### B. Headcount reduction without forced layoffs
Potential levers:
- voluntary exits,
- early retirement,
- natural attrition,
- hiring freeze,
- internal redeployment,
- nonreplacement of vacancies,
- temporary agency labor reduction.

**Typical magnitude:**  
This can be meaningful, but it is **slow**.  
In a large industrial workforce, voluntary/attrition-based reduction can usually deliver perhaps **1–3% of headcount per year** without coercion if designed well. More is possible, but costly and not always reliable.

### C. Capacity and operating model changes without closures
Potential levers:
- shift reduction,
- line balancing,
- longer cycles,
- product mix changes,
- temporary idling,
- insourcing/outsourcing mix,
- platform consolidation inside existing plants,
- lower overtime and premium hours.

**Typical magnitude:**  
This can reduce fixed cost and overhead absorption, but it rarely replaces the economics of closing a structurally redundant site.

### D. Non-labor savings
Potential levers:
- procurement,
- logistics,
- indirect overhead,
- SG&A reduction,
- capex deferral,
- administrative simplification,
- digital productivity.

**Typical magnitude:**  
These can contribute significantly, but they usually do **not** fully offset a high-cost manufacturing footprint.

## 4) The key question: is the problem fixed-cost overhang or variable-cost excess?

This is the central test.

### If the issue is mainly variable cost
Then a labor deal can help a lot:
- lower unit labor cost,
- higher flexibility,
- shift redesign,
- overtime discipline,
- internal redeployment.

### If the issue is mainly fixed-cost overhang / capacity duplication
Then a no-closure package is much weaker:
- fixed costs remain embedded,
- underutilized plants stay expensive,
- margin recovery is limited by volume absorption,
- savings are often temporary or accounting-based.

In plain terms:  
**You can negotiate labor cost down more easily than you can make a structurally redundant plant disappear without closing it.**

- suspension or reduction of bonuses/variable pay
- reduction in premiums and allowances
- longer working-time flexibility
- temporary reduction in paid hours or conversion to working-time accounts
- redeployment across plants
- voluntary exits and early retirement

### Capacity-side measures
- shift reduction at selected lines
- elimination of idle capacity through product reallocation
- platform consolidation within existing sites
- nonreplacement of vacancies
- limits on external hiring
- temporary production balancing among plants

### Management/owner contribution
- bonus restraint at top levels
- dividend/payout discipline if financially defensible
- capex prioritization
- fixed-cost reduction in corporate overhead
- stronger product and investment commitment to Germany

### Governance protections
- binding review milestones
- triggers if savings are missed
- monitoring by a joint committee
- quantified metrics, not vague promises

Without quantified triggers, a no-closure package often becomes a delay mechanism rather than a restructuring.

## 6) Why “no forced layoffs” is not automatically incompatible with restructuring
Economically, **no forced layoffs** is feasible if:
- voluntary exits are generously incentivized,
- early retirement channels are available,
- internal mobility is real,
- and the headcount reduction target is modest enough.

It becomes difficult if:
- the required headcount reduction is large,
- the workforce is older but not retirement-ready,
- many skills are site-specific,
- or the company needs rapid savings.

So the feasibility depends heavily on:
- age profile,
- skill transferability,
- pension obligations,
- and the time allowed.

may improve cash flow briefly, but not necessarily fix the underlying competitiveness gap.

If management’s target is to restore a **durable return on sales improvement**, then the package must create recurring savings, not just delay losses.

## 5. Areas of convergence
- A cosmetic compromise is unacceptable.
- Durable, executable restructuring is required.
- Voluntary instruments should come first.
- Management, owners, and capital must contribute too.
- Investment in future products and Germany-based capability is necessary.
- Co-determination is not optional; implementation must fit the governance structure.
- Prolonged industrial conflict would destroy value.

## 6. Key trade-offs for the decision-maker
- **Site security vs. economic flexibility**
- **No forced layoffs vs. depth of workforce adjustment**
- **Temporary concessions vs. durable competitiveness**
- **Lower conflict risk vs. stronger fixed-cost reset**
- **Near-term industrial peace vs. long-term margin recovery**
- **Political legitimacy vs. investor credibility**

The decision-maker must choose whether to prioritize a politically and socially acceptable compromise with potentially incomplete savings, or a more forceful reset with much higher execution and governance risk.

## 7. Integrative pathways
### Pathway 1: Time-bounded no-closure test
All German plants remain open for an initial period, with no compulsory redundancies, while the parties implement quantified savings, productivity gains, and temporary labour contributions. A pre-agreed review trigger reopens structural options if targets are missed.

### Pathway 2: Maximum internal adjustment
The company pursues aggressive internal measures first: voluntary exits, phased retirement, redeployment, retraining, working-time reductions, shift cuts, and line rationalization inside existing sites. This preserves the no-closure principle while testing whether the gap is actually bridgeable.

### Pathway 3: Protected sites with conditional exceptions
Specific sites are protected, but a rule-based exception process is created for any site that fails defined viability criteria. This gives labour and the state protection against arbitrary closures while preserving a narrow, governed route for exceptional cases.

### Pathway 4: Sequenced compromise
Immediate relief measures and temporary labour contributions are paired with binding investment and a later structural review. This is the strongest bridge between social acceptability and management’s desire for enforceable follow-through.

## 8. Open questions
- What is the actual quantified savings gap?
- How much of the cost problem is labor-related versus fixed-cost / capacity-related?
- Which sites, if any, are truly non-viable?
- What level of employee contribution is durable enough to matter?
- What management and owner contributions are visible and credible enough to satisfy labour?
- What review trigger would be acceptable if a no-closure package underperforms?
- How much margin improvement is required by 2030 for the package to be considered successful?

## 9. Recommended next step
Move immediately to a **quantified side-by-side comparison** of two packages:
1. a **no-closure / no-forced-layoff** package, and  
2. a **structural-adjustment** package.

Require both to show annual savings, headcount impact, site impact, one-time costs, and 2025–2030 margin trajectory. Use that comparison to test whether a time-bounded no-closure deal is credible or whether structural adjustment must be activated.

## 10. Emergent insights that only appeared through multi-turn interaction
Several important points emerged only after the parties were forced to respond to each other over multiple rounds:

- The debate is not about whether restructuring is needed, but about **what counts as proof** that closures are necessary or avoidable.
- The expert assessment made clear that a no-closure package is **not automatically unrealistic**; it depends on whether the gap is moderate and whether savings are durable. That nuance was absent from the initial positions.
- Management’s position hardened into a clear economic claim: if internal measures do not close the gap, the company will need footprint reduction. This clarified that its concern is fixed-cost overhang, not just labor cost.
- Labour and the state revealed they are not simply resisting change; they are willing to accept temporary concessions and monitoring, but only if the package preserves sites and job security.
- The family holding and investors converged on a middle position: they are not ideologically committed to closures, but they insist that a no-closure package must be proven in hard numbers or it becomes a delay tactic.
- The most workable compromise is not a permanent yes/no decision on closures, but a **sequenced deal with measurable triggers** and a later review point if the no-closure path fails.
