## Where I agree
I agree with the union, the state, and even the expert on one essential point: a cosmetic deal is worthless.

I also agree that:
- voluntary exits, redeployment, retraining, working-time tools, and internal transfers must come first;
- management, owners, and capital must contribute visibly;
- and any settlement must be executable and durable.

I further agree that numbers matter. If management wants credibility, then it should put real figures on the table instead of hiding behind vague references to “overcapacity.”

## Where I disagree
I disagree with the core assumption from management, the family holding, and investors that there are only two realistic options:
1. plant closures and forced layoffs, or
2. a weak compromise that leaves the company uncompetitive.

That is a false binary.

The expert said a no-closure, no-forced-layoff package is **plausible** if the gap is moderate and the measures are durable. That is exactly why I reject the closure-first framing. You have not yet demonstrated that the gap is so extreme that every internal, negotiated, reversible instrument is exhausted.

And let me be precise: the burden of proof is on those who want to tear up historic job-security guarantees, not on the workforce defending them.

## On the expert assessment
The expert’s contribution confirms something important: **no-closure packages can work** under certain conditions.

That matters because management keeps speaking as if closures were the natural baseline. They are not.

The expert also said something else I want to underline: if the issue is mainly a **fixed-cost overhang / capacity duplication** problem, then a no-closure deal is weaker. Fine. Then show the plant-by-plant and line-by-line economics. Show us which sites are supposedly “redundant,” by what metric, and why internal reconfiguration cannot solve it.

Until then, “capacity overhang” is just a managerial label, not a reason to close sites.

- no closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- and no erosion of the job-security framework.

That is not a negotiation. That is an ultimatum dressed up as economic realism.

If you want the works council to engage seriously, then put forward a package with:
- a quantified savings target,
- management and owner contributions,

Then we can judge the numbers.

## My response to the family holding and investors
You both keep saying that “all German plants open” is not, by itself, a value-preserving plan. I agree — **by itself** it is not enough.

But neither is “competitiveness” by itself. If your answer to every cost problem is to pressure workers until they accept closures, then you are destroying trust and creating the very conflict you say you want to avoid.

If you want industrial peace, then stop treating closures as a bargaining chip. A real value-preserving plan must include:
- binding investment in Germany,
- labor-cost contributions that are temporary but meaningful,
- management restraint,
- and internal capacity measures that do not sacrifice sites.

## On the two-package test requested by the moderator
I support the idea of comparing two packages, but only if the “structural adjustment” option is not pre-baked to include closures and dismissals as its defining feature.

### Package 1: No-closure / no-forced-layoff option
This is the only package I can responsibly support in principle.

It must include:
- all German plants open,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- binding job security through 2030,
- quantified employee contribution,
- management and owner restraint,

If this means closures and forced redundancies, then it is not acceptable and not a real negotiation basis from our side.

So yes, compare the packages — but do not pretend both are equally legitimate. One preserves the company’s social foundation; the other breaks it.
