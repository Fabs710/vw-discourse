We are in round 4, and I will not dilute our position.

## First: what I agree with
I agree with the expert, and I agree with parts of what even management and investors are now saying:

- This company needs a **real restructuring**.
- The status quo is not sustainable.
- A **cosmetic deal** would just postpone the crisis.
- Savings must be **measurable, durable, and enforceable**.
- Everyone should contribute, including management, owners, and labour.

That is not the dispute.

## What I reject
I reject the continued framing that **closures and compulsory redundancies are the benchmark** against which every alternative must be measured.

That is exactly the trap we warned about:
- declare a target,
- define “equivalence” in a way that privileges layoffs,
- then claim any job-protecting solution is insufficient.

We will not accept that logic.

## To management
You keep saying a non-closure package must match the “structural effect” of closures. Fine — but then you must also accept that the company has not yet proven that closures are the only way to get there.

You have still not answered the basic questions in a transparent, site-by-site way:
- What exact savings gap remains?
- What is the management contribution?
- What is the owner contribution?
- What procurement savings are real, not hoped-for?
- What capital expenditure is being postponed or reshaped?
- What productivity gains are actually locked in?

Until that is on the table, closures are not a necessity; they are a **preference**.

And I will say this plainly: **we do not accept plant closures in Germany**. Not as a first choice, not as a fallback, not as a bargaining threat. Same for **compulsory redundancies**.

## To institutional investors
You are demanding “risk-adjusted value” and “credible margin recovery.” We understand that.

What we do not accept is your assumption that the only credible risk reduction is to destroy jobs and industrial trust.

The expert has said that equivalence is possible in principle, but only with real data. Good. Then stop insisting on the conclusion before the data are complete.

A non-closure package is not a “risk transfer” if it is backed by:
- permanent working-time instruments,
- redeployment,
- qualification,
- management restraint,
- owner contribution,
- and binding investment commitments.

What you are asking for is not just numbers. You are asking the workforce to surrender its security so the market can feel reassured. That is not acceptable.

## To the controlling family
You keep talking about long-term value, and I will judge you by whether you actually protect the industrial base that creates that value.

If you want a durable settlement, then back:
- open German plants,
- no forced layoffs,
- binding investment,
- and a real shared-burden package.

Do not hide behind “economic equivalence” while refusing to commit to the one thing that would make a settlement stable: **job and site security to 2030**.

If you support a deal that leaves every German plant open and avoids compulsory redundancies, then prove that you are serious about stewardship, not just governance language.

## To the regional state government
You are right to reject closures and forced layoffs. That is the correct line.

But I want to be equally clear: we need more than political support statements. We need **binding protection**.

If you are serious, then use your leverage to insist on:
- no closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- a binding job-security framework to 2030,
- and co-determination that is not hollowed out by “performance tests” designed to produce the result management already wants.

A review clause is not protection if it merely delays the same threat. We know how that game works. The threat never disappears; it just becomes a permanent pressure mechanism.

## To the union
You are right to insist on no closures and no compulsory redundancies. That remains the correct line.

But I want to sharpen one point: it is not enough to say “we are open to a serious restructuring.” We already know that. The issue is what kind.

We can discuss:
- voluntary exits,
- retirement bridges,
- redeployment,
- qualification,
- temporary and fair working-time measures,
- and a negotiated employee contribution.

But only under one condition: **they must sit inside a binding framework that keeps every German plant open and preserves job security**.

## On the expert’s proposal
The expert says the current record does not yet prove equivalence. Correct.

But the expert also says equivalence is only a financial test. That is too narrow. For us, there is also a **social and institutional test**:
- Does the solution preserve co-determination?
- Does it preserve trust?
- Does it preserve the industrial base?
- Does it prevent a precedent of closure-by-pressure?

A “financially equivalent” package that destroys the job-security framework is not acceptable. Full stop.

So I do **not** accept the idea that a verified equivalence test would automatically legitimate closures or forced redundancies. It would not.

## On the facilitator’s compromise ideas

### 1) Hybrid restructuring with selective capacity reduction
No.  
If “selective capacity reduction” means shutting lines, hollowing out sites, or moving production in a way that prepares closure, then that is just closure by another name.

We can discuss internal rebalancing, but not disguised site weakening.

### 2) Time-limited moratorium with review triggers
A moratorium can only mean something if it is a **real guarantee**, not a pause before the next attack.

If review triggers are used to reopen the closure question, then that is not a compromise we can accept. It simply institutionalizes the threat.

### 3) Independent equivalence test
We have no problem with transparent data. In fact, we demand it.

But I will be explicit: an independent test must not be used to override co-determination or to normalize plant closures. It can inform the discussion, not decide over the heads of the workforce.

## Our non-negotiables remain
Let me restate them because they have not changed:

- **No plant closures in Germany**
- **No compulsory redundancies**
- **No hollowing out of co-determination**
- **No restructuring that makes job security conditional on permanent threats**
- **No deal where workers absorb the pain while management and owners remain protected**

## What would be acceptable to us
Only a package that keeps:
1. every German plant open,
2. jobs protected without forced layoffs,
3. co-determination intact,
4. and a binding job-security framework to 2030.

Within that framework, we are prepared to discuss:
- voluntary exits,
- retirement bridges,
- redeployment,
- qualification,
- temporary working-time adjustments,
- internal capacity shifts,
- and fair burden-sharing.

But I repeat: **those are instruments inside a protected framework, not substitutes for it.**

## Where the burden lies now
Management and owners are still trying to shift the burden of their strategic mistakes onto the workforce. That is the core conflict.

If the company wants competitiveness, then it should first prove it is willing to:
- reduce overhead at the top,
- make owners contribute,
- restructure procurement,
- and align capital spending with the real industrial plan.

Do not begin with workers’ jobs. That is morally wrong and economically short-sighted.

## Bottom line
We are not moving on our core line.

We support restructuring.  
We reject closures.  
We reject compulsory redundancies.  
We reject weakening co-determination.  
We reject any “equivalence” argument that is really just a justification for sacrificing jobs and sites.

If management, owners, and investors want our agreement, they need to bring a package that keeps **every German plant open** and secures employment to **2030**.

Otherwise, they should be honest that what they are pursuing is not restructuring with the workforce — it is restructuring against the workforce.
