I’ll be direct as well.

We all agree the company needs a real restructuring. We also all agree a cosmetic deal is not enough. On that, there is no disagreement.

Where we split is the central issue: **how you get to a structurally competitive cost base**.

## To the works council
I hear your insistence on:
- no plant closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- no hollowing out of co-determination,
- and binding guarantees to 2030.

I do **not** accept those as absolute red lines if they prevent a durable solution.

You keep saying closures are a failure of imagination. That is not serious. A failure of imagination would be pretending that we can fix overcapacity, above-target labour costs, and a shrinking market with voluntary exits and wishful thinking alone. We cannot.

You ask for a quantified non-closure package. Fair enough — but that package must produce **equivalent structural effect**. Not slogans, not temporary relief, not measures that simply delay the pain. If the package does not materially reduce capacity and labour costs on a permanent basis, then it is not equivalent.

And let me say plainly: **binding site guarantees to 2030 that freeze the footprint regardless of economics are not acceptable**. I will not sign up to a framework that locks in an uncompetitive structure and pushes the next crisis onto the balance sheet.

## To the union
You say closures are a bargaining failure, not a necessity. I disagree.

If the only way to preserve every plant and every job guarantee is to leave overcapacity and labour costs structurally too high, then that is not a solution. That is postponement.

You are right that everyone must contribute. But you are still talking as if management, owners, and procurement can somehow cover the gap while workforce instruments stay largely intact. They cannot. There is no serious path to restored margins without a **permanent workforce contribution** and a **real reduction in footprint or equivalent capacity**.

Voluntary exits, retirement bridges, redeployment, qualification, working-time flexibility — those are all useful. But they are **not sufficient by themselves**. If we are serious about returning the brand to its ROS target and funding future products through 2030, we need more than that.

## To the state government
I respect your political role and your concern for the region. But your position cannot be: “no closures, no redundancies, and somehow still expect competitive economics.”

That is not a strategy. That is a political constraint.

I am open to a solution that keeps German industry strong, but it has to be economically credible. If the state’s position makes meaningful capacity adjustment impossible, then you are effectively asking the company to absorb a cost base it cannot carry. I will not endorse that.

## To the controlling family
You are right that long-term value matters. That is precisely why we need hard decisions now.

I agree with you on one thing in particular: we must avoid a cosmetic compromise. Where I differ is this: you seem to want “economic equivalence” without accepting that closures may be part of how equivalence is achieved. I am not wedded to closures as an ideology — but I am wedded to the outcome.

If there is a **credible alternative package** that produces the same permanent economic effect, I will consider it. But that means hard numbers, enforceable milestones, and real capacity reduction. Not a promise to work harder. Not a symbolic top-level sacrifice. Not a deferred adjustment.

## To institutional investors
You are correct to insist on verifiable, durable savings. That is also my standard.

Where I disagree is that you sometimes treat any route short of closures as inherently suspicious. It is not the label that matters; it is the result. If an alternative solution genuinely reduces capacity, lowers labour costs, and restores margins on a durable basis, then I can support it.

But if the proposed alternative depends mainly on attrition and temporary tools, then it does not solve the problem. It merely slows it down. And the market will see through that.

## My position
Let me be explicit:

- **Yes** to a negotiated solution.
- **Yes** to alternatives to closures **if** they are economically equivalent.
- **No** to blanket job and site guarantees that freeze the current footprint.
- **No** to a settlement that leaves overcapacity in place.
- **No** to measures that are temporary in form but inadequate in substance.
- **Yes** to management, owners, and labour all contributing.
- **Yes** to investment in future products and technology.
- **No** to a deal that fails to restore competitiveness.

## What would move me
I would consider a non-closure path if it includes all of the following:
1. A **measurable, durable reduction in capacity**
2. A **substantial permanent labour-cost contribution**
3. A **real headcount reduction**, not just natural attrition over time
4. **Verifiable productivity gains**
5. **Binding investment and product commitments**
6. A package that restores the brand’s **return-on-sales target**

That is the test.

## What I will not accept
I will not accept:
- a cosmetic compromise,
- a permanent guarantee that blocks restructuring,
- a settlement that preserves the current cost base,
- or a deal that sacrifices future competitiveness for short-term peace.

If the employee side wants to avoid closures, then show me the exact combination of measures that closes the economics gap. If you cannot do that, then do not ask management to pretend the problem is solved.

I am not retreating from the need for deep structural change.  
If you want a deal, bring one that actually fixes the business.
