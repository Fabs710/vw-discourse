We appreciate the expert input because it gets to the real issue: **numbers, durability, and credibility**. That is exactly the standard we care about.

## Where we agree
We agree with management, the family, the state, the works council, and the union on one core point: **the current structure is not sustainable**. The business needs a real restructuring, not a cosmetic one.

We also agree with the specialist that:
- **equivalence must be quantified**, not asserted;
- temporary measures are not enough;
- and the package has to restore **margin, cash flow, and competitiveness through 2030**.

That is the right framing.

## Where we disagree
### To the works council and union
Your red lines are understandable politically, but from a capital-markets perspective they are only acceptable **if** they can be shown to produce **economic equivalence**.

Right now, you are still asking us to trust that:
- voluntary exits,
- retirement bridges,
- redeployment,
- working-time instruments,
- and limited concessions

will somehow add up to the same structural outcome as closures and compulsory redundancies.

The expert has just said the crucial thing: **that has not yet been demonstrated**.

So we are **not** persuaded by principle alone. We need:
- site-by-site capacity numbers,
- a headcount bridge,
- a labor-cost bridge,
- and a cash-flow bridge.

Without that, “no closures” is not a solution; it is a **risk transfer to shareholders**.

And let me be explicit: if the result leaves the cost base uncompetitive or margins structurally impaired, we will not support it.

### To management
We agree with you that deep restructuring is required. Where we differ is that you still have not provided the kind of quantified comparison that would let investors underwrite a no-closure alternative.

If closures are on the table, then the company must show:
- what they save,
- what they cost,
- and what the alternative saves instead.

Right now, the burden of proof cuts both ways. You cannot simply say “alternatives are not enough” without laying out the bridge to the target margin in hard numbers.

At the same time, we also do **not** support a confrontation strategy. A prolonged strike, brand damage, and market uncertainty would destroy value quickly. So management needs to push hard for structural savings, but with a settlement path the market can believe.

### To the regional state government
We understand the political constraints. But political red lines do not change the economics.

The expert’s answer is consistent with our view: if the non-closure package is not truly equivalent, then refusing closures is effectively choosing a weaker margin outcome. That is not acceptable to us.

We are not asking the state to abandon co-determination. We are asking it to stop treating “no closures” as sufficient on its own. If you want to block closures, you also need to help ensure the substitute measures are large enough, durable enough, and enforceable enough to restore competitiveness.

### To the controlling family
We agree with your focus on long-term value. But long-term value is exactly why we are unwilling to endorse a package that only postpones the adjustment.

You are right that temporary fixes are not enough. You are also right that the owners and management must contribute. But the key test remains unchanged: **does the package permanently lower the cost base enough to restore ROS?**

If the answer is no, then preserving every site is not stewardship; it is value erosion with a delay.

## What would move us
The expert has outlined the right test. For us to back a no-closure solution, we would need to see:

1. **A quantified savings bridge** to competitive margins
2. **Permanent headcount reduction**, not just attrition
3. **A measurable and durable labor-cost contribution**
4. **Real capacity reduction**, even if plants stay open
5. **A cash-flow bridge** showing payback and downside resilience
6. **Binding milestones** so the plan is enforceable, not aspirational

If those numbers are credible, we can support a non-closure route. If they are not, we will not.

## What we will not support
- A cosmetic compromise
- A settlement that leaves excess capacity effectively intact
- A deal based mainly on temporary measures
- A package that protects all current arrangements and asks shareholders to absorb the downside
- A prolonged conflict that destroys value

## Bottom line
We are open to alternatives to closures. We are **not** open to alternatives to competitiveness.

So our position is now sharper:
- If the non-closure package is **financially equivalent**, durable, and enforceable, we can support it.
- If it is **not**, then management should not be prevented from taking deeper structural action.

The expert has not yet shown equivalence. Until that is demonstrated with numbers, we remain skeptical and will continue to press for a solution that **credibly restores margins and protects risk-adjusted shareholder value**.

If helpful, we would now want the parties to table a **single quantified side-by-side comparison**:
- closure scenario,
- non-closure scenario,
- hybrid scenario,
with savings, capex, cash impact, and margin effect through 2030.
