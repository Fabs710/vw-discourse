I will be direct.

## Where I agree
I agree with everyone on one point: **the company needs real restructuring**.  
The current cost structure is not sustainable, and we should not pretend that a cosmetic compromise will carry this business to 2030.

I also agree that:
- savings must be **measurable**,
- the solution must be **durable**,
- management, owners, and labour must all contribute,
- and the company needs a credible investment and product path.

So no one should mistake my position for a defence of the status quo.

## Where I disagree
### On plant closures
I do **not** accept plant closures in Germany as the default or fallback solution.

That is not a minor preference. It is a red line, because closures would damage:
- regional employment,
- the industrial base in the state,
- trust in the company,
- and the long-term role of the company as an economic anchor.

If closures are being kept on the table as a bargaining weapon, then I will say plainly: that is not a responsible restructuring strategy. It is escalation.

### On compulsory redundancies
I also do **not** accept compulsory redundancies.

If the company wants a durable settlement, it must find a path through:
- voluntary exits,
- retirement bridges,
- redeployment,
- qualification,
- working-time measures,
- and fair burden-sharing.

If management says those instruments are not enough, then it must prove that with numbers — not with pressure.

### On “economic equivalence”
I am open to non-closure solutions, but only if they are genuinely equivalent in effect.

That means:
- real capacity reduction,
- real labour-cost reduction,
- real productivity gains,
- and a credible path to profitability.

What I will **not** accept is a package that calls itself equivalent while preserving uncertainty, hollowing out confidence, and forcing the workforce to pay the whole bill.

## To management
You keep saying the footprint must change. Fine. But your current logic is still too one-sided.

You say closures may be necessary unless equivalent savings can be shown. Then show what you yourself are contributing in a quantified way:
- management overhead reductions,
- procurement gains,
- capital allocation discipline,
- investment reprioritisation,
- and owner contribution.

Do not tell the workforce that only it must make permanent sacrifices while management and ownership stay comparatively insulated.

I also reject the idea that regional responsibility is somehow incompatible with competitiveness. If this company loses legitimacy in its home region, that is not a small political issue — it becomes a strategic business problem.

## To the works council and union
I support your core line:
- no closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- no hollowing out of co-determination.

That is the right starting point.

But I also need to be candid: **“no closures” cannot mean “no change.”** The company does need operational adjustment, and the question is how to do that without breaking the social compact.

So I want from you a **concrete, quantified alternative package**, not only a principled refusal. If you are confident that the company can be restructured without closures, then put the full package on the table and show the numbers:
- how much savings come from labour measures,
- how much from management and ownership,
- how much from productivity,
- how much from internal redeployment and capacity shifts,
- and what binding investment commitments accompany it.

That is the real test.

## To the controlling family
I respect your insistence on long-term value. On that, we are aligned.

But long-term value does **not** mean accepting a solution that weakens the company’s social foundation in Germany. A viable settlement must preserve the industrial base that makes the brand strong.

I am prepared to support a hard restructuring if it is truly balanced. I am **not** prepared to support one-sided cuts dressed up as strategic discipline.

## To institutional investors
You are right to insist on verifiable and durable savings. I do not dispute that.

Where I disagree is with any framing that treats plant closures as the only serious proof of discipline. The question is not how brutal the announcement sounds. The question is whether the company gets to a competitive structure on a durable basis.

If a non-closure package can produce that, it deserves support. If it cannot, then it is not a solution. But the burden of proof must apply to all sides, including management and ownership, not only to labour.

## My position
The state shareholder will support a restructuring that:
1. protects German industrial sites,
2. avoids compulsory redundancies,
3. delivers real and durable cost reduction,
4. preserves co-determination,
5. and secures investment and future products in Germany.

What we will **not** support:
- plant closures in Germany,
- forced layoffs,
- a one-sided burden on labour,
- or any weakening of our veto and co-determination role.

## What would move me
A credible package would need to include:
- quantified savings by source,
- site-by-site capacity adjustments **without** shutdowns,
- voluntary and internal workforce measures,
- management and owner contributions in parallel,
- binding investment commitments for German plants,
- and a timetable with enforceable milestones.

If management can show that this achieves the required effect without closures, I will engage constructively. If not, then we are not talking about an unavoidable necessity — we are talking about a choice to shift the burden onto regions and workers.

## Bottom line
We are prepared to support a serious restructuring.  
We are **not** prepared to support the dismantling of Germany’s industrial base.

If you want our support, bring a package that is:
- economically credible,
- socially fair,
- and consistent with the company’s role as a regional anchor.

Otherwise, do not expect us to sign off on closures and call that responsibility.
