I hear two things very clearly from management and from the employee side.

First: the company does need a credible restructuring. I agree with that. Pretending there is no structural problem would be irresponsible.

Second: I am not prepared to accept the logic that the only credible restructuring is one that destroys sites, tears up co-determination, and shifts the adjustment burden mainly onto the workforce. That I do **not** accept.

Let me be direct from the perspective of the state shareholder and regional actor:

- **Plant closures in Germany are not a lightly available option for me.**
- **Compulsory redundancies are politically and socially unacceptable as a default path.**
- **Any solution that weakens the state’s co-determination and veto role is off the table.**

I understand management’s cost pressure and the competitiveness problem. I do not dismiss it. But if the answer is to hollow out the company’s German base, then you are not solving a restructuring problem — you are creating a regional economic and political crisis that will damage the company itself in the long run.

Where I agree with management:
- The status quo is not sustainable.
- The company needs measurable productivity and cost improvements.
- Any settlement must be durable, not symbolic.
- Workforce contributions may be part of the package.

Where I agree with the works council and union:
- There must be no compulsory redundancies.
- German sites need real security, not vague promises.
- Co-determination is not an obstacle to be bypassed; it is part of the governance that has kept this company stable.
- A restructuring that treats employees as the sole adjustment variable will not be supported.

Where I disagree with management:
- I do **not** accept that closures are simply the benchmark for seriousness.
- I do **not** accept that “structural change” automatically means permanent damage to the German footprint.
- I do **not** accept a unilateral framing in which labour must first concede, while management and owners only “consider” their contribution.

If you want a serious settlement, then it must be built on a different logic:
1. **Protect the German plants**
2. **Avoid forced layoffs**
3. **Use negotiated capacity reductions, working-time instruments, voluntary exits, qualification, and redeployment**
4. **Bind management and owners to tangible contributions**
5. **Secure future investment and product allocation in Germany**
6. **Preserve the company as a regional industrial anchor**

That is not a call for cosmetic compromise. It is a call for a restructuring that is economically credible **and** socially sustainable.

To management: if you insist on closure-first thinking, you should expect firm resistance from me. You do not have a free hand here.

To the employee side: I appreciate the willingness to engage on a negotiated contribution, but I need to be equally clear that the state will not support an outcome where nothing changes operationally. We will need real efficiency gains, capacity adjustments, and a credible plan that restores competitiveness. Site protection must not become a synonym for freezing all change.

So my position is this:
- **No plant closures as the starting point**
- **No compulsory layoffs**
- **Yes to a serious restructuring agreement**
- **Yes to fair burden-sharing**
- **Yes to durable competitiveness measures**
- **No to sidelining regional interests or co-determination**

If management brings forward a package that preserves sites and jobs while delivering verifiable savings and a long-term industrial strategy, I am prepared to engage constructively. If the package relies on closure threats and one-sided workforce concessions, I will not support it.
