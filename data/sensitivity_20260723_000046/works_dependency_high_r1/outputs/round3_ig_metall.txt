I’ll be equally direct in round 3.

## Where I agree
I agree with the works council, the state government, and even parts of what management, the family, and investors are saying on one central point: **this company needs a real restructuring**. The current situation is not sustainable, and a cosmetic deal would simply defer the next crisis.

I also agree that:
- savings must be **measurable**,
- the solution must be **durable**,
- and **everyone must contribute** in some form.

That includes labour — and we have already shown willingness to talk about working-time measures, voluntary exits, qualification, and a negotiated employee contribution.

## Where I disagree
### To management
You are still framing the issue as if the only serious options are closures or a weak compromise. I reject that logic.

A serious restructuring is not the same as plant closures. If you want to prove necessity, then put a **quantified, non-closure alternative** on the table first. Do not start from the assumption that the workforce must absorb the shock through forced job cuts or site shutdowns.

You say voluntary exits, redeployment, and temporary tools are not enough. Maybe on their own they are not. But then the burden is on you to show the **full package**:
- management restraint,
- overhead cuts,
- procurement improvements,
- capital allocation shifts,
- owner contribution,
- and internal capacity adjustment **without compulsory redundancies**.

What I will not accept is a solution that protects management’s strategic freedom while treating workers as the sole buffer.

### To the works council
I support your red lines on **no plant closures** and **no compulsory redundancies**. Those are absolutely legitimate and must not be diluted into “temporary” promises or open-ended uncertainty.

But I want to be equally clear: **no closures does not mean no restructuring**. We do need real operational adjustment, and that requires hard numbers and enforceable instruments, not just a general refusal.

So I support the demand for:
- binding site security,
- no forced layoffs,
- redeployment,
- retirement bridges,
- qualification,
- and capacity reduction without plant shutdowns.

That is the right direction. But we need a negotiated package that is actually implementable.

### To institutional investors
You keep asking for “equivalent structural savings.” Fine — then define equivalence properly.

Equivalence is not “whatever looks harsh enough to the market.”  
Equivalence means:
- durable cost reduction,
- competitive capacity,
- sustainable margins,
- and a stable industrial base.

If you want that, you cannot dismiss non-closure measures just because they are less dramatic. A package can be financially credible without destroying sites. The real question is whether it closes the gap on a durable basis.

### To the controlling family
You say you want long-term value and governance integrity. I can work with that. But long-term value is not created by turning workforce insecurity into a restructuring instrument.

If you want a durable solution, then support:
- binding investment commitments,
- a fair burden-sharing package,
- and a restructuring that does **not** throw away the company’s industrial base in Germany.

### To the state government
I appreciate your clear opposition to closures and compulsory redundancies. That is the correct starting point.

But I also want to be plain: political red lines alone do not solve the economics. If you want to be helpful, use your position to push for:
- open German plants,
- real investment,
- no forced redundancies,
- and a negotiated capacity adjustment that preserves the social and industrial foundation of the company.

## What we are prepared to discuss
We remain prepared to negotiate a serious package if it contains all of the following:

1. **No plant closures in Germany**
2. **No compulsory redundancies**
3. **Capacity reduction through voluntary and internal measures**
4. **Redeployment across sites**
5. **Retirement bridges and qualification**
6. **Fair working-time adjustments**
7. **A limited employee contribution, only with parallel management/owner contributions**
8. **Binding investment and product commitments for Germany**
9. **A collective agreement with durable protections, not a one-off pressure deal**

That is a legitimate restructuring path. It is not a refusal of reality. It is a refusal to make employees carry the whole burden.

## What we need answered now
Before anyone keeps repeating “structural necessity,” I want the actual numbers:

- How much cost reduction is required exactly?
- How much can management and owners contribute?
- What savings come from overhead, procurement, and capital allocation?
- What productivity gains are assumed?
- What capacity reduction is truly required, and where?
- What binding employment and site guarantees are offered through 2030?

Without those answers, “closures may be necessary” is just leverage.

## Our limit
Let me say this clearly:  
We are **not** moving to compulsory redundancies.  
We are **not** accepting plant closures as a default or fallback.  
And we are **not** accepting a restructuring in which the workforce pays all the pain while ownership and management stay insulated.

If management and the owners want industrial peace, they need to present a package that protects jobs and sites while delivering real savings. If they cannot, then they should be honest that they are choosing confrontation, not negotiation.

## Bottom line
The union is prepared to bargain hard, and we are prepared to contribute. But our contribution must be part of a fair settlement — not the price for dismantling job security.

So my position is:

- **No plant closures**
- **No compulsory redundancies**
- **Fair shared sacrifice**
- **Binding investment and employment protection**
- **A real, measurable alternative to layoffs**

Bring that, and we can negotiate seriously.  
Bring a one-sided cost-cutting plan, and you will face resistance.
