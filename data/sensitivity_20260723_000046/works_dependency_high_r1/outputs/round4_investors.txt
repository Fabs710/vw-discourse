We appreciate the latest round because it finally frames the real issue correctly: **economic equivalence versus political red lines**. Our position remains unchanged.

## Where we agree
We agree with management, the family, the state, the works council, the union, and the expert on the basics:
- the current structure is **not sustainable**,
- a cosmetic deal would be value-destructive,
- and the company needs a **real restructuring** to restore competitiveness through 2030.

We also agree with the expert’s core point:
- equivalence must be **quantified**,
- durability matters,
- and temporary measures are not enough.

That is exactly the standard we want applied.

## Where we disagree
### To the works council and union
Your insistence on **no plant closures** and **no compulsory redundancies** may be understandable, but it is not sufficient on its own. You keep arguing as if those red lines should stand regardless of the economics. They cannot.

The expert has already said what we believe too: a non-closure package is only acceptable if it is **economically equivalent** in:
- fixed-cost reduction,
- capacity reduction,
- cash flow,
- and margin recovery.

Right now, that equivalence has **not** been demonstrated.

So we are not persuaded by the claim that every job-protecting instrument automatically equals restructuring. It does not. If the result leaves excess capacity in place or margins structurally impaired, then it is not a solution — it is delay.

We also reject the idea that closures are merely a bargaining weapon. They are an instrument of last resort if, and only if, the numbers require them.

### To the regional state government
We understand the political and regional concerns, but political discomfort does not change the financial arithmetic. If “no closures” is the starting point, then the substitute package has to be large enough to close the gap. So far, we have not seen that proof.

We also do not accept the framing that closures are automatically a failure of stewardship. If the economic model requires them and the alternative does not match the savings, then refusing them would be the failure — because it would preserve an uncompetitive cost base.

### To the controlling family
We agree with your focus on long-term value. But long-term value is not preserved by refusing hard choices if the economics demand them. We are aligned with you on one point: **no cosmetic compromise**.

Where we differ is that you sometimes seem to want a non-closure outcome on principle, provided it is “credible.” Credibility is not a feeling; it is a quantified bridge to competitive margins. Until that bridge is shown, closures remain a legitimate part of the restructuring toolset.

### To management
You are right that voluntary exits, redeployment, working-time measures, and productivity programs are not enough by themselves. But you still owe the market a proper side-by-side comparison:
- exact savings gap,
- plant-by-plant capacity effect,
- labor-cost bridge,
- management and owner contribution,
- capex reprioritization,
- and margin impact through 2030.

Without that, you are asking investors to accept a restructuring by assertion.

At the same time, we do **not** support a prolonged confrontation. Strike escalation, brand damage, and uncertainty are value-destructive. The company needs decisive action, but it also needs a settlement the market can underwrite.

## Our position on the expert’s assessment
The expert has made the correct point: a pure non-closure solution is **possible in principle**, but **not yet proven**.

That means:
- the employee side has not yet demonstrated equivalence,
- management has not yet proven necessity case-by-case,
- and investors should not be asked to sign off on either a weak compromise or an undefined threat.

So the burden now is not rhetoric. It is numbers.

## What would move us
We would support a no-closure package only if it can show, in audited form:
1. **Permanent capacity reduction**
2. **Substantial, durable labor-cost savings**
3. **Meaningful headcount reduction, not just time-buying**
4. **Verifiable productivity gains**
5. **Parallel management, owner, and overhead contributions**
6. **Clear cash payback and downside resilience**
7. **A credible path back to ROS target through 2030**

If those conditions are met, we can support the result even without closures. If they are not met, then we will not pretend that political comfort equals value creation.

## What we will not support
- A cosmetic compromise
- A package that leaves the cost base uncompetitive
- A deal based mainly on temporary measures
- A settlement that protects all current arrangements while asking shareholders to absorb the downside
- A prolonged industrial conflict that destroys brand and earnings
