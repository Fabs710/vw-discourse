Let me respond directly.

## Where I agree
I agree with everyone on the basic diagnosis:
- the current structure is **not sustainable**,
- a **cosmetic compromise** would fail,
- and the company needs a **real restructuring** to restore competitiveness and fund future products through 2030.

I also agree that any serious solution must be:
- measurable,
- durable,
- enforceable,
- and shared across management, owners, and labour.

On that, we are not apart.

## Where I disagree
What I do **not** accept is the attempt to turn “no closures” and “no compulsory redundancies” into absolute conditions regardless of the economics.

That position is not a restructuring plan. It is a **constraint**.  
And if that constraint prevents a permanent fix to overcapacity and above-target labour costs, then it simply shifts the crisis into the future.

The works council and union keep saying we are using closures as pressure. I reject that characterization.  
We are using the full range of instruments because the problem is structural, not cosmetic.

## To the works council
You say closures are a failure of imagination. I disagree.

What would be a failure of imagination is pretending that we can restore margins, reduce excess capacity, and reallocate capital to future products while leaving the cost base fundamentally unchanged.

You say:
- no closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- keep every plant open,
- binding security to 2030.

I have to be blunt: **I will not sign up to a framework that freezes the footprint regardless of economics**. That would lock in an uncompetitive structure and make the next crisis inevitable.

You ask for a quantified non-closure package. Fine. But it must deliver a **permanent structural effect**, not temporary relief.  
If the package does not materially reduce capacity and labour costs on a durable basis, then it does not solve the problem.

And no — a “social test” cannot override the financial reality. Co-determination matters, but it does not repeal arithmetic.

## To the union
You say closures are a bargaining threat, not a necessity. I disagree.

If the company has a shrinking European market, intense competition, and a factory cost base above target, then serious adjustment is required.  
Voluntary exits, retirement bridges, redeployment, and working-time tools help — but they are **not sufficient by themselves**.

You keep insisting on no compulsory redundancies. I understand the position. But if you rule out every hard instrument in advance, then you are effectively asking management to accept a weaker economic outcome. I will not do that.

If labour wants to avoid closures, then it needs to deliver a contribution that is:
- substantial,
- permanent,
- and verifiable.

Not just “fair” in principle — economically effective in fact.

## To the regional state government
I respect your role and your concern for regional stability. But I cannot accept the idea that political discomfort with closures should determine the company’s industrial footprint.

If the state wants to be constructive, then it should help secure:
- investment,
- competitiveness,
- and a durable employment base.

What it cannot do is insist on no closures and no compulsory redundancies while also expecting the company to reach ROS targets and finance technology through 2030.

That is not a strategy. It is a political boundary.  
And political boundaries do not change the economic task.

## To the controlling family
I agree with your focus on long-term value. That is exactly why we need hard structural decisions now.

You are right to reject a cosmetic compromise.  
Where I differ is that you still seem to want the outcome of restructuring without accepting that plant closures or deeper footprint change may be part of the mechanism.

I am open to alternatives — but only if they are economically equivalent in substance:
- real capacity reduction,
- real labour-cost reduction,
- real productivity gains,
- and a credible path back to margin target.

If that cannot be shown, then we are not protecting value; we are deferring impairment.

## To institutional investors
You are right to demand numbers, and I support that.  
But I also need to be equally clear: numbers cut both ways.

If a non-closure package is truly equivalent, show it.  
If it is not, then it is not reasonable to demand that management pretend otherwise.

And I will not accept the argument that any settlement which avoids closures is automatically preferable. That is not the test. The test is whether the company becomes competitive enough to survive and invest.

## On the expert assessment
The expert’s view was useful, and I agree with the core point: equivalence must be proven, not assumed.

But let’s not misuse that conclusion.

The expert did **not** say that a non-closure package is probably enough.  
The expert said the record does not yet demonstrate equivalence.

That means:
- the employee side has **not** proven their alternative,
- and management has **not** yet received a credible substitute with the same economic effect.

So the right conclusion is not “therefore closures are off the table.”  
The right conclusion is: **the burden of proof remains unmet**.

## What would move me
I would seriously consider a non-closure path only if it includes all of the following:
1. a measurable and durable reduction in capacity,
2. a substantial permanent labour-cost contribution,
3. a real headcount reduction,
4. verifiable productivity gains,
5. binding investment and product commitments,
6. and a credible return to the brand’s ROS target.

That is the minimum.

## What I will not accept
I will not accept:
- a cosmetic compromise,
- a permanent footprint guarantee that blocks restructuring,
- a settlement that leaves overcapacity in place,
- or a deal that asks shareholders to fund an uncompetitive structure indefinitely.

And I want to be explicit: **“no closures” is not a solution unless the alternative produces the same permanent economic effect.**

## Bottom line
We are open to a negotiated settlement.  
We are not open to a weak settlement.

If the employee side wants to avoid closures, then bring a package with:
- quantified savings,
- enforceable capacity reduction,
- a meaningful labour contribution,
- and a credible margin bridge through 2030.

If you cannot do that, then do not ask management to surrender the instruments needed to restore competitiveness.

If useful, I can now set out the **exact management threshold for accepting a no-closure package** in operational terms, so the discussion can move from rhetoric to proof.
