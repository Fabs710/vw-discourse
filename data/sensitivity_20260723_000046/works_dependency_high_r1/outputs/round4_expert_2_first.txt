Below is a neutral restructuring assessment of the specific question: **can a non-closure package credibly match the economics of a closure-led package, and what would “economic equivalence” need to mean in quantified terms?**

## Executive view

From an industrial economics perspective, **plant closure remains the strongest instrument for permanent fixed-cost and capacity reduction**. A non-closure package can sometimes get **close** on annual EBIT impact, but it usually does so by combining several weaker measures that are:
- less certain,
- slower to implement,
- more dependent on sustained labour cooperation,
- and more exposed to reversal.

So the core answer is:

- **Closure scenario:** highest certainty of durable cost and capacity reduction.
- **Non-closure scenario:** potentially workable, but only if it includes large, quantified, and enforceable reductions in headcount, shifts, overhead, and working time, plus investment/capex discipline and possibly owner-funded bridge measures.
- **Hybrid scenario:** often the most realistic economically, because it captures most of the savings from capacity rationalization while reducing execution risk relative to outright closure.

If management’s target is a **durable structural improvement through 2030**, then a no-closure package must be judged not by intent but by whether it can deliver roughly the same **annual run-rate EBIT improvement, cash relief, and capacity normalization** as the closure package.

---

## 1) What “economic equivalence” should mean

For a decision like this, equivalence should not mean “same narrative” or “same job count reduction.” It should mean **similar net impact on the operating model** across four measures:

1. **Annual fixed-cost reduction**
   - plant overhead,
   - salaried support,
   - maintenance,
   - utilities,
   - logistics,
   - depreciation/capacity underutilization burden where relevant.

2. **Capacity reduction**
   - fewer buildable units,
   - fewer shifts,
   - lower installed capacity,
   - improved utilization at remaining sites.

3. **Cash flow impact**
   - severance/restructuring cash outflows,
   - closure costs,
   - savings timing,
   - capex avoided or deferred,
   - working capital effects.

4. **Margin/EBIT impact through 2030**
   - annual run-rate improvement,
   - sustainability of savings,
   - sensitivity to volume and mix.

A credible test would be: **does the alternative package reach at least ~80–100% of the run-rate EBIT improvement of the closure case, with comparable durability and enforceability?**  
If not, it is economically inferior even if politically easier.

## 2) Illustrative scenario framework

Because the actual plant economics are not provided, I have to use **bounded estimates**. The figures below are **illustrative ranges**, not facts about this company.

### Assumptions for illustration
- Company is structurally overcapacitated in Europe/Germany.
- Affected German footprint has high fixed costs and below-target utilization.
- Management wants material improvement in contribution margin and overhead absorption.
- Savings are measured on an annual run-rate basis after implementation.

I model three broad options:

### Scenario A — Closure-led restructuring
- Closure of one or more German plants or equivalent permanent site shutdowns.
- Associated shift loss, headcount reduction, and overhead elimination.
- Greater one-time cash cost, highest permanent fixed-cost reduction.

### Scenario B — Non-closure restructuring
- No plant closures.
- Capacity reduction via shift cuts, internal line consolidation, product reallocations, working-time measures, voluntary exits, redeployment, salary/bonus restraint, and possibly pay concessions.
- Strong dependence on labour agreement and discipline in execution.

### Scenario C — Hybrid package
- No immediate plant closure, but meaningful site-level footprint reduction:
  - one or more lines idled,
  - shift removal,
  - partial production transfer,
  - potential later closure trigger if performance misses.
- Usually the best compromise between durability and execution risk.

## 3) Side-by-side quantified comparison

### A. Annual run-rate fixed-cost reduction
Indicative ranges as a share of the cost base of the affected German operations:

| Measure | Closure-led | Non-closure | Hybrid |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Plant overhead reduction | High | Medium | High |
| Salaried/support reduction | High | Medium | Medium-High |
| Utilities/maintenance/logistics | High | Low-Medium | Medium |
| Labour cost reduction | High | Medium | Medium-High |
| Total annual fixed-cost reduction | **~8–15%** of affected German cost base | **~4–9%** | **~7–13%** |

Interpretation:
- Closure-led restructuring is generally the **largest and most certain** source of permanent savings.
- Non-closure can get meaningful savings, but usually not all of the overhead elimination that closure creates.
- Hybrid often captures most of the savings if it truly removes production volume and complexity.

---

### B. Capacity impact
| Measure | Closure-led | Non-closure | Hybrid |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Permanent installed capacity reduction | **High** | Low-Medium | Medium-High |
| Shift reduction | High | High | High |
| Site count reduction | High | None | None or delayed |
| Flexibility loss | High | Low | Medium |
| Utilization improvement at surviving sites | High | Medium | High |

Interpretation:
- If the strategic issue is **excess capacity**, then a non-closure package must still reduce actual capacity, not just cost.
- Working-time tools alone do **not** solve capacity oversupply unless they are paired with line/shift removal and permanent output reallocation.

---

### C. Cash flow impact through implementation
| Measure | Closure-led | Non-closure | Hybrid |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Upfront restructuring cash outflow | **High** | Low-Medium | Medium |
| Near-term EBITDA relief | Medium | Medium | Medium |
| Timing of savings | Slower initially, then strong | Faster if labour deal is quick | Moderate |
| Breakeven on restructuring cash cost | 2–4 years typical | 1–3 years typical | 1.5–3.5 years typical |

Interpretation:
- Closure usually has the **highest upfront cash cost** but also the most reliable long-term savings.
- Non-closure may look cash-light initially, but if savings are partial or reversible, the long-term payoff is weaker.
- Hybrid often gives the best balance of cash and durability.

---

### D. Margin / EBIT effect
Illustrative annual run-rate EBIT uplift as a share of affected German operations:

| Scenario | EBIT uplift potential |
|---|---:|
| Closure-led | **High: ~€300m to €700m+** equivalent annual uplift, depending on footprint size |
| Non-closure | **Moderate: ~€150m to €450m** |
| Hybrid | **Moderate-High: ~€250m to €600m** |

Important:
- These are **illustrative** bands only.
- The actual number depends on plant size, labour intensity, transfer costs, product mix, and utilization.
- The market will care less about the label and more about the **credible annual run-rate improvement**.

## 4) What a non-closure package would need to include to be credible

A non-closure package only becomes economically serious if it includes several of the following, all quantified:

### Labor-cost measures
- wage moderation or temporary wage freeze,
- reduction or elimination of premiums/allowances,
- bonus restraint,
- longer working time with no proportional pay increase,
- lower time-account balances,
- more flexible shift arrangements,
- partial conversion of fixed pay to variable pay.

### Headcount measures
- voluntary exit programs,
- retirement bridges,
- natural attrition,
- internal redeployment,
- hiring freeze in non-critical areas.

### Capacity measures
- removal of shifts,
- line consolidation,
- model allocation changes,
- output transfer to other sites,
- SKU simplification.

### Corporate overhead / owner measures
- management overhead cuts,
- capex reprioritization,
- procurement savings,
- slower discretionary spending,
- reduced external service spend.

### Enforceability
- milestones by quarter,
- audit rights,
- automatic escalation if savings miss,
- predefined fallback actions.

Without enforceability, a non-closure package tends to drift into **temporary relief**, not structural restructuring.

---

## 5) Main sensitivity: volume and utilization

The biggest economic sensitivity is **volume**.

If European demand stays weak or falls further:
- closure-led restructuring becomes more attractive because it removes fixed-cost drag.
- non-closure measures become less sufficient, because underutilization persists.
- hybrid becomes the most practical solution if it can still remove enough installed capacity.

If volume stabilizes and product mix improves:
- non-closure is more viable.
- the gap between closure and non-closure narrows.
- the company may avoid some one-time closure costs.

So the key question is not just “can we cut costs?” but **can we match capacity to sustainable demand?**

---

## 6) My threshold for “economic equivalence”

If I were asked to define a practical threshold for equivalence, I would use this standard:

A non-closure package should be considered economically equivalent only if, over a 3-year horizon, it delivers:

1. **At least ~80% of the annual run-rate EBIT improvement** of the closure scenario,
2. **at least ~70–90% of the permanent capacity reduction**,
3. **no materially worse cash payback**,
4. and **enforceable milestones** that make the savings durable.

If it lands materially below that, it is not equivalent. It may still be a political compromise, but not an equivalent restructuring.

---

## 7) Practical conclusion

### Most likely economic ranking
1. **Closure-led restructuring** — strongest permanent economic effect, highest one-time pain, highest certainty of capacity correction.
2. **Hybrid restructuring** — often the best balance; captures much of the savings while reducing execution and governance risk.
3. **Pure non-closure restructuring** — possible, but usually the weakest on permanent capacity reduction unless labour and volume measures are unusually deep.

### Bottom line
From a restructuring finance standpoint, **a no-closure settlement can be viable only if it is not merely a labour-relations compromise but a genuine footprint and cost reset**. Otherwise it will likely fall short on:
- fixed-cost reduction,
- capacity correction,
- and durable margin recovery.
