I agree with one central point made by management, and I want to state that clearly: **this company needs a restructuring that is real, durable, and competitive**. If the result is only a postponement of the problem, that would destroy value for the owners and ultimately weaken the company’s future.

That said, I do **not** accept the idea that the only serious path is one built around plant closures and a one-sided transfer of the burden onto the workforce.

## Where I agree
- **The cost and capacity problem is real.** The German base must become more competitive.
- **A negotiated solution is preferable to a prolonged conflict.** Industrial peace matters for long-term value.
- **Everyone may need to contribute.** I am not starting from the assumption that only labour must adjust.

## Where I disagree
### To management
I do not accept a presentation of closures as if they were the only test of seriousness. A restructuring must be judged by whether it **restores margins and competitiveness**, not by whether it is the harshest possible on paper.

I also do not support any approach that creates **permanent damage to the enterprise**:
- no value-destructive instability,
- no erosion of the company’s industrial base without a compelling necessity,
- no settlement that looks decisive but actually undermines future performance.

If management wants support, it needs to show a **credible plan** that delivers:
1. substantial structural savings,
2. a viable footprint,
3. durable labour peace,
4. and a path back to profitability.

### To the works council and union
I understand the red lines you are setting, but I need to be equally frank: **“no closures, no compulsory redundancies” cannot automatically become “no material change.”** If the company’s structure remains unchanged in substance, then the problem is simply deferred and value keeps leaking away.

I can support alternatives to closures, but only if they are **economically equivalent in effect**:
- real capacity reduction,
- meaningful cost reduction,
- credible productivity gains,
- and enforceable commitments, not symbolic promises.

A solution that protects every current arrangement while asking the owners to absorb the consequences indefinitely is **not acceptable**.

### To the regional state government
I respect the role of the state as an anchor shareholder and the importance of co-determination. But I also need to be clear that governance cannot become a mechanism for freezing structural correction.

I agree that plant closures are not a starting point I would choose lightly. But I would also not support an outcome that simply rules out hard decisions in advance. The company must remain competitive, and that means the restructuring has to produce **real economic effect**, not just political reassurance.

## My position
The family holding is prepared to back a settlement that:
- restores competitiveness,
- protects long-term enterprise value,
- avoids unnecessary instability,
- and preserves the company’s governance integrity.

What I will **not** back:
- a cosmetic compromise,
- a deal that leaves overcapacity untouched,
- a settlement that permanently impairs value,
- or a process that weakens ownership control or forces governance to surrender to short-term pressure.

## What would move me
I would be prepared to support a negotiated path **if** it contains:
- measurable and durable cost reductions,
- meaningful capacity adjustment,
- a fair but limited employee contribution,
- management contribution as well,
- and a credible investment and product commitment for the German base.

That is the standard.  
Not “no change,” and not “closure at any cost,” but **competitiveness with stability**.

So my message is simple:  
I am open to an alternative to closures, but only if it is **serious enough to restore the business**. If it is not, then I will not lend support to it.
