We agree on the objective that matters most to us: **the company must return to competitive margins and a sustainable cost base**. On that point, I hear strong alignment across the table.

But I need to be equally direct: **we will not support a settlement that is only a political or social compromise while leaving the business structurally uncompetitive**. For us, that is the key test.

## Where we agree
- The status quo is not acceptable.
- A restructuring is necessary.
- Industrial peace has value if it is durable and credible.
- The company needs a path that restores profitability and preserves investment capacity.
- Management, owners, and labour all need to contribute in some form.

## Where we disagree

### To the works council and union
We respect the red lines you have stated, but from an investor perspective they are only acceptable **if** you can show a package that produces **comparable, durable savings**.

What we will not accept is:
- no closures, no compulsory redundancies, **and** no meaningful cost reduction,
- or a “site protection” outcome that simply defers the problem and keeps the cost base above competitors.

A no-closure principle is not enough on its own. We need to see:
- real capacity reduction,
- verifiable labour-cost improvement,
- productivity gains,
- and an enforceable timetable.

If the alternative to closures is just voluntary attrition and vague redeployment, that is not a solution. It is delay.

### To management
We support decisive restructuring, but we do **not** support a value-destructive confrontation that drags on, damages the brand, and weakens near-term margins through prolonged uncertainty and strikes.

If management’s plan depends on maximal disruption to force concessions, that creates execution risk that we will discount heavily. We want **certainty, speed, and durable savings** — not a showdown that burns cash.

We also do not want a cosmetic deal dressed up as bold restructuring. If the cost base remains structurally too high, then the market will punish the company and shareholders will pay for that.

### To the regional state government
We understand the political constraints, but as capital providers we cannot treat regional sensitivities as a substitute for economic reality.

A settlement that protects every site but leaves the business weak is not value-preserving. We need competitiveness first. If the state blocks all meaningful adjustment, then the burden simply shifts onto investors through lower returns and ongoing underperformance. That is not acceptable.

### To the controlling family
We appreciate the focus on long-term value and industrial stability. That said, “competitiveness with stability” must not become code for preserving too much of the existing structure.

If the family wants investor confidence, it should support:
- a credible footprint reduction,
- permanent cost lowering,
- and a deal that can actually lift margins.

A halfway settlement that avoids the hard choices will not restore confidence in the equity story.

## Our position
We are prepared to support **any** outcome that credibly delivers:
1. a durable reduction in the cost base,
2. a credible path back to competitive margins,
3. a clear timetable and measurable implementation,
4. and sufficient certainty to avoid prolonged industrial conflict.

We are **not** prepared to support:
- a cosmetic compromise,
- a prolonged standoff that destroys value,
- or a restructuring that leaves the company structurally impaired.

## Bottom line
If there is a negotiated package that achieves the needed savings without closures, we can back it.  
If not, then management must not be blocked from taking the measures necessary to restore competitiveness.

What matters to us is not the instrument chosen in principle. What matters is whether the outcome **actually fixes the margin problem**.
