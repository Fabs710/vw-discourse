I’ll respond plainly.

## Where I agree
I agree with everyone on one core point: **this company needs a serious restructuring**. The current cost and capacity structure is not sustainable if we want to restore margins, fund technology, and protect long-term enterprise value.

I also agree that **a cosmetic compromise would be worse than useless**. If we merely postpone the problem, the business leaks value, the pressure returns, and the eventual adjustment becomes harsher.

And I agree that **industrial peace matters**. A prolonged confrontation would damage the brand, waste cash, and undermine the very competitiveness we are all talking about.

## Where I disagree

### To the works council and union
I respect the red lines you are setting, but I do **not** accept that they can stand as absolute barriers **unless** you can show a restructuring with **comparable structural effect**.

“No plant closures” and “no compulsory redundancies” are understandable political positions. But they are not, by themselves, a business solution.

If the company keeps essentially the same footprint and headcount structure, then we do not solve the competitiveness problem — we just defer it. That is not acceptable to the owners, and it should not be acceptable to anyone who is serious about preserving jobs in the long run.

I am open to alternatives to closures, but only if they deliver:
- real and durable capacity reduction,
- a meaningful labour-cost contribution,
- productivity gains that are enforceable,
- and a footprint that is economically viable, not merely politically comfortable.

What I cannot support is a package that protects every existing arrangement and expects the owners to absorb the consequences indefinitely.

### To management
I support the need for deeper structural change, but I do **not** support a framing that treats plant closures or hard cuts as the only credible path.

If management wants support, it must present a **credible, quantified alternative path**. Threatening closure is not enough. The argument has to be operational, not rhetorical.

At the same time, I want to be clear that I will not back a deal that leaves overcapacity untouched or only delivers temporary measures. Temporary fixes are not value-preserving; they are value leakage with a delay.

So my position is not “closures at any cost,” and it is not “no change.” It is: **show me a package that actually restores competitiveness**.

### To the regional state government
I respect the state’s role and the importance of co-determination. But I cannot accept an approach that effectively freezes industrial adjustment because of political discomfort.

A state-supported outcome is only helpful if it is also economically credible. If it protects every site while failing to fix the cost base, it does not preserve the company — it postpones a harder problem.

### To institutional investors
I agree with your insistence on verifiable, durable savings. That is the right test.

Where I disagree is any suggestion that only severe and visible cuts count. The test is not how harsh the announcement sounds; the test is whether the economics actually improve.

If an alternative package can produce equivalent savings through capacity reduction, redeployment, working-time changes, voluntary exits, and negotiated labour contributions, then that is acceptable. If it cannot, then I will not support pretending it is enough.

## My position
The controlling family will support a settlement if it meets these conditions:

1. **It restores competitiveness and margins**
2. **It delivers durable structural savings**
3. **It avoids value-destructive instability**
4. **It preserves governance control**
5. **It does not merely postpone the adjustment**

That means I am open on the instrument, but not on the outcome.

## What would move me
To be concrete, I would support a negotiated package if it includes:
- measurable capacity reductions,
- a substantial permanent cost contribution,
- a credible plan for labour flexibility and productivity,
- management and owner contributions as well,
- and binding commitments on investment and future products in Germany.

That is the standard.

## What I will not support
I will not support:
- a cosmetic compromise,
- a settlement that leaves the company structurally uncompetitive,
- an arrangement that permanently impairs value,
- or any approach that weakens ownership governance.

So my message is simple:  
**No, I am not backing a “no-change” compromise.**  
But **yes, I am open to a non-closure solution if it is economically equivalent and durable**.

If the employee side can bring that, we can work with it. If not, then insisting on red lines alone will not solve the crisis.
