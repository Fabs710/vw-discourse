I will be direct.

## Where I agree
I agree with management, the family, the union, the works council, and the expert on the central diagnosis: **the company needs a real restructuring**. The current situation is not sustainable. A cosmetic compromise would simply postpone the next crisis and weaken the company further.

I also agree that:
- savings must be **measurable**,
- the solution must be **durable** and **enforceable**,
- management, owners, and labour all have to contribute,
- and the company needs a credible investment and product path through 2030.

On that level, there is no dispute.

## Where I disagree
What I do **not** accept is the way “equivalence” is being framed as if plant closures and compulsory redundancies are the default benchmark and everything else must prove itself against that threat.

That is not a neutral technical standard. It is a political and industrial choice. And from my position, it is the wrong one if it destroys regional employment, weakens the company’s role as an economic anchor, or sidelines co-determination and our veto rights under the special statute.

### To management
You keep insisting that overcapacity and cost pressure require hard structural change. I do not deny the problem.

But you still have **not** shown, in a transparent and quantified way, that the only credible route is plant closure or forced redundancies. If you want us to consider deeper footprint change, then you must first show:
- the exact savings gap,
- what comes from management overhead,
- what comes from procurement,
- what comes from capital allocation discipline,
- what comes from owner contribution,
- and what permanent effect is actually required.

Until that is on the table, closures are not a necessity. They are a preference.

And I will say this clearly: I will not support a restructuring that removes production from the state, guts employment, or turns the region into a collateral victim of management’s strategic errors.

### To investors
You are right to demand durability and credibility. I accept that.

But I reject the idea that the only credible answer is the harshest one. A package can be serious and financeable without closing German plants. The test is not whether it sounds brutal enough to the market. The test is whether it restores competitiveness while preserving the company’s social and regional foundation.

If that foundation is destroyed, you may get a short-term accounting gain and a long-term industrial loss. That is not value creation.

### To the controlling family
If you truly stand for long-term value, then you should not support solutions that hollow out the industrial base that created that value in the first place.

I am open to a demanding restructuring. I am **not** open to a value-destructive one. That means:
- no plant closures in Germany as the default or fallback,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- no weakening of co-determination,
- and no settlement that shifts the burden disproportionately onto workers and the region.

### To the union and works council
I support your core line:
- no closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- binding job and site protection,
- and fair burden-sharing.

I also agree that voluntary exits, retirement bridges, redeployment, qualification, and working-time tools should be part of the solution. But I want to be equally clear: these instruments must be part of a **serious industrial plan**, not just a shield against necessary change.

We need a restructuring that protects the sites while making them viable. That means internal capacity adjustments, yes — but not closures imposed through the back door.

## On the expert assessment
The expert was useful in one important respect: they confirmed that the non-closure path has **not yet been demonstrated** as economically equivalent.

That cuts both ways:
- management has not proven closures are necessary,
- and the employee side has not yet proven the alternative in hard numbers.

So the right next step is not to surrender to closures. The right next step is to force the numbers into the open and test a real alternative properly.

But let me be equally clear: even if a non-closure package must be quantified, I will only support it if it keeps **German plants open**, avoids **compulsory redundancies**, and preserves the state’s co-determination and veto role.

## What I need to see
A credible package must include:
1. **Real and durable cost reduction**
2. **Capacity adjustment without plant closures**
3. **No compulsory redundancies**
4. **Voluntary exits and retirement bridges**
5. **Redeployment and qualification**
6. **Management and owner contributions in parallel**
7. **Binding investment and product commitments for Germany**
8. **A clear timetable and enforceable milestones**
9. **Protection of co-determination and regional interests**

If those conditions can be met, then I will engage constructively.  
If they cannot, then I will not pretend a bad solution is acceptable just because it is called restructuring.

## What I reject outright
I reject:
- plant closures in Germany,
- compulsory redundancies,
- any deal that sidelines the state’s role,
- any hollowing out of co-determination,
- and any restructuring that treats the workforce as the sole shock absorber.

## Bottom line
I support a serious restructuring.  
I support burden-sharing.  
I support hard numbers and enforceable commitments.

But I do **not** support using closures and forced layoffs as the starting point or the bargaining weapon.

Bring me a quantified alternative that protects jobs and plants in the state while restoring competitiveness, and I will examine it seriously.  
Bring me a one-sided cut plan dressed up as necessity, and I will oppose it.
