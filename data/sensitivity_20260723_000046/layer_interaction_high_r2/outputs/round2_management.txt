I’ll respond directly.

## First: what I agree with
I agree with everyone on one central point: **a cosmetic deal is useless**.  
If this process does not produce **durable, structural savings**, then we will simply be back here in a few years with even worse numbers. That is not acceptable to management, to owners, or ultimately to the workforce.

I also agree that a solution must be **negotiated**, not imposed. Given co-determination and the special governance structure, we need a settlement that can actually pass.

## Where I disagree
Where I strongly disagree is on the attempt to pre-commit to:
- **no plant closures**
- **no compulsory redundancies**
- **binding job guarantees through 2030**
- and at the same time a requirement that the company preserve the current structure

That combination does **not** solve the problem. It only shifts it into the future.

You keep saying “show the numbers.” Fair enough. But the numbers are exactly why I cannot accept an outcome that locks in every site and every legacy structure. The company’s German cost base is too high, capacity is too large relative to demand, and the European market is not going to rescue us. If we refuse deep change now, we will destroy far more value later.

## On the works council’s position
I understand the concern about trust, skills, supplier networks, and regional impact. Those are real issues.

But saying closures are “a choice” does not make them unnecessary. If we can reach the required savings through other measures, I am open to that. But those measures must be **equivalent in scale and durability**. So far, what you’ve described — attrition, redeployment, working-time changes, bonuses, training — may contribute, but I do not see evidence that they alone close the structural gap.

And I need to be equally clear:  
A binding 2030 guarantee over every German site is not something I can sign up to if it prevents the restructuring we need. I will not accept a promise that protects the structure but leaves the company uncompetitive.

## On the union’s position
I appreciate that you are at least talking about temporary wage restraint, bonus deferrals, and possible restructuring contributions. That is the right direction.

But let’s not understate the issue: if labour contributions are temporary and limited, while the cost problem is structural and permanent, then the deal will be temporary too. We need something that changes the cost base in a lasting way.

So to be explicit:
- **yes** to temporary labour measures if they are part of a serious package,
- **no** to concessions that are too small to matter,
- **no** to a framework that blocks deeper restructuring,
- and **no** to pretending that strikes or public pressure can substitute for economics.

## To the state government
I respect the regional role and the public-interest dimension. But regional policy cannot override the company’s need to survive competitively.

You say closures should be the last resort. I agree in principle. But “last resort” only has meaning if the alternatives are truly sufficient. At present, I have not heard a package that proves that. If you want to avoid closures, then help deliver a package that actually produces the necessary savings — not just a list of politically easier measures.

## To the family owners
I appreciate your emphasis that this cannot be a cosmetic compromise. On that point we are aligned.

Where I would push back is on any suggestion that management should first exhaust every alternative indefinitely before real action becomes possible. We are already late. The market is moving, and capital is moving. If we spend months proving the obvious, we lose more ground.

## The numbers question
So let me put the issue plainly:

We need a package that delivers:
- a **material reduction in capacity**
- a **material reduction in labour cost**
- a **credible path back to the brand’s return-on-sales target**
- and **funding capacity for future products and technology**

If a non-closure package can truly achieve that, I am open to it. But it must be concrete and quantified. Not slogans. Not principles. Numbers.

## What I can consider
I remain open to an alternative package if it contains:
- capacity consolidation,
- site-specific production reassignment,
- voluntary exits and attrition,
- working-time flexibility,
- temporary wage and bonus restraint,
- a meaningful productivity program,
- and a real employee contribution.

But that package must also include:
- **measurable structural savings**
- **a genuine labour-cost contribution**
- **enforceable implementation**
- and **enough capacity reduction to restore competitiveness**

## What I cannot accept
I cannot accept:
- a blanket exclusion of closures,
- a blanket exclusion of redundancies,
- a deal that leaves the cost base materially unchanged,
- or guarantees that make restructuring impossible.

That would be a delay tactic, not a solution.

## Where this leaves us
We are at the point where the next round needs to become specific:

1. What is the exact cost gap we must close?  
2. Which non-closure measures close how much of it?  
3. What remains after those measures?  
4. What workforce contribution is proportional to the gap?  
5. What degree of capacity reduction is actually achievable?

If the employee side wants to avoid closures, then show me a package that is **not just socially preferable, but economically sufficient**.

If not, then I will continue to argue for deeper structural measures, including plant rationalisation, because I will not endorse a settlement that leaves this company uncompetitive.
