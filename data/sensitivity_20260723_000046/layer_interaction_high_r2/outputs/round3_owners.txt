I will be equally direct.

## Where I agree
I agree with management on the central diagnosis: **this is a structural competitiveness problem, not a temporary wobble**. If the German cost base stays above target and capacity remains misaligned with demand, then the company will continue to underperform and will not generate the cash needed for future investment.

I also agree with the experts and the investors that **delay is expensive**. A drawn-out confrontation that postpones hard decisions would destroy value, weaken the brand, and eventually hurt jobs more than a disciplined restructuring would.

And I agree with the state and the employee side on one important point: **a cosmetic deal is useless**. We need a durable package, not another short-lived truce.

## Where I disagree
I do **not** accept the position that a full, permanent ban on plant closures and compulsory redundancies can be the starting point of a serious settlement.

That position may be understandable politically, but it is not automatically compatible with restoring competitiveness. If the company’s structural cost base cannot be brought down enough through alternatives, then insisting on absolute prohibitions would simply freeze the problem and transfer the damage to future years. I will not support that.

At the same time, I do **not** accept management’s attempt to treat closures as if they were already the answer before the alternatives have been properly tested. The expert assessment did **not** say closures are the only serious route. It said non-closure measures can be substantial, but may or may not be enough depending on the size of the gap.

That means the correct position is not “closures at any cost,” and it is also not “no closures under any circumstances.”

## On the numbers
The finance assessment is useful because it makes clear this is not a small-gap problem. If the brand is materially below its sustainable return-on-sales target, then the required improvement is likely in the **low single-digit billions of euros annually** at brand level. That is a serious structural challenge.

So I accept this:
- non-closure measures can and should contribute materially,
- but they must be **quantified, durable, and large enough to matter**,
- and if they leave a substantial residual gap, deeper footprint reduction cannot be ruled out.

What I will not accept is a package that delivers a “socially pleasant” result but leaves the company structurally uncompetitive.

## To the works council
You are right to insist that trust, skills, and social peace matter. They absolutely do. A strategy that triggers prolonged conflict would be value-destructive.

But I have to say plainly: your current red lines are too absolute to be a credible basis for final governance approval if the numbers do not work out.

A binding 2030 guarantee for every site, combined with no closures and no compulsory redundancies, may be a negotiator’s starting position. It is **not** automatically a viable end state for the owners if it prevents the company from reaching competitiveness.

So my position is:
- I am open to **strong employment and site protections**,
- I am open to **time-limited guarantees** tied to a serious industrial plan,
- I am open to **no forced layoffs if alternatives genuinely deliver**,
- but I will not endorse a permanent straightjacket that blocks necessary restructuring.

## To the union
I appreciate that you are willing to discuss temporary wage restraint, deferred payments, bonus reductions, working-time flexibility, productivity, and an employee contribution fund. That is constructive.

But temporary concessions alone do not fix a permanent cost problem. If the economics require durable savings, then the package has to be durable too.

I also want to be clear: a deal that asks only labour to contribute, while management and owners are left with no visible discipline, will not be supported from the governance side. Shared sacrifice must be real, not rhetorical.

That includes:
- executive restraint,
- disciplined capital allocation,
- and visible commitment to Germany’s future product pipeline.

## To management
You are right that we cannot spend months debating abstractions. But you still have to prove the residual gap. I want the numbers in a form the supervisory board can actually use:

1. exact savings target,
2. contribution from attrition, redeployment, working-time changes, bonus restraint, productivity,
3. remaining gap after those measures,
4. and which restructuring instruments close that gap.

If the remaining gap is small enough to bridge through a negotiated non-closure package, then that is preferable. If it is not, then management must say so clearly and not hide behind procedural impatience.

What I will not support is an indefinite cycle of “prove every alternative” used as a tactic to preserve the status quo.

## To the state government
I respect the state’s role and its blocking rights. But stewardship also means being honest about economics.

A regional anchor is only an anchor if it remains competitive. If the company becomes weaker because necessary restructuring is blocked, the region loses more in the end. So I support the state being part of a negotiated solution — but not as an absolute veto on all hard choices.

## On the expert assessment
The expert made the key point: non-closure measures can produce real savings, but they often only partially substitute for footprint reduction when overcapacity is structural.

That is exactly why I do **not** accept the employee side’s attempt to turn “closures are painful” into “closures are therefore unacceptable.”

Painful does not mean avoidable. The question is whether the company can reach competitive margins without them. If it can, fine. If it cannot, then we should not pretend otherwise.

## What I can support
I can support a package with:
- meaningful capacity consolidation,
- attrition and voluntary exits,
- internal redeployment,
- working-time flexibility,
- temporary labour-cost restraint,
- productivity and overhead reduction,
- executive restraint,
- and binding investment commitments in Germany,

**if** it is quantified and durable enough to restore competitiveness.

I can also support a **time-limited no-closure framework** if it includes:
- clear savings milestones,
- a joint verification process,
- and a review point that does not automatically block future structural action if the numbers fail.

What I cannot support is:
- a blanket exclusion of closures,
- a blanket exclusion of compulsory redundancies,
- or guarantees that eliminate governance flexibility regardless of economic outcome.

## Bottom line
My position is simple:

- **I support a negotiated restructuring.**
- **I support real labour and management contributions.**
- **I support avoiding closures if the numbers prove that is sufficient.**
- **I do not support locking the company into an uncompetitive structure.**

So the next step should be factual and decisive:
- the exact savings target,
- the quantified effect of each non-closure measure,
- the residual gap,
- and a clear judgment on whether that gap can be closed without irreversible footprint reduction.

If yes, I will back a non-closure solution.  
If no, then deeper structural measures must remain on the table.
