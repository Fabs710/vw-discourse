Below is a neutral, numbers-based bridge from **non-closure measures** to the point where **footprint reduction becomes economically necessary**.

I will keep this within industrial restructuring and automotive manufacturing economics. Where figures are not company-specific, they are **scenario estimates** based on typical large German OEM cost structures.

---

# 1) Core conclusion

A negotiated package **without plant closures** can often deliver **roughly €1.5–2.5 billion per year** of recurring savings in a large German passenger-car operation if it combines:

- attrition / hiring freeze
- redeployment
- working-time changes
- bonus restraint
- temporary wage restraint
- productivity and overhead cuts
- management and executive restraint
- investment / product allocation improvements

That is a **material** amount.  
But it is not always enough.

## Practical decision threshold
- If the required recurring improvement is around **€1.5–2.0 billion p.a.**, a no-closure package can be credible.
- If the required improvement is around **€2.5 billion p.a. or more**, the package becomes strained.
- If the required improvement is around **€3.0 billion p.a. or more**, **footprint reduction** usually becomes economically dominant.

---

# 2) Savings bridge by measure

## A. Attrition / hiring freeze
**Estimated recurring savings:**
- Low: **€150–250m**
- Central: **€250–400m**
- High: **€400–600m**

**Logic:**  
This is slow but durable. It works best where retirement-eligible headcount is sizable and vacancies can remain unfilled.

---

## B. Internal redeployment / transfers
**Estimated recurring savings:**
- Low: **€100–200m**
- Central: **€200–350m**
- High: **€300–500m**

**Logic:**  
This mainly avoids external hiring and reduces surplus headcount. It is not a full cost cure, but it contributes.

---

## C. Working-time changes / shift optimization
**Estimated recurring savings:**
- Low: **€150–250m**
- Central: **€250–450m**
- High: **€400–700m**

**Logic:**  
This comes from fewer paid idle hours, lower shift premiums per unit, and better line loading.

---

## D. Bonus restraint / deferred payments
**Estimated recurring savings:**
- Low: **€100–200m**
- Central: **€200–350m**
- High: **€300–500m**

**Logic:**  
Useful as bridge relief, but only partly structural unless it becomes embedded in base compensation architecture.

---

## E. Temporary wage restraint / labor-cost contribution
**Estimated recurring savings:**
- Low: **€300–500m**
- Central: **€600–900m**
- High: **€900m–1.3bn**

**Logic:**  
This is usually the largest non-closure lever.  
It is also the hardest to negotiate and the most likely to be temporary unless converted into a lasting change.

---

## F. Productivity and overhead reduction
**Estimated recurring savings:**
- Low: **€200–350m**
- Central: **€400–700m**
- High: **€700m–1.0bn**

**Logic:**  
This includes line efficiency, scrap reduction, maintenance optimization, indirect headcount cuts, and corporate overhead reduction.

---

## G. Executive restraint / management overhead
**Estimated recurring savings:**
- Low: **€50–100m**
- Central: **€100–200m**
- High: **€200–300m**

**Logic:**  
Small in absolute terms, but symbolically important and usually required for deal legitimacy.

---

## H. Investment commitments / platform allocation
**Estimated recurring economic effect:**
- Low: **€100–200m**
- Central: **€200–400m**
- High: **€400m+**

**Logic:**  
This is not a direct labor-cost saving. It improves plant loading, reduces duplication, and protects future throughput.

# 4) What is actually realistic?

For a major German OEM with strong co-determination, I would treat the **most credible negotiated range** as:

> **€1.5–2.5 billion p.a.**

That is the range most likely to be achievable without plant closures, if the package is serious and enforceable.

The high case is possible only if:
- surplus headcount is very large,
- wage concessions are deep and durable,
- and productivity measures are implemented quickly and credibly.

---

# 5) When footprint reduction becomes economically dominant

Footprint reduction becomes the dominant answer when one or more of the following holds:

## A. Residual gap remains above roughly €0.7–1.0bn p.a.
If the non-closure package still leaves that much recurring shortfall, management will usually need:
- line exits,
- sub-activity closures,
- plant rationalization,
- or full site closure.

## B. Utilization stays structurally low
Even after labor concessions, a low-load plant still carries:
- utilities
- maintenance
- logistics
- depreciation
- fixed overhead

Those costs do not fall enough with headcount cuts alone.

## C. The market outlook is weak through 2030
If European demand remains flat or declines, future volume cannot be assumed to absorb excess capacity.

## D. Product allocation is insufficient
If next-gen EV or platform volumes are not allocated to the German footprint, then headcount savings alone often delay rather than solve the problem.

---

# 6) Scenario interpretation

## Scenario 1: No-closure negotiated package
**Likely savings:** **€1.5–2.5bn p.a.**

Works if:
- the required gap is moderate
- labor concessions are real
- product allocation supports utilization

Risk:
- savings may be insufficient if overcapacity is severe

---

## Scenario 2: Hybrid package with limited footprint reduction
Examples:
- no compulsory redundancies
- voluntary exits
- closure of sub-activities
- partial line exits
- site downsizing rather than total closure

**Extra savings beyond no-closure package:**  
roughly **€0.5–1.5bn p.a.**

This is often the economically cleaner compromise if the non-closure package leaves a material gap.

---

## Scenario 3: Full closure / major site exit
**Additional recurring savings:** often **€1.0–2.0bn+ p.a.**, depending on site size and product load.

This is the strongest fixed-cost step, but also the most disruptive and slowest to execute in Germany.

---

# 7) Time to realize savings

A key issue is not just the annual run-rate, but the timing.

Typical realization:
- **Partial savings:** 6–18 months
- **Material savings:** 18–36 months
- **Full structural effect:** often **3+ years**

So a package can be economically credible on paper but still fail the earnings window if execution is slow.

---

# 8) Bottom line

## The likely answer to the dispute
- A **serious non-closure package** can plausibly deliver **€1.5–2.5bn p.a.**
- That may be enough if the true gap is moderate
- If the required improvement is materially above that, then **capacity reduction and footprint rationalization** become economically difficult to avoid

## Clean decision rule
Use a jointly audited bridge:
1. exact annual savings target
2. savings from each non-closure measure
3. residual gap
4. time to realization
5. durability through 2030

If the residual gap is still large after the package, footprint reduction becomes the economically dominant option.
