Below is a neutral restructuring assessment focused on **how much savings non-closure measures can plausibly deliver, how they compare with plant closure, and what implementation risks matter**. I’ll stay within industrial restructuring, operations, and labour-cost economics.

## 1) Core conclusion

For a large German carmaker with **structural overcapacity, high domestic fixed costs, and a weak demand outlook in Europe**, a **non-closure package can meaningfully reduce cost**, but in most realistic cases it **does not fully substitute for one or more plant closures** if management’s target is a **large, durable step-down in the cost base**.

### Practical rule of thumb
- **Non-closure measures** can often deliver roughly **30–60% of the savings associated with a plant closure** if they are ambitious and well executed.
- If the company needs a **very large permanent reduction** in capacity and fixed cost, then **some closure or equivalent irreversible footprint reduction** usually becomes necessary.
- If the required improvement is more moderate, a combination of **capacity consolidation, working-time adjustment, attrition, redeployment, and labour-cost concessions** can sometimes bridge most of the gap without closure.

That said, the answer depends heavily on the **size of the gap**.

---

## 2) What each measure typically contributes

The following ranges are indicative for a large German passenger-car operation. Actual values depend on product mix, labour agreements, automation level, and how much fixed overhead can really be removed.

### A. Capacity consolidation without closure
This means shifting production between sites, lowering line count, rationalising models, and running fewer shifts or fewer variants.

**Likely savings:**
- **Moderate:** removes some variable and semi-fixed costs
- Typical annual effect: **€100m–€400m** per major site rationalisation package
- If it materially reduces an entire plant’s output but keeps the site open, savings are often **well below full closure value** because the site overhead remains.

**Durability:** medium to high  
**Risk:** medium  
**Key limitation:** you often keep the expensive infrastructure, site overhead, and political burden.

---

### B. Working-time changes
Examples: shorter working time, shift redesign, fewer overtime hours, reduced premiums, more flexible rosters.

**Likely savings:**
- Usually **single-digit to low double-digit percent** of direct labour cost, depending on baseline
- Annual effect across a large workforce: often **€50m–€200m**, sometimes more if overtime and shift premiums are very high

**Durability:** medium  
**Risk:** medium  
**Key limitation:** useful for cyclical smoothing, but not a full substitute for structural capacity reduction.

---

### C. Attrition and voluntary exits
No forced layoffs; workforce shrinks through retirement, turnover, voluntary severance.

**Likely savings:**
- Annual payroll reduction can become meaningful over time
- Typical effect in the near term is limited; over **3–5 years** it can remove a substantial number of positions
- Savings depend on whether vacancies are truly not backfilled

**Durability:** high if enforced  
**Risk:** low to medium  
**Key limitation:** too slow if the company needs immediate fixed-cost relief.

---

### D. Redeployment / internal transfers
Move staff from declining areas to growing ones, or from one plant to another.

**Likely savings:**
- Mostly avoids redundancy costs and retains skills
- Direct cash savings are modest; it is more of a **cost-avoidance** measure than a cost-cutting measure

**Durability:** high  
**Risk:** medium to high operationally  
**Key limitation:** works only if there is genuinely spare demand elsewhere and skill compatibility.

---

### E. Temporary labour-cost concessions
Examples: bonus cuts, wage freezes, deferred payments, temporary hours-for-pay adjustments.

**Likely savings:**
- Often **material in year 1–3**
- Annual effect might be **€100m–€300m+** in a large OEM if the base is broad and the concession is substantial

**Durability:** low to medium unless converted into structural change  
**Risk:** low to medium industrially, high if seen as one-sided  
**Key limitation:** if temporary, it buys time but does not solve the long-term cost problem.

---

### F. Productivity and process improvement
Examples: cycle-time reduction, automation, indirect labour cuts, logistics simplification, better OEE, variant reduction.

**Likely savings:**
- Often the most underrated lever
- If executed well: **€100m–€500m+** annually across a large production network
- But it requires disciplined implementation, capex, and management attention

**Durability:** high if sustained  
**Risk:** medium to high execution risk  
**Key limitation:** rarely fast enough on its own.

---

## 3) What a closure usually saves

A German plant closure has a very different profile because it removes:
- direct labour,
- indirect labour,
- local overhead,
- maintenance,
- utilities,
- logistics complexity,
- and some corporate support cost.

### Typical savings profile
A closure at a major auto assembly or powertrain site can generate:
- **€300m–€800m+ annual run-rate savings** for a large, high-cost plant, depending on size and complexity
- plus a one-time restructuring charge that can be very large

This is why closures are so powerful: they eliminate **fixed cost**, not just variable cost.

### Important caveat
If production is simply shifted elsewhere at high transport cost, with duplicated tooling or lost scale, the net benefit can be lower. But in most cases, **closure remains the most decisive lever** for permanent overcapacity.

---

## 4) Can non-closure measures match closure savings?

### Short answer
**Sometimes partly, rarely fully, and usually only with a large, multi-year package.**

### Plausible equivalence range
A well-designed non-closure package might replace:
- **one-third to one-half** of the economic benefit of a plant closure in the first phase
- and perhaps more if it includes:
  - deep headcount reduction through attrition,
  - major shift/working-time redesign,
  - supplier and overhead rationalisation,
  - and strong productivity gains

But if the company needs to remove a **large block of permanent fixed cost**, non-closure tools usually run into limits:
- the site overhead remains,
- management complexity remains,
- some labour agreements remain sticky,
- and the savings may be less durable.

### When non-closure is most credible
It is credible if:
1. The capacity gap is **moderate**, not severe.
2. There is **unused flexibility** in shifts, model allocation, and internal transfers.
3. The company can make **binding workforce reductions without closures** over time.
4. Wage and bonus concessions are large enough to matter.
5. Product investment is redirected to keep the remaining German footprint busy.

### When closure becomes hard to avoid
Closure or equivalent site exit becomes likely if:
1. There is a **persistent structural demand shortfall**.
2. The site has **high fixed costs** and no realistic future model allocation.
3. The company needs **fast cash relief**.
4. Non-closure measures would only defer the same decision.

---

## 5) Comparative durability

### Plant closure
**Durability:** very high  
**Economic effect:** strongest, immediate fixed-cost removal  
**Risks:** high labour, legal, political, execution, and supply-chain disruption

### Non-closure restructuring
**Durability:** medium to high depending on design  
**Economic effect:** can be substantial, but often slower and less complete  
**Risks:** industrial conflict, “savings leakage,” partial reversibility, underdelivery

### Temporary concessions
**Durability:** low unless converted into permanent structural change  
**Economic effect:** good for bridge financing, not sufficient alone  
**Risks:** rebound of costs later, recurring renegotiation

## 6) Implementation pathways

## Scenario A: Deep non-closure package
This is the best-case alternative to closure.

### Components
- shift reduction / line consolidation
- internal redeployment across German sites
- attrition and voluntary exits
- working-time reductions and reduced premiums
- temporary wage restraint
- productivity program
- limited product investment commitments

### Likely outcome
- Can generate **material savings**
- Can preserve sites
- Usually needs **3–5 years** to fully mature
- Requires strict governance and measurable milestones

### Main risk
Savings may be **too slow** or **too small** if the overcapacity is large.

---

## Scenario B: Hybrid package
A mix of non-closure measures plus one or more hard footprint cuts.

### Components
- non-closure measures on a broad base
- closure of one plant or sub-site, or sale/exit of a specific activity
- targeted headcount reduction
- investment in remaining German sites

### Likely outcome
- Most realistic if the required savings are large
- Better chance of achieving durable restructuring
- Still expensive and conflict-prone

### Main risk
Negotiation deadlock and implementation delay.

---

## Scenario C: Closure-led restructuring
### Components
- closure/exit of one or more sites
- centralisation of production
- headcount reduction
- labour-cost concessions

### Likely outcome
- fastest route to maximum structural savings
- highest short-term disruption
- strongest guarantee that the cost base changes decisively

### Main risk
Industrial action, political intervention, court disputes, and supply-chain instability.

---

## 7) Legal and industrial execution risks in Germany

For German automotive manufacturing, the main constraints are:

### Co-determination and supervisory-board veto dynamics
- Major footprint decisions are hard to force through
- A negotiated solution is often required
- Delays can be expensive because uncertainty affects suppliers and investment timing

### Works council and union power
- They can resist implementation through bargaining, public pressure, and operational disruption
- They can also help implement a deal if they get credible guarantees

### Collective bargaining and strike risk
- Once peace obligations lapse, the risk of strikes rises sharply
- Even short industrial action can destroy production plans and increase costs

### Redeployment constraints
- Internal transfers are only feasible if qualification profiles align
- Otherwise the company ends up paying for idle or underused labour

### Site-specific asset issues
- Specialized tooling, logistics, and product integration make “moving” capacity harder than it sounds
- The more bespoke the plant, the higher the execution risk

---

## 8) Decision guidance: what numbers matter

Management and stakeholders should focus on five figures:

1. **Required annual savings gap**  
   The amount needed to hit the target return on sales and fund investment.

2. **Run-rate savings from non-closure measures**  
   Not just first-year savings; the fully loaded annual effect after implementation.

3. **One-time restructuring cost**  
   Severance, write-downs, transfer costs, and retraining.

4. **Time to value**  
   Whether savings arrive in 12 months or only over 3–5 years.

5. **Savings durability**  
   Whether the measure survives the next downturn or renegotiation.

---

## 9) Bottom line

If the question is **“Can non-closure measures deliver meaningful savings?”**  
**Yes.**

If the question is **“Can they usually match the full durable savings of a plant closure in a large German carmaker with structural overcapacity?”**  
**Usually not, unless the required cut is modest or the non-closure package is exceptionally deep and multi-year.**

### Most likely outcome in practice
- **Non-closure measures can bridge a large part of the gap**
- but **one or more irreversible footprint decisions** are often needed if management’s target is a genuinely large structural reset
