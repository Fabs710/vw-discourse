Below is a finance-and-manufacturing assessment of the specific question: **how large the brand’s return-on-sales gap likely is, how German plants contribute to the cost problem, and what minimum structural savings are needed to fund investment through 2030.**

## 1) The core economics
For a mainstream premium or near-premium passenger-car brand in Europe, a **sustainable return-on-sales target is typically in the high single digits** if it is meant to fund:
- EV platform conversion,
- battery- and software-related capex,
- model renewal,
- supplier risk buffer,
- and cyclical downturn resilience.

If the brand is **below target by roughly 2–4 percentage points of sales**, that gap is large in industrial terms. On a revenue base of, say, **€80–120 billion**, every **1 percentage point of ROS equals €0.8–1.2 billion** of operating profit.  
So a **2–4 point gap** implies approximately **€1.6–4.8 billion** of annual profit shortfall versus target.

That is the scale of the problem management is usually trying to fix when it speaks of “structural overcapacity” and “uncompetitive cost base.”

## 2) What German plants contribute to the cost gap
German assembly and component plants are typically affected by four structural cost drivers:

1. **Labour cost per vehicle**
   - German direct labour is usually materially above Central/Eastern Europe and often above southern European alternatives.
   - More important than hourly wages is **labour cost per vehicle**, which rises when utilization falls.

2. **Fixed overhead absorption**
   - Plant depreciation, maintenance, logistics, engineering support, and salaried overhead are fixed or semi-fixed.
   - If a plant runs below efficient volume, **unit cost rises sharply** even if wage rates are unchanged.

3. **Working-time rigidity and staffing buffers**
   - Shorter effective working hours, high protection against headcount adjustment, and shift constraints reduce flexibility.
   - This forces the company to carry more labour than a lean volume base would require.

4. **Legacy guarantees and “above-market” arrangements**
   - Site guarantees, employment guarantees, special payments, and historical job security provisions can preserve capacity that is no longer economically warranted.

### Practical implication
A German plant is usually not “too expensive” only because wages are high.  
It is too expensive when **wages + overhead + underutilization + inflexibility** combine. In that case, the cost problem is structural, not cyclical.

## 3) How much a plant closure can save
A single plant closure or major footprint reduction can often remove a large share of:
- fixed site overhead,
- depreciation and maintenance burden,
- indirect labour,
- local logistics complexity,
- and excess capacity.

Depending on size and product mix, the annual savings can easily be in the **low hundreds of millions of euros**, sometimes more if a site is deeply underutilized.

However:
- closure savings are **not equal to the full plant cost base**, because some costs are transfer costs, severance costs, or redeployed elsewhere;
- and a closure can create **transition costs** and temporary disruption.

So, as a rule of thumb:
- **gross savings** from shutting a major site can be large,
- but **net steady-state savings** may be materially smaller after one-off charges and residual network costs.

## 4) Can non-closure measures match closure economics?
Usually, **not fully**, unless the overcapacity is modest or the alternative package is very deep.

Non-closure measures can deliver meaningful savings through:
- attrition,
- redeployment,
- reduced shifts,
- working-time reductions,
- variable pay restraint,
- productivity improvements,
- and voluntary exits.

But their limitation is that they mostly attack the **variable and semi-variable** part of the cost structure.  
They are often weaker on:
- site overhead,
- idle capacity,
- duplicated infrastructure,
- and long-term fixed cost removal.

### Typical economic conclusion
A serious non-closure package may deliver roughly **30–70% of the steady-state savings of a plant closure**, depending on:
- utilization gap,
- union acceptance,
- number of surplus employees,
- and whether the company can permanently simplify the product and production footprint.

If management says the brand needs “large, durable” savings, that usually means **non-closure measures may help, but are unlikely to fully substitute for footprint reduction** if the current German base is materially oversized.

## 5) Minimum structural savings required through 2030
To assess the minimum required, I would frame it like this:

### A. Restore target ROS
If the brand needs an extra **€1.6–4.8 billion** of annual operating profit to hit its target range, structural savings must cover a major share of that amount.

### B. Fund capex and product transition
The company also needs to fund:
- EV platform investment,
- software and electronics architecture,
- battery supply chain commitments,
- model renewals,
- and possibly China/EU market-specific localization.

If annual capex and development spending pressure is rising by, say, **€1–2 billion per year**, then the restructuring has to do more than just “improve margin” — it must **free cash flow**.

### C. Maintain investment capacity through 2030
A reasonable minimum objective is not merely breakeven competitiveness, but enough annual savings to:
- restore ROS to target,
- preserve positive free cash flow after capex,
- and protect the balance sheet against cyclical weakness.

That means the restructuring likely needs to generate **a recurring annual benefit in the low single-digit billions of euros** at the brand level, not merely a few hundred million.

## 6) What this means for the German footprint
If German operations are the highest-cost part of the network and carry excess capacity, then:
- some combination of **capacity consolidation, headcount reduction, and labour-cost normalization** is usually necessary;
- and if the residual gap remains large after those measures, **footprint reduction becomes economically hard to avoid**.

In plain terms:
- **If non-closure measures close the gap to within roughly 10–20% of target**, management may be able to avoid closures.
- **If a material gap remains after full non-closure measures**, closures or major site rationalisation usually become the economically dominant tool.

## 7) Bottom-line assessment
My quantitative judgment is:

- The brand’s ROS problem is likely **structural and material**, not marginal.
- The required remedy is likely a **recurring annual improvement of roughly €2–5 billion** at brand level, depending on revenue base and target margin.
- German plants likely account for a **disproportionate share of the avoidable cost gap** because of fixed-cost absorption, labour rigidity, and overcapacity.
- Non-closure measures can be substantial, but they are **unlikely to fully replace** the cost effect of real footprint reduction if the current German base is significantly oversized.
- Therefore, if the company cannot secure a deep enough labour and productivity contribution, **some irreversible capacity reduction is economically justified** from a finance and manufacturing standpoint.
