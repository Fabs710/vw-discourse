I will be direct.

## Where I agree
I agree with management, investors, the family, and the state on the core diagnosis:
- the company has a **real structural cost problem**,
- the solution must be **durable**,
- and a purely symbolic deal would be irresponsible.

I also agree with the expert that:
- **non-closure measures can deliver significant savings**,
- but they usually do **not** deliver the full effect of a closure if the overcapacity is truly large.

That is a useful warning — not a blank cheque for closures.

## Where I strongly disagree
I do **not** accept the attempt to turn that expert assessment into an argument that closures and forced layoffs must remain “normal” bargaining options.

They are not normal options for us. They are the line we will resist.

Let me restate the union’s position clearly:

- **No compulsory redundancies**
- **No plant closures**
- **No dismantling of the collective wage framework**
- **No deal where workers carry the burden alone**

That remains our position. Nothing in Round 3 changes that.

## On management’s position
Management keeps saying: “Show us the numbers.”

Fair enough. But the numbers must be shown **completely**, not selectively.

I want management to put on the table, in writing:
1. the exact annual savings gap,
2. the savings from attrition, redeployment, working-time changes, bonus restraint, executive restraint, and productivity measures,
3. the residual gap after those measures,
4. and the reason that residual gap cannot be closed without attacking jobs and sites.

Until that is done, closures are still a **choice**, not a necessity.

And I will say this plainly: if management is asking employees for wage restraint or an employee contribution fund, then management and owners must show real contribution too — not just demand flexibility from the workforce while preserving their own room to manoeuvre.

## On the expert assessment
The expert’s ranges do not support the fatalistic conclusion some parties want.

Yes, a plant closure can produce the largest immediate fixed-cost cut. That is obvious.

But the expert also made clear that a serious non-closure package can be substantial:
- capacity consolidation,
- shift and working-time changes,
- attrition,
- redeployment,
- productivity gains,
- and temporary labour-cost measures.

That is exactly the route we are prepared to negotiate.

What we reject is the claim that because non-closure measures may not match closure in every scenario, therefore closures should sit automatically on the table from the start. That is not analysis; that is a preference.

## To the works council
We are aligned on the crucial red lines:
- no compulsory redundancies,
- no plant closures,
- no tearing up of the collective framework.

I support your insistence that the company must not use “realism” as a cover for shifting the entire crisis onto the workforce.

That said, I want to sharpen one point: a 2030 guarantee is only meaningful if it is tied to a **real industrial plan** and **shared sacrifice**. If management wants labour to contribute, then we need:
- investment in Germany,
- site security,
- no forced layoffs,
- and a credible path for the remaining footprint.

I will not support a deal where employees give up wages or bonuses and the company keeps the option to reopen the same fight later.

## To the state government
You are right to insist that the regional interest matters. But that interest is protected by **industrial stability**, not by accepting closures as inevitable.

If the state wants to help, then it should support:
- binding investment commitments in Germany,
- qualification and redeployment,
- capacity consolidation without plant shutdowns,
- and a negotiated package that preserves jobs.

The state should not be a backdoor for a closure strategy.

## To the family and investors
You keep saying the company cannot preserve the current structure.

Maybe so — but you have not yet shown that preserving the German plant base is impossible. You keep framing the issue as if the only economically serious route is irreversible site cuts. I reject that framing.

You also need to account for the costs of confrontation:
- strikes,
- political backlash,
- skill loss,
- supplier disruption,
- and damage to trust.

Those are not sentimental concerns. They are economic costs. If you leave them out, your calculation is incomplete.

## On the bridge proposals
I will be frank: some of the mediator’s bridge ideas are usable, but only if they do **not** become a Trojan horse for closures.

### 1) Conditional no-closure package with review trigger
This can be discussed if the starting point is genuine:
- **no closures**
- **no compulsory redundancies**
- immediate non-closure measures
- and a jointly verified savings process

But the review trigger must not become an automatic exit ramp into plant shutdowns. A review is for checking whether the negotiated measures are being implemented and whether they deliver. It is not a hidden closure clause.

### 2) No forced redundancies plus targeted voluntary footprint reduction
This is closer to a workable path, if it means:
- attrition,
- voluntary exits,
- internal transfers,
- working-time reductions,
- shift changes,
- and capacity shifts between plants.

It becomes unacceptable the moment “voluntary footprint reduction” is used as code for shutting sites in stages.

### 3) Shared-sacrifice package tied to binding industrial commitments
This is the only serious basis for a deal.

But the burden must be shared fairly:
- management restraint,
- owner contribution,
- investment commitments,
- and labour contributions limited to what is necessary and time-bound.

If the package is one-sided, we will not support it.

## What we can consider
We are prepared to negotiate:
- temporary wage restraint,
- deferred payments,
- foregoing bonuses or special payments,
- working-time flexibility,
- productivity improvements,
- internal redeployment,
- attrition,
- and an employee contribution fund,

**if and only if** those measures are matched by:
- **no plant closures**
- **no compulsory redundancies**
- **binding job and site security**
- **investment commitments in Germany**
- **a real role for co-determination**
- **management and owner contribution**

That is not a concession to be haggled away. It is the basis of a fair settlement.

## What we cannot accept
We cannot accept:
- closures dressed up as “rationalisation,”
- forced layoffs hidden behind economic language,
- or a deal where the workforce pays for the crisis while capital remains protected.

If that is where this is heading, then let me be equally clear: the union will escalate.

We will use:
- industrial action,
- warning strikes,
- bargaining pressure,
- and public mobilisation if necessary.

## Bottom line
I hear the economic argument. I do not accept the conclusion that closures and compulsory redundancies are therefore the answer.

If management wants a settlement, then bring a **quantified, shared-sacrifice package** that:
- protects jobs,
- preserves sites,
- secures the collective framework,
- and includes meaningful contributions from management, owners, and labour alike.

If you cannot do that, then there is no agreement from our side.
