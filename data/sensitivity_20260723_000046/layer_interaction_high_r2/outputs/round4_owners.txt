## Where I agree
I agree with management, the experts, the investors, and the state on the basic diagnosis:
- this is a **structural** competitiveness problem,
- the current cost base is too high,
- and a cosmetic settlement would destroy value.

I also agree that **delay is expensive**. A drawn-out conflict that postpones action will hurt the brand, the balance sheet, and ultimately the workforce and owners alike.

And I agree with the expert assessments that:
- non-closure measures can deliver **material** savings,
- but they are not automatically enough if the structural overcapacity is large.

That is the right analytical frame.

## Where I disagree
I do **not** accept the employee-side attempt to turn:
- no plant closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- and binding 2030 guarantees for every site

into the default starting point for a final settlement.

That may be understandable from a labor position, but it is **not** automatically compatible with restoring competitiveness. If that framework locks in an above-peer cost base, then it simply postpones the problem and transfers the damage into the future. I will not support that.

At the same time, I do **not** accept management’s implied shortcut that because closures are theoretically available, they should remain the normal bargaining threat from day one. The expert did not say closures are the only serious route. It said non-closure measures can be substantial, but may not fully replace footprint reduction if the gap remains material.

So the correct position is **not** “closures at any cost,” and it is also **not** “no closures under any circumstances.”

## To management
You are right that we need hard numbers. But you still owe the governance side a proper bridge:
1. exact annual savings target,
2. quantified savings from attrition,
3. redeployment,
4. working-time changes,
5. bonus restraint,
6. executive restraint,
7. productivity and overhead reduction,
8. and the residual gap after those measures.

If that residual gap is small enough, then a no-closure package may be justified. If it is not, then you cannot pretend otherwise and still claim to be acting responsibly.

What I will not support is an indefinite process of “prove every alternative” used as a tactic to avoid hard decisions. That would be value-destructive drift.

## To the works council
I respect the need for trust, skills, and social peace. Those are real assets. But your current red lines are too absolute to be the final end state if the numbers do not work.

A permanent straightjacket of:
- no closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- full site security through 2030

may be a negotiating position, but it is not automatically a viable governance outcome if it prevents the company from reaching target margins and funding future investment.

What I can support is:
- **strong employment and site protections**,
- **time-limited guarantees** tied to a serious industrial plan,
- **no forced layoffs if alternatives truly deliver**,
- and meaningful co-determination in implementation.

What I cannot support is a structure that rules out necessary restructuring regardless of economic outcome.

## To the union
I appreciate that you are willing to discuss:
- temporary wage restraint,
- deferred payments,
- bonus restraint,
- working-time flexibility,
- productivity measures,
- and an employee contribution fund.

That is constructive. But temporary concessions alone do not solve a structural cost problem. If the gap is durable, then the savings must be durable too.

And I want to be equally clear: management and owners must also contribute. That means:
- executive restraint,
- disciplined capital allocation,
- overhead reduction,
- and visible commitment to Germany’s future product pipeline.

A one-sided burden on labor will not be acceptable, and it would not be stable.

## To the regional state government
I respect your role and the regional responsibility you carry. But the state’s blocking rights cannot be used to preserve an uncompetitive structure indefinitely.

A regional anchor is only an anchor if it remains competitive. If the company becomes weaker because necessary restructuring is blocked, the region loses more in the end.

So I support the state being part of a negotiated solution — but not as an absolute veto on all hard choices. If a serious non-closure package closes the gap, fine. If it does not, then the state must not pretend that economic reality can be legislated away.

## To institutional investors
I agree with your central message: **staged delay without structural savings is not acceptable**.

But I also want to be clear that a closure-first mindset can destroy value too, if it triggers prolonged industrial conflict, legal battles, skill loss, and political backlash. We should not confuse severity with wisdom.

If a negotiated package delivers the required savings and restores margins, I will support it. If it does not, then deeper structural measures must remain available. That is the responsible stewardship position.
