The expert assessment reinforces our central point: **this has to be a numbers-based restructuring, not a symbolic settlement**.

## Where we agree
We agree with management, the family, and the expert on the core economics:
- the German cost base is too high,
- capacity is misaligned with demand,
- and **delay is value-destructive**.

We also agree that a package based on working-time flexibility, attrition, redeployment, and productivity improvements can be part of the answer.

## Where we disagree
We do **not** accept the employee side’s effort to turn **“no plant closures, no compulsory redundancies, no 2030 job guarantee”** into the default starting point.

That position is not credible if the savings gap is large. It may be socially desirable, but from a shareholder perspective it is only acceptable if it still gets the company to **competitive margins and durable fixed-cost relief**. If it doesn’t, then it is not a solution — it is a postponement.

The expert was clear: non-closure measures often deliver only a portion of the savings of a closure, especially when structural overcapacity is serious. That matters. If the company needs a large permanent reset, then an absolute ban on closures risks leaving the cost base impaired and margins structurally weak.

## What we take from the expert
The key takeaway is not “close plants at any cost.” It is:
- non-closure measures can help,
- but they usually need to be **large, enforceable, and multi-year**,
- and they may still fall short if the gap is substantial.

That means management must do the work of proving the gap and comparing options. But it also means labour cannot simply declare the economically hardest tools off-limits and still expect investors to endorse the outcome.

## Our position on the options
We would support:
- a **hybrid package** if it is genuinely quantified,
- with meaningful capacity consolidation,
- material labour-cost restraint,
- productivity measures,
- attrition and voluntary exits,
- and, if necessary, **some irreversible footprint reduction** if the numbers require it.

We would **not** support:
- a cosmetic deal,
- a package that preserves every site regardless of economics,
- or guarantees that lock in an uncompetitive structure.

## On the current bargaining positions
### To works council and union
Your insistence on no closures and no forced layoffs is understandable politically, but it is not sufficient economically. If you want investor support, you need to show:
1. what savings your preferred package actually delivers,
2. why it is durable,
3. and how it gets the company back to competitive margins.

If the answer is “it preserves jobs but leaves the company expensive,” then you are asking shareholders to fund stagnation. We will not support that.

### To management
You are right that the restructuring must be deep enough to matter. But “we need closures” is not yet a complete argument. You still need to provide the gap analysis:
- target savings,
- contribution from non-closure measures,
- residual shortfall,
- and the exact reason why that shortfall cannot be closed another way.

If you can demonstrate that, then deeper structural action becomes much easier to support from our side.

### To the family and state
We understand the concern for social stability and regional impact. But that concern cannot override the economics indefinitely. A package that protects every site but leaves the business weak will destroy more value later. That is not stewardship.

## What would move us
A credible package would need:
- **material and durable cost reductions**
- **clear capacity adjustment**
- **enforceable implementation**
- **measurable labour contributions**
- and **a believable path back to margin targets**

If that can be achieved without closures, fine. If not, then closures or equivalent irreversible rationalisation must remain on the table.

## Bottom line
We are not asking for unnecessary destruction.  
We are asking for a settlement that **actually fixes the business**.

At this point, the employee side has not shown that a full no-closure, no-redundancy, 2030-guarantee package can do that. Until they do, that position remains unacceptable from a shareholder perspective.

If management can now quantify the residual gap after the non-closure measures, we can assess whether the remaining shortfall justifies deeper structural cuts. Without that, this discussion risks drifting back into slogans.
