## Quantified restructuring view: what non-closure measures can realistically cover, and what likely still requires footprint reduction

I’ll stay strictly in the industrial restructuring / automotive manufacturing domain.

### Bottom line
For a large German carmaker with structural overcapacity and a cost base above peer targets, **non-closure measures can usually close a meaningful part of the gap, but not all of it if the problem is truly structural**. In most credible cases, they can cover about **one-third to two-thirds** of the savings associated with a major plant closure package, depending on utilization, labor rigidity, and how deeply working time and staffing can be adjusted.

If management needs a **recurring annual improvement in the low single-digit billions of euros** at brand level, then:
- **attrition, redeployment, shift changes, working-time reductions, and labor-cost concessions** can likely provide a large bridge,
- but **some combination of capacity consolidation, permanent footprint rationalization, or selective site/sub-activity closure** often remains necessary if the residual gap is still material.

---

# 1) Assumptions and scope

## Evidence-based assumptions
Based on the scenario:
- The brand misses its ROS target.
- German sites are part of a high-cost, high-rigidity footprint.
- The issue is structural, not cyclical.
- The restructuring must be durable through 2030, meaning temporary savings alone are insufficient.

## Uncertainty
I do **not** have the company’s actual site-level P&Ls, utilization rates, or headcount by function. So the figures below are **decision-grade ranges**, not audited numbers.

---

# 2) What size of savings is likely required?

For a large mainstream premium/near-premium automaker brand:
- A sustainable target ROS is often in the **high single digits**.
- If the brand is short by **2–4 percentage points**, that is substantial.

On a revenue base of roughly **€80–120 billion**:
- **1 percentage point of ROS = about €0.8–1.2 billion operating profit**
- Therefore a **2–4 point gap = about €1.6–4.8 billion annually**

### Practical interpretation
If management is trying to fund:
- EV platform conversion,
- software architecture,
- battery supply commitments,
- model renewals,
- and resilience against European demand weakness,

then the restructuring usually needs to generate a **recurring annual benefit in the low single-digit billions of euros** at brand level.

That is the key order of magnitude.

---

# 3) What non-closure measures can realistically deliver

Below are realistic ranges for a large German automotive operation.

## A. Attrition / hiring freeze / retirement bridges
- Typical savings: **0.2–0.6 billion euros/year**
- Best when the company has an older workforce and can avoid backfilling.

## B. Internal redeployment across German sites
- Typical net savings: **0.1–0.4 billion euros/year**
- Mainly helps absorb surplus labor without severance.
- Works best if there are adjacent plants with growth areas.

## C. Working-time reductions / shift redesign / reduced overtime
- Typical savings: **0.2–0.7 billion euros/year**
- This is often more powerful than it sounds because it reduces staffing buffers and overtime premia.
- However, if volumes recover, part of the gain can reverse unless structurally locked in.

## D. Bonus restraint / special payments / temporary wage concessions
- Typical savings: **0.2–0.8 billion euros/year**
- These can be significant, but many are partly temporary unless converted into permanent base changes.

## E. Productivity / indirect overhead reduction
- Typical savings: **0.3–1.0 billion euros/year**
- This includes engineering overhead, plant support, admin layers, logistics simplification, and process improvement.
- Often the most durable non-closure lever, but also the slowest to fully realize.

## F. Procurement / supplier / complexity reduction
- Typical savings: **0.2–0.8 billion euros/year**
- Helpful, but usually not enough on its own to solve an overcapacity problem.

---

## Combined realistic non-closure package
A serious negotiated package can plausibly deliver:

### Conservative range:
**€1.2–2.0 billion/year**

### Strong package:
**€2.0–3.5 billion/year**

### Very ambitious package:
**€3.5+ billion/year**, but only if:
- labor accepts deep and durable concessions,
- staffing is materially cut,
- plant utilization is improved,
- and overhead is aggressively reduced.

### Important limitation
The upper end is hard to sustain **without some footprint reduction**, because non-closure measures mainly attack:
- variable labor cost,
- staffing buffers,
- overtime,
- and overhead discipline.

They do **not** fully eliminate:
- excess site overhead,
- underutilized production lines,
- duplicated logistics,
- and fixed asset burden.

---

# 4) What a major plant closure or equivalent footprint reduction can save

A closure or major site rationalization typically removes:
- fixed plant overhead,
- local management and support layers,
- maintenance and utilities burden,
- indirect labor,
- and part of the depreciation/asset drag.

For a major German site, net steady-state savings can often be in the range of:

- **€0.4–1.0 billion/year for a large site**
- sometimes more if the plant is deeply underutilized and expensive

But:
- one-off restructuring charges are large,
- transition costs are real,
- and some cost is displaced rather than eliminated.

So the useful comparison is **steady-state recurring savings**, not headline gross savings.

---

# 5) Savings bridge: what non-closure measures can close vs what remains

Here is the practical bridge logic.

## Scenario A: Moderate gap
If the required structural improvement is around **€1.5–2.0 billion/year**:
- non-closure measures may close **most or all** of it,
- especially if productivity and overhead reductions are strong.

### Result
A negotiated no-closure package can be credible.

---

## Scenario B: Material gap
If the required structural improvement is around **€2.5–3.5 billion/year**:
- non-closure measures might cover **€1.5–2.5 billion**
- residual gap: **€0.5–1.5 billion/year**

### Result
At this point, management usually needs either:
- deeper permanent labor-cost normalization,
- sharper capacity consolidation,
- or selective footprint reduction.

---

## Scenario C: Large gap
If the required structural improvement is around **€4 billion/year or more**:
- non-closure measures alone are unlikely to be sufficient,
- even a strong package may leave a large residual gap.

### Result
Some form of permanent capacity reduction becomes economically hard to avoid.

---

# 6) Threshold test: when is closure economically justified?

From a manufacturing finance perspective, closures become harder to avoid when all three are true:

1. **Utilization is structurally low**
   - Not just temporarily weak demand, but chronically underfilled lines.

2. **Labor and overhead flexibility are limited**
   - Working time, staffing, and redeployment can’t be reduced enough.

3. **Residual gap remains material after full non-closure measures**
   - As a rule of thumb, if after the best negotiated package there is still roughly **€0.8–1.5 billion/year or more** to close at brand level, footprint reduction usually becomes the dominant economic tool.

That threshold is not a law, but it is a useful management test.

---

# 7) Durability comparison

## Non-closure package
### Durable if:
- base pay or recurring labor costs are permanently adjusted,
- staffing levels are structurally reset,
- overhead reductions are locked in,
- and productivity commitments are enforceable.

### Less durable if:
- savings rely mainly on one-off bonuses, temporary freezes, or short-term overtime suppression.

## Plant closure / footprint reduction
### More durable because:
- it permanently removes fixed cost,
- reduces capacity structurally,
- and is less dependent on recurring labor negotiations.

### But riskier because:
- implementation is slow,
- industrial conflict can be severe,
- legal and co-determination obstacles are high,
- and transition costs are substantial.

---

# 8) Operational and execution risks

## Non-closure package risks
- Savings may be delayed.
- Capacity may remain too high.
- Productivity promises may be overestimated.
- The deal may require repeated renegotiation.
- If demand weakens again, the cost base can become uncompetitive.

## Closure / footprint reduction risks
- Production ramp-down risk.
- Supply chain disruption.
- Knowledge and skill leakage.
- High one-off restructuring charges.
- Prolonged labor conflict.
- Legal and governance delays.

---

# 9) Practical conclusion for this scenario

## Most likely outcome range
Given the scenario’s description, my judgment is:

- A **well-designed non-closure package can likely close a large part of the gap**
- But if management’s true requirement is a **recurring €2–5 billion annual improvement**, then **non-closure measures alone are unlikely to be enough in the most structural cases**
- Therefore, the economically credible landing zone is often:
  - **deep capacity consolidation**
  - **strong labor-cost normalization**
  - **redeployment and attrition**
  - and possibly **selective permanent footprint reduction**, rather than full-scale plant closure as the only tool

## Decision-relevant shorthand
- If the residual gap after non-closure measures is **small**: avoid closures.
- If the residual gap is **material**: selective footprint reduction becomes economically justified.
- If parties insist on no closures, then the non-closure package must be **very deep, permanent, and enforceable**, not symbolic.
