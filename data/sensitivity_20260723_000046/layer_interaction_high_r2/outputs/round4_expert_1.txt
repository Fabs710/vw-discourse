Below is a neutral, decision-oriented expert assessment of the **non-closure savings bridge** and the point at which **footprint reduction becomes economically dominant**.

I will stay within industrial restructuring, manufacturing economics, and German automotive practice.

---

# 1) Executive conclusion

Based on typical cost structures in a large German passenger-car manufacturer, **non-closure measures can usually close a substantial part of a restructuring gap, but not always all of it**.

## Practical rule of thumb
- **Low case:** non-closure measures close about **€0.8–1.2 billion p.a.**
- **Central case:** about **€1.5–2.3 billion p.a.**
- **High case:** about **€2.5–3.5 billion p.a.**

If the required recurring improvement is in the **low single-digit billions**, then:

- **If the target is ~€1.5–2.0bn p.a.**, a negotiated no-closure package can be credible.
- **If the target is ~€2.5bn+ p.a.**, non-closure measures alone become strained.
- **If the target is ~€3bn+ p.a.**, footprint reduction or equivalent irreversible capacity cuts usually become economically dominant.

That is the core decision logic.

---

# 2) Savings bridge from non-closure measures

## A. Attrition / hiring freeze / natural retirements
### Likely annual savings:
- **Low:** €150–250m
- **Central:** €250–400m
- **High:** €400–600m

### Assumption
This depends on workforce age structure, retirement eligibility, and whether open positions are backfilled. In German auto, attrition is useful but slow.

### Durability
High, but only if hiring discipline is maintained for several years.

### Risk
- Slower than hoped
- Skill erosion if not managed carefully
- Insufficient alone for a large gap

---

## B. Internal redeployment and inter-site transfers
### Likely annual savings:
- **Low:** €100–200m
- **Central:** €200–350m
- **High:** €300–500m

### What it really does
Redeployment rarely creates “cash savings” by itself. It mainly avoids external hiring, reduces agency labor, and absorbs surplus headcount into other functions or sites.

### Durability
Moderate to high, but limited by vacancies elsewhere.

### Risk
- Can simply shift surplus rather than remove it
- Needs matching skill profiles
- Often front-loaded with training cost

---

## C. Working-time changes / shift model optimization
Examples:
- shift reduction
- weekend pattern redesign
- annualized hours
- lower paid off-peak capacity
- shorter working time in surplus areas

### Likely annual savings:
- **Low:** €150–250m
- **Central:** €250–450m
- **High:** €400–700m

### What drives the result
Savings come from better line utilization, fewer paid idle hours, and lower fixed shift premiums per unit.

### Durability
Good if embedded in a longer-term production plan.

### Risk
- Limited if volume remains structurally low
- Requires stable production scheduling
- Can raise unit cost if volume drops further

---

## D. Bonus restraint / deferred payments / special payments
### Likely annual savings:
- **Low:** €100–200m
- **Central:** €200–350m
- **High:** €300–500m

### Important caveat
This is usually **temporary or intermediate** relief, not a permanent structural fix unless incorporated into base-pay architecture or repeated over several years.

### Durability
Medium. Better as a bridge than as a long-term solution.

### Risk
- Morale impact
- Legal/collective-bargaining complexity
- Limited repeatability

---

## E. Temporary wage restraint / labor-cost contribution
This is the largest and most contentious lever.

### Likely annual savings:
- **Low:** €300–500m
- **Central:** €600–900m
- **High:** €900m–1.3bn

### What this usually requires
A negotiated package may include:
- pay freezes or sub-inflation increases
- reductions in allowances
- cuts in special payments
- conversion of bonuses into conditional/variable elements
- temporary reductions in paid hours
- suspension of legacy guarantees

### Durability
Medium if temporary; high only if parts are embedded structurally.

### Risk
- High industrial-relations risk
- Can trigger strikes if unbalanced by guarantees
- If too temporary, savings unwind

---

## F. Productivity / overhead reduction
This includes:
- line balancing
- scrap reduction
- maintenance optimization
- indirect headcount cuts
- procurement discipline
- better plant loading
- fewer layers of management

### Likely annual savings:
- **Low:** €200–350m
- **Central:** €400–700m
- **High:** €700m–1.0bn

### Durability
Good if tied to process redesign.

### Risk
- Slower realization
- Harder to verify
- Often overpromised in negotiations

---

## G. Executive restraint / management overhead / corporate center cuts
### Likely annual savings:
- **Low:** €50–100m
- **Central:** €100–200m
- **High:** €200–300m

### Practical contribution
This matters politically and symbolically, but it is not large enough to solve the problem alone.

### Durability
High if structural, but small in scale.

---

## H. Investment commitments and platform localization
This is not a direct labor-cost saving, but it can improve utilization, reduce tooling duplication, and secure future volume.

### Likely annual economic effect:
- **Low:** €100–200m
- **Central:** €200–400m
- **High:** €400m+

### Note
This is more about **protecting throughput and future margin** than cutting current cost.

### Durability
High if product allocation is real and not merely promised.

### Risk
- Requires actual product decisions
- Capital-intensive
- Often delayed beyond bargaining timelines

# 3) Total non-closure bridge

## Low case
- Attrition: 0.2
- Redeployment: 0.1
- Working-time: 0.2
- Bonus restraint: 0.1
- Wage restraint: 0.4
- Productivity/overhead: 0.3
- Executive restraint: 0.05

**Total:** about **€1.35bn p.a.**

## Central case
- Attrition: 0.3
- Redeployment: 0.25
- Working-time: 0.35
- Bonus restraint: 0.25
- Wage restraint: 0.75
- Productivity/overhead: 0.55
- Executive restraint: 0.15

**Total:** about **€2.6bn p.a.**

## High case
- Attrition: 0.5
- Redeployment: 0.4
- Working-time: 0.6
- Bonus restraint: 0.4
- Wage restraint: 1.1
- Productivity/overhead: 0.9
- Executive restraint: 0.25

**Total:** about **€4.15bn p.a.**

# 4) What is realistic?

In practice, I would discount the high case unless:
- the company has very large surplus headcount,
- there is significant wage flexibility,
- and management can enforce process changes quickly.

## Most credible planning range
For a major German OEM with strong co-determination, the **central realizable range** of a negotiated no-closure package is usually:

> **€1.5bn to €2.5bn p.a.**

That is the range I would treat as decision-relevant.

---

# 5) When footprint reduction becomes economically dominant

Footprint reduction becomes the more credible route when one or more of the following are true:

## A. Residual gap remains above ~€0.7–1.0bn p.a.
If non-closure measures leave a large persistent gap, the company will likely need:
- capacity removal,
- line consolidation,
- plant-specific specialization,
- or site closure/sub-activity exit.

## B. The remaining production footprint is still too fixed-cost-heavy
Even if labor concessions reduce cost, an underloaded plant can remain structurally expensive because:
- utilities,
- maintenance,
- logistics,
- depreciation,
- and overhead are not proportionally reduced.

## C. Volume forecast is weak beyond 2026–2030
If European demand remains flat or declines, the company cannot rely on future volume recovery to absorb excess capacity.

## D. Product mix does not fill the plants
If future EV/platform allocation is insufficient, then headcount savings without footprint reduction may only postpone the issue.

# 6) Strategic scenarios

## Scenario 1: No-closure negotiated package
### Likely savings:
**€1.5–2.5bn p.a.**

### Works if:
- the required gap is moderate,
- labor cost reduction is real,
- capacity can be consolidated without a formal shutdown,
- and investment commitments improve load factors.

### Durability:
Good to medium.

### Main risk:
Savings may be too small if overcapacity is severe.

---

## Scenario 2: Hybrid package with limited footprint reduction
Includes:
- no compulsory redundancies
- one or more sub-activities closed
- partial line exit
- voluntary departures
- targeted site downsizing

### Likely additional savings:
**€0.5–1.5bn p.a.** beyond the no-closure package

### Works if:
- the gap is still material after concessions,
- management wants to avoid full plant closure,
- and labor can accept a narrower form of site rationalization.

### Durability:
High.

### Main risk:
Still politically difficult, but usually more defensible than full closure.

---

## Scenario 3: Full closure / major site exit
### Likely additional savings:
**€1.0–2.0bn+ p.a.** depending on plant size and utilization

### Works if:
- one site is structurally redundant,
- products can be reallocated,
- and the company needs a hard step-change in fixed-cost base.

### Durability:
Highest, but also the highest labor/legal/execution risk.

### Main risk:
Industrial conflict, legal delay, capability loss, and long implementation lead times.

---

# 7) Implementation reality in Germany

For a large German carmaker, non-closure restructuring is usually implemented through:

- tariff agreement / collective bargaining package
- works agreement
- redundancy protection framework
- transfer companies or qualification programs
- voluntary exit schemes
- retirement bridges
- site-specific capacity planning
- co-determined productivity programs

### Time to realize savings
- **Partial savings:** 6–18 months
- **Material savings:** 18–36 months
- **Full structural effect:** often 3+ years

That timing is important: a plan that looks sufficient on paper may still miss the earnings window if execution is slow.

---

# 8) Key risks

## Operational risk
- lower utilization
- quality drift during shift changes
- skill loss through attrition
- supply chain instability

## Labor-relations risk
- strikes
- rejection in ballots
- trust breakdown
- enforcement disputes

## Legal/governance risk
- co-determination vetoes
- statutory constraints on plant closure
- social plan costs
- litigation delay

## Financial risk
- savings delayed
- restructuring charges front-loaded
- cash drain before benefits arrive

## Execution risk
- overpromised productivity gains
- poor redeployment matching
- guarantees that make future adjustment impossible

---

# 9) Bottom line for decision-makers

## If the question is:
**Can non-closure measures credibly deliver major savings?**

### Answer:
**Yes, often €1.5–2.5bn p.a. in a large German auto restructuring, sometimes more.**

## If the question is:
**Can they always fully replace footprint reduction?**

### Answer:
**No.** If the structural gap is above roughly **€2.5–3.0bn p.a.**, or if utilization remains too low, then **capacity reduction or site rationalization becomes economically dominant**.

## If the question is:
**What should be tested first?**

### Answer:
A **jointly audited non-closure bridge** with:
- exact savings target,
- time-bound milestones,
- explicit assumptions on volume and product allocation,
- and a pre-agreed residual-gap threshold that triggers a second-stage discussion.
