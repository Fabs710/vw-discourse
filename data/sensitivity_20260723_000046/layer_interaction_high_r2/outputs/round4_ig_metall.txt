I will be equally direct.

## Where I agree
I agree with the works council, the state, and even management on one basic point: **this cannot be a cosmetic agreement**. The company has a structural competitiveness problem, and it needs a durable answer. I also agree that we need **numbers, not slogans**.

I further agree with the expert assessment that:
- non-closure measures can produce **substantial** savings,
- but they usually do **not fully replace** the fixed-cost effect of footprint reduction if overcapacity is truly structural.

That is a real warning. It is **not** a blank cheque for closures.

## Where I disagree
What I do **not** accept is the repeated attempt to turn that warning into an argument that plant closures and forced redundancies must remain normal bargaining options.

They are **not** normal options for us. They are the line we will resist.

So let me restate the union’s position clearly:

- **No compulsory redundancies**
- **No plant closures**
- **No dismantling of the collective wage framework**
- **No one-sided burden-sharing**
- **A durable settlement only if employees contribute fairly and management/owners contribute as well**

That position has not changed.

## To management
You keep asking for flexibility, but you still have not provided the full decision-grade bridge.

If you want us to consider your restructuring logic seriously, then put the numbers on the table in a form that is actually usable:

1. exact annual savings target,
2. savings from attrition,
3. savings from redeployment,
4. savings from working-time changes,
5. savings from bonus restraint,
6. savings from executive restraint,
7. savings from productivity and overhead reduction,
8. and the residual gap after all of that.

Until that is done, closures are not a necessity. They are a **preference**.

And I will be blunt: if management keeps presenting closures as the default fallback, then industrial action remains on the table.

## To the works council
We are aligned on the central red lines:
- no compulsory redundancies,
- no plant closures,
- no tearing up the collective framework.

That is correct.

But I also want to sharpen one point: if the company asks for labour concessions, then those concessions must be **matched by binding guarantees** and **visible management and owner contribution**. Workers will not give up wages, bonuses, or security just so the same fight comes back in 18 months.

So yes, security first. But security must be backed by a real industrial plan, not just a promise.

## To the state government
I agree the region’s interest matters. But the state should not treat closures as an acceptable baseline. If the state wants to act in line with its role, then it should support:
- investment commitments in Germany,
- qualification and retraining,
- transfer and redeployment,
- working-time flexibility,
- and capacity consolidation **without** plant shutdowns.

The state’s leverage should defend the industrial base, not manage its shrinkage.

## To the controlling family and investors
You are right about one thing: delay destroys value.

But so does a confrontation that tears apart trust, destroys skills, and triggers prolonged industrial conflict. Those costs are part of the economics too. If you leave them out, your calculation is incomplete.

You also need to acknowledge the governance reality: co-determination is not decorative. You do not get to speak about “flexibility” as if closures were a managerial free choice. They are not.

## On the expert’s numbers
The expert’s assessment says the brand may need a recurring annual improvement in the low single-digit billions of euros. I do not dispute that the requirement may be large.

What I dispute is the hidden conclusion that this automatically authorizes plant closures.

The correct next step is:
- a full quantified non-closure package,
- a residual-gap calculation,
- and then a serious test of whether that gap is truly unbridgeable without destroying sites.

Until that is done, closures remain a choice, not a necessity.

## What we can consider
We are prepared to negotiate a serious package that includes:
- temporary wage restraint,
- deferred payments,
- foregoing bonuses or special payments,
- working-time flexibility,
- productivity improvements,
- internal redeployment,
- attrition,
- and an employee contribution fund,

**if and only if** those measures are matched by:
- **no plant closures**
- **no compulsory redundancies**
- **binding job and site security**
- **investment commitments in Germany**
- **a real role for co-determination**
- **management and owner contribution**

That is the basis of a fair settlement.

## What we cannot accept
We cannot accept:
- closures dressed up as rationalisation,
- forced layoffs hidden behind technical language,
- or a deal where the workforce pays for the crisis while capital keeps full flexibility.

If that is where this is heading, then the union will escalate:
- through bargaining pressure,
- warning strikes,
- industrial action,
- and public mobilisation if necessary.

## Bottom line
I hear the economic argument. I do not accept the conclusion that closures and compulsory redundancies are therefore the answer.

If management wants a settlement, then bring a **quantified, shared-sacrifice package** that:
- protects jobs,
- preserves sites,
- secures the collective framework,
- and includes meaningful contributions from management, owners, and labour alike.

If you cannot do that, then there is no agreement from our side.
