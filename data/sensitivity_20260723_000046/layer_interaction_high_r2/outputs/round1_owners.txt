I’ll be equally direct.

I agree with everyone on one essential point: **the company cannot afford a cosmetic restructuring**. If the cost base stays structurally uncompetitive, we will not restore margins, and without margins there will be no sustained investment in products, software, battery technology, or the plants themselves.

Where I disagree is with the idea that the choice is simply between:
- **full preservation of the current structure**, or
- **immediate closures and forced layoffs**.

That is too crude, and it is not the only way to create a durable reset.

### To the works council and union
I understand your red lines. I also understand the warning that people will resist if they believe this is just an excuse to dismantle labor protections. That concern is legitimate.

But I need to be clear: **a blanket “no plant closures, no compulsory redundancies, no deep labour contribution” is not a viable basis for a settlement**. If that is the position, then there is no real restructuring, only delay. Delay would erode the company, weaken the investment case, and ultimately put more jobs at risk later.

So I would not support any package that locks in:
- every site as untouchable,
- every employment guarantee regardless of economic reality,
- and only symbolic concessions from labour.

That would be value-destructive and unfair to the long-term owners and the company’s future.

### To management
I also do not accept that “credible restructuring” automatically means plant closures. Closures are a grave step in this company’s history and should not be treated as the default instrument. If management wants support for such measures, it must show that **all serious alternatives have been exhausted** and that the proposed cuts are truly necessary.

Right now, the burden is on management to prove that the savings target cannot be reached through:
- capacity consolidation,
- working-time flexibility,
- voluntary exits and attrition,
- renegotiated labour-cost components,
- internal redeployment,
- and a targeted productivity program.

If management can show a numbers-based case that those measures are insufficient, then I will not rule out deeper structural action. But I am not prepared to endorse closures as a first move simply because they are administratively cleaner.

### To the regional state government
I respect the state’s role and the fact that industrial capacity matters to regions, suppliers, and public trust. But the state also has to recognize that **preserving every structure indefinitely is not stewardship** if it weakens the company’s competitiveness.

The state should support a solution that secures the company’s long-term viability, not freeze it into an unsustainable cost position. If the company becomes permanently weaker, then the region loses anyway.

### What I can support
I would support a package that combines:
- meaningful but negotiated labour contributions,
- capacity reductions without immediate plant closures if they can deliver the required effect,
- strong investment commitments in Germany,
- productivity and efficiency improvements,
- voluntary and attrition-based workforce reductions,
- and a clear timeline for restoring competitiveness and margins.

What I would **not** support is:
- permanent impairment of governance control,
- a deal that leaves the company structurally uncompetitive,
- or an arrangement where the burden falls almost entirely on shareholders while operational realities are ignored.

### Bottom line
My position is this:

- **I am open to an alternative to closures if it is real, quantified, and durable.**
- **I am not open to preserving the status quo under the banner of social responsibility.**
- **I will not support a value-destructive compromise.**

If management and the employee side want my backing, bring me a concrete package that demonstrably restores competitiveness and protects the company’s long-term value. If that cannot be done, then more forceful restructuring measures remain on the table.
