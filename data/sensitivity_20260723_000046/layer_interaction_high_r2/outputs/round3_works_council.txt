I will respond plainly, because the situation is too serious for diplomatic fog.

## Where I agree
I agree with everyone on one point: the company needs a **durable** restructuring, not a cosmetic pause. I also agree that the next step must be **numbers-based**.

And I acknowledge the expert’s basic point: non-closure measures can produce real savings, but they are often slower and less complete than closure-led restructuring. That is not news to us.

## Where I strongly disagree
What I do **not** accept is the leap from “non-closure measures may be imperfect” to “therefore plant closures must remain on the table as a normal option.”

That is exactly the kind of reasoning that destroys trust and triggers resistance.

Let me be clear:

- **No plant closures**
- **No compulsory redundancies**
- **No dismantling of the job-security framework**
- **No fait accompli**

Those are not bargaining chips for us. They are the boundary of any serious discussion.

## On management’s argument
Management says it needs “large, durable savings.” Fine. Then show the full calculation.

What we have heard so far is still not enough to justify closure as inevitable. The expert gave ranges, not proof that a German plant must be shut. A range like “non-closure measures can deliver 30–60% of closure savings” does **not** mean the remaining gap automatically requires closure. It means management has to work harder on the package.

So my question remains unchanged:

1. What is the exact annual savings target?
2. What do attrition, redeployment, working-time changes, bonus restraint, executive restraint, and productivity measures deliver?
3. What is the residual gap?
4. Why is that gap not bridgeable through a negotiated non-closure package?

Until management answers that with hard numbers, closures are still just a preference, not a necessity.

## On the expert assessment
I take the expert seriously, but I reject the fatalistic reading some parties seem to want to attach to it.

The expert also said:
- non-closure packages can be substantial,
- they can preserve sites,
- they can be durable if enforced well,
- and they can bridge a large part of the gap.

That is exactly why we insist on trying them first.

What the expert did **not** say is that closures are automatically required. The conclusion depends on the size of the gap and the ambition of the alternative package. So let’s not use technical language to smuggle in a political decision.

## To management, the family, and investors
You all keep saying that protecting every site is “not economically sufficient.” That may be your view, but it is not yet demonstrated.

And you need to reckon with the cost of your preferred path:
- industrial conflict,
- loss of trust,
- political intervention,
- damage to supplier networks,
- skill drain,
- and long-term instability.

Those costs are not side effects. They are part of the economic equation. If you ignore them, your “numbers-based” argument is incomplete.

## To the state government
I agree that the regional interest cannot be treated as an afterthought. But I will also say this: the state’s role is only meaningful if it is used to defend the industrial base, not to manage its shrinkage.

So if the state wants to be constructive, then it should support:
- investment commitments in Germany,
- qualification and redeployment,
- capacity consolidation without closure,
- and binding protection for plants and jobs.

Not a hybrid deal that quietly opens the door to closures later.

## To the union
We are aligned on the red lines, and I welcome that.

But I want to sharpen one point: “temporary” concessions are only acceptable if they are **strictly matched** by binding guarantees and a real industrial plan. We will not sign workers up to wage restraint just so management can reopen the same question in 18 months.

If labour is asked to contribute, then the company must commit to:
- every German plant remaining open,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- job security through 2030,
- and co-determination being respected in implementation, not bypassed.

Without that, there is no fair deal.

## On the so-called bridge proposals
I have to be direct: the mediator’s bridge ideas are only acceptable if they do **not** weaken the core protections.

### Proposal 1: Conditional no-closure package with review trigger
This is only useful if the starting point is truly **no closures and no compulsory redundancies**.  
A “review trigger” cannot be a backdoor to plant shutdown.

We can discuss milestones, yes. But milestones must measure the success of non-closure measures, not create an automatic path to closures.

### Proposal 2: “No forced redundancies” plus targeted voluntary footprint reduction
This is closer to a real discussion, but only if “targeted voluntary footprint reduction” does **not** mean plant closures in disguise.

We can discuss:
- attrition,
- voluntary exits,
- internal transfers,
- working-time reductions,
- shift changes,
- and capacity shifts between plants.

We cannot accept a model where “voluntary” is used to soften up workers for later closures.

### Proposal 3: Shared-sacrifice package tied to binding industrial commitments
Yes — but only if the commitments are strong enough:
- clear investment in Germany,
- real product allocation,
- management and owner contribution,
- and enforceable job and site guarantees.

If the burden is not shared, then it is not a shared-sacrifice package.

## What would actually move us
Let me be very clear about the only things that could move the works council from its position:

1. **Binding site and job guarantees through 2030**
2. **No plant closures**
3. **No compulsory redundancies**
4. **A real co-determination role in shaping implementation**
5. **A credible, quantified restructuring package that does not rely on sacrificing German sites**

That is the floor.

If management wants concessions, it must first provide that security. Not promises. Not aspirations. Binding guarantees.

## What I am prepared to discuss
I am prepared to discuss:
- how much capacity can be reduced through attrition and redeployment,
- where working-time flexibility can be used,
- how temporary labour contributions could be structured,
- what productivity gains are realistic,
- and what investment commitments can make the package durable.

I am **not** prepared to discuss:
- closures as a normal option,
- compulsory redundancies,
- or any arrangement that breaks the historic job-security pledge.

## Bottom line
The other parties keep presenting closure as the “responsible” option. I disagree.

The responsible option is to preserve the German industrial base, secure jobs, and achieve savings through a negotiated, socially responsible restructuring that does not destroy trust.

If management wants agreement, it must come back with:
- exact numbers,
- a no-closure package,
- and binding guarantees.

If it cannot do that, then we will continue to resist — through co-determination, through industrial action, and through public opposition — because protecting jobs and sites is not negotiable.
