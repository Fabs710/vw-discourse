I’ll be equally direct.

## Where I agree
I agree with everyone on the basic diagnosis:
- the company has a **structural competitiveness problem**,
- the current cost base is too high,
- and a cosmetic agreement would be a mistake.

I also agree with the expert assessment that:
- non-closure measures can deliver **real** savings,
- but they may not fully substitute for structural footprint reduction if the overcapacity is large.

That is an important point. It does **not** prove closures are inevitable, but it does prove we need a package that is materially stronger than business as usual.

## Where I disagree
I do **not** accept the employee side’s attempt to make
- no plant closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- and binding 2030 guarantees for every site

the starting minimum for talks.

That would freeze the current structure and delay the correction we need. It does not answer the cost problem; it postpones it.

I also do **not** accept the argument that because closures are painful, they are therefore off-limits. Pain is not the same as unacceptability. If the economics require structural change, then we have to face that honestly.

## To the works council
You keep saying closures are “a choice, not a necessity.” That may be true in the abstract, but it has not yet been demonstrated in the numbers.

So let me be clear: I am prepared to test a strong non-closure package. But that package must be:
- **quantified**,
- **durable**,
- **enforceable**,
- and **economically sufficient**.

What I cannot accept is a framework that rules out the main structural lever before we know whether the alternatives are enough.

If you want me to rule out closures, then show me a package that closes the gap in a way that is credible through 2030.

## To the union
I appreciate that you are willing to discuss:
- temporary wage restraint,
- deferred payments,
- bonus restraint,
- working-time flexibility,
- productivity measures,
- and an employee contribution fund.

That is constructive.

But temporary concessions alone do not fix a permanent cost problem. If the gap is structural, then savings must be structural too. We need a package that actually changes the base, not just one that buys a few quarters of peace.

And to be equally clear: management and owners are also expected to contribute. We are not asking the workforce to do this alone. Executive restraint, overhead reduction, disciplined capital allocation, and investment commitments are part of the equation.

## To the state government
I respect the state’s role and its interest in regional stability. But the state’s blocking rights cannot be used to preserve an uncompetitive structure indefinitely.

If the state wants to avoid closures, then it should help deliver a package that truly works:
- capacity consolidation,
- attrition,
- redeployment,
- working-time changes,
- productivity gains,
- and meaningful labour-cost reduction.

If that is enough, then I will not insist on closures for their own sake. But if it is not enough, then the company cannot be held hostage to a no-change position.

## To the controlling family and investors
I agree with you on one thing: delay destroys value.

But I also want to be clear that value destruction is not avoided by simply shutting plants without a credible transition plan. Confrontation, legal conflict, and prolonged strikes also destroy value.

So I am not arguing for closures as a first instinct. I am arguing that **deep structural savings** are non-negotiable, and if the non-closure package does not achieve them, deeper footprint reduction must remain available.

## On the expert assessment
The expert’s analysis is useful because it gives us the right question:
- What does the non-closure package save?
- What remains?
- And how large is that residual gap?

That is the correct decision framework.

What the expert did **not** say is that closures are automatically required. The correct conclusion depends on the gap after the full package. So let’s stop arguing in absolutes and quantify the bridge.

## What I can support
I can support a serious negotiated package that includes:
- attrition,
- voluntary exits,
- redeployment,
- shift and working-time changes,
- productivity and overhead reduction,
- temporary labour-cost restraint,
- executive restraint,
- and investment commitments in Germany.

I can also support a **time-limited no-closure implementation phase** if it is tied to:
- clear savings milestones,
- joint verification,
- and a defined review point.

But I will not support a permanent straightjacket that rules out necessary restructuring regardless of the results.

## What I cannot accept
I cannot accept:
- a blanket exclusion of closures,
- a blanket exclusion of compulsory redundancies,
- or guarantees that lock in today’s structure through 2030 even if the economics do not work.

That would not be stewardship. It would be avoidance.

## Where this leaves us
So here is the honest position:

- If the employee side can present a package that materially reduces capacity and labour cost, is enforceable, and restores competitiveness without closures, I will back it.
- If not, then we must keep deeper structural measures on the table, including plant rationalisation.

That is not a threat. It is the responsibility we have to the company, its future products, and its ability to fund investment through 2030.

## What I propose next
We need the next step to be factual, not rhetorical:
1. exact annual savings target,
2. quantified savings from each non-closure measure,
3. residual gap after those measures,
4. labour contribution in cost terms,
5. and what capacity reduction, if any, is still required.

If the numbers support a non-closure solution, fine — show me.  
If they do not, then we should have the courage to say so openly instead of pretending the gap has vanished.
