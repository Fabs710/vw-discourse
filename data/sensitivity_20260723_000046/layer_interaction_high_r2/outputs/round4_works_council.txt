## Where I strongly disagree
What I reject is the repeated leap from:

> “The gap is large”  
to  
> “Therefore plant closures must remain available.”

That is still not proven.

The expert assessment was useful, but it did **not** establish that a German plant must be closed. It established only that:
- non-closure measures can deliver substantial savings,
- they may not fully match the fixed-cost effect of closure in every case,
- and the residual gap must be judged carefully.

That is very different from saying closures are economically inevitable.

And that is why I do **not** accept management’s attempt to keep closures on the table as a normal bargaining instrument. Once you do that, trust collapses, and then the company pays for years.

## On the mediator’s three bridge proposals
I will go through them plainly.

### Proposal 1: “No forced measures first, then gated escalation”
This is only acceptable if “gated escalation” does **not** mean a pre-approved route to closures.

I can support:
- immediate non-closure measures,
- verified milestones,
- and a joint review of savings delivery.

I cannot support a mechanism that silently becomes a closure trigger once management decides it wants one.

If there is a review, it must review whether the non-closure package is being implemented properly and fairly — not create a backdoor to site shutdown.

### Proposal 2: “Temporary site protection with predefined review triggers”
This is closer to a real discussion, but only if the starting point is genuine site protection, not conditional patience.

A review trigger can exist. But it must not weaken the baseline principle:
- **no plant closures**
- **no compulsory redundancies**

If management wants to reopen the discussion later, it will need a completely new justification and a new political battle. It does not get a standing invitation to close a German plant.

### Proposal 3: “Shared-sacrifice package with asymmetric safeguards”
This is the only direction that makes sense — but only if the safeguards are real and not cosmetic.

I can discuss:
- temporary wage restraint,
- deferred payments,
- bonus restraint,
- working-time flexibility,
- voluntary exits,
- redeployment,
- productivity measures,
- and an employee contribution fund,

**if and only if** the company gives:
- binding site protection,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- no closures,
- investment commitments,
- and a real role for co-determination.

That is not compromise for its own sake. That is the minimum condition for fairness.

## What I will not accept
Let me make this completely explicit:

- I will **not** accept a staged process that is designed to normalize closures later.
- I will **not** accept “temporary” guarantees that are only temporary for labour, while management keeps permanent flexibility.
- I will **not** accept a settlement where workers carry the burden and the company retains the right to shrink German sites anyway.
- I will **not** accept compulsory redundancies hidden behind technical language.
- I will **not** accept a package that breaks the historic job-security pledge.

If that is the direction, then we are not in a restructuring dialogue. We are in a conflict.

## What would genuinely move us
Only three things could move the works council:

1. **Binding site and job guarantees through 2030**
2. **No closures**
3. **No compulsory redundancies**

If the company wants labour contributions, then those guarantees must come first, not last.

After that, we can discuss:
- capacity consolidation through attrition and redeployment,
- working-time changes,
- productivity gains,
- temporary labour-cost contributions,

But I will not reverse the sequence. Security first. Contribution second.

## My response to the financial argument

The correct next step is:
- a full quantified non-closure package,
- a residual-gap calculation,

Until that is done, closures remain an option of choice, not necessity.

And as works council, I will resist that choice.
