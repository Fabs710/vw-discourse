From the perspective of dispersed capital-market shareholders, the key question is not whether the restructuring is “tough” or “socially balanced.” It is whether it **credibly restores competitive margins and protects enterprise value**.

So I’ll be direct:

## Where I agree
I agree with management, and partly with the family, on the core diagnosis:  
the company cannot remain stuck with a cost base that is structurally above peers while demand is weaker and competition is intensifying. If the result is only a symbolic agreement that avoids hard choices, then shareholders will absorb the downside through weaker margins, weaker cash flow, and a weaker valuation.

I also agree that **delay is expensive**. A prolonged conflict, or a deal that merely postpones the problem, would be value-destructive.

## Where I disagree
I do **not** accept the employee side’s attempt to pre-emptively rule out plant closures and compulsory redundancies as if those were off the table under all circumstances. If the numbers genuinely require deeper structural change, then saying “no closures, no forced layoffs” before the negotiation even begins is not a credible answer to a competitiveness crisis.

That said, I also do **not** support closures for their own sake. Management still has to prove that closures are necessary, not just convenient. The burden is on management to show:
- what savings are required,
- what measures have already been tested,
- what alternative package delivers equivalent cost reduction,
- and why the chosen path is the best risk-adjusted outcome.

## What matters to us
We would support the path that most credibly delivers:
- **durable cost reduction**
- **improved return on sales**
- **clear capacity adjustment**
- **labor-cost flexibility**
- **deal certainty**
- and **timely execution**

What we will not support is an arrangement that leaves the company with:
- an uncompetitive cost base,
- structurally impaired margins,
- or another round of crisis restructuring in a few years.

A “socially responsible” deal that fails on economics is not responsible from an investor point of view.

## On the alternatives being discussed
A package based on working-time flexibility, attrition, voluntary exits, productivity improvements, and temporary concessions can be acceptable **only if the savings are real, large enough, and durable enough**. If those measures genuinely substitute for closures and still get the company to a competitive cost base, fine.

But if they are just a softer language for partial delay, then they do not solve the problem. We would not regard that as a credible restructuring.

## On labor’s demands
Binding 2030 job guarantees, no closures, and no compulsory redundancies sound attractive, but they are only acceptable if they do **not** lock in an economically unsustainable structure. Guarantees that prevent necessary adjustment simply shift the cost to shareholders through lower returns and weaker future investment.

Likewise, if labor is asking for management and shareholders to “contribute first,” that is reasonable only if the final package actually restores competitiveness. We are not interested in paying for a settlement that preserves legacy inefficiencies.

## On the family and the state
I note the family’s emphasis on value creation. On that point, we are aligned: value creation requires a credible reset, not a cosmetic compromise.

I also note the state’s emphasis on regional employment. We understand that, but from a shareholder standpoint regional policy cannot override economic reality indefinitely. If the company’s German base remains structurally too expensive, then protecting every site may end up destroying more jobs and more value over time.

## Our bottom line
We would back:
- a **credible, quantified restructuring**
- with **material and durable cost reductions**
- that **restores competitive margins**
- and is **implemented with enough certainty to be believable**

We would **not** back:
- a cosmetic settlement,
- a deal that freezes the current cost structure,
- or an outcome that leaves the company permanently disadvantaged.

So our message to management is: bring a real numbers-based package, not aspirations.  
To labor: if you want support for preserving sites, you need to show how the resulting structure still gets the company back to competitive margins.  
To the family and state: we are not demanding reckless destruction, but we are demanding an outcome that actually fixes the business.

If the final package does not credibly restore competitiveness, then from our side it is not acceptable.
