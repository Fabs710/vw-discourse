The financial assessment is useful, but I do **not** accept the way some are already using it to pre-justify plant closures.

## Where I agree
I agree with management, the family, and the experts on three points:

1. **This is a structural problem, not a cosmetic one.**  
   The cost base and capacity situation must be corrected.

2. **The outcome must be durable.**  
   Temporary fixes alone are not enough.

3. **We need hard numbers, not slogans.**  
   The savings gap, the contribution of each measure, and the residual shortfall all have to be transparent.

On that, the discussion is finally in the right place.

## Where I disagree
I strongly disagree with the leap from “the gap is large” to “therefore closures are necessary.”

The expert has shown that:
- non-closure measures can deliver **real savings**,
- they can be **material**,
- and in some cases they can cover a very large part of the gap.

That is not a trivial finding. It means plant closure is **not** the only serious option. It is one option, and a very destructive one.

For a company that is central to this region, closure is not just an industrial measure. It is a permanent regional shock:
- loss of jobs,
- loss of suppliers,
- loss of skills,
- loss of trust,
- political destabilization,
- and damage to the company’s social legitimacy.

Those costs are part of the economic equation too. Anyone pretending otherwise is doing incomplete accounting.

## To management
You keep saying “if the package is not enough, closures remain on the table.”  
I will be equally direct: **that is not an acceptable default assumption** for this company.

Before any irreversible site decision is even contemplated, management must demonstrate that all serious alternatives have been fully quantified and that they fail **despite a genuinely ambitious package**.

That means a package of the following kind must be tested first:
- capacity consolidation **without closure** where possible,
- attrition and voluntary exits,
- redeployment across German sites,
- working-time adjustments,
- temporary wage and bonus restraint,
- executive restraint,
- productivity and overhead reduction,
- and investment commitments that keep the German footprint viable.

If management can show that such a package still leaves a residual gap, then we discuss how to close that gap. But not by starting from closure as the default answer.

## To the union and works council
I support your core red lines:
- no compulsory redundancies,
- no plant closures as a starting point,
- no dismantling of co-determination,
- no sacrifice of the workforce alone.

That said, I need to be frank about one point: a **blanket refusal to discuss any structural adjustment beyond the existing footprint** is also not a sustainable position. We cannot simply preserve the status quo and call that a strategy.

What I can support is:
- serious capacity consolidation,
- voluntary schemes,
- redeployment,
- working-time changes,
- temporary labor contributions,
- and a strong social-partnership package,

**if** it is tied to:
- binding investment in Germany,
- meaningful management and owner contribution,
- and a credible industrial plan that preserves the state’s economic anchor role.

I will not support a deal where workers give up pay or security and the company keeps all flexibility for later closure threats. That would be a one-sided bargain.

## To the family and investors
I hear your concern about value destruction. But you should also hear this clearly: **value destruction is not limited to high wage costs or underutilized plants**.

A closure-led strategy can destroy value through:
- prolonged industrial conflict,
- legal and co-determination battles,
- supply chain disruption,
- brand damage,
- political backlash,
- and a weakened German industrial base that undermines the company’s long-term strength.

So yes, the company needs cost reduction. But no, it does not automatically follow that the hardest instrument is the best one.

If you want my support for deeper restructuring, you need to accept that preserving regional industrial capacity has real strategic value, not just sentimental value.

## To the state’s own role
I will be very clear here: the state’s blocking-minority and co-determination role are not ornamental. They exist because this company is not just another financial asset. It is a regional economic anchor.

I will not endorse any approach that sidelines the state or treats its rights as an obstacle to be engineered around. That is not an acceptable governance path.

## What would move me
What could move the state is a negotiated package that:
- **protects German sites wherever possible**,
- avoids compulsory redundancies,
- relies first on attrition, redeployment, working-time changes, and productivity gains,
- includes real contributions from management and owners,
- and secures future investment in Germany.

If that package is sufficiently strong, I can support it.  
If it is only a prelude to closures later, I cannot.

## My position on the expert’s numbers
The finance assessment suggests the company may need **several billion euros** of recurring improvement. I do not dispute that the requirement may be large.

But the crucial question is not whether the gap is large. The question is:
**Can a negotiated, socially responsible package close it without sacrificing the regional industrial base?**

That question has **not** been answered yet. Therefore closures cannot be treated as inevitable.

## What I propose as the next step
I want a concrete bridge package, with numbers, not ideology:

1. **Exact savings target** at brand level  
2. **Quantified savings from each non-closure measure**  
3. **Residual gap after those measures**  
4. **Labour contribution** that is temporary, proportionate, and matched by management/owner contribution  
5. **Investment commitments in Germany** that make the remaining footprint viable  
6. **A review mechanism** that checks delivery without predetermining closures

## Bottom line
I am prepared to support a hard restructuring.  
I am **not** prepared to support plant closures as the assumed answer.

If management and investors want my backing, they need to prove that a serious non-closure package cannot work. Until then, my position remains:

- protect jobs,
- protect plants,
- preserve the state’s co-determination role,
- and only accept restructuring that keeps the company a regional economic anchor.

If they refuse that framework, then I will use the rights and leverage the state holds under the special statute.
