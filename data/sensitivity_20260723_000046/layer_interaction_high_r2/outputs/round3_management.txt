I’ll respond clearly, because we are now at the point where ambiguity is just another form of delay.

## Where I agree
I agree with the works council, the union, the state, the family, and investors on one fundamental point: **this must be a durable restructuring, not a cosmetic pause**.

I also agree that a solution has to be **quantified**. We need to know:
- the exact savings gap,
- what non-closure measures deliver,
- what remains,
- and what is needed to close that residual gap.

On that, the expert’s assessment was useful. It confirmed what management has said from the start: **non-closure measures can contribute materially, but they often do not fully replace the fixed-cost effect of footprint reduction when overcapacity is structural**.

## Where I disagree
I do **not** accept the employee-side position that:
- no plant closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- and binding job and site guarantees through 2030

can be treated as the default minimum for talks.

That is not a solution to the cost problem. It is a demand to freeze the current structure and hope the market improves. It won’t.

I also do **not** accept the argument that because closures carry political and social costs, they are therefore off-limits. Of course they carry costs. So do strikes, chronic overcapacity, lost competitiveness, and underinvestment. The question is not whether a hard choice is painful. The question is whether the company survives competitively.

## To the works council
You say closures are a choice, not a necessity. I’m prepared to test that — but not by pretending the numbers are irrelevant.

What I reject is your conclusion that we must start from an absolute no-closure, no-redundancy, 2030-guarantee position and then ask management to prove why that should change.

That reverses reality. Management has a fiduciary duty to fix the cost base. If a non-closure package can truly close the gap, I will support it. But it has to be **economically sufficient** and **durable**. So far, you have not shown that a fully protected footprint can do that.

## To the union
I acknowledge your willingness to discuss:
- temporary wage restraint,
- bonus deferrals,
- working-time flexibility,
- productivity measures,
- and an employee contribution fund.

That is constructive. But I have to be equally clear: **temporary concessions do not solve a structural cost problem on their own**.

If the cost gap is permanent, then temporary measures are only a bridge. They may buy time, but they do not restore competitiveness unless they are paired with real structural change.

I also reject the idea that management and owners are somehow not contributing. We are proposing the restructuring, bearing the execution risk, and exposing the company to confrontation because the status quo is worse. But if labour wants to see more visible management restraint, I’m willing to discuss that as part of a serious package. I am not willing to trade it for a refusal to adjust capacity.

## To the state government
I respect the state’s role and the importance of regional stability. But the state’s special rights cannot be used to preserve an uncompetitive structure indefinitely.

Your position seems to be: restructure, but do not touch sites. That is not a complete answer unless you can show how the cost gap gets closed anyway.

If the state wants to avoid closures, then it should help deliver a package that actually achieves the required savings:
- capacity consolidation,
- working-time changes,
- redeployment,
- attrition,
- productivity gains,
- and real labour-cost reduction.

If that package is enough, I will not insist on closures for their own sake. But it must be enough.

## To the family and investors
I agree with your core message: **delay destroys value**.

I also agree that a one-sided settlement that preserves every site and shifts the burden onto the company’s future would be irresponsible. We need a real reset, not a symbolic compromise dressed up as social peace.

## On the expert assessment
The expert was careful and, frankly, realistic:
- non-closure measures can produce real savings,
- but they often deliver only a portion of the savings of a plant closure,
- especially where overcapacity is significant and fixed costs are high.

That is exactly why management cannot accept a blanket no-closure commitment in advance.

The expert also made clear that the decisive questions are:
1. what is the annual savings target,
2. what do non-closure measures deliver,
3. what remains,
4. and whether that remaining gap justifies footprint reduction.

That is the correct structure for this discussion.

## What I can support
I can support a serious hybrid package if it is real, quantified, and enforceable:
- capacity consolidation,
- shift reduction,
- internal transfers and redeployment,
- attrition and voluntary exits,
- working-time adjustments,
- temporary labour-cost concessions,
- productivity and overhead reduction,
- and targeted investment commitments in Germany.

I am also prepared to discuss:
- time-limited job-security arrangements,
- if they are tied to measurable restructuring outcomes,
- not if they amount to a blanket veto on change.

## What I cannot accept
I cannot accept:
- a blanket exclusion of closures,
- a blanket exclusion of compulsory redundancies,
- guarantees that lock in today’s structure through 2030,
- or a package that leaves the cost base materially uncompetitive.

That would not be a settlement. It would be a delay mechanism.

## The decision point
So here is where I stand:

### If the employee side can show a package that:
- materially reduces capacity,
- materially reduces labour cost,
- is enforceable,
- and restores competitiveness without closures,

then I am open to it.

### If not,
then we must keep deeper structural measures on the table, including plant rationalisation.

That is not an empty threat. It is the only responsible position if the goal is to restore the brand’s return on sales and free funds for the next product cycle to 2030.

## What I propose next
I want the next round to be factual, not rhetorical. We need:
1. the exact savings target,
2. the savings from each non-closure measure,
3. the residual gap,
4. the wage and productivity contribution labour is prepared to make,
5. and the capacity reduction required to close the gap.

If the numbers support a no-closure package, fine — show me.  
If they do not, then we need the courage to say so openly and stop pretending otherwise.
