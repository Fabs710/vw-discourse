I will be direct.

## Where I agree

I agree with management, the state, and the investors on one central point: **this company needs a real structural reset, not a symbolic agreement**. If the German cost base remains above peers and capacity stays misaligned with demand, then margins will remain weak and the company will not generate the cash needed for future product and technology investment.

I also agree that **delay is expensive**. A drawn-out confrontation that simply postpones decisions would destroy value and, in the end, hurt both the business and the workforce.

## Where I disagree

I do **not** accept the current employee-side position as a viable basis for a final settlement if it means:
- an absolute ban on plant closures,
- an absolute ban on compulsory redundancies,
- and binding site/job guarantees that eliminate the company’s ability to adjust structurally.

That combination is not “social responsibility.” It is a demand to freeze an uncompetitive structure and push the adjustment problem into the future. I cannot support that, because it would erode the company’s long-term value.

At the same time, I do **not** accept management’s implication that closures are automatically the right answer. They are a serious step, especially in a company of this history. If there is a credible alternative that achieves the same durable effect, it should be taken seriously.

## On the numbers

Management is right to insist on numbers, but those numbers must be shown clearly.

Before any irreversible step is taken, I want a structured comparison of:
1. the exact savings gap,
2. what non-closure measures deliver,
3. what remains unresolved,
4. and whether that remaining gap is truly large enough to justify closures or compulsory redundancies.

That said, I am not prepared to let “show us the numbers” become a tactic for indefinite delay. If the analysis shows that non-closure measures do not close the gap, then that conclusion has to be accepted.

## To the works council and union

You are right that trust, skills, and social peace matter. They do. But trust cannot be built on a promise that the company will remain structurally protected from any hard choices.

I can support:
- attrition,
- internal redeployment,
- capacity consolidation,
- working-time flexibility,
- temporary wage restraint,
- bonus deferral or reduction,
- productivity measures,
- and a genuine employee contribution,

**if** these measures are large enough and durable enough to restore competitiveness.

What I cannot support is a package that is politically comfortable but economically insufficient.

A 2030 guarantee may be discussed, but only if it is tied to a realistic industrial plan and does not prevent necessary restructuring. I will not endorse a guarantee that becomes a straightjacket.

## To management

I share your concern that temporary or cosmetic concessions are not enough. But I also expect management to prove discipline and seriousness in how it approaches the workforce.

If you want labour to contribute, then the package must be visibly balanced:
- management restraint,
- credible execution,
- investment commitments,
- and clear evidence that the burden is not being pushed almost entirely onto employees.

I will not support a settlement that looks like a one-sided cost cut dressed up as transformation.

## To the regional state government

I respect the state’s role and its concern for regional employment. But stewardship also means recognizing when protecting every site becomes self-defeating.

The state should be part of a solution that secures the company’s future, not a shield that preserves an unsustainable cost structure. If the company becomes weaker because necessary restructuring is blocked, the region will lose more in the end.

## What I can support

I can support a package that combines:
- meaningful labour-cost contributions,
- capacity reduction through non-blunt instruments where possible,
- voluntary exits and attrition,
- internal transfer and redeployment,
- productivity improvements,
- targeted investment in Germany,
- and a clear, enforceable path back to competitive margins.

I cannot support:
- permanent impairment of the company’s governance flexibility,
- a deal that leaves the business uncompetitive,
- or an outcome that merely delays the next crisis.

## Bottom line

My position is simple:

- **I support a negotiated restructuring.**
- **I do not support preserving the status quo.**
- **I do not support a settlement that blocks real structural change.**
- **I do support alternatives to closures if they are real, quantified, and durable.**

So the next step must be concrete:
- exact savings target,
- quantified effect of each measure,
- residual gap,
- and a decision on whether that gap can be closed without plant closures or compulsory redundancies.

If the answer is yes, I am open.  
If the answer is no, then deeper structural measures must remain on the table.
