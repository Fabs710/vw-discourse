I share the concern that this company cannot drift into permanent uncompetitiveness. The regional government is not interested in preserving structures that slowly erode jobs, investment, and the company’s future. We need a viable industrial base, not a managed decline.

That said, I need to be very clear about where I draw the line.

## Where I agree
I agree with management that:
- the cost problem is real,
- the current situation requires a durable solution,
- and a purely cosmetic package would be irresponsible.

I also agree with the works council and the union that:
- this cannot be solved by simply loading all the pain onto the workforce,
- industrial peace is valuable,
- and a negotiated restructuring is far preferable to a destructive confrontation.

## Where I disagree with management
What I cannot support is a restructuring logic that starts from plant closures and mass layoffs as the default answer.

This company is not a financial shell. It is a central industrial anchor in Germany and especially in our region. German sites are not just cost centers; they are part of the company’s long-term technological and industrial capacity. If we destroy that base too aggressively, we may buy short-term savings at the price of long-term weakness.

I also do not accept any approach that sidelines the state as a shareholder and co-governing actor. Under the special statute, our role is not decorative. We have a legitimate veto interest precisely because decisions like this can have lasting regional consequences.

## Where I disagree with the “no flexibility” line
At the same time, I have to speak candidly to the employee side: if the answer is simply “no closures, no compulsory redundancies, no reductions, no structural change,” then that is not a real solution either.

I understand the need to protect jobs and sites. That is my priority too. But the company must become competitive again. So I am open to:
- working-time adjustments,
- voluntary instruments,
- negotiated capacity reductions,
- temporary labor-cost contributions,
- productivity measures,
- and binding investment commitments for the German footprint.

What I am **not** open to is a solution that:
- guts regional employment,
- eliminates the state’s co-determination and veto role,
- or uses the crisis to push through a one-sided transfer of risk onto workers and the region.

## What would be acceptable
A serious path forward would need to satisfy three conditions:

1. **Preserve the core German industrial footprint**
   - No blanket plant closure logic.
   - No irreversible damage to regional employment.

2. **Deliver genuine competitiveness gains**
   - Not symbolic savings.
   - Not just deferral.
   - A structurally credible package.

3. **Be negotiated and socially balanced**
   - Workforce contribution, yes.
   - But paired with investment, product commitments, and fairness across all stakeholders.

## My message
So I am not here to block restructuring. I am here to block an outcome that sacrifices jobs and regional stability without exhausting alternatives.

If management can show that equivalent savings are achievable without closure decisions that undermine the company’s role as a regional anchor, I will engage seriously. If not, then I will not support a package that turns German plants and employees into the sole adjustment reserve.

In short:
- **Yes to a durable turnaround**
- **No to a solution that destroys the industrial and social base**
- **Yes to negotiation**
- **No to sidelining the state’s role or treating closures as the easy answer**

I expect a proposal that proves there is a path to competitiveness **with** the German footprint intact as far as possible, not one that assumes its erosion from the start.
