I will respond clearly, because the situation is too serious for vague language.

## Where I agree
I agree with the central assessment from all sides that this company has a **real competitiveness problem**. I also agree that a purely cosmetic solution would be irresponsible. We do need a durable settlement, not a temporary patch.

I also agree that the workforce cannot be the only side making sacrifices. If there is a restructuring package, it must involve **shared burden**, including contributions from management, owners, and capital.

## Where I disagree
I do **not** accept the framing that **plant closures or compulsory redundancies are “necessary”** just because management says so. That is not an economic law; it is a decision. And it is a decision we reject.

I also do **not** accept the repeated attempt to make the employee side carry the burden of adjustment while keeping the deepest pain off management and ownership. If workers are asked to contribute, then there must be **real guarantees** in return:
- no forced redundancies,
- no German plant closures,
- protection of the collective bargaining framework,
- and binding site and investment commitments.

If those conditions are not met, then there is no fair basis for a deal.

## On the works council’s line
We are aligned on the core red lines:
- **No plant closures in Germany**
- **No compulsory redundancies**
- **No dismantling of co-determination**
- **No tearing up of wage and job-security standards**

That is the minimum for any serious negotiation.

## On management’s argument
Management says it needs “durable structural savings.” Fine. But you have not yet shown that those savings can only be achieved through closures and forced layoffs. You keep asserting equivalence, but you have not proven that negotiated alternatives are insufficient.

If you want us to consider labor concessions, then bring a **numerically credible package** that shows:
- the exact savings gap,
- which measures close it,
- how much each side contributes,
- and how job and site security are preserved.

Until then, your position is still too one-sided.

## What we are prepared to discuss
We are prepared to negotiate a serious package, but only under fair conditions. That can include:
- temporary wage moderation,
- foregoing bonuses,
- working-time flexibility,
- voluntary exit instruments only,
- internal redeployment,
- qualification and training,
- productivity measures,
- capacity adjustments through negotiated means,
- and a workforce contribution to the turnaround fund.

But that contribution is **conditional**. It is not a blank cheque for management to destroy jobs later.

## What would be unacceptable
Let me state this plainly:
- **forced redundancies are unacceptable**
- **German plant closures are unacceptable**
- **open-ended insecurity is unacceptable**
- **a deal where workers pay and others do not is unacceptable**

If management insists on those paths, then you should expect industrial resistance. We do not threaten strikes lightly, but the peace obligation has ended, and our members expect action if talks are just a cover for cuts.

## To the state and the family
If you want a stable industrial future, then use your governance power to support a **negotiated settlement that protects the German footprint**. A short-term accounting fix that provokes labor conflict and long-term industrial damage is not value-preserving.

## Bottom line
We are ready for a **fair, shared-sacrifice settlement**:
- employees contribute,
- management and owners contribute,
- sites and jobs are protected,
- and the company gets a durable path forward.

We are **not** ready to accept closures, forced layoffs, or a restructuring that treats the workforce as the adjustment reserve.

If management wants to move, it should stop speaking in generalities and put a concrete alternative on the table — with binding commitments, real numbers, and no hidden closure agenda.
