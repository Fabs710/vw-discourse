I’ll be direct.

## First, what I agree with
I agree with all parties on the core diagnosis: **the company has a real structural competitiveness problem**. The German cost base is too high, the European market is shrinking, and we are not where we need to be on return on sales. If we do not materially reduce fixed cost and overcapacity, we will not fund the next product cycle, software, and technology to 2030.

I also agree that a **cosmetic compromise is worthless**. A deal that merely defers the pain is not a solution.

And I agree with the expert that the right test is not rhetoric but **durable, enforceable, risk-adjusted savings**.

## Where I disagree
I do **not** accept the employee side’s position that we can rule out:
- German plant closures,
- compulsory redundancies,
- and any meaningful job or site adjustment,

while still claiming to have a serious restructuring.

That position is internally inconsistent. You cannot demand:
- full protection of all sites,
- full job guarantees through 2030,
- no forced reductions,
- no closure option,
- and at the same time insist the company become structurally competitive.

Those things do not automatically fit together.

### To the Works Council
You keep saying closures are a choice, not a necessity. Fine — then show the numbers.

But so far, I have not seen a package from your side that closes the gap on a **recurring, durable basis**. Voluntary exits, working-time flexibility, redeployment, specialization, and temporary moderation can help, but I do not see how they deliver the scale of fixed-cost and capacity reduction we need **without** any plant closures or meaningful headcount reduction.

So I reject the idea that “no closures” is already a viable answer. It is not. Not yet.

### To the Trade Union
I agree workers should not carry the burden alone. They won’t. But your red lines are too absolute to be credible if they prevent a real turnaround.

A package built only on:
- voluntary exits,
- temporary wage moderation,
- and flexible hours,

will not be enough if the network remains structurally too large. If you want to avoid closures, then bring a package that is genuinely equivalent in:
- scale,
- permanence,
- and enforceability.

So far, that has not happened.

### To the Regional State Government
I respect your concern about irreversible regional damage. But “protecting the industrial base” means keeping it viable, not preserving every existing structure.

A state-backed position that says “no closures under any circumstances” risks protecting today’s footprint at the cost of tomorrow’s company. That is not responsible stewardship either.

### To the Controlling Family and Investors
You are right on the principle: structural uncompetitiveness is unacceptable. A deal that preserves excess capacity and high fixed cost is not value-creating.

But I also caution against pretending that closures are automatically the best answer in every case. The value destruction from prolonged strikes, political deadlock, and trust breakdown is real. So I will not accept closures as a reflex. I will accept them if they are needed to achieve the required savings or if no equivalent package exists.

## What management needs
We need to stop debating abstractions.

[SUMMON: independent restructuring finance specialist to build a site-by-site, 5-year comparison of closure, hybrid, and non-closure options, including recurring savings, one-time costs, strike risk, reversal risk, and net present value.]

That analysis must show, by site and by year:
1. the exact savings gap,
2. the savings from closures,
3. the savings from a hybrid package,
4. the savings from a no-closure package,
5. and the risk-adjusted 5-year NPV of each.

If the alternative package truly matches closure economics, I will consider it. If it does not, then we need the deeper structural restructuring on the table.

## My position, clearly
I am prepared to negotiate **hard** on the mechanism.

I am **not** prepared to give up the outcome we need:
- a structural reduction in cost,
- a structural reduction in overcapacity,
- and a durable improvement in competitiveness.

That means:
- closures remain an option,
- compulsory redundancies remain an option if necessary,
- and legacy guarantees cannot be treated as untouchable if they block viability.

## What I am willing to consider
I am open to a **hybrid settlement** if — and only if — it is genuinely equivalent on economics and enforceability. That could include:
- capacity consolidation,
- site specialization,
- voluntary exits at scale,
- working-time changes,
- temporary wage and bonus moderation,
- productivity commitments,
- and investment commitments.

But it must produce real, recurring savings. It must not be a repackaged status quo.

## What is not acceptable
- A no-closure deal that leaves the cost base uncompetitive
- A job guarantee that prevents necessary restructuring
- A compromise that preserves overcapacity and just delays the crisis
- A settlement that asks management to sign up to future failure

## Bottom line
We are not here to preserve every existing site and guarantee. We are here to restore the company’s competitiveness and future investment capacity.

If the employee side can produce a package that is **quantitatively sufficient, durable, and enforceable**, I will engage seriously.

If not, then I will continue to insist on structural measures, including closures if necessary.

That is the reality of the situation.
