We need to stop talking past each other.

## Where I agree
I agree with everyone on one fundamental point: **this company has a real structural competitiveness problem**. We are in a shrinking European market, under severe price pressure, and our German cost base is too high. If we do not fix that, we will fail the return-on-sales target and we will not have the cash to invest in future products, software, and technology to 2030.

I also agree that a **cosmetic compromise is unacceptable**. A deal that merely buys a few months of peace while leaving excess capacity and high fixed costs in place would be irresponsible.

And I agree with the specialist’s core logic: the test is not whether a package sounds fair, but whether it delivers **durable, enforceable, risk-adjusted savings**.

## Where I disagree
I do **not** accept the employee side’s repeated claim that:
- no plant closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- full job guarantees through 2030,
- and preservation of all legacy protections

can coexist with a serious structural turnaround.

That is not a restructuring plan. That is a status quo defense.

You keep saying closures are a political choice, not an economic necessity. But if the result is that we preserve overcapacity, leave fixed costs too high, and fail to restore competitiveness, then we have not solved the problem — we have just postponed it.

## To the Works Council
I hear your willingness to discuss flexibility, voluntary exits, redeployment, qualification, and productivity. That is not nothing. But I need to be direct: **those measures alone are not enough** unless they produce hard, permanent cost and capacity reduction at the scale required.

You ask us to prove that closures are necessary. Fair enough. The burden is not on rhetoric; it is on numbers. But the same applies to you: if you want to rule out closures, then you must produce a package that is **quantitatively equivalent** in recurring savings, durability, and enforceability.

So far, I have not heard that package.

## To the Trade Union
You are right that workers should not carry the burden alone. They will not. But your red lines are too rigid if they prevent a solution that actually restores the business.

I am not demanding pain for its own sake. I am demanding:
- real capacity reduction,
- a material labor-cost contribution,
- lower fixed costs,
- and enough flexibility to make the German footprint competitive again.

If you want to avoid closures, then bring measures that are not temporary gestures:
- not just voluntary exits,
- not just short-term moderation,
- not just promises of productivity.

Show me the durable savings.

## To the Regional State Government
I appreciate your concern about irreversible damage to the industrial base. I share that concern. But protecting the industrial base means keeping it **viable**, not freezing it in its current cost structure.

You cannot say “protect jobs and sites” and at the same time reject any serious capacity reduction. Those two goals are in tension. We need a solution that preserves the company’s long-term role in the region by making it competitive enough to survive.

## To the Controlling Family and Investors
I understand your insistence on value creation and margin recovery. You are right that a deal that preserves structural uncompetitiveness is not acceptable. But you also need to recognize that a crude closure-first approach can destroy value through strikes, reputational damage, and political deadlock.

So the right question is not “closures or no closures.” The right question is: **what package produces equivalent savings with acceptable execution risk?**

That is where I remain open — but only if the package is real.

## On the expert analysis
The specialist made the key point very clearly: a non-closure package can only be credible if it includes:
- real fixed-cost removal,
- measurable headcount reduction,
- capacity consolidation,
- multi-year durability,
- and enforceability.

I agree. And that is exactly why I am skeptical of the current employee-side position. A promise of flexibility or voluntary exits is not enough if the network remains structurally too large.

## What management requires
Let me be explicit again:

1. **Structural reduction in overcapacity**
   - not symbolic reshuffling;
   - not merely shifting volume between plants.

2. **Material labor-cost contribution**
   - working-time changes alone will not close the gap;
   - we need a durable cost reduction.

3. **Lower fixed costs**
   - enough to improve margins sustainably.

4. **Investment headroom for 2030**
   - otherwise we will not fund the next generation of products.

## What I am prepared to consider
I am not attached to closures as a principle. If the employee side can produce a package that is genuinely equivalent on:
- scale,
- timing,
- durability,
- and enforceability,

then I will consider it seriously.

That could include:
- capacity consolidation,
- site specialization,
- voluntary departures at scale,
- working-time adjustments,
- wage or bonus moderation,
- productivity measures,
- and removal of legacy cost structures.

But it must add up. If it does not, then it is not a turnaround.

## What is not acceptable
- A deal that preserves structural overcapacity
- A deal that leaves the brand uncompetitive
- A deal that postpones the hard decision
- A deal that protects every legacy guarantee while asking management to solve the crisis anyway

## My bottom line
I want a negotiated settlement, but it has to be a **real restructuring settlement**.

So here is the question to the employee side, in plain language:
**What exact annual savings do you deliver, from which sites and functions, by when, and with what binding mechanism that prevents reversal?**

Until that is answered with hard numbers, I will not accept a package that simply rules out the measures needed to restore competitiveness.

If no equivalent package exists, then management must pursue the deeper structural restructuring required. That is not a threat; it is the reality of the situation.
