I will engage directly.

## First: where I agree
I agree with nearly everyone on the basic diagnosis:
- the company has a **real competitiveness problem**,
- the German cost base is too high,
- a cosmetic settlement would be pointless,
- and the workforce cannot be expected to carry the entire burden.

I also agree with the expert that the debate must be grounded in **numbers, durability, and enforceability**, not slogans.

That is the correct standard.

## Where I disagree — and strongly
I do **not** accept the repeated move in this discussion that starts from:
1. management says closures are needed,
2. owners and investors echo that,
3. then everyone asks labor to prove a non-closure package is equivalent,
4. while closures remain the implicit default.

That reverses the burden in a way I reject.

If management wants to impose plant closures or keep compulsory redundancies on the table, then **management must prove necessity**, site by site and year by year. Not in general terms. Not with hand-waving about “structural overcapacity.” We need hard evidence:
- exact savings gap,
- by site and function,
- implementation costs,
- strike and delay risks,
- and the 5-year risk-adjusted comparison versus negotiated alternatives.

Until that is shown, closures are not “necessary.” They are a choice — and for us, an unacceptable one.

## To management
You keep asking for “equivalent” savings. Fine. But you are still treating closure as the benchmark and the fallback. That is not an even bargaining position.

If you want to talk seriously, then put forward a package that is:
- **no German plant closures**
- **no compulsory redundancies**
- **preserved collective bargaining**
- **binding job security through 2030**
- **real investment commitments**
- **capacity reduction through specialization, not shutdown**

Then we can compare it against your closure scenario.

What we will not do is pre-authorize plant destruction to make your numbers work.

## To the Works Council
I support your line on the essentials:
- no closures,
- no forced redundancies,
- no dismantling of co-determination,
- no breaking of job guarantees.

That is the right line.

But I also want to be practical: if we are serious about a negotiated turnaround, then we need a package that actually produces **durable fixed-cost relief**, not just temporary relief. That means labor can discuss:
- voluntary exits,
- working-time flexibility,
- temporary wage moderation,
- bonus moderation,
- redeployment,
- retraining,
- productivity measures,
- and site specialization.

However — and this is crucial — those are only on the table inside a framework that rules out closures and forced layoffs. Without that framework, there is no fair bargaining.

## To the state government
You say you want to avoid “cosmetic” deals. I agree. But “not cosmetic” does **not** mean “therefore closures.”

If the state is serious about preserving the regional industrial base, then it should use its blocking position to support a settlement that:
- protects sites,
- preserves jobs,
- keeps co-determination meaningful,
- and forces restructuring through negotiated means.

A state that merely says “no cosmetic deal” while keeping closure options alive is not protecting the region; it is licensing irreversible damage.

## To the family and investors
I hear the concern about long-term value. That concern is legitimate.

But I reject the idea that worker concessions should be used to legitimize a one-way restructuring where:
- labor gives,
- management keeps strategic exit options,
- owners preserve flexibility,
- and the workforce absorbs the social risk.

That is not shared sacrifice. That is asymmetry.

If you want our cooperation, then the burden must be shared in a real way:
- owners accept lower short-term returns,
- management accepts binding constraints,
- labor contributes through moderation and flexibility,
- and the company commits to the German footprint.

## On the expert’s comparison
The expert’s proposed model is useful **if** it is genuinely independent and not designed to justify pre-decided closures.

[SUMMON: independent restructuring valuation specialist to model site-by-site, 5-year risk-adjusted savings for closure, hybrid, and no-closure options; assess strike risk, reversal risk, and enforceability.]

That specialist should be asked to compare:
- closure-led restructuring,
- hybrid restructuring,
- and no-closure restructuring,

using:
- recurring savings,
- one-time costs,
- implementation delay,
- strike risk,
- reversal risk,
- and net 5-year NPV.

I would welcome that **because it tests management’s claim**. I do **not** accept it as a license to close plants if the numbers are merely convenient.

## What a serious labor-side contribution looks like
We are not refusing burden-sharing. We are refusing to let workers bear **all** the pain.

A serious package could include:
- temporary wage moderation,
- bonus suspension or deferral,
- working-time adjustments,
- voluntary departures,
- internal redeployment,
- retraining,
- productivity improvements,
- site specialization,
- and capacity reduction through consolidation of lines and functions.

But it must be paired with:
- **no closures**
- **no compulsory redundancies**
- **job security through 2030**
- **protected collective wage framework**
- **binding investment in German sites**

That is the minimum for a fair bargain.

## What is not acceptable
Let me be explicit:
- “We need closures to make the deal serious.”
- “The closure option must stay on the table.”
- “Forced redundancies are a backstop.”
- “Job guarantees are too rigid.”
- “Labor must prove equivalence before management proves necessity.”

No. Those are exactly the positions that will prolong conflict.

## Bottom line
We are prepared to make a fair contribution to a durable turnaround.

We are **not** prepared to trade away:
- German plants,
- job security,
- or the collective bargaining framework

so that management can have a cleaner restructuring chart.

If the table wants a settlement, it must be one that:
- closes the savings gap through negotiated, non-destructive measures,
- shares the burden fairly,
- and rules out compulsory redundancies and plant closures.

Anything less will not be accepted.
