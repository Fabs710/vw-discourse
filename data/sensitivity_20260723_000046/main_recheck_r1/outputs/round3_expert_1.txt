Yes — the key issue in my domain is **equivalence**: whether a non-closure package can generate savings that are **recurring, durable, measurable, and enforceable enough** to stand in for closure-led restructuring.

## What data points are essential
To judge that, I would need at minimum:

1. **Baseline cost structure by site and function**
   - labor cost per vehicle
   - fixed overhead by plant
   - depreciation / underutilization burden
   - logistics and interplant transfer costs
   - warranty / quality / ramp-up impacts if capacity is moved

2. **Closure scenario economics**
   - annual gross savings by plant
   - one-time closure costs
   - transfer / decommissioning costs
   - stranded overhead
   - labor severance / transition costs
   - timing of savings realization

3. **Alternative package economics**
   - wage moderation, bonus suspension, working-time changes
   - voluntary exits and their profile
   - redeployment/training costs
   - productivity assumptions
   - capacity consolidation benefits
   - duration of concessions and reversion risk

4. **Implementation mechanics**
   - who is bound, for how long, and by what instrument
   - what happens if targets are missed
   - whether savings are automatic or discretionary
   - whether site guarantees are conditional or absolute

5. **Risk adjustments**
   - strike risk
   - execution slippage
   - legal / governance delay risk
   - chance of concession reversal after 2–3 years
   - impact on product quality, launch cadence, and utilization

## The decisive metric
If the parties disagree, the single most decisive metric I would use is:

**Risk-adjusted recurring annual EBITDA or cash-cost saving, net of implementation cost, measured at full run-rate and discounted for reversal/execution risk.**

Why this one:
- it captures **magnitude**,
- it captures **durability**,
- it captures **net benefit**,
- and it is closer to what determines whether the restructuring actually improves enterprise value and solvency headroom.

## Important caveat
A non-closure package can only be called “equivalent” if the savings are:
- **permanent or very long-dated**,
- **not dependent on goodwill alone**,
- and **large enough to offset the lost fixed-cost removal from closures**.

In practice, many “alternatives” fail because they are:
- temporary,
- hard to enforce,
- or sufficient only in year 1–2, not over the full restructuring horizon.

So the real test is not whether the package sounds balanced; it is whether it delivers **closure-like economics without closure-like assets being removed**. That is a high bar.
