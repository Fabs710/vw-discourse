Yes. From a restructuring finance standpoint, the model needs to test whether the non-closure or hybrid package can deliver **risk-adjusted recurring savings** that are economically equivalent to the closure case.

## Essential data points
You need, by **site and year**, at least:

### 1) Baseline operating data
- headcount by plant/function
- direct labor cost per unit
- fixed overhead by site
- utilization / capacity
- current production volume
- contribution margin by model/site
- planned product allocations

### 2) Closure scenario inputs
- one-time closure costs
  - severance
  - transfer/redeployment
  - decommissioning
  - write-downs
  - logistics disruption
- timing of savings
- lost output / transition loss
- strike or legal-delay risk if relevant
- whether savings are truly permanent or partly offset elsewhere

### 3) Non-closure / hybrid package inputs
- wage moderation level and duration
- bonus suspension / deferral
- working-time flexibility
- voluntary exit assumptions
- retraining/redeployment costs
- productivity gain assumptions
- line specialization / consolidation savings
- investment commitments needed to secure labor terms
- duration of guarantees and escape clauses

### 4) Execution and enforceability
- probability of strike or work stoppage
- probability of implementation slippage
- probability of later reversal
- remedies for non-performance
- whether targets are objectively measurable
- whether commitments are binding on all relevant entities

## Decisive metric
If I had to choose one metric, I would use:

> **risk-adjusted 5-year recurring savings net of one-time costs**

That is the cleanest test because it captures:
- permanence,
- net economics,
- and probability of delivery.

## Pass/fail test for equivalence
A non-closure package is only comparable if it has:
1. **similar annual recurring savings** to closure,
2. **credible durability** through the savings horizon,
3. **acceptable execution risk**,
4. **enforceable remedies** if targets are missed.

If savings are temporary, contingent on goodwill, or likely to unwind after 2–3 years, then the package is **not equivalent** to closure-based restructuring.

## What I would model explicitly
For each option:
- annual gross savings
- one-time costs
- timing of savings ramp-up
- probability-weighted implementation
- 5-year NPV at the group’s hurdle discount rate
- sensitivity to wage inflation, utilization, and strike duration

## Practical note
A “no-closure” package can still be economically valid if it produces:
- durable fixed-cost reduction,
- real capacity consolidation,
- and enforceable labor concessions.

But if it relies mainly on temporary moderation or voluntary behavior, it usually underperforms closure economics on a risk-adjusted basis.
