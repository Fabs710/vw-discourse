I will be direct.

## Where I agree
I agree with management, the state, investors, and the experts on the central diagnosis: **this company has a structural competitiveness problem**. We are not debating a minor optimization. If the German cost base stays too high and capacity stays misaligned, long-term value erodes. That is unacceptable for the company, its workforce, and the owners.

I also agree that a **cosmetic compromise would be worse than no deal**. A short-lived truce that preserves overcapacity and high fixed costs simply pushes the crisis into the next cycle.

And I agree that any solution must be **durable, enforceable, and numerically sufficient**. If the package does not close the savings gap in a real way, it is not a solution.

## Where I disagree
I do **not** support treating **German plant closures and compulsory redundancies as the default answer**.

That may be one possible restructuring path, but it is not automatically the best one. A forced solution can destroy more value than it creates through:
- strike escalation,
- reputational damage,
- loss of trust,
- political backlash,
- and governance deadlock.

For a controlling owner with a long horizon, that risk matters. A clean chart in a turnaround deck is not enough if execution becomes a multi-year conflict.

At the same time, I also do **not** accept the employee side’s position that:
- no closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- full preservation of guarantees,
- and unchanged structures

can coexist with meaningful competitiveness restoration.

That is not a restructuring settlement. That is a defense of the status quo.

## My position
The right question is not “closures or no closures.” The right question is:

**What package delivers structurally sufficient, recurring, enforceable savings with the least value destruction?**

That can include a hybrid solution, but only if it is hard enough.

## What I need to see
The expert analysis was useful because it clarified the standard. If a non-closure or hybrid package is to be credible, it must show:
1. **real fixed-cost removal**, not just temporary flexibility,
2. **capacity consolidation**, not just internal reshuffling,
3. **measurable headcount reduction**,
4. **multi-year durability**,
5. **enforceability with consequences if targets are missed**.

If those are absent, then the package is not equivalent. It is a delay mechanism.

## To the Works Council and Union
I respect the insistence that workers should not bear the burden alone. I agree.

But I do **not** accept a line that rules out structural adjustment in advance and then asks everyone else to make the numbers work around that veto. If you want the family holding to support a non-closure solution, then it has to be more than a promise of flexibility and voluntary exits.

I would need to see:
- the exact annual savings,
- by site and function,
- over several years,
- with a binding mechanism that prevents reversal.

Without that, your proposal does not meet the standard of a serious turnaround.

## To Management
I agree with you that the current cost base is too high and that the margin target will not be reached without meaningful structural change.

But I am not prepared to endorse closure as the first and only serious option unless you can demonstrate that:
- the alternative really fails on savings,
- the conflict risk is manageable,
- and the long-term value impact is better than a negotiated hybrid.

If you want governance support for a harder path, you need to show the case in hard numbers, not just in principle.

## To the Regional State Government
I understand your concern about regional employment and the industrial base. That concern is legitimate.

But “protect the footprint” cannot mean freezing the structure. The company must remain viable. If you want our support for avoiding closures, then the alternative must still produce enough savings to restore competitiveness. Otherwise, you are protecting jobs today at the expense of jobs tomorrow.

## My governance view
As controlling shareholders, we do not manage day-to-day operations, but we do have a duty to protect the company’s long-term value and governance stability. That means:

- **yes** to a real restructuring,
- **yes** to shared sacrifice,
- **yes** to a negotiated hybrid if it is economically equivalent,
- **no** to a cosmetic peace deal,
- **no** to preservation of structural overcapacity,
- **no** to value-destructive escalation.

## What would move me
I would support a non-closure or hybrid settlement only if it demonstrates:
- recurring savings close to the closure path,
- credible capacity reduction,
- binding labor contributions,
- enforceable triggers,
- and investment commitments that strengthen the German footprint.

If those conditions are not met, then I will not back a deal just because it is politically easier.

## Bottom line
I am open to a **hard, quantified hybrid solution**.

I am **not** open to:
- a symbolic agreement,
- a freeze on change,
- or a settlement that protects every existing guarantee while leaving the company uncompetitive.

The burden is now on the parties to bring a package that is **numerically sufficient, durable, and enforceable**. If they cannot, then we should not pretend that a weak compromise is in the company’s long-term interest.
