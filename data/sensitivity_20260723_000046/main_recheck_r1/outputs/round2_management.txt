We need to stop circling the same red lines and confront the economic reality.

## Where I agree
I agree with all sides on one point: this company needs a **durable, credible turnaround**, not a symbolic exercise. I also agree that a negotiated solution is preferable to a prolonged conflict. Nobody benefits from weeks of lost production, reputational damage, and further erosion of confidence.

I also agree that the workforce should not be treated as the only adjustment variable. Management, owners, and the business as a whole must contribute to the solution.

## Where I disagree — and this is the core issue
What I cannot accept is the repeated claim that:
- **no plant closures**,
- **no compulsory redundancies**,
- **full job guarantees through 2030**,
- and **preservation of the existing framework**
can all remain intact **and** still produce a structurally competitive cost base.

That combination does not add up.

You keep saying closures are a political choice, not an economic necessity. But if the end result is that we preserve excess capacity, keep a structurally high fixed cost base, and fail to reach the return-on-sales target, then we have not restructured anything. We have only postponed the problem.

## To the Works Council and union
I hear your willingness to discuss working-time flexibility, voluntary exits, redeployment, training, productivity, and temporary contributions. That is useful, but I have to be blunt: **those instruments are not enough on their own unless they produce hard, durable savings of the necessary scale**.

A package that avoids closures in name but leaves the cost structure broadly unchanged is not acceptable. Nor is a deal that preserves every legacy guarantee while expecting management to somehow create competitiveness out of thin air.

If the employee side wants to rule out plant closures and compulsory redundancies entirely, then it must bring forward a package that is **quantitatively equivalent** in savings and permanence. So far, I have not heard that number.

## To the state and the family
I appreciate the point that this is not just a financial spreadsheet and that the German footprint matters. I agree. But preserving the industrial base means making it viable, not freezing it in place.

I also agree that a disorderly confrontation would be damaging. That is precisely why I am insisting now on a serious structural package. Delay only increases the risk of a worse outcome later.

## What management requires
Let me be explicit about what we need:

1. **Real capacity reduction**
   - not just temporary utilization fixes,
   - not just internal shuffling,
   - but a structural reduction in overcapacity.

2. **Material labor-cost contribution**
   - working-time measures alone will not close the gap,
   - we need a genuine and lasting contribution to fixed-cost reduction.

3. **Lower fixed costs and more flexibility**
   - enough to restore the brand’s competitiveness in Europe.

4. **Investment capacity for 2030**
   - we need to free funds for future products and technology,
   - otherwise we will simply preserve today’s problem and lose tomorrow’s market.

## What I am open to
I am not attached to closures as a principle. If the employee side can produce a package that is **economically equivalent** in terms of:
- scale,
- durability,
- timing,
- and enforceability,

then we can discuss the mechanism.

That could include a mix of:
- capacity consolidation,
- site specialization,
- working-time reductions,
- voluntary exits,
- productivity measures,
- wage or benefit moderation,
- and removal of legacy constraints.

But I will not pretend that “voluntary” measures automatically solve a structural overcapacity problem. They often do not. And I will not sign up to a cosmetic compromise that preserves the same cost base under a new label.

## What is not acceptable
- A deal that preserves structural overcapacity
- A deal that leaves the company unable to hit its margin target
- A deal that postpones difficult decisions until the next crisis
- A deal that gives us industrial peace today and uncompetitiveness tomorrow

## My position
I want a negotiated settlement, but it must be a **real restructuring settlement**, not a symbolic protection pact.

So the question is simple:
- If you reject closures and compulsory redundancies, **what exact savings do you deliver instead?**
- On which sites?
- By when?
- With what binding effect?
- And with what guarantee that this is not reversed in 12 or 24 months?

Bring that package, with hard numbers, and we can talk. Until then, I will not pretend that your current red lines solve the crisis.

If the table cannot produce a credible equivalent solution, then management will have to pursue the harder restructuring path that restores competitiveness — because the alternative is managed decline, and that is not an option.
