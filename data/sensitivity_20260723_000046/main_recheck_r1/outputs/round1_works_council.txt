As Works Council, I will be very clear from the outset:

We will not accept plant closures in Germany and we will not accept compulsory redundancies. That is the line.

Management cannot solve a strategic problem by throwing thousands of workers, families, and entire regions overboard. The workforce did not create this crisis alone, and it will not be made to pay for it alone. We are prepared to contribute to a viable turnaround, but only on a fair basis and only if jobs and sites are secured.

Our priorities are:

1. Keeping every German plant open.
2. Excluding forced layoffs.
3. Securing binding job guarantees through 2030.
4. Preserving co-determination and the existing job-security framework.
5. Ensuring any restructuring is negotiated, not imposed.
6. Protecting long-term industrial capacity in Germany, not just short-term margins.

If management wants savings, then we are ready to discuss alternatives such as working-time adjustments, internally negotiated flexibility, productivity measures, training, voluntary instruments, and a fair contribution from all sides. But the burden cannot fall primarily on employees, and any solution must come with real investment commitments and a credible future for the German sites.

I also want to say plainly: after the breach of trust caused by the attack on historic job guarantees, confidence in management is extremely low. A fait accompli will not work here. If necessary, the workforce is ready to resist with warning strikes and public mobilization. We would prefer a negotiated solution, but not at the price of closures or forced job cuts.

So my position is simple: restructure, yes — but not by destroying jobs and plants. If there is to be a deal, it must protect the workforce and secure the German footprint for the long term.
