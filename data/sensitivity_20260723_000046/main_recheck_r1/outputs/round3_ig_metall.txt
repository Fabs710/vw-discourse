## Where I agree
I agree with the repeated diagnosis that the company has a **real competitiveness and cost problem**. I also agree that a purely cosmetic deal would be irresponsible. We are not here to freeze an unviable status quo.

I also agree with the specialist’s core point: the issue is **not rhetoric**, but whether a package produces **durable, enforceable, quantified savings**. That is the right standard.

And I agree with the state, the family, and investors on one further point: **the burden cannot land on workers alone**. If there is to be a turnaround, it must be shared.

## Where I disagree
I do **not** accept the repeated implication that the company can only become competitive if it imposes:
- German plant closures,
- compulsory redundancies,
- and open-ended insecurity for the workforce.

That is not an economic law. It is a management preference unless proven otherwise.

### To management
You are still asserting, not proving, that closures are unavoidable. The expert actually made the burden of proof clearer: if you want closures or forced layoffs, then show:
1. the exact savings gap,
2. the savings by site and year,
3. why negotiated alternatives cannot close that gap,
4. and why your path is more durable than a negotiated package.

Until that is shown, closure is not the only credible option. It is one option — and for us, an unacceptable one.

### To the family and investors
You are right that preserving structural uncompetitiveness is not acceptable. But you are wrong if you think the solution is to shift adjustment onto the workforce while preserving the option to close plants later. That would turn any labor contribution into a one-way concession.

### To the state
You are right to want a durable industrial base. But if you truly mean that, then you cannot treat closures as a normal fallback. The regional economy cannot be secured by keeping the threat of irreversible plant shutdown over the workforce.

## What we are prepared to do
We are prepared to discuss a **serious shared-sacrifice package**, provided it is built on firm guarantees:
- **no German plant closures**
- **no compulsory redundancies**
- **protection of the collective bargaining framework**
- **binding job security through 2030**
- **real investment commitments in German sites**

Within that framework, we can discuss:
- working-time flexibility,
- voluntary exits only,
- internal redeployment,
- training and qualification,
- temporary wage moderation,
- bonus suspension,
- productivity measures,
- and capacity reduction through specialization and utilization changes.

But let me be explicit: **labor concessions are conditional**. They are not a blank cheque for management to keep closure options alive.

## On the specialist’s model
The specialist said something important: non-closure measures can work only if they remove enough **fixed cost** and are **durable and enforceable**. I accept that. But that does **not** mean closures are therefore acceptable. It means management must bring a package that actually does the job without destroying sites and jobs.

If the company needs structural savings, then it must accept structural commitments from all sides:
- owners accept lower short-term returns,
- management accepts binding constraints and real negotiation,
- labor contributes through moderation and flexibility,
- and the company commits to the German footprint.

That is a real restructuring settlement. Anything else is just pushing risk downward.

## What is not acceptable
Let me be completely clear:
- **No compulsory redundancies**
- **No German plant closures**
- **No dismantling of co-determination**
- **No deal where workers pay and others retain all strategic options**
- **No open-ended job insecurity disguised as “flexibility”**

If those are the conditions, there is no agreement.

If management wants a serious bargaining round, it should put forward a **quantified hybrid package** with:
- the exact savings gap,
- site-by-site measures,
- duration of labour concessions,
- investment amounts,
- and binding job/site guarantees.

Not slogans. Numbers.

We are **not** ready to accept closures or forced layoffs as the price of doing so.

If the company wants industrial peace, it must build a settlement that protects jobs and sites while sharing the burden fairly. If it cannot do that, then the conflict will continue.
