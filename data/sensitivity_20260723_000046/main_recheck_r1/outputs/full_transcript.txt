From a turnaround finance and plant-economics perspective, the key question is not whether a non-closure package is politically preferable — it is whether it can deliver **the same net present value of savings, with similar certainty and durability, as a closure/relocation package**.

## Bottom line
A **non-closure package can sometimes match closure economics**, but only if it includes **multiple hard measures simultaneously** and if the resulting savings are:

2. **durable for several years**,  
3. **measurable and auditable**, and  
4. **enforceable without requiring repeated renegotiation**.

- capacity consolidation,
- product/site specialization,
- headcount reduction via voluntary exits,
- a meaningful labor-cost concession,
- and hard constraints on future cost drift.

If those are absent, the alternative is usually **deferral, not equivalence**.

## How to judge equivalence properly
The right test is not “can we avoid closures?” but:

### 1) What savings are closures expected to deliver?
For each plant under review, management should quantify:
- fixed-cost avoided annually,
- variable-cost differences,
- overhead absorbed elsewhere,
- restructuring cash costs,
- asset write-downs / exit costs,
- and timeline to realization.

In automotive manufacturing, closure economics are often attractive because they remove:
- plant overhead,
- maintenance and utilities,
- indirect labor,
- underabsorbed depreciation,
- and some corporate support burden.

But closures also carry:
- severance,
- site remediation,
- transfer costs,
- supply-chain disruption,
- and transition risk.

So the relevant number is **net annual run-rate savings after restructuring costs**, not gross payroll reduction.

### 2) What does the alternative need to replace?
A credible non-closure package must replace:
- **fixed-cost elimination** from shuttered capacity,
- **headcount reduction** from closing operations,
- **overcapacity removal** from the network,
- and **unit-cost improvement** from better utilization.

If the closure plan removes, say, €300 million per year of net run-rate cost, the alternative must show an approximately comparable **sustained** effect, not a one-off gain.

### 3) Is the saving durable?
This is the hardest part. Many non-closure measures are fragile:
- temporary wage moderation expires,
- voluntary exits are one-time only,
- working-time reductions can reverse,
- internal transfers shift cost rather than remove it,
- productivity pledges often decay unless embedded in process redesign.

Durability matters more than headline size. A €200 million one-year concession is not equivalent to €150 million of recurring annual fixed-cost reduction.

---

## Which non-closure measures can be economically meaningful
### A. Voluntary exits / early retirement
This can be material if:
- the workforce is aged,
- severance is affordable,
- and the exits are targeted to overhead-heavy or low-utilization areas.

**Strengths:** real headcount reduction, relatively fast.  
**Weaknesses:** one-time, selective, and often too small unless very large numbers leave.

### B. Working-time reduction / flexible schedules
Useful for cyclical demand smoothing.

**Strengths:** quick, avoids compulsory layoffs.  
**Weaknesses:** often a **cost deferral** unless accompanied by permanent productivity gains; if demand remains structurally weak, it just spreads underutilization.

### C. Wage moderation / bonus suspension / benefit changes
This can generate real labor-cost relief if sustained.

**Strengths:** broad-based, measurable.  
**Weaknesses:** can be politically difficult, time-limited, and may not touch the fixed-cost base enough.

### D. Productivity and process redesign
Potentially very valuable, but only if already identified in a detailed operating model:
- labor minutes per vehicle,
- scrap/rework reduction,
- logistics simplification,
- line balancing,
- indirect headcount reduction,
- downtime reduction.

**Strengths:** can be durable.  
**Weaknesses:** implementation risk is high; benefits are often overestimated.

### E. Capacity consolidation without plant closure
This is the most economically serious non-closure substitute if it means:
- one site loses product lines,
- another site absorbs volume,
- shifts are reduced,
- shared services are centralized,
- and a plant becomes specialized.

**Strengths:** can remove fixed cost without formal closure.  
**Weaknesses:** if a plant remains “open but half-empty,” the economics may still be weak unless overhead is actually stripped out.

These measures often sound large but are rarely fully equivalent:
- vague “efficiency improvements,”
- unquantified “flexibility,”
- promises of future competitiveness,
- temporary deferment of bonuses,
- relying only on natural attrition,
- across-the-board wage freezes without structural headcount change,
- shifting volumes without reducing total network capacity.

In turnaround terms, these are often **bridge measures**, not end-state solutions.

A negotiated alternative is only credible if it contains **hard controls**.

### The package should specify:

- by measure,
- by site,
- with implementation dates,
- and with an audit mechanism.

### It should also include:
- binding workforce commitments on hours, pay elements, and headcount corridors,
- penalties if savings are not delivered,
- management commitments on investment and product allocation,
- a trigger mechanism if market volumes deteriorate,
- and a review date that does not reopen the entire deal every few months.

Without these, the package is not “enforceable”; it is aspirational.

# 1) How to compare the packages correctly

### D. Durability
Are savings recurring for 3–5+ years, or only one-off?

### E. Enforceability
Can the company actually rely on the commitments without re-opening them every year?

Discount the expected savings net of costs and risk.

- Multi-year collective agreement with explicit savings formulas
- Site-specific capacity and workforce corridors
- Automatic wage moderation / bonus suspension linked to EBITDA or volume triggers
- Hard headcount ceilings by function
- Clauses tied to investment release conditions
- Step-in rights for management if targets are missed
- Expiry dates with extension only by mutual consent

- General declarations of intent
- Broad “consultation” commitments
- Non-binding investment promises
- Vague productivity pledges
- No audit right over actual savings
- No consequence if assumptions fail

A package with weak enforceability should be discounted heavily in the NPV model.

[Expert: Industrial restructuring finance special — Round 3]
Yes. In restructuring finance terms, the decision hinges on whether the alternative package can replicate the **risk-adjusted, recurring cash savings** of closure-led restructuring, after allowing for implementation cost, timing delay, and reversal risk.

## What I would require to evaluate equivalence
At minimum, I need these inputs by site and by year:

1. **Current cost baseline**
   - labor cost per vehicle
   - fixed overhead per plant
   - underutilization burden
   - logistics / transfer costs
   - quality, ramp-up, and warranty impacts if volume is moved

2. **Closure scenario**
   - annual gross savings per plant
   - one-time closure and severance costs
   - decommissioning / transfer costs
   - stranded overhead remaining elsewhere
   - timing of when savings actually start

3. **Alternative package**
   - wage moderation / bonus suspension
   - working-time changes
   - voluntary exits
   - redeployment and retraining costs
   - productivity assumptions
   - duration of concessions
   - reversion risk at expiry

4. **Enforceability**
   - who is legally bound
   - what instruments are used
   - whether obligations are automatic or discretionary
   - remedies if savings targets are missed
   - whether site guarantees are conditional or absolute

5. **Risk assumptions**
   - strike / disruption risk
   - execution slippage
   - governance delay
   - chance of concession reversal
   - operational knock-on effects

## Decisive metric
If the parties want one finance metric to test equivalence, I would use:

**Risk-adjusted recurring annual cash saving / EBITDA uplift, net of implementation cost, at full run-rate.**

That metric is best because it captures:
- size of the savings,
- timing,
- durability,
- and execution risk.

## How I would frame the comparison
A credible model should compare three cases:

### 1) Closure-led package
Usually strongest on gross fixed-cost removal, but with:
- high one-time costs,
- higher industrial conflict risk,
- possible political / governance delay,
- potential loss of know-how and transfer friction.

### 2) Hybrid package
Can be finance-equivalent if it includes:
- real capacity consolidation,
- measurable headcount reduction,
- wage and working-time moderation,
- enforceable multi-year duration,
- and enough fixed-cost removal.

### 3) No-closure package
Only credible if it produces savings close enough to closure economics after risk adjustment. In practice, these packages often fail on:
- insufficient permanency,
- weak enforcement,
- or too much reliance on voluntary behavior.

## Finance judgment
From a turnaround finance perspective:

- **A non-closure package can be acceptable** if its discounted, risk-adjusted savings are close to the closure case.
- **A cosmetic compromise is not acceptable** if it only delays the cost problem.
- **Durability matters as much as headline savings.** A high year-1 saving that reverses after two years is worth much less than a smaller but permanent one.
- **Execution risk must be priced.** If a closure path triggers prolonged strikes, the nominal savings can be materially eroded.
