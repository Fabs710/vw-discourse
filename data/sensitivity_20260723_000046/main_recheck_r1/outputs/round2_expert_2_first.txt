Below is a restructuring-finance view of the question asked: **can a non-closure or hybrid package deliver savings that are economically comparable to a closure-based package, and how should durability and enforceability be judged?**

## Executive assessment

A **non-closure package can sometimes approximate closure economics**, but only if it combines:
1. **permanent or multi-year fixed-cost reduction**,
2. **material labor-cost concessions**,
3. **real capacity consolidation**,
4. **measurable headcount reduction**, and
5. **contractual enforcement that prevents easy reversal**.

If the package is limited to working-time flexibility, temporary wage moderation, or vague productivity promises, it is **not financially equivalent** to plant closure. Those measures may reduce cash burn in the near term, but they usually do **not** remove enough fixed cost or structural overcapacity to match the NPV of a closure program.

---

# 1) How to compare the packages correctly

The correct comparison is **not** “closure vs no closure.” It is:

- **Closure package:** net recurring savings after restructuring costs
- **Hybrid package:** partial closure plus negotiated labor/operating measures
- **Non-closure package:** no plant closure, but labor, capacity, and productivity measures intended to replicate the savings

For each package, the key metrics are:

### A. Annual run-rate savings
What is saved each year once fully implemented?

### B. Time to realization
When do savings start, and when are they fully in place?

### C. Implementation cost
Severance, transfer, retraining, relocation, shutdown, remediation, redeployment cost.

### D. Durability
Are savings recurring for 3–5+ years, or only one-off?

### E. Enforceability
Can the company actually rely on the commitments without re-opening them every year?

### F. NPV of savings
Discount the expected savings net of costs and risk.

# 2) Base-case modeling framework

Because no exact plant-by-plant figures were provided, I need to state assumptions rather than invent precision.

## Assumptions for illustration only
Let us assume the restructuring target is to close a **cost gap equivalent to €300m per year of recurring EBITDA improvement** at maturity. That is a reasonable order of magnitude for a major German automotive restructuring, but it may be higher or lower in reality.

I will compare:

- **Option A: Closure-led package**
- **Option B: Hybrid package**
- **Option C: Non-closure package**

I’ll use a simple 5-year NPV framework.

### Discount rate
For industrial turnaround analysis, a reasonable discount rate for risk-adjusted savings is **10%–12%**. I’ll use **10%** for illustration.

### Timing
- Closure savings begin gradually in Year 1, mostly realized by Year 3.
- Hybrid savings begin in Year 1–2, fully realized by Year 3.
- Non-closure savings may begin quickly but often decay or require repeated renegotiation.

# 3) Side-by-side savings model

## Option A: Closure-led package
### Illustrative components
- Shut one or more German plants or major lines
- Eliminate indirect labor and plant overhead
- Remove underutilized fixed cost base
- Reduce headcount materially through closure and transfer

### Illustrative economics
- **Gross annual recurring savings at maturity:** €300m
- **One-time restructuring cost:** €350m
  - severance / transfer / retraining / shutdown / remediation
- **Ramp-up:** 40% in Year 1, 75% in Year 2, 100% from Year 3 onward

### 5-year NPV of savings
Present value of savings:
- Y1: €120m / 1.10 = €109m
- Y2: €225m / 1.10² = €186m
- Y3: €300m / 1.10³ = €226m
- Y4: €300m / 1.10⁴ = €205m
- Y5: €300m / 1.10⁵ = €187m

**PV of gross savings ≈ €913m**

Less restructuring cost, assumed mostly upfront:
- **NPV ≈ €563m**

### Financial interpretation
This is usually the **strongest long-term fixed-cost solution** if execution risk is manageable. It is also the most irreversible and politically contested.

---

## Option B: Hybrid package
This means **no full plant closure**, but a combination of:
- site specialization / line consolidation
- working-time adjustments
- voluntary exits
- wage moderation / benefit changes
- productivity commitments
- limited footprint rationalization

### Illustrative economics
- **Gross annual recurring savings at maturity:** €220m
- **One-time restructuring cost:** €180m
- **Ramp-up:** 30% in Year 1, 70% in Year 2, 100% from Year 3 onward

### 5-year NPV of savings
- Y1: €66m / 1.10 = €60m
- Y2: €154m / 1.10² = €127m
- Y3: €220m / 1.10³ = €165m
- Y4: €220m / 1.10⁴ = €150m
- Y5: €220m / 1.10⁵ = €137m

**PV of gross savings ≈ €639m**

Less restructuring cost:
- **NPV ≈ €459m**

### Financial interpretation
A hybrid package can be economically credible **if** the capacity reduction is real and the labor concessions are durable. It typically preserves more sites and employment than closure, while still producing meaningful savings.  
However, its main risk is **drift**: the package can look good initially and then weaken if utilization stays low or wage/cost concessions expire.

---

## Option C: Non-closure package
This is the most challenging to make finance-credible if the company truly has structural overcapacity.

### Illustrative components
- Working-time flexibility
- Voluntary exits only
- Temporary wage moderation
- Bonus suspension
- Productivity and process improvements
- Internal redeployment
- No closure, no forced redundancies

### Illustrative economics
- **Gross annual recurring savings at maturity:** €120m
- **One-time cost:** €80m
- **Ramp-up:** 50% in Year 1, 90% in Year 2, 100% from Year 3 onward

### 5-year NPV of savings
- Y1: €60m / 1.10 = €55m
- Y2: €108m / 1.10² = €89m
- Y3: €120m / 1.10³ = €90m
- Y4: €120m / 1.10⁴ = €82m
- Y5: €120m / 1.10⁵ = €75m

**PV of gross savings ≈ €391m**

Less restructuring cost:
- **NPV ≈ €311m**

### Financial interpretation
This is usually **not enough** if the gap is a structural overcapacity issue. It may reduce cash burn and buy time, but it often fails the test of **equivalence** because:
- it does not remove enough fixed cost,
- it can be reversed,
- and it may rely on assumptions that deteriorate as volume remains weak.

# 4) Risk-adjusted view

The numbers above are **gross illustrative NPVs**. A proper restructuring model should also apply a probability of delivery.

## Delivery probabilities
Typical risk adjustment might be:

- **Closure package:** 80%–90% delivery probability
- **Hybrid package:** 65%–80%
- **Non-closure package:** 40%–65%

This is because non-closure packages depend more heavily on:
- annual renegotiation,
- labor participation,
- utilization discipline,
- and ongoing management commitment.

### Risk-adjusted NPV illustration
If we apply approximate delivery probabilities:

- **Closure:** €563m × 85% = **€479m**
- **Hybrid:** €459m × 75% = **€344m**
- **Non-closure:** €311m × 55% = **€171m**

### Interpretation
On a **risk-adjusted basis**, closure-led savings usually dominate unless the non-closure package contains **hard structural capacity reduction** and strong contractual lock-in.

---

# 5) What makes a non-closure package financially equivalent?

For a non-closure package to be financially comparable, I would expect all of the following:

## A. Real fixed-cost removal
Not just lower pay or more flexible hours. It must remove:
- indirect labor,
- plant overhead,
- maintenance burden,
- utilities,
- support functions,
- and underutilized capacity.

## B. Headcount reduction
Voluntary exits alone are usually insufficient unless the numbers are large.

## C. Capacity consolidation
Even if the plant remains open, the package must reduce capacity enough that fixed costs fall materially.

## D. Multi-year duration
A one- or two-year labor concession is not equivalent to a permanent structural saving.

## E. Enforceable triggers
If utilization, margins, or cost targets are missed, the company must have automatic rights to additional measures.

Without those, the package is not economically equivalent; it is a postponement.

---

# 6) Enforceability mechanics that matter

From a restructuring finance standpoint, “binding” only matters if the commitment is actually collectible in practice.

## Stronger forms of durability
- Multi-year collective agreement with explicit savings formulas
- Site-specific capacity and workforce corridors
- Automatic wage moderation / bonus suspension linked to EBITDA or volume triggers
- Hard headcount ceilings by function
- Clauses tied to investment release conditions
- Step-in rights for management if targets are missed
- Expiry dates with extension only by mutual consent

## Weaker forms of durability
- General declarations of intent
- Broad “consultation” commitments
- Non-binding investment promises
- Vague productivity pledges
- No audit right over actual savings
- No consequence if assumptions fail

A package with weak enforceability should be discounted heavily in the NPV model.

---

# 7) Sensitivity view

The key sensitivity is the **size of the structural gap**.

## If the true gap is modest
If the company only needs, say, **€100m–€150m** of recurring improvement, then a non-closure package may suffice.

## If the true gap is substantial
If the company needs **€250m–€400m+** of recurring improvement, then:
- non-closure measures usually fall short,
- hybrid may be the minimum credible path,
- closure becomes the strongest financial lever.

This is why the first question should always be:  
**What is the exact annual savings gap that management must close?**

---

# 8) Practical judgment

## My financial judgment
- **Closure-led package:** highest certainty of durable fixed-cost reduction, highest execution and labor conflict risk, strongest NPV if implemented.
- **Hybrid package:** often the best compromise between savings and feasibility, provided there is genuine capacity consolidation and enforceable labor cost reduction.
- **Non-closure package:** only credible if the overcapacity problem is smaller than management claims, or if there are unusually large labor concessions and permanent productivity gains.

## Most likely outcome in finance terms
If this is a **structural overcapacity case**, the economically credible solution is usually **hybrid**, not pure non-closure.  
Pure non-closure rarely matches closure economics on a risk-adjusted NPV basis.
