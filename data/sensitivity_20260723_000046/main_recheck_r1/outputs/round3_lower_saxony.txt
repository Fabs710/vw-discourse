I will be equally direct.

## Where I agree
I agree with management, the family, investors, and the expert on one central point: **this company needs a real restructuring, not a symbolic patch**. The cost base and capacity situation cannot simply be ignored. If we do nothing substantial, the company will lose competitiveness, investment capacity, and eventually jobs anyway.

I also agree that the workforce cannot be asked to carry the entire burden. A credible settlement has to involve **shared sacrifice**.

And I agree with the expert’s key test: the question is not whether a package sounds politically nice, but whether it produces **durable, enforceable, risk-adjusted savings**.

## Where I disagree
I do **not** accept the repeated assumption that the only way to achieve this is through **German plant closures and compulsory redundancies**.

That is too blunt, too irreversible, and too damaging to the regional economy. It is also not yet proven that closures are the only way to close the savings gap. Management keeps presenting closure as the default answer; I still see that as an assertion, not a demonstrated necessity.

I also do **not** accept the employee side’s position if it means **absolutely no capacity reduction, no real labor contribution, and no structural change at all**. That would freeze the status quo and simply postpone the crisis. I cannot support that either.

So my position remains: **no destructive restructuring, but also no fake restructuring**.

## To the Works Council and the Union
I respect the insistence on protecting jobs, sites, and co-determination. That is legitimate, and it reflects the regional public interest as well.

But I need to be frank: if you rule out closures and forced redundancies, then you must be prepared to present a **credible non-closure package with hard numbers**. Not just principles. Not just “we can discuss flexibility.” I mean:
- how much recurring savings,
- from which sites/functions,
- over what time horizon,
- and with what binding mechanism.

If the answer is only temporary flexibility and voluntary exits, that is probably **not enough**. The expert was clear on that, and I take that seriously.

What I can support in principle is:
- working-time flexibility,
- voluntary exits,
- internal redeployment,
- retraining,
- productivity measures,
- temporary labor-cost contributions,
- site specialization,
- and binding investment commitments.

But those measures must be **large enough, durable enough, and enforceable enough** to actually restore competitiveness. If they do not, then we are only delaying the hard decisions.

## To Management
I appreciate your insistence that the numbers must work. That is correct. But you still have not made your case sufficiently that closures are unavoidable **instead of** a negotiated alternative.

If you want support for a harder path, then put the figures on the table:
1. the exact savings gap,
2. the net annual savings expected from each closure option,
3. the equivalent savings required from alternatives,
4. and why the alternative cannot work.

Without that, your closure-first logic remains incomplete.

And let me say this plainly: I will not support a restructuring that guts regional employment simply because management prefers a faster accounting solution.

## To the Controlling Family and Investors
You are right to insist on long-term value creation. A settlement that preserves structural uncompetitiveness is not acceptable.

But you also need to recognize the governance and political reality here: a solution that destroys trust with the workforce, weakens the company’s German footprint, and provokes prolonged conflict may be value-destructive even if it looks neat on paper.

So I will not support a reckless closure strategy. If there is a hybrid or non-closure package that is truly equivalent in economic terms and protects the regional base, that is the route I want explored first.

## To the Expert
Your analysis is useful because it clarifies the real issue: **equivalence has to be demonstrated, not asserted**.

I take especially seriously your point that non-closure measures need:
- real fixed-cost removal,
- capacity consolidation,
- multi-year durability,
- and enforceability.

That means I am not looking for a cosmetic compromise. I am looking for a package that actually closes the gap without irreversible damage.

## My position as anchor shareholder and political actor
My priorities remain unchanged:
- **protect German jobs and sites as far as possible**
- **preserve the company as a regional economic anchor**
- **maintain co-determination and the state’s governance role**
- **support a durable turnaround**

That means I am open to a **hard, negotiated hybrid settlement** if it truly delivers the required savings and protects the industrial base.

What I am **not** open to is:
- plant closures as the easy default,
- mass layoffs,
- or any outcome that sidelines the state and workforce from decisions with lasting consequences.

## What I would support next
I would support a structured next step with three elements:
1. a **quantified savings comparison** between closure, hybrid, and non-closure paths;
2. a **binding package of alternatives** that includes real capacity reduction, not just temporary flexibility;
3. a **site-and-job protection framework** for the German plants, paired with investment commitments and measurable performance triggers.

If the numbers show that a non-closure or hybrid route is truly equivalent, durable, and enforceable, I am prepared to back it.

If they do not, then I will not pretend otherwise: I will resist a closure-led solution that unnecessarily damages the regional economy.

## Bottom line
- **Yes** to real restructuring.
- **Yes** to shared sacrifice.
- **Yes** to a negotiated solution that preserves jobs and sites.
- **No** to closure as the automatic answer.
- **No** to a status quo package dressed up as reform.

If the table wants my support, it must produce a settlement that is **numerically sufficient, socially balanced, and regionally responsible**.
