We agree with the basic diagnosis, but not with any solution that leaves the cost base structurally uncompetitive.

## Where we agree
- The company has a **real structural competitiveness problem**.
- A **cosmetic deal** would be value-destructive.
- The workforce should **not carry all the burden**.
- Any settlement must be **durable, enforceable, and numerically sufficient**.
- A prolonged strike would destroy value and should be avoided if possible.

## Where we disagree

### To the Works Council and the Union
Your insistence on **no German plant closures, no compulsory redundancies, and full job guarantees through 2030** is understandable politically, but it is **not acceptable** if it prevents a package that restores margins and competitiveness.

You have not shown that voluntary exits, working-time changes, redeployment, and wage moderation can **fully replace** the fixed-cost and capacity reduction that management says is needed. If those measures can genuinely deliver the same recurring savings, fine — but so far that has not been proven.

What we will not support is a settlement that:
- preserves excess capacity,
- protects every legacy guarantee,
- and still expects shareholders to live with structurally weak returns.

That is not a turnaround. It is deferred decline.

### To Management
We also do **not** support a blunt closures-first strategy if it is avoidable and if it triggers value-destructive conflict without producing a better long-term outcome.

You need to prove, not merely assert, that closures or compulsory redundancies are necessary. The burden is on management to quantify:
1. the exact savings gap,
2. the net recurring savings by site,
3. the implementation costs,
4. the execution risk,
5. and the NPV difference versus a negotiated hybrid.

If you cannot show that closures are superior on a **risk-adjusted** basis, then pushing them as the default is not disciplined capital allocation.

## Our position
We support the package that delivers the **best risk-adjusted, durable, enforceable savings**.

That means we are open to a **hybrid restructuring**, but only if it is hard enough to matter:
- real capacity consolidation,
- measurable headcount reduction,
- working-time and wage moderation,
- removal of legacy cost guarantees,
- productivity gains that are audited,
- and binding investment commitments that support future products.

A “no closure” package can be acceptable only if it delivers **equivalent recurring savings** and not just temporary relief. Anything less leaves margins impaired.

## What we need to see next
We want the parties to stop debating slogans and put numbers on the table:
- closure scenario savings,
- hybrid scenario savings,
- non-closure scenario savings,
- by site and by year,
- with enforcement mechanisms.

If non-closure measures truly match closure economics, we can support them. If they do not, then the resistance to structural measures is simply asking shareholders to underwrite uncompetitiveness.

## Bottom line
- **Yes** to real restructuring.
- **Yes** to burden-sharing.
- **Yes** to a negotiated hybrid if it is economically sufficient.
- **No** to a cosmetic settlement that protects every plant and every guarantee while leaving the company uncompetitive.
- **No** to closure for its own sake if a demonstrably equivalent alternative exists.

We remain neutral on mechanism, but not on outcome: the outcome must restore **competitive margins** and **disciplined cost reduction**.
