I agree with the Works Council on the core line: **no plant closures in Germany and no compulsory redundancies**. That is not a negotiable detail for us; it is the central test of whether management is serious about a future for this company or just shifting the crisis onto the workforce.

Management keeps talking about competitiveness, but competitiveness cannot mean that employees carry the full adjustment while owners, board members, and management ask for sacrifice from one side only. If there is a structural cost problem, then the response must be **shared sacrifice** and a credible industrial plan — not layoffs as the first and easiest lever.

So our position is clear:

- **No forced redundancies**
- **No closure of German plants**
- **No dismantling of the collective bargaining framework**
- **Any savings must be negotiated**
- **Employee contributions are possible only if jobs, sites, and future investment are secured**

We are prepared to discuss a serious package that includes:
- working-time flexibility,
- voluntary exits instead of forced job cuts,
- temporary labour-cost contributions,
- productivity and training measures,
- possible capacity reductions through negotiated instruments,
- and a fair contribution from owners and management as well.

But if the proposal on the table still relies on closures, mass layoffs, or open-ended insecurity, then we reject it outright. That is not restructuring; that is social dumping.

If management wants peace, it must bring forward a plan that protects the industrial base in Germany and treats the workforce as partners, not as the adjustment reserve.
