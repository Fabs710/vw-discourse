I’ll be direct.

## Where I agree
I agree with management, the state, the union, and the works council on one essential point: **this company needs a durable turnaround, not a symbolic compromise**. If we preserve structural uncompetitiveness, value erodes for everyone — employees, the region, and the owners.

I also agree that a **prolonged strike or a disorderly confrontation would be destructive**. That would not be a victory for anyone; it would burn cash, damage the brand, and weaken the company’s investment capacity.

And I agree with the expert’s basic test: the question is not whether a deal sounds fair, but whether it is **economically equivalent, durable, and enforceable**.

## Where I disagree
I do **not** accept two extreme positions:

### 1) To management
I do not support treating **plant closures and compulsory redundancies as the default or only credible answer**.

If the restructuring logic starts and ends with closing German plants, you risk triggering exactly the kind of industrial instability that destroys more value than it creates. A forced solution may look clean on paper and become expensive in practice through strikes, reputational damage, political resistance, and governance deadlock.

### 2) To the employee side
I do **not** accept the line that **no plant closures, no compulsory redundancies, and full preservation of all guarantees** can remain untouched regardless of the economics.

That is not a restructuring position; it is a status-quo defense. If the company’s cost and capacity structure remains unchanged, then we are simply postponing the problem and handing the next crisis to future owners, workers, and regions.

## What I need to see
The expert’s contribution is useful because it makes the real issue clear: **equivalence must be demonstrated, not assumed**.

So my test is this:

- If the non-closure package can deliver **the same net, recurring, auditable savings** as a closure-based path,
- and if it does so with **real capacity reduction**, not just deferral,
- and if it is **enforceable** over several years,

then it deserves serious support.

But if the proposal is merely:
- temporary flexibility,
- vague productivity promises,
- short-term wage moderation,
- or a freeze on change,

then it is **not sufficient**. That would preserve overcapacity and weaken margins.

## What would be acceptable in principle
I am open to a **hybrid restructuring** if it is genuinely hard-nosed and numbers-based. That could include:

- capacity consolidation,
- product and site specialization,
- voluntary exit programmes,
- working-time adjustments,
- temporary labor-cost contributions,
- removal of legacy cost guarantees,
- productivity and process redesign,
- and binding investment commitments for German sites.

But I repeat: **all of this has to add up to real and lasting cost reduction**. If it does not, then it is not a viable turnaround.

## What is not acceptable
I will not support:
- a deal that merely postpones closure decisions,
- a package that preserves structural overcapacity,
- a settlement that leaves margins structurally below target,
- or any outcome that permanently impairs the enterprise’s value creation.

And I will also not support a pure closure strategy if there is a credible, equally effective alternative with less damage.

## My position on the next step
Management should now put forward **a quantified bridge**:
- what savings the closure path would deliver,
- what savings the negotiated alternative would deliver,
- by site and by year,
- with enforcement mechanisms.

Without that, we are still debating slogans.

If the employee side wants the family holding to back a non-closure solution, it must prove equivalence in hard numbers. If management wants support for harsher measures, it must show why less destructive alternatives fail.

That is the standard I will apply.

### Bottom line
- **Yes** to a real restructuring.
- **Yes** to a negotiated, durable solution if it is numerically sufficient.
- **No** to cosmetic protection of the status quo.
- **No** to closure-first dogma if a genuinely equivalent alternative exists.
