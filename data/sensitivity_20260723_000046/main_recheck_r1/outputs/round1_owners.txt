I will be direct.

I agree with one central point from management: **the current cost and capacity structure is not sustainable**. If the company does not regain competitiveness, then value erodes for everyone — employees, the region, and the owners. As controlling shareholder, I cannot support a path that turns this into a slow decline.

At the same time, I agree with the employee side and the state on another point: **a purely confrontational solution would be damaging**. A prolonged labor conflict, public escalation, or a symbolic victory that leaves the economics unchanged would destroy value and weaken the company’s position for years. That is not acceptable either.

Where I disagree is on two extremes:

1. **To management:** I do not support treating plant closures and compulsory redundancies as the default answer. If that becomes the only logic, you risk pushing the company into industrial instability, reputational damage, and a governance crisis that could cost more than the savings you are chasing.

2. **To the workforce:** “No closures, no compulsory redundancies, no structural change” is not a viable final line if it leaves the company uncompetitive. Protecting every existing arrangement at all costs would preserve today’s comfort and erode tomorrow’s value.

What I need to see is a **credible, durable turnaround package** that delivers real cost reduction and capacity adjustment **without permanently impairing the enterprise**. That means:

- measurable and lasting competitiveness gains,
- a negotiated contribution from labor,
- protection of the company’s core industrial capabilities in Germany,
- and a stable governance outcome that does not invite endless conflict.

I am open to alternative mechanisms **if they are economically equivalent** to closures and hard cuts. But they must be real, not cosmetic. Temporary flexibility, voluntary programs, productivity gains, working-time measures, and contribution through bargaining can be part of a solution — **if** the numbers add up and the savings are durable.

What I will not support is:
- a deal that merely postpones the problem,
- a settlement that leaves structural overcapacity intact,
- or any outcome that permanently weakens the company’s value creation.

So my position is this:

- **Yes** to a serious restructuring.
- **Yes** to negotiation and social balance.
- **No** to a weak compromise that buys peace at the expense of competitiveness.
- **No** to an aggressive plant-closure strategy if there is a credible alternative that achieves the same economic result with less damage.

Management should bring forward a concrete package with hard numbers, not slogans. The employee side should be prepared to accept real contributions, not just defend the status quo. If both sides stay at their current lines, then we are heading toward conflict and value destruction — and I will not support that outcome.
