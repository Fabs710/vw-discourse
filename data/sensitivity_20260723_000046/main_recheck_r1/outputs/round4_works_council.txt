I will respond plainly.

## Our position remains unchanged
- **No German plant closures**
- **No compulsory redundancies**
- **No dismantling of co-determination**
- **No breaking of job-security guarantees**
- **No unilateral fait accompli**

That is the minimum basis for any serious discussion. If management or shareholders want to call that “inflexible,” then so be it. We call it responsible.

---

## Where I agree

### With management, the family, investors, and the state
Yes, the company has a real competitiveness problem.  
Yes, the cost base must come down.  
Yes, a cosmetic compromise would be irresponsible.

I am not denying any of that.

### With the expert
I also agree that the discussion must be based on **numbers**, not slogans. The relevant question is whether a package can deliver **durable, enforceable, risk-adjusted savings**.

That is a fair test.

---

## Where I disagree

### 1) Closures are still being treated as the default
Management keeps saying closures are not ideological, only “necessary if the numbers require it.” But the burden of proof has not been met.

You have not shown:
- the exact savings gap by site and year,
- why voluntary exits, working-time measures, internal redeployment, specialization, and productivity gains cannot close it,
- and why closures are the only way to get there.

So no, closures are not justified at this stage. They are being used as a threat to force acceptance of a weaker bargaining position.

### 2) “Equivalent savings” is not a blank cheque for plant destruction
I reject the idea that if we refuse closures, we are refusing restructuring.

That is false.

A real restructuring can happen through:
- voluntary departures,
- working-time reductions,
- temporary wage and bonus moderation,
- internal redeployment,
- retraining,
- line specialization,
- and productivity improvements.

What it cannot do, if it is to remain socially and industrially legitimate, is sacrifice plants and livelihoods first and ask workers to approve the wreckage afterward.

### 3) The finance logic is incomplete
The expert’s comments were useful, but one part must not be abused: a model that shows higher short-term NPV for closures does **not** automatically make closures the right decision.

A finance model that ignores:
- strike risk,
- loss of know-how,
- political backlash,
- damage to trust,
- weakened future cooperation,
- and long-term instability in the workforce,

is not a full model. It is a narrow one.

A closure-led path may look neat in a spreadsheet and disastrous in the plant network.

## To management
You ask for hard numbers. Fine. Then put them on the table in full:
1. the annual savings gap,
2. by site and by function,
3. the implementation costs,
4. the strike and delay risks,
5. the impact on quality, know-how, and future launches,
6. and the 5-year risk-adjusted comparison with a non-closure package.

Until that is done, you are not demonstrating necessity. You are asserting it.

And let me be explicit: **we will not pre-approve closures to help your model work**.

## To the union
I support your core line:
- no compulsory redundancies,
- no closures,
- no erosion of collective protections.

That is the correct position.

But I also want to be clear: the workforce side should not be forced into a one-sided concession package while management preserves the option to cut plants later. Any labor contribution must sit inside a binding framework of:
- no closures,
- no forced layoffs,
- job security through 2030,
- and real investment commitments.

Without that, there is nothing to negotiate.

---

## To the state government
You say you want to avoid irreversible regional damage. Then use your blocking position to do exactly that.

Do not talk about “avoiding cosmetic deals” and then leave plant closures on the table as if they were normal. They are not normal. They are irreversible damage.

If the state is serious, it should back a solution that:
- protects all German sites,
- preserves jobs,
- keeps co-determination meaningful,
- and forces management to restructure through negotiated means.

---

## To the family and investors
You keep saying workers cannot be the only ones to bear the burden. I agree.

But the answer is not to preserve closure options and call that balanced. That is just asymmetric power with nicer language.

If you want burden-sharing, then burden-sharing means:
- owners accept lower returns,
- management accepts binding constraints,
- and labour contributes only under protected sites and secure jobs.

We are not going to sign up to a deal that gives away our leverage and leaves your strategic options intact.

---

## On the expert’s proposed comparison
A quantified comparison of closure, hybrid, and no-closure options is fine. I do not oppose an independent model.

But let me be absolutely clear: a model is not a license to close plants. It is a tool to test whether management’s claim is true.

If the model shows that a non-closure package can work with binding commitments, then closures are off the table.

If the model is used as a pretext to say “see, the plant must go,” then it will simply confirm our distrust.

## What a serious non-closure package must include
If management wants us to even consider further discussion, it must commit in advance to:

1. **No German plant closures**
2. **No compulsory redundancies**
3. **Job security through 2030**
4. **Binding investment in German sites**
5. **Preserved co-determination**
6. **Savings through non-destructive measures only**
   - voluntary exits
   - working-time flexibility
   - temporary wage moderation
   - bonus moderation
   - retraining
   - redeployment
   - specialization and productivity gains

And crucially: these are not concessions to enable later closures. They are part of a protected restructuring framework.

- “Forced layoffs are off the table for now, but may return later.”

No. Those are precisely the moves we reject.

We are prepared to engage in a real turnaround.

So the next move is not more pressure. The next move is:
