## Our position remains unchanged
- **No German plant closures**
- **No compulsory redundancies**
- **No dismantling of co-determination**
- **No breaking of job-security guarantees**
- **Any restructuring must be negotiated, reversible where possible, and socially fair**

That is not a talking point. It is the minimum acceptable basis for any agreement.

## Where I agree with others
I agree with management, the state, the family, and investors on one point: **the company has a real competitiveness problem**. I am not denying that. If the cost base is too high, then we must address that.

I also agree that a cosmetic response would be useless. A deal that merely postpones the issue would be irresponsible to workers and to the company.

I further agree with the expert’s core point: the question is **not** whether the company needs savings, but whether the proposed savings are **genuinely equivalent, durable, and enforceable**.

---

## Where I strongly disagree

### 1) Management is still treating closures as a default rather than a last resort
You say the math does not add up without closures. That is your claim, not a proven fact.

So far, what I hear is:
- a demand for structural savings,
- a demand for flexibility,
- a demand for labour to contribute,
- and an implied threat that if we do not accept closures, then job destruction will follow.

That is not a negotiated restructuring. That is pressure politics.

If management wants credibility, then show us the **exact savings gap**, by **site and year**, and show why it cannot be closed by:
- voluntary exits,
- working-time changes,
- internal redeployment,
- product and line specialization,
- temporary labor-cost moderation,
- and productivity improvements.

Until that is proven, closures remain a **choice**, not a necessity.

### 2) “Equivalent savings” cannot be used as a code word for plant closure
I want to be very clear here: I do not accept the argument that if employees reject closures, then we are somehow refusing restructuring.

That is false.

There is a difference between:
- **structural adjustment**, and
- **destroying sites and jobs as the easiest accounting solution**.

If the company wants to reduce capacity, then do it through negotiated workload shifts, specialization, and voluntary instruments — not by shutting down plants and forcing people out.

### 3) The expert analysis actually confirms our concern
The specialist said what we have been saying all along: a non-closure package can be credible **only if** it includes strong fixed-cost reduction, real capacity consolidation, enforceable commitments, and durability over several years.

Fine. Then let us be honest: that means management must bring a package that **protects sites and jobs while making the savings real**.

What it does **not** mean is that closures become acceptable if the company repeats the word “structural” often enough.

---

## On the expert’s side-by-side model
The expert’s framework is useful, but I want to challenge its implication.

Yes, closures may generate higher short-term NPV in a narrow finance model. But a finance model that ignores:
- industrial trust,
- strike risk,
- political backlash,
- loss of know-how,
- damage to future labor relations,
- and the long-term cost of destroying the workforce’s confidence,

is incomplete.

A closure-led path may look cleaner on paper and create a far more dangerous company in practice.

That is exactly why we insist that closures are not the benchmark for “rationality.” They may be the easiest way to shift pain onto workers. They are not automatically the best strategic choice.

---

## On the state government
You say you want to protect German jobs and sites “as far as possible.” That is not enough if the next step is to leave closures on the table as an acceptable option.

If the regional state government is serious about protecting the industrial base, then it should use its role to block irreversible damage, not merely soften it.

I appreciate that you reject a purely cosmetic deal. So do we. But “no cosmetic deal” does **not** equal “therefore closures.” There is a third path:
- binding site guarantees,
- negotiated cost contributions,
- investment commitments,
- and capacity adjustment through non-destructive means.

That is the path you should support if you are consistent.

---

## On the family and investors
You both keep saying the company must not preserve structural uncompetitiveness. I understand that concern. But the answer cannot be to treat workers as the adjustment reserve.

If the company needs savings, then the burden must be shared:
- owners accept lower short-term returns,
- management accepts strategic constraints and genuine negotiation,
- labor contributes only under binding job and site guarantees.

What is unacceptable is this logic:
1. demand worker concessions,
2. reserve the right to close plants anyway,
3. keep co-determination formally intact while emptying it of power.

That is precisely what we will not accept.

---

## On the claim that labour concessions need closure as a backstop
No. A “backstop” of closure is not a neutral bargaining tool. It is a threat against the workforce and local communities.

If you insist on keeping closures as an option, you are not asking for partnership — you are asking us to negotiate under the shadow of forced job destruction.

We reject that framing.

Any contribution from labour must be tied to:
- **no closures**
- **no forced layoffs**
- **binding 2030 job security**
- **real investment**
- **preserved co-determination**

Without those, there is no basis for labour-side concessions.

## What a serious alternative would require
If management truly wants a negotiated path, then bring a package with all of the following:

1. **Exact savings target**
   - the real annual gap that must be closed,
   - by plant and function,
   - with a multi-year timeline.

2. **Non-closure measures only**
   - voluntary departures,
   - working-time flexibility,
   - internal redeployment,
   - retraining,
   - productivity improvements,
   - product and site specialization,
   - temporary labor-cost moderation.

3. **Binding guarantees**
   - no German plant closures,
   - no compulsory redundancies,
   - job-security through 2030,
   - co-determination protected.

4. **Investment commitments**
   - future products and technology allocated to German sites,
   - not vague promises, but concrete amounts and timing.

5. **Enforcement**
   - if management wants credibility, the agreement must be enforceable and not subject to unilateral reversal.

That is what a real deal looks like.
