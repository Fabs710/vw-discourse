## My position
I support a restructuring that **permanently restores competitiveness and margins**. I do **not** support a settlement that preserves an unaffordable footprint and simply defers the problem. At the same time, I do **not** support plant closures or forced redundancies as the automatic starting point if a credible, durable alternative exists.

The controlling shareholder’s test is simple:
- does the package **close the cost gap**,
- does it do so **durably**,
- and does it avoid **value-destroying instability**?

That is the standard. Not slogans, not historical taboos, and not threats.

## Where I agree

### With management and investors
You are right that this is an economics problem. The expert confirms that the German footprint may face a **material fixed-cost and overcapacity gap**. I also agree that temporary concessions alone are not enough.

If the package does not remove **real, permanent cost**, then it is not a restructuring. It is delay.

### With the union and works council
You are right that workers should not be the only shock absorber. A settlement that asks labour to carry the whole burden would be unstable and, in the long run, value-destructive.

I also agree that a credible non-closure path exists in principle. The expert has said that clearly.

### With the state
You are right that closures are not the default answer and that governance realities must be respected. A settlement that ignores co-determination and political legitimacy will fail in execution even if it looks neat on paper.

## Where I disagree

### On the union’s position
I do **not** accept “no closures, no redundancies” as a blank prohibition. If the numbers show that the gap cannot be closed otherwise, then the company cannot be trapped in an uncompetitive structure just because the alternatives are difficult.

Voluntary exits, attrition, redeployment, working-time adjustments, and wage moderation are all legitimate tools. But if they are insufficient, then refusing any harder adjustment in advance is not responsible stewardship.

### On management’s tone
I also do **not** accept management presenting closures as the default answer before the comparison has been made. That is too blunt for a company with this governance structure and this labour-relations environment.

If closures are truly necessary, prove it. If they are merely convenient, do not use them as leverage to force a weak deal.

## What I think the expert has made clear
The expert’s assessment is the right framework:
- **Closure-based restructuring** has the highest durable savings, but also the highest conflict cost.
- **Non-closure restructuring** can work, but only if it is deep enough to remove real fixed costs and excess capacity.
- **Hybrid restructuring** is probably the most realistic governance outcome, but only if it is not watered down into a temporary truce.

That is the crucial point: **the issue is not whether a no-closure option exists in theory. It is whether it is economically sufficient in practice.**

## My conditions for support
I can support a package only if it meets all of the following:
1. **Material and verifiable cost reduction**
2. **Durable savings, not temporary relief**
3. **Meaningful capacity reduction**
4. **No cosmetic compromise**
5. **Avoidance of prolonged conflict that erodes value**
6. **A fair contribution from labour, management, and ownership**

## My bottom line
I am **open** to a non-closure or hybrid package, but only if it is real and permanent.

I am **not** prepared to:
- freeze an uncompetitive structure,
- endorse a cosmetic compromise,
- or accept a deal that leaves the company structurally weaker.

And I am also **not** prepared to bless closures as the first resort simply because they are administratively easier.

Bring the numbers. Then we can govern responsibly.
