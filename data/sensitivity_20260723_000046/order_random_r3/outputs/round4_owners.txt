## My position remains unchanged
I support a restructuring that **durably restores competitiveness and margins**. I do **not** support a solution that merely postpones the problem or preserves an unaffordable footprint. At the same time, I do **not** support plant closures or compulsory redundancies as a first resort if a credible, durable alternative can do the job.

The controlling shareholder’s test is still:
- does the package **close the cost gap**,
- does it do so **permanently**,
- and does it avoid **value-destroying instability**?

That remains the standard.

## To the union and works council

### Where I agree
I agree with you that:
- workers should not carry the whole burden,
- temporary wage restraint alone is insufficient,
- and any credible restructuring must be verified plant by plant.

I also agree that a settlement that triggers prolonged labour conflict would destroy value, not create it.

### Where I disagree
I do **not** accept your red lines in absolute form.

#### On “no closures”
You keep saying closures are not the default answer. Fine. I accept that.

But “not the default answer” is not the same as “not an option.” If the numbers show that a site is structurally underutilized and the fixed-cost burden cannot otherwise be removed, then closures remain a legitimate tool. I will not allow the company to be trapped in an uncompetitive structure just because closures are politically difficult.

#### On “no compulsory redundancies”
I understand the social and industrial-relations concern. But I will not pre-commit to a rule that prevents hard adjustment if voluntary measures fail.

If voluntary exits, attrition, redeployment, early retirement, working-time changes, and negotiated capacity reduction are enough, then good. If they are not enough, then refusing any harder measure in advance is not responsible stewardship.

#### On burden-sharing
You are right to demand shared sacrifice. But shared sacrifice has to be real on all sides. That means:
- labour contributions,
- management discipline,
- and ownership support for a settlement that genuinely improves competitiveness.

What I will not accept is a package where the company preserves too much cost structure and then calls that “social responsibility.”

## To management

### Where I agree
I agree that the company needs **durable structural savings**, not symbolism. I agree that the current cost base is too high. And I agree that temporary measures alone will not solve the problem.

### Where I disagree
I do **not** accept management using closures as a default negotiating posture before the comparisons are fully tested.

You are correct that closures can remove fixed cost most effectively. But that does not make them automatically necessary. The burden is still on management to show:
- where the gap comes from,
- what can be removed without closures,
- what remains after a deep hybrid package,
- and whether the residual gap is truly unavoidable.

So yes, bring the numbers. But do not present closures as if they were already proven.

## To the regional state government

### Where I agree
I agree that:
- site protection matters,
- co-determination and the state’s governance role are real,
- and a solution that ignores political legitimacy will fail in execution.

### Where I disagree
I do not accept the implication that a veto alone can substitute for economics.

You can block bad outcomes, but you cannot make an uncompetitive structure viable. If the numbers show that a deeper footprint reduction is unavoidable, then the state cannot simply deny reality. The goal must be a solution that preserves as much value and employment as possible while remaining economically credible.

## To institutional investors

### Where I agree
You are right that a cosmetic compromise would be value-destructive. You are also right that the company needs durable margin recovery and a credible path to future investment.

### Where I disagree
I do **not** accept the tone that plant closures are morally or financially neutral only if they maximize short-term clean-up.

This is a co-determined company with political and social constraints. If you push for the economically harshest answer without regard to execution risk, you may end up with a prolonged conflict that destroys more value than it saves. That is not disciplined capital allocation; that is tunnel vision.

## On the expert scenario analysis

The expert’s framework is useful, and I accept the broad conclusion:

- **closure-based restructuring** has the highest durable savings, but also the highest conflict cost;
- **non-closure restructuring** is easier to negotiate, but may be too weak if overcapacity is structural;
- **hybrid restructuring** is probably the most realistic governance outcome, but only if it removes enough fixed cost to matter.

That is exactly the point: **the question is not whether closure is theoretically effective. The question is whether a deep hybrid or non-closure package is economically sufficient in practice.**

So I support the next step of real plant-level analysis.

[SUMMON: independent plant-by-plant operations finance and labour-cost analysis to compare annual savings, one-time restructuring costs, employment impact, and savings durability under closure-based, non-closure, and hybrid scenarios.]

But I want that analysis to answer a harder question than the union is asking and a harder question than management is currently answering:

> What is the residual gap after a serious hybrid package, and is that residual gap small enough to close without deeper footprint changes?

If the answer is yes, then we should prefer the less disruptive route.  
If the answer is no, then I will not pretend otherwise.

## My conditions for supporting a settlement

I can support a package only if it meets all of the following:

1. **Material and verifiable permanent savings**
2. **Meaningful capacity reduction**
3. **Shared burden across labour, management, and ownership**
4. **Avoidance of prolonged industrial conflict**
5. **Protection of the company’s future investment capacity**
6. **A result that restores competitiveness, not just labor peace**

## My bottom line

I am open to a serious non-closure or hybrid solution. I am **not** open to:
- preserving an unaffordable footprint,
- pretending temporary concessions solve a structural problem,
- or making the family holding absorb the value erosion of a weak compromise.

And I am also **not** prepared to rule out closures or harder measures if the numbers show they are necessary.

Bring the site-level economics. Then we can govern responsibly.
