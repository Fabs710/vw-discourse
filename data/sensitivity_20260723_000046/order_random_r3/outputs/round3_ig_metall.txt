I will be direct.

We are finally talking about numbers, which is necessary. But I want to be equally clear: **the existence of a cost gap does not automatically justify plant closures or compulsory redundancies**. That leap is still being made too quickly by management and investors.

## Where I agree

I agree on three things:

1. **A durable restructuring is needed.**  
   No one can seriously argue for cosmetic fixes.

2. **The company must close a real cost gap.**  
   I accept that competitiveness has to improve and that the current structure is too expensive.

3. **A serious package must include more than wages alone.**  
   Capacity changes, working-time flexibility, productivity gains, overhead cuts, and investment commitments all belong on the table.

So yes: we are prepared to talk about a real restructuring.

## Where I disagree

### 1) Closures are not the default answer
Management keeps speaking as if closures are the natural benchmark and everything else is an exception. I reject that framing.

The expert analysis did **not** say closures are inevitable. It said:
- closure-based restructuring has the highest fixed-cost savings,
- but non-closure and hybrid packages can still be credible if they are deep enough.

That is the point. **Credible alternatives exist.**  
So before anyone treats closures as unavoidable, management must show why a non-closure or hybrid package cannot close the gap.

### 2) Compulsory redundancies remain unacceptable
I am not softening on this. If the “solution” depends on forcing people out, then the restructuring has already crossed a line.

Voluntary exits, attrition, redeployment, early retirement, and working-time adjustments are one thing.  
**Forced layoffs are another.** They would destroy trust, provoke conflict, and break the collective framework.

### 3) Workers must not carry the main burden
I hear management, investors, and ownership all demanding “material” contributions from labour. Fine. But that must be matched by visible contributions from the other side.

What is not acceptable:
- wage moderation for workers while overhead remains bloated,
- bonus waivers while management shields itself,
- job cuts while the ownership side offers only verbal support,
- and site sacrifices while the company’s future commitments remain vague.

That is not shared sacrifice. That is one-sided burden shifting.

## What a serious labour-side package can include

We are still prepared to negotiate a package with:
- **temporary or time-limited wage moderation**
- **bonus waivers / special payment restraint**
- **working-time flexibility**
- **shift and capacity adjustments**
- **productivity improvements**
- **voluntary exit programs**
- **internal redeployment**
- **binding investment and product commitments**
- **site and job guarantees**

That is a real contribution. It is not symbolic.

But I will not present that as a blank cheque. Those concessions have to buy something real:
- no compulsory redundancies,
- no closure-first strategy,
- protection of core German sites,
- and a durable collective agreement.

## To the controlling family and investors

If you want labour to take this seriously, then the ownership side must be seen to contribute too:

- support for investment in future products,
- willingness to back a durable settlement instead of short-term optics,
- and acceptance that the workforce is not the only shock absorber.

If the package is to restore value, then the owners must also share in the sacrifice.

That means:

- insisting on investment commitments,

A strong veto is not enough if it is not paired with a serious alternative. We need a solution that protects regional employment **and** is economically credible.
