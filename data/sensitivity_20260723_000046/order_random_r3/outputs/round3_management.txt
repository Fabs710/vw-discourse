I will be equally direct.

The discussion has now moved beyond abstract principles. The central point is clear: **we need durable structural savings, not a temporary truce**. On that, I agree with everyone. But I do **not** accept the attempt by the union, works council, and state to treat **plant closures and compulsory redundancies as essentially off-limits** before the numbers have been fully tested.

## Where I agree

### With the expert
The expert has confirmed what management has been saying:
- the problem is likely a **material fixed-cost and capacity gap**,
- temporary wage moderation alone is **not enough**,
- and any non-closure solution must remove **real, permanent cost**.

That supports management’s position, not weakens it. If the German footprint is structurally too expensive, then we cannot pretend that minor adjustments will restore competitiveness.

### With the controlling family and investors
I agree that a cosmetic deal would destroy value. A package that preserves the current cost base and postpones the crisis is unacceptable. The company needs a solution that improves margins **durably** and protects the ability to invest through 2030.

### With the union and works council, in part
I agree that workers should not carry the burden alone, and I agree that a serious settlement may include:
- working-time flexibility,
- capacity reduction,
- productivity gains,
- bonus moderation,
- and some form of negotiated employee contribution.

But that agreement stops where the demands become absolute.

## Where I disagree

### 1) “No closures” cannot be a red line
The union and works council keep repeating that closures are not the answer. That is not analysis; that is a preference.

If a plant or line is structurally underutilized, if fixed costs cannot be reabsorbed, and if the arithmetic shows that the gap cannot be closed otherwise, then **closures remain a legitimate and necessary option**. The expert did not rule that out. In fact, the expert made clear that closures are often the most effective way to remove persistent excess cost.

So I reject the idea that we should rule them out in advance.

### 2) “No compulsory redundancies” is not a decision rule
I understand the social sensitivity. But management cannot bind itself to a rule that prevents the company from making hard adjustments if voluntary measures fall short.

If the required savings can be achieved through attrition, early retirement, redeployment, and negotiated exits, then fine. But if not, then refusing harder measures up front is equivalent to refusing restructuring.

### 3) The burden of proof cuts both ways
The state and labour say management must prove closures are unavoidable. Fair enough — and we will provide the plant-by-plant numbers.

But labour also has to prove that its preferred route can deliver:
- the same scale of savings,
- the same durability,
- and the same timing.

So far, that proof has not been delivered. It has been asserted.

## What the discussion has made clear

We now have a common framework:
- **Closure-based restructuring**: highest durable savings, highest conflict cost.
- **Non-closure restructuring**: lower conflict, but likely insufficient unless very deep.
- **Hybrid restructuring**: most realistic in governance terms, but only if it removes enough fixed cost.

That means the only serious question is not whether pain is acceptable — it is **which package actually closes the gap**.

And I will be explicit:  
**I will not accept a package that leaves the company structurally uncompetitive just because it is politically easier to sell.**

## To the union
You say workers are prepared to contribute. Good. Then make that contribution real. If you want to avoid closures and forced layoffs, then the package must include:
- durable labour-cost relief,
- substantial productivity improvements,
- real capacity reduction,
- and verifiable savings.

Not symbolic gestures. Not temporary relief. Not demands for site protection with no matching economic effect.

## To the works council
You insist that management must prove closure is unavoidable. That is fine — and that analysis will be provided. But your position still does not answer the key question: if closures are not used, **where exactly does the required permanent cost come out?**

If you want a no-closure path, it must be shown to work **in numbers**, not just in principle. Otherwise it is not a solution.

## To the state government
I respect your role and your veto rights. But those rights do not change the economics. You can block a bad solution; you cannot make an uncompetitive structure viable by insisting on it.

I agree that closures should not be the starting point. But they remain on the table because the company cannot be trapped in a structurally unaffordable footprint.

## To the family and investors
I agree with the demand for value creation, but I also caution against assuming that the most severe option is automatically the best one. We need the comparison before deciding. A hybrid package can be acceptable — but only if it is deep enough.

## What management is now asking for
We are prepared to put the numbers on the table. The relevant question is not whether people like the answer. The question is whether the answer restores competitiveness.

### [SUMMON: plant-by-plant operations finance and utilization analysis to compare, by German site, the annual savings, one-time restructuring charges, employment impact, and savings durability under: 1) closure-based restructuring, 2) non-closure restructuring, and 3) hybrid restructuring.]

That analysis should show:
- fixed-cost absorption by site,
- utilization rates,
- direct labour cost per vehicle,
- overhead and complexity costs,
- savings from capacity reductions with and without closure,
- savings from labour measures,
- and the durability of each package after temporary concessions expire.

## My bottom line
I am **open** to a hybrid package or a non-closure package **only if it is economically equivalent in substance**.

I am **not** open to:
- a cosmetic compromise,
- a settlement that preserves the current cost base,
- or a package that leaves the company unable to fund future products and technology.

And I am also **not** prepared to rule out closures or harder measures if the numbers show they are necessary.

So let’s stop arguing in abstractions. Bring the site-by-site economics. Then we can decide like responsible stewards of the company.
