We are now at the point where the only thing that matters is whether the package can **restore competitive margins, permanently**, with enough certainty to support the share price and future capital allocation.

## Where we agree
We agree with all parties on one basic point: **the current cost base is too high** and a cosmetic settlement would be value-destructive. We also agree that:
- the restructuring must be **durable**,
- temporary wage restraint alone is **not enough**,
- and any credible solution must address **overcapacity**, not just labour costs.

The expert’s analysis was useful because it confirmed that the company may indeed face a material fixed-cost gap.

## Where we disagree
### 1) “No closures” cannot be a hard red line
We do **not** accept plant closures as a taboo. If the numbers show that a site is structurally underutilized and that the fixed-cost gap cannot otherwise be removed, then closures are a legitimate tool.

The union, works council, and state keep arguing as if closure is morally or politically disqualifying. From a capital-market perspective, that is not sufficient. **If a site has to go to restore competitiveness, it has to go.**

### 2) “No compulsory redundancies” is not a financial plan
Voluntary exits, attrition, redeployment, and early retirement are preferable. But if they do not deliver the necessary permanent savings, then ruling out harder measures in advance just weakens the restructuring. We would not support a settlement that preserves comfort today and leaves the company under pressure again in 12–24 months.

### 3) The hybrid route is only acceptable if it really works
We are open to a hybrid deal. In fact, that is likely the most realistic outcome in this governance setup. But it must do the arithmetic:
- meaningful fixed-cost reduction,
- real capacity reduction,
- durable labour-cost relief,
- overhead and procurement cuts,
- and verified savings that survive beyond temporary concessions.

A hybrid that merely postpones closure decisions is not acceptable.

## Our reading of the expert’s comparison
The expert essentially showed:
- **closure-based restructuring**: highest durable savings, highest conflict cost;
- **non-closure restructuring**: lower conflict, but often insufficient unless very deep;
- **hybrid restructuring**: the most plausible compromise, but only if it removes enough cost.

That is the right framework. It also means the burden of proof is on whoever says, “we can do this without closures and still hit the margin target.” So far, that proof has not been shown.

## What we need next
We support the requested plant-by-plant analysis. But we want it used to answer one question only:

> Which option most credibly restores risk-adjusted shareholder value through durable margin recovery?

That means the analysis must include:
- plant-by-plant fixed cost and utilization,
- direct labour cost per vehicle,
- overhead absorption,
- annual savings under each scenario,
- one-time restructuring charges,
- payback period,
- execution risk,
- and savings durability after any temporary concessions expire.

[SUMMON: independent plant-by-plant operations finance and valuation analysis to compare closure-based, non-closure, and hybrid restructuring scenarios, with focus on margin recovery, payback, and execution risk.]

## Our bottom line
We will support **any** package that credibly delivers:
- durable cost reduction,
- restored competitiveness,
- and a clear path back to competitive margins.

We will **not** support:
- cosmetic compromise,
- preservation of an uncompetitive footprint,
- or a deal that asks capital markets to fund delay.

So:
- **Yes** to a serious hybrid if it is economically real.
- **Yes** to non-closure options only if they are truly equivalent in substance.
- **Yes** to closures if the numbers show they are necessary.
- **No** to any outcome that leaves the cost base structurally impaired.
