I appreciate the clarity from all sides, but I need to be equally clear: **the company does not have the luxury of a cosmetic compromise**.

I agree with one part of what has been said by everyone here: this must be a **structural** solution. But that is exactly why I cannot accept a package that preserves the current cost base and capacity footprint while simply adding temporary concessions and optimistic promises.

### To the union
I hear your red lines: no compulsory redundancies, no plant closures, no dismantling of the collective framework. But if those lines are absolute, then you are asking management to accept an uncompetitive domestic structure and hope that future product cycles somehow fix it. They will not.

Let me be direct:
- **We need real capacity reduction.**
- **We need a genuine labour-cost contribution.**
- **We need a durable shift in the economics of German production.**

If the only acceptable outcome is that every site remains open and every current structure remains intact, then there is no restructuring — only delay. And delay is exactly what the company cannot afford.

That said, I am not insisting that closures are the only route. But I am insisting that whatever is agreed must produce **equivalent and verifiable savings**. If the employee side wants to avoid closures and forced layoffs, then it must come forward with a package that closes the cost gap in a credible way: working-time flexibility, reduced compensation, waiver of certain payments, productivity gains, and concrete capacity adjustments that are large enough to matter. Not symbolism.

### To the controlling family
I agree with your core point that value destruction is not an alternative to restructuring. Where I differ is on the urgency and scale of what is required. We are not talking about incremental trimming. We are dealing with a business whose German factory costs are materially above target while the European market is shrinking and competitors are moving faster and cheaper.

So I support your view that:
- temporary optics are not enough,
- the result must be durable,
- and investor confidence depends on real margin repair.

But I would not want this discussion to drift into an expectation that management will tolerate endless negotiation while the cost base remains unchanged. If we cannot secure a serious settlement, then we will have to pursue the hard measures available to us within the governance framework.

### To the regional state government
I understand the political and social consequences you are highlighting. I do not dismiss them. But I must also point out that **preserving every site in its current form is not the same thing as preserving industrial strength**. A company that fails to adapt will ultimately endanger more jobs, more investment, and more regional value than a difficult restructuring now.

I agree that the state’s role is real and that co-determination matters. But that also means the responsibility is shared: the state should help secure a solution that restores competitiveness, not freeze the company into an unsustainable structure.

### My position
Here is the bottom line:

- I am **not** looking for a symbolic compromise.
- I am **not** prepared to accept a package that leaves the cost base fundamentally uncompetitive.
- I am **open** to solutions other than closures, **if and only if** they deliver comparable, durable savings.
- I am **prepared** to pursue hard measures if no such package emerges.

So the question is not whether there will be sacrifice. There will be. The question is **who contributes, how much, and whether it is enough** to restore the brand to competitiveness by 2030.

If the employee side is serious about avoiding closures, then we need a concrete offer that includes:
- measurable labour-cost relief,
- meaningful capacity reductions,
- flexibility in working time and utilisation,
- and a binding acceptance that the old cost structure is over.

Without that, we are simply postponing the inevitable.
