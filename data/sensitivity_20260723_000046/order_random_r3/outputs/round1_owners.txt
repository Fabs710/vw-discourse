We acknowledge the union’s point that a crisis cannot be solved by loading everything onto the workforce alone. That would be neither sustainable nor acceptable from an ownership perspective.

At the same time, I need to be equally clear: a restructuring that leaves the company structurally uncompetitive is not a viable compromise. If German operations remain too expensive and overcapacitated, then we are only postponing the problem and eroding long-term value further.

So my position is:

- I do not support a one-sided solution based purely on plant closures and compulsory redundancies.
- I also do not support preserving every current structure if that means the business continues to miss its return targets.
- I do support a package that credibly restores competitiveness, protects the core enterprise, and avoids avoidable instability.

What that means in practical terms is that we should be looking at a serious combination of measures:
- capacity reductions where they are economically necessary,
- productivity and working-time adjustments,
- a meaningful employee contribution,
- restraint on bonuses and other cost items,
- and binding commitments on future investment where the business case supports them.

But I will be equally explicit about the other side of the equation: any package has to deliver real and durable cost relief. Symbolic concessions or temporary optics will not be enough. The company needs a structural solution, not a pause before the next crisis.

On plant closures and forced redundancies, I am not eager to see them become the default answer. But I will not rule out hard measures if no credible alternative is presented that closes the cost gap and restores margins. Value destruction is not an acceptable substitute for social comfort.

So my message to all sides is simple:
- labor peace matters,
- site stability matters,
- but competitiveness matters just as much.

If the union is prepared to negotiate a settlement that actually changes the economics of the business, then there is room for a durable agreement. If not, then management will still have to pursue the measures required to protect the company’s long-term value.
