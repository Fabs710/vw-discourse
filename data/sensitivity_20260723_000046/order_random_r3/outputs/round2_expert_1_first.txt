Below is a neutral restructuring assessment focused on the industrial-economics question: **what kind of cost gap typically has to be closed, how savings break down by measure, and whether a credible non-closure package can match closure-based restructuring**.

## 1) The core economic question

For a large German carmaker, the relevant issue is not whether there is “pain” on one side or another. It is whether the German footprint has a **durable unit-cost gap** and/or **excess fixed capacity** that can be closed **without** plant closures and compulsory redundancies.

In practice, management usually needs to close some combination of:

- **variable labour cost per vehicle**
- **fixed overhead cost**
- **capacity underutilization**
- **logistics / complexity cost**
- **restructuring drag from duplicate or low-productivity processes**

If the company’s German plants are materially below efficient utilization, then the cost problem is often driven as much by **fixed-cost absorption** as by direct wages.

## 2) What size of gap is typically relevant?

Without company-specific internal data, I can only give scenario ranges. For a high-cost German OEM with slack capacity and weaker EV economics, a plausible annual gap at a large passenger-car operation can be in the range of:

- **€500 million to €1.5 billion per year** for a single brand/large domestic footprint,
- sometimes more if the plant network is highly duplicated or utilization is sharply below breakeven.

Per vehicle, that often corresponds to a gap of roughly:

- **€500 to €2,000 per vehicle** depending on volume, model mix, and how much fixed cost is being spread over too few units.

That is the scale at which management starts talking about structural restructuring rather than incremental savings.

## 3) Where savings usually come from

### A. Workforce cost measures
These are real, but usually **not sufficient alone**.

Typical annual savings ranges:
- **Wage moderation / bonus waivers / tariff concessions:**  
  often **1–3% of direct labour cost**, sometimes more if bonuses or special payments are material.
- **Working-time changes / flexible shifts / shorter paid idle time:**  
  can yield meaningful savings, but usually mostly by improving utilization; direct cash effect depends on volume.
- **Headcount reduction through attrition / hiring freeze / internal redeployment:**  
  can save substantially if it removes permanent overhead, but the savings depend on how many roles are truly eliminated.

For a large carmaker, a serious labour package might produce roughly:
- **€100 million to €400 million annually** if substantial and well designed,
- more if it includes large-scale permanent headcount reduction and shift rationalization.

### B. Capacity and complexity reduction
This is often the largest source of durable savings.

Measures include:
- shutting one production line or product family,
- reducing number of shifts,
- consolidating production into fewer plants,
- reducing duplicate logistics, maintenance, and planning layers.

Typical savings:
- **€200 million to €800 million+ annually** depending on the amount of capacity removed and the fixed-cost base.

This is why closures are attractive economically: they remove **fixed costs**, not just payroll concessions.

### C. Overhead and procurement
Often underestimated in public debate.

Potential measures:
- management overhead reduction,
- procurement simplification,
- supplier base rationalization,
- engineering footprint consolidation,
- IT and administrative headcount cuts.

Typical savings:
- **€100 million to €300 million annually** in a large restructuring,
- sometimes more if functions are duplicated across brands or regions.

### D. Inventory, footprint, and working-capital efficiency
These are real but usually secondary in annual P&L terms.

Savings and cash improvement:
- lower inventory,
- lower expediting costs,
- less premium freight,
- reduced plant complexity.

These can improve cash flow materially, but often do **not** fully substitute for structural P&L savings.

## 4) Can non-closure measures match closure-based savings?

### Short answer: sometimes partially, rarely fully
A credible non-closure package can close a **material share** of the gap, but if the issue is genuine overcapacity, it is hard for non-closure measures alone to match the economics of a closure.

Why:
- Wage moderation affects only one part of cost.
- Working-time changes help utilization, but if demand is structurally below installed capacity, they do not remove fixed cost.
- Productivity measures take time and have diminishing returns.
- If capacity remains too high, the cost gap reappears as soon as temporary measures expire.

### Typical outcome
In many automotive restructurings, a non-closure package can cover maybe:
- **30% to 60% of the required annual savings**,
depending on the size of the labour contribution and the scope of overhead reductions.

To cover the full gap without closures, the package usually needs all of the following:
- substantial and durable labour concessions,
- headcount reduction through voluntary exits and attrition,
- shift/working-time compression,
- major overhead cuts,
- and often product or investment commitments that improve utilization.

If the required gap is large, the question becomes not “can non-closure measures generate savings?” but “can they generate them fast enough and with enough certainty?”

## 5) What a credible non-closure package would need to contain

If the goal is to avoid plant closures and forced layoffs, a credible package would generally require:

1. **Permanent capacity reduction without closure**
   - fewer shifts,
   - lower output targets,
   - elimination of duplicate lines,
   - product consolidation.

2. **Verified headcount reduction**
   - mostly voluntary exits,
   - early retirement,
   - internal redeployment,
   - hiring freeze,
   - no replacement of departing staff in overhead functions.

3. **Time-limited wage moderation plus permanent cost relief**
   - temporary pay restraint alone is not enough;
   - there should also be permanent structural savings elsewhere.

4. **Management and overhead savings**
   - corporate overhead reduction,
   - procurement simplification,
   - engineering and administrative consolidation.

5. **Investment and model allocation commitments**
   - otherwise utilization stays weak and the savings do not hold.

6. **Hard verification**
   - annual savings tracking,
   - baseline and target volume assumptions,
   - trigger clauses if volumes or costs deviate.

Without these elements, a “no closure” settlement is usually not economically robust.

## 6) Trade-offs between closures and non-closure alternatives

### Closures
Pros:
- largest and most durable fixed-cost savings,
- fastest route to rebalancing capacity,
- clearer economic signal.

Cons:
- high social and political cost,
- labor conflict risk,
- execution risk,
- potential loss of skills and future flexibility,
- large one-time restructuring charges.

### Non-closure restructuring
Pros:
- preserves industrial relations and local capability,
- lower political and reputational cost,
- may protect future production optionality.

Cons:
- savings may be slower, smaller, or less durable,
- requires broad cooperation and sustained discipline,
- may not fully resolve excess capacity,
- can become a temporary truce rather than a structural fix.

## 7) My assessment of credibility

On the facts you provided, a **non-closure solution is economically possible only if the real gap is moderate and a large share of the burden can be shifted into:**
- capacity reduction without closure,
- headcount attrition,
- overhead elimination,
- and long-duration labour moderation.

If the company’s internal analysis shows a **large fixed-cost gap tied to underutilized plants**, then closures remain the most credible way to achieve the full savings target.

So the answer is:

- **Yes, a non-closure restructuring can be credible**
- **No, it is usually not credible unless it produces savings close to those of closure-based restructuring**
- **The burden of proof is on the numbers**: volume, capacity utilization, fixed cost per plant, and the share of savings that can be locked in permanently.

## 8) What data would be needed to judge this properly

To move from scenario analysis to firm conclusion, the missing items are:

- plant-by-plant fixed and variable cost
- utilization rates by line and model
- labor cost per vehicle and per hour
- overhead allocation by site
- restructuring charges for closure vs alternative measures
- expected volume by model over 3–5 years
- savings durability after any temporary concessions expire

Without that, any claim that closures are strictly necessary, or strictly avoidable, remains incomplete.
