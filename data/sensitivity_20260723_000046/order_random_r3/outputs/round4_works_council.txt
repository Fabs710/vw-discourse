I will be just as direct.

## My position remains unchanged
A restructuring that **keeps every German plant open**, **avoids compulsory redundancies**, and **preserves co-determination and the job-security framework** is the only acceptable path. Anything else would mean management is asking the workforce to pay for a crisis it did not create.

I do not accept plant closures as a bargaining chip, a default option, or a “legitimate tool” in the abstract. And I do not accept forced layoffs disguised as “hard adjustment.”

---

## Where I agree

### With the expert
Yes: we need **plant-by-plant numbers**, not slogans.  
Yes: temporary concessions alone are not enough.  
Yes: the issue is structural cost and utilization, not sentiment.

That is precisely why we need to compare real alternatives rather than treating closure as the benchmark from the start.

### With the state
I agree that closures are not the default answer and that political and social legitimacy matter.  
I also agree that workers must not carry the whole burden.

### With the union
I agree that:
- a durable solution is needed,
- labour can contribute through flexibility, voluntary exits, productivity, and temporary moderation,
- and workers should not be treated as the shock absorber of last resort.

### With ownership and investors, only in part
I agree that a cosmetic solution would be useless.  
I agree that the company needs durable savings.

But I do **not** accept the conclusion that closures or forced redundancies become acceptable if they are merely financially tidy.

---

## Where I disagree

### 1) I reject the “closures are legitimate if the numbers say so” framing
Management, investors, and the family keep saying closures are simply an available tool if the economics justify them.

I reject that.  
For this company, with this co-determined governance structure, closures are not a neutral tool. They are a rupture in the trust framework, a breach of the social contract, and a direct attack on jobs and regions.

If the company cannot achieve competitiveness without closing plants, then the problem is not labour’s red lines — it is the business model management has presented.

### 2) I reject the normalization of compulsory redundancies
Voluntary exits, early retirement, redeployment, and internal mobility are one thing.  
**Forced layoffs are a hard red line.**

If management believes it can threaten people out the door and still call it restructuring, then it has misunderstood the power structure in this company.

### 3) I reject the idea that co-determination is an obstacle to be worked around
The state is right to emphasize veto rights. I go further: co-determination is not a decorative feature. It is a core governance principle that cannot be treated as an inconvenience when management wants a faster cut.

If a plan depends on overruling the workforce, then it is not a viable plan.

---

## On the management argument

You say permanent cost reduction is required. Fine.  
You say temporary wage moderation is not enough. Fine.  
You say the cost gap may be material. Fine.

But you have **not** proven that the only way to close the gap is to shut plants or impose layoffs.

That proof does not exist yet.

And let’s be honest about what is really being argued here: management wants to preserve strategic flexibility by keeping the threat of closures alive. That is not neutral analysis. That is leverage.

I will not accept a negotiation where the workforce is told: “Concede now, or we may close a plant later.”

That is not a fair test. That is coercion.

---

## On the investors’ argument

You keep saying value must be protected and a weak compromise would be destructive.

I agree with the principle — but your definition of “value protection” is too narrow.

A settlement that triggers:
- long industrial conflict,
- loss of trust,
- damaged productivity,
- reputational harm,
- political backlash,
- and weaker implementation

can destroy value faster than it creates it.

So no, closure is not “risk-adjusted value creation” by default.  
It is risk. Large risk.

And you are underweighting that.

---

## On the family holding

You say you want competitiveness restored permanently and that closures are not automatic. That is the right starting point.

But your position still leaves the door open to closing plants once you decide the numbers are “sufficiently bad.”

I will not negotiate under that shadow.

If the controlling shareholder wants trust, then it must accept a genuine commitment to **site preservation**, not just a promise to evaluate closures later if labour resists enough.

Ownership must show it is prepared to co-own the solution:
- investment,
- training,
- product commitment,
- and restraint on extracting value at the workforce’s expense.

---

## On the state government

Your veto power is real. Use it.

Do not just say closures are not the default.  
Say clearly: **German plants stay open.**

If you believe in regional industrial policy, then back it with an enforceable framework:
- site guarantees,
- job security,
- investment commitments,
- and a binding restructuring path that does not rely on closures.

Anything less leaves the workforce exposed to a “temporary” peace that can be broken later.

---

## What a serious path actually looks like

If management truly wants to close the gap, then it must build a package around:
- capacity reduction **without closures**
- line consolidation inside existing sites
- working-time flexibility
- productivity improvements
- overhead and complexity cuts
- voluntary exits only
- internal redeployment
- investment and product commitments
- and a binding extension of job-security protections

That is a real restructuring.

What is not acceptable is:
- plant closures,
- compulsory redundancies,
- or using fear to force concessions.

---

## My response to the request for numbers
Yes, I support the plant-by-plant analysis — but it must not be a one-sided exercise designed to justify pre-selected closures.

The analysis must show, by site:
- current output and capacity,
- utilization,
- fixed-cost burden,
- direct labour cost per vehicle,
- overhead allocation,
- savings from internal capacity reduction,
- savings from productivity and complexity cuts,
- savings from voluntary exits and redeployment,
- and the one-time cost of each non-closure measure.

And it must compare those numbers against a closure scenario **only as a reference point**, not as the assumed baseline.

## My bottom line

I am not here to negotiate the workforce into accepting closures with better wording.  
I am here to prevent closures, prevent compulsory redundancies, and preserve the job-security and co-determination framework.

So my position is:

- **No plant closures**
- **No compulsory redundancies**
- **No weakening of co-determination**
- **No one-sided burden shifting onto workers**

What I can discuss:
- voluntary exits,
- redeployment,
- working-time flexibility,
- productivity improvements,
- temporary moderation,
- bonus restraint,
- and real internal restructuring inside existing sites.

If that is not enough for management or investors, then they should say openly that they want to break the historic social compact.  
Because that is what this would amount to.
