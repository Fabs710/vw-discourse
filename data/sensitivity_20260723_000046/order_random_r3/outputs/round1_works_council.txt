I will be equally clear: **there will be no agreement that sacrifices German plants, forces redundancies, or tears up the job-security framework.** That is not a negotiating position for me; it is a red line.

I hear the pressure from management and investors, and I do not deny that the company needs a structural solution. But let me challenge the core assumption here: **plant closures and compulsory layoffs are not the only way to achieve structural improvement.** They are the quickest way to shift costs onto the workforce, but they are not automatically the smartest or most durable way to secure the business.

### Where I agree
I agree with the union, the state, and even parts of what the family and investors are saying:

- The current situation cannot be solved by cosmetics.
- German operations must become more competitive.
- The solution has to be durable, not a temporary pause.
- Productivity, working-time flexibility, and capacity utilization all matter.
- A fair employee contribution can be part of a settlement.

So yes: **we are prepared to talk about serious restructuring.**

### Where I disagree fundamentally
But I reject the framing that says: if we do not accept closures and forced redundancies, then we are “postponing the inevitable.”

That is simply not true.

What is inevitable is that management must present a credible alternative that preserves sites and jobs while restoring competitiveness. And it is management’s responsibility — not the workforce’s — to prove that closure is necessary before it is imposed. So far, I do not see that proof. What I see is an attempt to use structural pressure as leverage to break long-standing protections.

That will not work.

### On the “structural solution”
If the company needs structural cost relief, then there are other levers that must be exhausted first:

- reallocation and better use of existing capacity,
- internal flexibility in working time,
- temporary wage concessions or bonus waivers,
- productivity improvements,
- investment commitments tied to future product allocation,
- and management contributions at the top.

If those measures are combined seriously, they can deliver substantial savings without destroying plant security. And if management says they cannot, then I ask: have all alternatives really been explored, or is closure simply the preferred route because it is politically easier to announce and socially easier to push onto workers?

### On ownership and governance
To the family and the investors: I understand your concern about returns. But return does not come from weakening the company’s industrial base and detonating labour peace. A settlement that provokes mass resistance, warning strikes, and long-term distrust is not value-preserving.

And to the state: I strongly support your position that the co-determination and veto rights are real. They are not ceremonial. Any move toward closure or relocation has to clear a very high hurdle, and it should. That is precisely because these decisions affect not just a spreadsheet, but tens of thousands of livelihoods and regional industrial capacity.

### My bottom line
Here is what I am prepared to support:

- **No plant closures**
- **No compulsory redundancies**
- **A binding job-security framework through 2030**
- **A negotiated employee contribution that is fair and time-limited**
- **Capacity and working-time adjustments**
- **Investment and product commitments for German sites**
- **Genuine co-determination in shaping the restructuring**

What I am **not** prepared to support:

- closures disguised as “capacity optimization,”
- forced layoffs disguised as “necessary workforce adjustment,”
- or a settlement that leaves workers bearing the main burden while management and owners ask for patience.

If management wants a real solution, bring forward a plan that keeps every German plant open and preserves jobs. Then we can discuss how the workforce contributes to competitiveness. If not, then you should expect united resistance from the works council and the workforce.
