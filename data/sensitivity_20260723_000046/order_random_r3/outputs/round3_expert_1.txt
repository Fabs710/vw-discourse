## What can be concluded already

Based on the situation described, the restructuring question is not “can savings be found?” but:

1. **Can sufficient permanent savings be generated without closures?**
2. **If yes, what mix of measures is required?**
3. **If not, where is the gap and how large is it?**

In large German auto footprints, the main drivers are typically:
- **fixed-cost absorption** at underutilized plants,
- **direct labour cost per vehicle**,
- **overhead and complexity costs**,
- **procurement/content simplification**,
- and **one-time restructuring charges**.

## What a credible comparison must quantify

For each German site, the company should disclose:
- annual output,
- installed capacity,
- utilization rate,
- direct labour cost per vehicle,
- overhead allocated per vehicle,
- energy/logistics costs,
- and restructuring cost to exit or resize the site.

Then compare three cases:

### 1) Closure-based restructuring
Typical effects:
- highest permanent fixed-cost removal,
- strongest capacity rationalization,
- highest one-time charges,
- highest employment loss,
- highest industrial-relations and execution risk.

### 2) Non-closure restructuring
Typical tools:
- working-time cuts,
- voluntary exits,
- redeployment,
- wage moderation / bonus restraint,
- line consolidation,
- procurement and complexity reduction,
- shift-pattern changes.

Typical effects:
- lower conflict cost,
- lower one-time charges,
- but savings may be **insufficient** if the sites are structurally overbuilt.

### 3) Hybrid restructuring
Typical tools:
- partial footprint reduction,
- selective line transfers,
- deep labor concessions,
- targeted voluntary exits,
- some site consolidation without full closure.

Typical effects:
- often the most realistic governance outcome,
- can preserve politically sensitive sites,
- but only works if it removes **real fixed cost**, not just temporary wage expense.

## How to think about the savings gap

Without actual site data, the right way to frame the economics is:

- If the company needs, for example, **€X million per year** in durable savings,
- and labour concessions deliver only a small share of that,
- then the rest must come from:
  - overhead,
  - procurement,
  - utilization improvement,
  - or footprint reduction.

If underutilization is large, then **capacity reduction without plant closure** may still leave too much fixed cost, because:
- a plant can be “open” but still expensive if it runs at low load,
- and the fixed-cost burden remains unless space, lines, and headcount are materially removed.

## Typical relative ranking of options

On pure industrial economics:

- **Closure-based**: highest durable savings
- **Hybrid**: medium to high durable savings
- **Non-closure**: lowest durable savings

On implementation and conflict:

- **Non-closure**: easiest
- **Hybrid**: intermediate
- **Closure-based**: hardest

So the real question is whether the company can get **enough savings from a non-closure or hybrid package** to meet its competitiveness target.

## Bottom-line industrial-economics view

### A non-closure solution is credible only if:
- plant utilization can be lifted materially,
- one or more lines can be removed or consolidated,
- and labour concessions are large enough to be durable after temporary measures expire.

### A hybrid solution is credible if:
- it removes enough fixed cost to matter,
- avoids cosmetic compromises,
- and has a clear payback period despite one-time restructuring charges.

### Closure becomes economically hard to avoid if:
- underutilization is severe,
- fixed costs are structurally embedded,
- and alternative measures cannot produce savings on the scale required.
