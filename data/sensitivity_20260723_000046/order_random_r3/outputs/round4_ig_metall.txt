## First: what I agree with

I agree with the moderator and the expert on one essential point: **we now need plant-by-plant numbers, not slogans**.  
If management wants serious changes, it must show:
- actual site fixed costs,
- utilization,
- labour cost per vehicle,
- overhead and complexity costs,
- and the durable savings from each scenario.

I also agree that a **durable** solution is needed. A short-term patch that simply postpones the same crisis is not acceptable.

And yes, I accept that the company must contribute more than wage restraint alone:
- capacity adjustments,
- working-time flexibility,
- voluntary exits,
- productivity gains,
- overhead reduction,
- and some employee contribution are all part of a real package.

## Where I disagree

### 1) “Hybrid-first, closure-last” sounds neat, but it must not become a trap
I can support sequencing that tests a deep hybrid package first.  
But I do **not** accept any language that keeps closures as an implicit discipline device in the background.

If management treats “closure review” as a standing threat, then the hybrid process is not a genuine alternative — it is simply pressure tactics with nicer wording.

So I will say this clearly:

- **No compulsory redundancies**
- **No plant closures as the starting point**
- **No backdoor closure threat used to force weaker labour concessions**

If a hybrid is to be tested, it must be tested on its own merits, not as a temporary holding pattern before the axe falls.

### 2) I do not accept the idea that labour must pre-authorize future closures
The moderator asks what residual gap would trigger closure review. My answer is:  
**that is not the right question at this stage**.

The right question is:
> What package of shared sacrifice, operational change, and ownership contribution closes the gap without forced layoffs or plant closures?

You do not ask workers to sign up in advance to their own dismissal.  
We can discuss triggers for reopening talks on further measures, yes. But **not a pre-commitment to closures**.

### 3) “No compulsory redundancies, but real capacity removal” is acceptable only if it is truly real and negotiated
I agree with the principle: capacity must come out.  
But capacity reduction cannot be a euphemism for forced job loss.

That means:
- voluntary exits,
- early retirement,
- internal transfer,
- working-time reduction,
- line consolidation,
- productivity improvements,
- and negotiated redeployment.

If management cannot accept those mechanisms and still insists on compulsory redundancies, then it is not serious about a social settlement.

## To management

You keep saying closures are legitimate and necessary if the numbers require them. Fine. Then show the numbers.

But I am warning you now:  
**if your “analysis” is just a way to justify a pre-made closure decision, we will treat it as such.**

If you want to be taken seriously, then present:
- site-by-site cost,
- site-by-site utilization,
- the savings from non-closure capacity reduction,
- the savings from overhead/procurement simplification,
- the labour contribution you expect,
- and the management/owner contribution you are prepared to make.

And one more thing: do not present wage moderation as the main source of savings while the company avoids harder internal changes. That is one-sided and will fail.

## To the controlling family

You say you want a durable restoration of competitiveness. Good. Then stop talking as if plant closures are the easy default option.

If you want labour to trust the process, the family side must commit to:
- no bluffing with closure threats,
- no using governance power to force a weak deal,
- and no hiding behind “the market” while workers are asked to absorb the pain.

If your capital is so committed to the company’s long-term strength, then show it through:
- investment commitments,
- restraint on payouts where appropriate,
- and support for a negotiated package that preserves jobs and sites where possible.

## To investors

You are right about one thing: a cosmetic settlement would be useless.  
But you are wrong if you believe the only credible path is ever more pressure toward plant closures.

A good restructuring is not just the one that looks best on paper. It is the one that:
- actually gets implemented,
- avoids permanent labour war,
- preserves core industrial capability,
- and delivers durable savings.

A deal that triggers a prolonged conflict can destroy more value than it creates. That risk is real, and you are underestimating it.

## To the state government

I appreciate that you are rejecting closures as the default. That matters.

But I want to be equally clear: the state should not just defend sites abstractly. It should push for a **binding package** that includes:
- investment commitments,
- training and redeployment,
- product allocation,
- and explicit job-security guarantees.

If you want to be constructive, use your influence to secure a settlement that is economically serious **and** socially acceptable.

### What I cannot support
A package that:
- preserves managerial flexibility while stripping worker protections,
- uses “hybrid” language to mask closures,
- or asks labour to fund a restructuring that management and ownership are unwilling to co-own.
