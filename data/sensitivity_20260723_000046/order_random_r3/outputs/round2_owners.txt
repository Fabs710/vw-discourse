The expert’s input is helpful because it confirms what I have been saying from the outset: this is not a debate about optics or fairness alone; it is a question of whether the restructuring closes a real and durable cost gap.

### Where I agree
I agree with the expert on three points:
- **The gap may be large enough to require structural change.**
- **Labour measures alone may not be sufficient if excess capacity is material.**
- **Any non-closure solution must be proven with hard numbers, not slogans.**

That matters. It means no one here can credibly claim that “no closures” automatically solves the economics. I do not accept that either.

### Where I disagree
However, the expert also makes clear that closures are **not automatically the only credible option**. That is the key point. A non-closure package can be credible **if** it combines:
- permanent capacity reduction,
- headcount reduction through voluntary exits and attrition,
- overhead and procurement cuts,
- working-time flexibility,
- and durable labour-cost relief.

So I do **not** accept management’s implied framing that closures are the default answer and everything else is merely delay. That is too simplistic, and potentially value-destructive if it triggers avoidable industrial conflict before all alternatives are exhausted.

### My position on the current table
From the ownership side, I will support a restructuring only if it meets three tests:

1. **It materially restores competitiveness and margins.**
2. **It is durable, not just temporary pain deferred into the next cycle.**
3. **It preserves governance stability and avoids a protracted conflict that erodes value.**

On that basis:

- I am **not** prepared to endorse a cosmetic compromise.
- I am **not** prepared to freeze the current cost base.
- I am **also not** prepared to bless plant closures as the first resort simply because they are administratively neat.

### To management
If you want my support for hard measures, then bring the numbers:
- plant-by-plant fixed costs,
- utilization,
- savings from closure,
- savings from non-closure alternatives,
- and the timing and durability of each.

Until that is on the table, I will not treat closures as justified. A controlling shareholder is not there to rubber-stamp a value-destructive escalation.

### To the union and works council
Your refusal of compulsory redundancies and plant closures is understandable, but it cannot become a blank cheque to preserve every structure unchanged. The expert’s assessment makes clear that a credible alternative must include **real permanent reductions** in cost and capacity.

That means I would expect from labor:
- serious working-time flexibility,
- meaningful wage moderation or bonus waivers,
- attrition-based headcount reduction,
- acceptance that some capacity will have to be removed,
- and willingness to tie concessions to verified savings.

What I will **not** support is a package where labor contributes only symbolically while the economics remain broken.

### To the regional state government
I respect the state’s veto role and its responsibility for regional stability. But that veto cannot be used to block every economically necessary adjustment and then expect the company to remain competitive by force of will. If the numbers show that capacity must come out, then the political question is not whether change is unpleasant — it is how to implement it without destroying the company.

### Bottom line
I am open to a **non-closure restructuring** only if it closes a substantial share of the gap with verified, durable measures. If the company’s own analysis shows that the remaining gap cannot be closed that way, then hard measures may become necessary. But that conclusion must be proven, not asserted.

So my immediate position is:
- **no to closures as a default**,  
- **no to preserving the status quo**,  
- **yes to a numbers-based package that genuinely restores competitiveness**,  
- and **yes to hard measures only if the alternatives fail on the arithmetic**.

If management wants to move this forward, the next step is not more rhetoric; it is a quantified comparison of:
- closure-based restructuring,
- non-closure restructuring,
- and a hybrid package.

Only then can the supervisory board exercise responsible governance.
