We are at the point where slogans must give way to substance.

I agree with everyone on one point: this needs to be a **durable structural settlement**. But I do **not** accept the conclusion that the only credible way to achieve that is through plant closures or compulsory redundancies. That is a management and investor preference, not a demonstrated necessity.

### What I accept
I am still prepared to negotiate a package that includes:
- capacity reductions,
- working-time adjustments,
- productivity improvements,
- temporary or time-limited wage moderation,
- waiver of bonuses or other collective financial contributions,
- and binding investment and product commitments for German sites.

That is the basis for a serious compromise. Workers are not refusing to contribute. We have already signaled that we are willing to do so.

### What I reject
I reject three things outright:

1. **Compulsory redundancies**  
   That would break the social contract and shift the crisis onto the workforce.

2. **Plant closures as a default tool**  
   If closures are treated as the starting point rather than the last resort, then there is no trust left in the process.

3. **A settlement where workers pay, while management and owners contribute only rhetorically**  
   If the burden is not shared, there will be no legitimacy for the deal.

### To management
You keep saying “equivalent and verifiable savings,” but you have not yet shown why those savings cannot come from non-closure measures. If you claim no alternative exists, then show the numbers:
- What exact cost gap must be closed?
- How much comes from labour costs?
- How much from capacity utilization?
- How much from management restraint, procurement, overhead, and investment discipline?
- What savings do you expect from closures versus internal restructuring?

Until that arithmetic is on the table, closures are not a solution; they are a threat.

### To the controlling family and investors
You are right that the company must be competitive. But competitiveness is not served by detonating industrial peace or destroying trust in the German sites. A settlement that triggers warning strikes, prolonged conflict, and long-term damage to the collective framework will not protect value.

If you want durable cost relief, then the owner side must also contribute visibly:
- through investment commitments,
- through restraint on payouts and overhead,
- and through acceptance that the workforce cannot be the sole shock absorber.

### To the regional state government
I support your position that closures must not be the default and that co-determination is real. But that also means the state should help force a credible alternative into the room, not just repeat that it dislikes closures. We need concrete leverage for a negotiated solution: investment guarantees, site commitments, and a binding framework that prevents management from treating labour as the easy adjustment variable.

### My bottom line for this round
If management wants a settlement, then bring forward a **numbers-based alternative** that does all of the following:
- keeps the core German sites open,
- rules out compulsory redundancies,
- preserves the collective wage framework,
- achieves verified durable savings,
- and includes fair contributions from owners and management.

If that package can be made credible, we can negotiate. If not, then the workforce will not be asked to sign away jobs and standards so that others can call it restructuring.

And let me be equally clear: if management continues to insist that the workforce must bear the main burden, then industrial action remains available and legitimate. We are not here to provide cover for one-sided social dumping.
