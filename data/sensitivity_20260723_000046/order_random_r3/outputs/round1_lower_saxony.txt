I agree with both sides on one essential point: this company needs a structural solution, not another postponement. But I do **not** accept the idea that the only credible structural solution is plant closures and forced redundancies.

From the perspective of the regional state and as a long-standing anchor shareholder, I have three priorities that must be kept together:

1. **The company must regain competitiveness.**
2. **German sites and core jobs must be preserved wherever economically possible.**
3. **The state’s co-determination and veto role is not negotiable.**

On the union side, I am aligned with the refusal to accept a one-sided burden on the workforce. No compulsory redundancies and no blanket closure program are legitimate red lines in my view as well. The workforce has already carried major transformation costs over the years, and social partnership cannot mean that employees are asked to pay for every strategic mistake made above them.

At the same time, I cannot simply endorse a “status quo with cosmetics” package. If there are genuine overcapacities and structural cost problems, then the answer must include real operational change. That can mean capacity reductions, working-time adjustments, productivity improvements, and a fair employee contribution. It can also mean targeted site adjustments if they are truly unavoidable — but not a blanket logic of closing plants first and asking questions later.

To the controlling shareholder: I understand the concern about value destruction, and I share the view that the company must be fit for the long term. But I would strongly caution against treating closures as the default fallback. In a company of this importance, plant closures in Germany are not just “hard measures”; they have regional, political, and social consequences that can damage the company itself for years. A settlement that tears up trust may “solve” an accounting problem while creating a governance and industrial-relations crisis that is worse.

So my position is this:

- **Yes** to a negotiated restructuring.
- **Yes** to meaningful savings and productivity gains.
- **Yes** to a real employee contribution, if it is fair, time-limited, and paired with investment commitments.
- **No** to compulsory redundancies as the first or primary tool.
- **No** to a restructuring model that treats plant closures as politically easy but economically and socially reckless.
- **No** to any path that sidelines the state’s board and veto rights.

If management can present a package that:
- preserves core German sites,
- avoids forced layoffs,
- secures verifiable cost relief,
- and commits to future products and investment,

then I am prepared to work constructively toward it.

But if the proposal remains fundamentally “workers lose jobs, sites close, and the burden falls mainly on labour,” then I will oppose it. That is not a fair restructuring and, in my view, not a sustainable one either.
