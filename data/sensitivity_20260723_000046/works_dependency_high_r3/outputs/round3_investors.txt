We have heard enough principle. What matters now is whether the package can **restore competitive margins, durably, and with real implementation certainty**.

## Where we agree
We agree with management, the family holding, the state, and even labour on one key point: **this business needs a serious restructuring, not a cosmetic settlement**.

We also agree that:
- internal flexibility should be used,
- qualification and redeployment matter,
- voluntary exits and early retirement are preferable to blunt forced measures,
- prolonged conflict would destroy value,
- and any deal has to be **quantified** and **durable**.

That is the baseline.

## Where we disagree
### 1) “No closures” cannot be an absolute
I understand the political and social argument. But from a capital-market perspective, **a blanket rule excluding German closures regardless of economics is not acceptable**.

If the company can prove a non-closure package closes the gap, fine — we would prefer that route.  
But if the numbers do **not** work, then ruling out closures in advance is just protecting the current footprint at the expense of future value.

We will not support a settlement that preserves every site simply because closures are painful.

### 2) “No compulsory redundancies” is only acceptable if the alternative is genuinely sufficient
Same logic here.

We prefer voluntary exits, attrition, redeployment, and internal adjustment. But if those instruments do not produce a **material** and **durable** cost base reset, then an absolute ban on compulsory redundancies becomes a constraint that leaves the company structurally impaired.

That is not acceptable to us.

### 3) Job guarantees are not valuable if they freeze overcapacity
A guarantee through 2030 sounds attractive only if it comes with a viable industrial plan.

If it locks in excess capacity, weakens the ability to respond to market reality, and delays the margin recovery, then it is not security — it is deferred decline.

We will not back a deal that buys social peace today by damaging return on sales tomorrow.

## What we need to see
The debate is still too abstract. We need a **side-by-side quantified comparison** of:
- a no-closure scenario,
- a closure scenario,
- and a mixed compromise scenario.

That comparison must show:
- recurring annual savings,
- one-off restructuring costs,
- capacity reduction,
- headcount impact,
- investment headroom,
- and the expected path back to target margins.

Without that, “credible” is just a word.

## Our position on the current arguments
### To the works council and union
You are right that workers cannot be the only adjustment variable. But you are **not** yet showing a package that can close the economics without closures or deeper workforce reduction.

We are open to a non-closure path **only if** it is genuinely strong enough.  
So far, what we hear is mostly red lines and trust language. That is not a financial plan.

### To management
You are right to demand numbers, but you are still leaning too quickly toward closure as if it were self-evidently necessary. It is not. Prove it.

Bring the hard comparison, plant by plant and function by function. Show:
- what savings come from labor measures,
- what remains after those measures,
- and whether the residual gap is truly large enough to justify irreversible action.

### To the regional state
Your role as anchor shareholder matters, but not as a mechanism to freeze economics. If a serious non-closure solution exists, support it. If it does not, do not use governance to preserve value destruction.

### To the controlling family
We agree with your insistence on durability. But durability means the numbers have to work. It does **not** mean preserving every legacy structure no matter what.

## What would move us
We would support a deal that includes:
- meaningful labor-cost contribution,
- clear capacity reduction,
- voluntary exits and internal redeployment first,
- binding investment and product allocation commitments,
- a fixed review mechanism,
- and a **credible** path back to competitive margins.

If that can be done **without** closures and forced redundancies, fine — that is the preferred outcome.

If it cannot, then we will not support an outcome that leaves the company with a structurally uncompetitive cost base just to avoid difficult decisions.

## Bottom line
Our position is simple:

- **No cosmetic compromise.**
- **No deal that leaves margins structurally impaired.**
- **No settlement that asks shareholders to fund permanent overcapacity.**
- **Yes to the least destructive package that actually restores competitiveness.**

So the next step is not more rhetoric. It is the quantified comparison. Until then, we remain open-minded on instruments, but not on value destruction.
