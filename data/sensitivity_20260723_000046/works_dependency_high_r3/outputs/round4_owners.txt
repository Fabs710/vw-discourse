I will be straightforward.

## Where I agree
I agree with management, the expert, the state, and even labour on the basic diagnosis:

- this company needs a **durable restructuring now**,
- cosmetic delay will destroy value,
- internal flexibility, redeployment, qualification, working-time changes, and voluntary exits should be used aggressively,
- and a negotiated solution is far better than a prolonged strike cycle.

I also agree that numbers matter. We need a quantified comparison, not rhetoric.

## Where I disagree
### 1) “No closures” cannot be an absolute governing principle
I do **not** accept the position that German plant closures are off limits in principle.

The expert has made the key point clear: a non-closure package can close part of the gap, sometimes a large part, but not necessarily the full structural fixed-cost gap. If that turns out to be the case here, then ruling out closures in advance is not stewardship — it is a commitment to preserve overcapacity at the expense of future value.

I am not saying closures are desirable. I am saying they cannot be ruled out unless and until a serious alternative proves it can restore competitiveness.

### 2) “No compulsory redundancies” is not a universal veto
The same logic applies to forced layoffs.

If voluntary exits, attrition, redeployment, and working-time tools are sufficient, then fine — they should be used. But I will not support a settlement that bans compulsory redundancies regardless of whether the company can otherwise reach a viable cost base.

That would simply shift the burden forward and leave the company structurally impaired.

### 3) A blanket 2030 guarantee is too rigid if it freezes the footprint
I understand why the workforce wants security. But security only has value if it sits on top of a viable industrial plan.

A guarantee that locks in the current footprint while costs remain structurally too high is not protection. It is deferred decline. That is not acceptable to me as a long-term owner.

### 4) Co-determination is a framework, not a denial of economics
I respect the governance structure and the state’s role. But co-determination does not remove the need for hard decisions. It means those decisions must be negotiated, not that they can be excluded beforehand.

If a site is structurally uncompetitive, we should not pretend otherwise for the sake of political comfort.

## Where the others are right — and where they stop short

### To the works council
You are right that labour cannot be the only adjustment variable. You are also right that workers should not be asked to contribute to a hollow promise.

But you are **not** yet showing a package that proves the no-closure route is sufficient. Red lines are not a financial plan. If you want the family holding and the supervisory board to support a non-closure settlement, then show me how the economics close.

### To the union
You are right that any labour contribution must be reciprocal and time-limited. I agree with that.

What I do not agree with is your attempt to make no closures and no forced redundancies absolute, even if the arithmetic does not work. A bargain cannot be defined only by what labour refuses. It must also show what actually restores the margin.

### To the regional state
I understand your political responsibility and your duty to protect the industrial base. That is legitimate.

But protecting the industrial base means protecting a **viable** industrial base, not freezing every site regardless of performance. If a non-closure scenario truly works, I would support it. If it does not, I will not support using governance to preserve value destruction.

### To institutional investors
You are right that cosmetic compromise is not acceptable. I agree.

But closures are also not a free solution. They carry high one-off costs, labour-relations damage, and execution risk. If a non-closure route can materially close the gap, it may well be the better value-preserving option. That is why I want the comparison done properly.

## What I would support
I would support a package with these features:

- a **fully quantified no-closure scenario first**,
- measurable recurring savings,
- meaningful employee contributions,
- working-time and flexibility measures,
- voluntary exits and early retirement,
- redeployment and qualification,
- binding investment commitments for German sites,
- and a fixed review mechanism with hard triggers.

If that package demonstrably restores margins and competitiveness, then I would prefer it.

## What I will not support
I will not support:

- a deal that preserves uncompetitive overcapacity,
- a symbolic compromise that avoids the hard math,
- or a settlement that demands permanent owner funding for structural weakness.

And I will not support a permanent governance position that makes deeper restructuring impossible if it proves necessary to protect the company’s long-term value.

## My position, plainly
I am open to alternatives to closures and forced redundancies.  
I am **not** open to treating those instruments as permanently off the table.

So the next step is clear:

**Bring the quantified comparison.**

I want side-by-side scenarios for:

1. no-closure / no-forced-redundancy package,  
2. mixed compromise,  
3. closure-based restructuring.

Each must show:
- recurring annual savings,
- one-off restructuring costs,
- headcount impact,
- capacity impact,
- investment needs,
- and the path back to target margins.
