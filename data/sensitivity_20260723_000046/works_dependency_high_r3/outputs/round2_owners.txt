I will be direct as well.

I agree with the diagnosis that this company needs a durable restructuring, not another postponement. I also agree that the workforce cannot simply be handed the bill while management and owners do nothing. And I agree that a prolonged industrial conflict would be value-destructive for everyone.

But I do **not** agree with the current labour-side framing that “no closures, no compulsory redundancies” can simply be declared as absolute red lines and then the rest of the company must somehow make the economics work around them. That is not a restructuring plan. It is a political boundary.

## Where I agree

### On the need for change
Management is right that the cost base and capacity must come down. The capital markets are right that this has to be durable and quantified. The regional state is right that random conflict and reputational damage would be harmful. And the workforce representatives are right that internal flexibility, qualification, redeployment, and voluntary instruments should be used aggressively before anything more destructive.

### On the need for negotiation
I also agree that a negotiated settlement is preferable to a strike-driven breakdown. The company’s long-term value is not protected by social warfare.

## Where I disagree

### 1) “No closures” cannot be treated as a governance veto in itself
I understand the social and political weight of German plants. I understand the history. But I cannot support a position that effectively says the existing footprint is untouchable no matter what the economics say.

If management can demonstrate, with hard numbers, that the required savings and capacity reduction cannot be achieved otherwise, then closures cannot be ruled out in principle. I am not saying closures are desirable. I am saying they cannot be excluded ex ante.

What I **do** insist on is this: closures must be a last resort, not the first instinct, and not a bargaining bludgeon.

### 2) “No compulsory redundancies” is not credible unless the alternative is equally strong
I would prefer to avoid compulsory redundancies. They are disruptive and politically toxic. But if the alternative package does not produce the necessary structural adjustment, then simply banning them is not a solution.

A serious offer would need to show how the company gets the headcount and cost base down through:
- voluntary exits,
- early retirement,
- phased transition,
- internal redeployment,
- qualification,
- working-time adjustments,
- and real labour-cost relief.

If that combination is enough, fine. If it is not enough, then refusing every harder instrument is just shifting the problem forward.

### 3) A blanket job guarantee through 2030 is too rigid
I understand why the works council and the state want binding security. But a promise that freezes everything through 2030 is only acceptable if it does not lock in today’s overcapacity.

A guarantee must be earned by a credible industrial plan. It cannot be a way to prevent restructuring while still expecting the shareholders to fund the gap.

## To management

I support the direction you are taking only if it is backed by real evidence. You need to show:

- the size of the cost gap,
- the exact savings required,
- which measures deliver them,
- and why those measures are durable.

Right now, you have made the case that change is necessary. You have **not yet fully proven** that every plant closure or compulsory redundancy you are keeping on the table is unavoidable.

So I want to see a fully quantified alternative scenario as the moderator requested:
- one scenario with closures,
- one without closures,
- one with and without compulsory redundancies,
- and the effect on margin, capacity, and investment headroom.

If the “no-closure” path is materially weaker, say so clearly. If it is sufficient, then we should prefer it. But we need numbers, not just pressure.

## To the works council and the union

I hear your red lines. I also hear your willingness to discuss capacity reduction, flexibility, redeployment, and contribution. That is the right part of your message.

But I reject the idea that the mere assertion of “no closures” and “no layoffs” is enough to solve a structural competitiveness problem.

If you want the company to preserve its German industrial base, then you need to engage with the size of the adjustment required. That means accepting that:
- some capacity reduction is unavoidable,
- some workforce reduction may be unavoidable,
- and the burden cannot be avoided by rhetoric alone.

What I would consider supportable:
- voluntary exit programmes,
- early retirement,
- internal mobility,
- qualification and redeployment,
- working-time flexibility,
- temporary and conditional employee contributions,
- and binding investment commitments.

What I would not support:
- a deal that preserves too much excess capacity,
- a deal that produces only cosmetic savings,
- or a deal that leaves management unable to restore margins.

## To the regional state

I respect the political role and the special governance structure. But I need to be clear: co-determination and blocking rights are not instruments for freezing an uncompetitive structure indefinitely.

If the state wants to protect the industrial base, it has to support a viable industrial base, not an uneconomic one. That may mean accepting difficult adjustments if they are the price of long-term survival.

## Bottom line

My position is this:

- I support a negotiated restructuring.
- I support serious employee contributions if they are part of a broader settlement.
- I support avoiding closures and compulsory redundancies **if** a quantified alternative truly delivers the required savings and capacity reduction.
- I do **not** support a blanket refusal to consider either if that blocks a viable turnaround.
- I will not back any package that preserves value-destroying overcapacity or leaves the company structurally uncompetitive.

So the next step is not more principle statements. It is a quantified comparison of options.

If you want, I can help force that discipline from the supervisory board side. But I will not endorse a settlement that protects comfort over competitiveness.
