I’ll be just as clear as the works council: **we are not negotiating away jobs, sites, and the collective wage framework under threat of closures and forced layoffs.**

## Where I agree
I agree with management, the family holding, and investors on one point: **this company needs a durable restructuring**. A cosmetic fix or endless delay is not acceptable. The market pressure is real, and a negotiated solution is better than a destructive confrontation.

I also agree that the workforce can and should contribute. We have already signaled that in our counter-proposal:
- capacity reduction is discussable,
- working-time tools are discussable,
- qualification and redeployment are discussable,
- and a fair employee contribution is discussable.

That remains true.

## Where I disagree
### 1) Plant closures in Germany are not an acceptable bargaining lever
Management says closures remain “on the table.” I reject that as a starting point.

If you want a negotiated settlement, you do **not** begin by threatening to shut German plants and then asking the workforce to pay for the privilege of avoiding it. That is not restructuring by consensus; that is coercion.

The union will not support a deal that makes closures the price of the collective wage agreement.

### 2) Compulsory redundancies are a red line
Management keeps saying forced redundancies are “not preferred” but possible. That is not enough.

We will not accept a package that puts compulsory redundancies on the table as a normal adjustment tool. The adjustment must come first through:
- working-time reductions,
- internal redeployment,
- qualification,
- natural attrition,
- voluntary exits,
- and socially responsible early-retirement or phased-transition instruments.

If the company insists on compulsory redundancies, then it is refusing the shared-sacrifice logic it keeps talking about.

### 3) The burden cannot fall mainly on workers
I also disagree with the way “competitiveness” is being used here.

Yes, the company needs cost relief. But **fairness matters**. Workers cannot be asked to absorb wage pressure, job insecurity, and loss of guarantees while management and owners preserve their position untouched. If there is a real crisis, then everyone contributes:
- management through operational changes and accountability,
- owners through a realistic return expectation,
- and labour through a negotiated contribution tied to concrete job and site security.

That is the only basis for a durable settlement.

## To management
You say you need a “structural and sustainable reduction in cost and capacity.” Then show us the package that does that **without closures and without compulsory redundancies**.

If you cannot do that, then your claim is not a necessity claim; it is a preference for shifting risk onto employees.

Bring forward:
- quantified savings,
- a clear industrial plan,
- investment commitments for future products in Germany,
- and a binding no-forced-layoff framework.

Without that, there is no basis for a wage concession.

## To the works council
We are aligned on the core red lines: no closures, no forced layoffs, no one-sided burden-sharing. That is good.

Where I would sharpen the position is this: we should not let management split “job security” from “collective bargaining.” The collective agreement is part of the protection framework. If it is weakened, workers lose twice: first through restructuring pressure, then through wage erosion.

So any employee contribution must be:
- time-limited,
- conditional,
- and explicitly linked to binding guarantees on jobs, sites, and investment.

## To the regional state
Your blocking position matters, and I expect it to be used. But I want clarity: if you say no closures and no forced redundancies, then that should not just be political language. It should translate into governance pressure on management to present a real alternative.

The state should not help create a false choice between “closures” and “nothing.” There are alternatives:
- capacity reduction,
- internal flexibility,
- product commitments,
- qualification,
- voluntary transition models,
- and negotiated labour-cost relief.

Use your position to force that package onto the table.

## To the family holding and investors
You keep saying “durable competitiveness” and “long-term value.” Fine. Then prove that your definition of value does not mean immediate labor cutbacks at the expense of the company’s social and industrial base.

A settlement that destroys trust, triggers conflict, and hollows out the workforce is not value-preserving. It is value-destructive.

If you want labour to contribute, then owners and management must contribute too:
- by dropping closure threats,
- by giving binding investment security,
- and by accepting that the workforce is a partner, not a cost item to be carved out.

## What we can live with
We are prepared to discuss a package along these lines:
- no German plant closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- protection of the collective wage framework,
- temporary and fair employee contribution,
- capacity reduction through internal means,
- working-time flexibility,
- qualification and redeployment,
- voluntary exit instruments only,
- and binding investment and site commitments.

That is a serious deal. It is not a giveaway. It is a fair settlement under pressure.

## What we will not accept
- closures as a threat strategy,
- compulsory redundancies,
- open-ended insecurity,
- wage cuts without reciprocity,
- or a restructuring where employees carry the pain while management and owners keep all options.

If that is the direction management insists on, then the workforce will not wait quietly. The peace obligation has ended, and we have already shown we are prepared to act. We prefer a settlement — but not at any price.

If management wants progress, it needs to stop talking in ultimatums and put a real alternative on the table.
