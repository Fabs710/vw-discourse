I will be equally direct.

The state understands the pressure on this company. We are not denying the need for restructuring, and we are not defending inefficiency for its own sake. But I will not allow “credibility” to become a synonym for **German closures and compulsory redundancies**. That is not the only serious path, and it is certainly not the one we can accept as a first choice.

## Where I agree
I agree with management, the family holding, and investors on one point: the company needs a **durable, quantified restructuring**.  
I also agree with labour that:
- internal flexibility must be used,
- qualification and redeployment matter,
- voluntary transitions matter,
- and a negotiated solution is far better than a strike spiral.

So there is common ground on the need for change.

## Where I disagree
### 1) German plant closures are not acceptable as a starting point
Management keeps saying closures “cannot be ruled out.” The family and investors say the same. I reject that framing.

For this company, with its special governance, its role in the regional economy, and its long-standing social partnership, closures in Germany are **last resort territory**. They are not a bargaining chip. They are not a default adjustment tool. And they are certainly not something to float before all serious alternatives have been tested.

If management believes a closure is unavoidable, then it must prove that no alternative package can deliver comparable savings and capacity reduction. That proof has not been delivered.

### 2) Compulsory redundancies are the wrong adjustment method
The same applies to forced layoffs.

Saying they are “not preferred” is not enough. Once they are admitted as a routine fallback, the burden is simply pushed onto the workforce after years of concessions and trust erosion. That is politically and socially unacceptable, and it would damage the company’s long-term standing in the regions where it operates.

I can support:
- voluntary exits,
- early retirement,
- phased transitions,
- redeployment,
- qualification,
- and internal capacity reduction.

I cannot support a restructuring that makes compulsory redundancies a normal part of the package.

### 3) A blanket 2030 guarantee cannot be dismissed out of hand
Labour is right that security is needed if employees are to contribute. Management is right that a fake guarantee would be worthless. But that does not mean job and site security are off the table.

If the company wants a labour contribution, it must offer something real in return:
- no German plant closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- protection of co-determination,
- and binding investment commitments for future products and technologies in Germany.

That is not an ideological demand. It is the basis of trust.

## On the “numbers first, guarantees later” argument
I understand the call for quantified alternatives. We also want numbers. But numbers alone do not settle the matter.

If management brings a scenario that shows a residual gap, my first question is:
- What is the exact gap?
- Why can it not be closed by internal measures, investment commitments, management action, and owner contribution?
- Why is the answer still immediately jobs and sites?

If the remaining shortfall is small, then closures and forced redundancies are not the only rational answer — they are the easiest answer. I am not prepared to accept that.

## To management
Bring forward a **fully quantified alternative scenario** that shows:
- the cost reduction,
- the capacity reduction,
- the headcount effects,
- the labour contribution,
- and the investment path.

But understand this clearly: a scenario that depends on German closures and compulsory redundancies is not the only serious benchmark. You still have to prove that the non-destructive route fails before you can insist on the destructive one.

## To the works council and the union
I hear your red lines, and I share the core of them:
- no closures,
- no forced layoffs,
- no one-sided burden shifting.

That is not obstruction. That is responsible stewardship of a regional industrial base.

At the same time, I want to be honest with you: if we want to keep the industrial footprint in the state, we must be prepared to discuss real adjustments in working practices, capacity, and contribution. A pure defense posture will not be enough.

But those contributions must be matched by genuine security. I will not support wage concessions or flexibility measures if closures and redundancies remain live threats.

## To the family holding and investors
You speak of long-term value. I agree that the company must be viable. But long-term value is not preserved by hollowing out the industrial base or triggering a conflict that poisons labour relations for years.

A company that breaks faith with its workforce may improve a short-term financial model. It also weakens its own ability to produce, adapt, and recruit in the long term.

If you want labour to contribute, then you must also accept that value is created by:
- stable industrial relations,
- retained skills,
- predictable production,
- and co-determined governance.

## What the state would support
We could support a restructuring that contains all of the following:
- no German plant closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- capacity reduction through internal and negotiated measures,
- a fair employee contribution,
- binding investment commitments for German sites and future products,
- and protection of co-determination and the state’s governance role.

That is a serious package. It is not a refusal to change. It is a refusal to destroy the company’s regional anchor function in the name of speed.

## My position, plainly
- I support a negotiated restructuring.
- I support serious employee contributions if they are part of a balanced settlement.
- I support a quantified comparison of options.
- I do **not** support pre-committing to closures or forced layoffs.
- I will not back any package that guts regional employment or weakens the company’s role in the state.

If management wants the state’s support, it must come back with a plan that is economically credible **and** socially sustainable. If it cannot, then it should not expect us to help force through a solution that undermines the company’s future as a regional economic anchor.
