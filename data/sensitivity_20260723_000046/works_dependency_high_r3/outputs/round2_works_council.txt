Management and the capital side keep repeating the same word — “credible” — while avoiding the real question: **credible for whom, and at whose expense?**

Let me be absolutely clear in round two:

## Where we agree
- Yes, the company faces serious competitive pressure.
- Yes, restructuring is necessary.
- Yes, a negotiated solution is better than an open industrial war.
- Yes, internal flexibility, qualification, redeployment, and capacity adjustments are legitimate tools.

That is not in dispute.

## Where we fundamentally disagree
### 1) German plant closures are not an acceptable option
I heard management, the family holding, and investors all say closures “cannot be ruled out.”  
I reject that framing.

This company is not a spreadsheet. German plants are not disposable assets to be shut once management decides the cost base is inconvenient. A closure is not just a line item; it destroys skills, communities, supply chains, and trust in the company’s entire social contract.

We will not accept German plant closures — not as a first move, not as a bargaining chip, and not as a disguised threat to force concessions.

If management claims there is no alternative, then it must **prove it with a fully quantified alternative plan** before even talking about closures. So far, that proof has not been delivered.

### 2) Compulsory redundancies are also unacceptable
The same applies to forced layoffs.

Management and capital keep saying they are “not preferred” but remain on the table. That is not good enough. Once you open the door to compulsory redundancies, the burden will be dumped on the workforce after years of broken trust.

We will not agree to a restructuring that tells thousands of workers: “You are the adjustment variable.”

No. The workforce has already shown willingness to contribute. But that contribution must be through:
- internal redeployment,
- qualification,
- working-time solutions,
- voluntary transitions,
- early retirement and phased exits,
- and negotiated capacity reduction.

Not forced layoffs.

### 3) A blanket attack on job guarantees through 2030 is unacceptable
Management says a guarantee would “freeze overcapacity.” That is a false choice.

A job and site guarantee is exactly what is needed to restore trust after management has already broken historic assurances. Without it, any “negotiation” is just a demand for concessions under threat.

If you want employee contribution, then you must offer something real in return:
- **binding no-closure commitments for all German plants,**
- **no compulsory redundancies,**
- **preservation of co-determination rights,**
- and **clear investment commitments for future products and technologies in Germany**.

Without that, there is nothing to negotiate.

## On the proposed trade-offs
### To management
You say you need a “durable reduction in cost and capacity.” Fine. Then bring forward the numbers and show me a version that achieves that **without plant closures and without forced layoffs**.

Do not hide behind generalities about competitiveness. If you want labour to move, then you must show:
- how much capacity you need to remove,
- from where,
- by what non-destructive means,
- and how jobs will be secured through 2030.

If you cannot do that, then your closure argument is not a necessity argument — it is a power argument.

### To the family holding and investors
You both talk about “long-term value.” Then stop treating the workforce as the thing to be cut first and asked questions later.

Long-term value depends on:
- stable industrial relations,
- retained skills,
- production know-how,
- social legitimacy,
- and a workforce that still trusts the company.

Destroying plants and forcing redundancies may improve a short-term financial model. It can also destroy the company’s future ability to produce, adapt, and recruit.

So no, I do not accept your claim that closures are merely a neutral economic tool. They are a profound strategic damage.

### To the regional state
I appreciate your support for no closures and no forced redundancies. But let me be equally direct: your blocking minority and governance role are only meaningful if you use them to defend the industrial base, not to stand aside while management tears it apart.

The state should make clear now:
- no German plant closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- and no stripping back of co-determination.

That is the minimum for any legitimate restructuring.

## What we are prepared to discuss
We are not refusing change. We are refusing destruction.

A serious package could include:
- capacity reduction through internal measures,
- reductions in working time,
- qualification and redeployment,
- voluntary exit instruments only,
- a fair and limited employee contribution,
- investment guarantees for German sites,
- and a binding job-security framework through 2030.

But let me stress: **an employee contribution is not a blank cheque.**  
There will be no wage sacrifice and no flexibility concessions if the company insists on keeping closures and layoffs on the table.

## Bottom line
The works council’s position remains unchanged:
- **No German plant closures.**
- **No compulsory redundancies.**
- **Binding site and job guarantees through 2030.**
- **Preserve co-determination and the job-security framework.**

If management and the capital side want a real settlement, they must come back with a plan that respects those conditions. Otherwise, they are not negotiating — they are escalating.

And if that is their choice, then they should not be surprised by the response from the workforce.
