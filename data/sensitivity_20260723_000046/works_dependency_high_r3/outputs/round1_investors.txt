We agree with the core diagnosis: this company needs a **durable** restructuring, not a symbolic one. The cost base cannot remain structurally above competitors’, and if margins stay weak, the market will punish the equity and constrain future investment.

Where we **agree**:
- Management is right that the current structure must change.
- The family holding is right that vague, politically comfortable solutions are not enough.
- A negotiated outcome is preferable to prolonged conflict and strike damage.
- Capacity reduction, working-time adjustments, redeployment, and qualification should all be part of the toolkit.

Where we **disagree** is on the acceptable route.

### On plant closures
We are **not ideologically attached** to any individual plant. But closures should not be the default answer, and they should not be used to force through a faster but inferior settlement. If management can produce a package that delivers the same durable savings and capacity adjustment through alternative measures, that is clearly better from a risk-adjusted value perspective.

That said, if the only alternatives are cosmetic and leave the cost base uncompetitive, then a refusal to close anything would simply preserve overcapacity and delay the value destruction. So we are not saying “never”; we are saying **prove necessity and durability**.

### On compulsory redundancies
Compulsory redundancies are highly undesirable because they increase disruption, litigation risk, execution risk, and strike risk. But we will not treat them as taboo if they are part of a genuinely credible reset. What matters to us is not the method in the abstract; it is whether the result materially improves margins and competitiveness.

### On job guarantees through 2030
A blanket guarantee is only acceptable if it does not freeze the company into an uncompetitive structure. We are not prepared to back a settlement that locks in today’s overcapacity and then expects shareholders to fund the gap. Security for employees has to be earned through a credible industrial plan, not promised irrespective of economics.

### On the union and works council position
We respect the insistence on negotiation, but the red lines as stated — no closures, no compulsory redundancies — are too rigid if they prevent a real turnaround. If the workforce wants protection, then it must be part of a package that actually restores value, not one that shifts the burden entirely onto capital.

### On the state’s role
We understand the political constraints, but the state’s interest as anchor shareholder should not be to preserve every site at any price. Its role should be to support a viable enterprise, not an uneconomic footprint. If governance becomes a mechanism to block necessary change, that hurts the company’s valuation and investment case.

### Our bottom line
We will support **whatever credibly restores competitive margins**, provided it is:
- quantified,
- enforceable,
- durable,
- and not merely a postponement of the underlying problem.

We are **not** demanding closures as a slogan. But we are equally **not** prepared to back a package that protects all current structures while leaving the cost base structurally impaired.

If management comes back with a plan that demonstrably closes the margin gap and reduces overcapacity in a lasting way — whether via closures or sufficiently strong alternatives — we are open. If not, we will view it as value-destructive delay.
