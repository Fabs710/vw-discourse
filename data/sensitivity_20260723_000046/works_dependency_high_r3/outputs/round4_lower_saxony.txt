I will be equally direct.

## Where I agree
I agree with management, the expert, and even parts of the capital side on one essential point: **this company needs a durable restructuring, not a cosmetic pause**.  
I also agree that:
- the cost base is too high,
- internal flexibility, qualification, redeployment, and voluntary exits must be used,
- and a quantified comparison of options is overdue.

So yes: numbers, not slogans.

## Where I disagree
### 1) German plant closures are not the first answer
Management keeps speaking as if closures are the natural benchmark of seriousness. I reject that framing.

For this company, in this region, with this governance structure, **closures are last resort territory**. They are not the starting point of a credible social settlement. If you want them to be considered, you must first demonstrate that a serious non-closure package cannot close the gap.

That proof has not yet been provided.

### 2) Compulsory redundancies are not an acceptable default fallback
The same applies to forced layoffs.  
I understand the logic of “if the numbers do not work, something harder may be needed.” But that cannot become a blanket permission to shift the burden onto workers after years of concessions.

From the state’s perspective, compulsory redundancies are especially damaging because they do not just affect individuals; they destabilize the regional economy, supplier networks, and trust in the company’s social partnership model.

So I am not moving on this point: **no mass forced redundancies as a routine restructuring tool**.

### 3) A guarantee through 2030 is not a fantasy if it is tied to a real plan
Management and investors keep treating job and site security as if it were incompatible with competitiveness. That is too simplistic.

A binding no-closure, no-forced-redundancy framework can be legitimate **if** it is paired with:
- measurable employee contributions,
- binding investment commitments,
- product allocation for German sites,
- and a clear, monitored path back to margin.

A guarantee is not a substitute for economics. But neither is it a mere “nice-to-have.” It is the condition that makes employee contribution politically and socially possible.

### 4) Co-determination is not a veto on change — but it is a veto on being sidelined
I agree with management that co-determination is not a license for stagnation.  
I do **not** agree that it should be treated as a procedural obstacle to be worked around.

The special statute, our shareholding, and the supervisory-board position exist for a reason: this company is not just a financial asset. It is an industrial anchor. Decisions that alter sites, jobs, and regional value creation must be negotiated within that governance reality, not imposed over it.

## To management
Bring the quantified side-by-side comparison, but do it honestly:
- What savings come from working time, redeployment, and voluntary exits?
- What savings come from overhead and product allocation?
- What is the residual gap after non-destructive measures?
- And why exactly is that residual gap so large that closures or compulsory redundancies must enter the discussion?

If the residual gap is small, then I will not support irreversible measures.  
If it is large, then I still expect you to explain why your own strategic choices are not part of the problem.

## To the works council and union
I hear your red lines, and I share the core concern: **the workforce must not pay for management failure by losing sites and jobs first**.

But I also have to be honest with you: a pure “no” posture is not enough. If we want the state to use its blocking power responsibly, we need a package that is economically credible.

That means:
- internal capacity reduction,
- working-time flexibility,
- redeployment and qualification,
- voluntary exits and phased retirement,
- and a defined employee contribution.

I will support that. What I will not support is a settlement where the state is asked to defend the industrial base while the economics remain unproven.

## To the controlling family and investors
You are right on one thing: **cosmetic compromise is not acceptable**.

But I also want to caution against treating closures as if they were automatically the cleanest answer. They are not. They are expensive, socially destructive, and politically destabilizing. If a no-closure route can materially close the gap, it is the better long-term solution.

So yes, I support a serious comparison. No, I do not support making closure the default truth before the comparison is done.

## What I would support
I would support a settlement with these elements:
- a fully quantified no-closure scenario first,
- meaningful employee contributions,
- binding investment and product commitments,
- clear review points and milestones,
- preservation of co-determination,
- and a strong presumption against German plant closures and compulsory redundancies.

That is not weakness. That is how you protect a regional industrial anchor without pretending competitiveness does not matter.

## What I will not support
I will not support:
- plant closures treated as inevitable,
- compulsory redundancies as a routine fallback,
- a deal that guts regional employment,
- or a restructuring that weakens the state’s co-determination and veto role.

Those are not bargaining chips for me. They are the core of my stake in this company.

## Bottom line
I remain open to a negotiated restructuring. But it must meet a simple test:

**Can the company regain competitiveness while preserving the German industrial base, jobs, and co-determination?**

If yes, I am a constructive partner.  
If no, then I will use the leverage that the statute and our shareholding give us.

I support the moderator’s and expert’s next step: a **hard quantified comparison** of:
1. no-closure / no-forced-redundancy,
2. mixed compromise,
3. closure-based restructuring.

But I say this clearly: the burden is not on the state to prove that closures are unnecessary. The burden is on management to prove that they are unavoidable. Until then, I will not treat closures as an acceptable baseline.
