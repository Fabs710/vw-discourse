Yes. From an industrial-footprint perspective, the right way to test this is to separate **what can plausibly be fixed by internal measures** from **what requires physical footprint reduction**.

## Short answer
A **no-closure package can plausibly close a meaningful part of the gap** if the problem is mainly:
- labor cost above benchmark,
- excess indirect overhead,
- shift imbalance / underutilization,
- and some product-allocation inefficiency.

But if the gap is driven materially by **structural underutilization of plant capacity** and a persistently weak fixed-cost absorption base, then **some footprint reduction becomes mathematically hard to avoid**. In that case, no-closure measures can reduce the gap, but not fully erase it without unrealistic assumptions.

## The key arithmetic
The decisive formula is:

**Unit cost = Variable cost + Fixed cost / volume**

Non-closure measures can reduce:
- variable labor cost,
- overhead,
- working-time cost,
- and some SG&A / plant support costs.

But they do **not** remove the fixed-cost denominator problem if volume stays low. If the company has too much capacity for its demand, then even a good labor deal may still leave:
- too many plants,
- too much indirect structure,
- too much depreciation / maintenance burden,
- too many shift patterns for the actual throughput.

That is why in automotive restructuring, **utilization** is usually the hardest constraint.

## What can be closed without closure
A no-closure package can be economically credible if it includes several of these together:

- **wage moderation / temporary concessions**
- **working-time reductions or flexibility**
- **redeployment across sites**
- **voluntary exits / phased retirement**
- **overhead cuts**
- **product and volume reallocations**
- **shift rationalization**
- **supplier and logistics simplification**
- **investment to improve productivity**

If those measures are large enough, they can often close **a substantial share** of the recurring gap.

## What usually cannot be closed without footprint action
If the following are true, then non-closure measures usually hit a ceiling:

1. **Persistent utilization is too low**
   - e.g. plants or lines operating far below efficient thresholds.
2. **Model allocation is fragmented**
   - too many variants and too little scale per site.
3. **Fixed costs are structurally high**
   - the network was built for a larger volume base.
4. **The market has shrunk durably**
   - so the old capacity will not come back.
5. **EV transition reduces labor content but not network costs proportionally**
   - making old footprint economics worse.

In that case, keeping all plants open may preserve jobs today but still leave the company with a cost base that remains above target.

## A practical rule of thumb
For a large automotive assembly network, if after all internal measures you still have:
- **materially underutilized plants**, and
- a **residual recurring gap** that requires permanent concessions year after year,

then the problem is no longer just labor cost. It is **footprint economics**.

At that point, management can maybe avoid closures temporarily, but it is hard to argue the gap is truly closed.

## What I would need to determine sufficiency
To answer this rigorously, I would need:
- site-by-site utilization,

- labor hours per vehicle,

Without that, nobody can honestly say whether:
- the no-closure route is enough,
- a mixed compromise is enough,
- or closures are structurally required.

## Bottom line
So the answer is:

- **Plausibly yes**, a non-closure package can close the gap **if** the gap is mostly labor/overhead/productivity related.
- **Not usually**, if the gap is mostly **capacity and fixed-cost absorption related**.
- In the latter case, **some footprint reduction is structurally hard to avoid** unless the company accepts a permanently weaker margin profile or assumes unrealistically strong volume recovery.
