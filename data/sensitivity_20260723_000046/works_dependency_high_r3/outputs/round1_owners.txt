I will be direct.

I agree with everyone on one central point: **this company needs a durable restructuring, not another round of postponement.** The market reality is real. Costs are too high, capacity is too large, and the brand must recover margin if it is to fund future technology and remain globally competitive.

But I also need to be equally clear about the boundary from the owners’ side: **we will not support a solution that preserves the status quo at the expense of the company’s long-term value.** If the package does not materially improve competitiveness and margins, it is not a restructuring. It is delay.

### To management
I support the direction of a structural reset, but I need stronger proof that the package is not just a demand for labor concessions in general terms.

What I would consider credible:
- a quantified cost and capacity plan,
- a clear path to restoring returns,
- real savings from operations, not only headcount reduction,
- and a structure that is durable enough to survive beyond one bargaining round.

What I do **not** support:
- vague references to competitiveness without hard numbers,
- a plan that depends entirely on plant closures as the first answer,
- or a confrontation strategy that creates prolonged instability and reputational damage.

If plant closures are genuinely unavoidable, that is a serious matter and cannot be ruled out by slogan. But they should not be treated as the only lever before all economically serious alternatives are tested.

### To the works council and the union
I hear the red lines, but I do not accept them as absolute if they make the company structurally uncompetitive.

A blanket “no closures, no compulsory redundancies, no deep change” is not a viable long-term position if the business model no longer supports today’s footprint. That would protect today’s sites while weakening tomorrow’s company — and that would ultimately hurt everyone, including the workforce and the regions.

That said, I do see value in:
- capacity reductions without unnecessary social disruption,
- redeployment and qualification,
- working-time solutions,
- and a negotiated employee contribution if it is part of a genuine overall settlement.

What I will not support is a one-sided approach where owners absorb all the value destruction while labor costs and excess capacity remain untouched.

### To the regional state
I understand the political responsibility and the importance of the industrial base. But I need to say plainly: **ownership control and governance integrity cannot be traded away for short-term political comfort.**

We can discuss a socially balanced restructuring. We cannot endorse a governance deadlock that prevents the company from adapting to a changed market. Co-determination is a reality, yes — and a serious one. But it must not become a mechanism for freezing an uncompetitive structure in place.

### My position
The family holding will support a settlement only if it achieves all three of these things:
1. **durable competitiveness and margin improvement,**
2. **a credible, enforceable reduction in overcapacity and cost,**
3. **stability that avoids prolonged destructive conflict.**

That means I am open to negotiated alternatives to closures and forced redundancies — but only if they are quantitatively sufficient and durable. If the alternative package is too weak, then I will not endorse it just because it is easier politically.

So my message is simple:

- I am **not** here to defend every existing site regardless of economics.
- I am **not** here to accept a cosmetic compromise that delays the problem.
- I am **open** to a serious negotiated restructuring that restores competitiveness.
- And I will support the board and management only if the outcome protects the long-term enterprise value of the company.

Bring forward a package that is economically real, not merely socially comfortable. If it is credible, I will back it. If it is not, I will not help bless it.
