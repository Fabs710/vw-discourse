## Where I agree
I agree with everyone on the basic diagnosis:
- this company needs a **durable restructuring now**,
- a cosmetic deal will fail,
- internal flexibility, qualification, redeployment, working-time tools, voluntary exits, and phased retirement all matter,
- and a negotiated settlement is preferable to a prolonged strike cycle.

I also agree that numbers matter. I welcome the call for a quantified comparison.

## Where I fundamentally disagree

### 1) “No closures” is not a strategy
The works council and union keep repeating red lines as if repetition creates economics.

It does not.

If you want me to rule out closures, then you need to show a package that delivers the **same durable reduction in cost and capacity**. So far, what I hear is:
- no closures,
- no forced redundancies,
- no real acceptance of structural pain.

That is not a plan. That is a refusal to face the scale of the problem.

I am not prepared to pretend that preserving every German site automatically preserves competitiveness. It does not.

### 2) “No compulsory redundancies” cannot be an absolute if the numbers do not work
I understand why the workforce wants security. But an absolute ban on compulsory redundancies, regardless of whether the company reaches a viable cost base, is not responsible industrial policy.

If voluntary exits, redeployment, early retirement, and working-time measures are sufficient, then great — use them.  
If they are not sufficient, then refusing harder instruments means preserving overcapacity and passing the bill to the future.

That is not fairness. It is delay.

### 3) A blanket job guarantee through 2030 cannot freeze competitiveness
A broad 2030 guarantee sounds attractive, but it is only credible if the industrial plan underneath it actually works.

If it locks in today’s footprint while leaving factory costs structurally uncompetitive, then it will not protect jobs — it will undermine them later by weakening the company’s ability to invest, launch products, and earn returns.

Security cannot be detached from viability.

### 4) Co-determination is not a veto on economic reality
To the state and labour: yes, governance matters. I respect that. But co-determination does not eliminate the need for hard choices. It means hard choices must be negotiated, not that they can be ruled out in advance.

If a site is structurally uncompetitive, I will not pretend otherwise because the answer is politically difficult.

If the package works, the review will confirm it.  
If it does not, then we must have the ability to escalate. That is not cruelty. It is management discipline.

That I will not do.

So I expect the state to support a quantified process, not a political veto that freezes the outcome regardless of the evidence.

If the no-closure route succeeds, great. If it fails, then it is irresponsible to block the next step.
