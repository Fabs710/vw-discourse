We agree on the destination, but not yet on the route.

## What we agree with
We agree with management, the family holding, the state, and even labour on one essential point: **this company needs a durable restructuring that restores competitive margins**. A cosmetic deal, a prolonged stalemate, or a strike-driven drift would destroy value.

We also agree that:
- internal flexibility should be used,
- voluntary exits are preferable to blunt forced measures,
- qualification and redeployment matter,
- and any settlement must be **quantified, credible, and durable**.

That is where our agreement starts and, for now, mostly ends.

## Where we disagree

### 1) No closures cannot be an absolute rule
To the works council and union: we understand why you insist on this. But from an investor perspective, **a blanket exclusion of German plant closures is not acceptable if the economics do not work**.

If a non-closure package can genuinely close the gap, we would prefer that route. But if it cannot, then ruling out closures in advance is not “social responsibility” — it is a decision to preserve overcapacity and accept structurally impaired margins.

We will not back a settlement that locks in an uncompetitive footprint.

### 2) No compulsory redundancies is only acceptable if the alternative is strong enough
We prefer voluntary exits, attrition, redeployment, and working-time adjustments. But an absolute ban on compulsory redundancies is only defensible if the non-destructive package is actually sufficient.

If it is not sufficient, then the company is simply postponing the problem and leaving shareholders to absorb the cost of delay.

That is not acceptable to us.

### 3) A 2030 guarantee is not valuable if it freezes decline
The repeated demand for job and site guarantees through 2030 sounds reassuring, but security without competitiveness is illusory.

If a guarantee prevents the company from reaching a viable cost base, it does not protect jobs — it delays the point at which jobs become vulnerable anyway. We will not support a deal that buys short-term peace at the expense of medium-term value destruction.

### 4) “Credibility” must mean real fixed-cost reduction, not just labor peace
Management is right that a serious restructuring must reduce cost and capacity. But capital cannot endorse “credibility” as a synonym for threatening closures before testing alternatives.

At the same time, labour cannot define credibility as “no closures, no layoffs, no pain.” That is not a financial plan. It is a refusal to confront the fixed-cost problem.

## What the expert input means to us
The expert did **not** prove that closures are necessary. But they did establish the critical point: **non-closure measures may close a large share of the gap, but not automatically the full structural cost gap if utilization remains weak**.

That means the burden is now on management and labour to show, in numbers, whether the non-closure route is enough.

We support that test. We do **not** support pre-committing to a conclusion that leaves the company structurally uncompetitive.

## Our position on each party

### To management
Bring the full comparison:
- no-closure scenario,
- mixed compromise,
- closure scenario.

We need recurring savings, one-off costs, headcount effects, capacity effects, investment needs, and the margin path. Not slogans. Not threats.

But do not assume that closures are automatically the right answer either. They are expensive, risky, and destructive if used unnecessarily. Prove the case.

### To the works council
You keep saying closures are destruction. We do not deny the pain. But preserving every site regardless of utilization can also destroy value, just more slowly.

If you want investors to support a no-closure deal, show us that it closes the recurring gap and not just the political gap.

### To the union
We agree that any employee contribution must be reciprocal. But reciprocity cannot mean that workers give concessions while the company remains structurally overbuilt.

If you want support for a no-closure package, it must produce a materially lower cost base. Otherwise, it is just a transfer of burden.

### To the regional state
Your role as anchor shareholder matters. But governance should be used to force a viable solution, not to freeze the current structure.

If a non-closure package works, support it. If it does not, do not use your blocking power to defend value destruction.

### To the family holding
We agree with your insistence on durability. But durability is not the same as preserving every legacy site. It means choosing the least destructive package that actually restores competitiveness.

## What would move us
We would support a deal that includes:
- meaningful employee contributions,
- working-time and flexibility changes,
- voluntary exits and early retirement,
- redeployment and qualification,
- binding investment commitments,
- and a credible, quantified path back to target margins.

If that can be done **without** closures and forced redundancies, fine — that is the preferred outcome.

If it cannot, then we will not endorse a settlement that leaves the cost base uncompetitive just to avoid hard choices.

## Bottom line
Our position is straightforward:

- **No cosmetic compromise.**
- **No deal that leaves margins structurally impaired.**
- **No settlement that asks shareholders to fund permanent overcapacity.**
- **Yes to the least destructive package that actually restores competitiveness.**

So the next step is not more rhetoric. It is the quantified comparison.
