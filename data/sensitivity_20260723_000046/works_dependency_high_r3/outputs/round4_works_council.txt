I will be direct, because the situation is too serious for euphemisms.

## Where I agree
I agree with one thing from almost everyone at the table: **the company needs a durable restructuring**.  
I also agree that:
- internal flexibility matters,
- qualification and redeployment matter,
- working-time instruments matter,
- voluntary exits are legitimate,
- and numbers matter.

I am not defending the status quo for its own sake. I am defending the workforce from a restructuring that would turn “competitiveness” into a euphemism for **closing plants and forcing people out**.

## Where I fundamentally disagree

### 1) Management keeps treating closures as if they are a normal option
They are not normal. They are irreversible destruction.

The CEO and the capital side keep speaking as if the only credible restructuring is one that begins from closure threats. I reject that completely. If you want credibility, **show that the gap can be closed without shutting German plants**. Until then, closure is not “necessary”; it is an unproven preference.

And let me be very clear: **German plant closures remain unacceptable.**

### 2) Compulsory redundancies are still a red line
The same logic applies to forced layoffs.  
Every round of this discussion tries to dress them up as a fallback or a last resort. For the workforce, that is not a technicality. It is the point where management shifts its failure onto employees.

We will not accept a deal where:
- workers give up protections,
- and then management keeps the right to dismiss them anyway.

That is not partnership. That is leverage.

So again: **no compulsory redundancies.**

### 3) “A trial period” is only useful if it is real security, not a pause before the knife
I heard the moderator and the expert talk about a time-bound, quantified package with review points. Fine — but only if that is a genuine settlement and not a delayed threat.

A short trial period that leaves closures and forced layoffs hanging over the workforce is not security. It is blackmail by timetable. We will not sign up to a temporary truce that expires once management decides the pain should resume.

If there is to be a no-closure path, then it must be **binding and durable**, not a conditional pause designed to soften resistance.

---

## On the expert’s argument
The expert is helpful in one respect: they confirm what labour has been saying all along — **the non-closure route has to be tested seriously before anyone starts talking about irreversible measures**.

But the expert also says something management keeps trying to exploit: that some footprint reduction may be “structurally likely” if the gap is fixed-cost driven.

That may be true in the abstract. It is **not a licence** to impose closures or forced layoffs. The question is not whether management can imagine a model where closures are useful. The question is whether it has actually proven that there is no other way.

So I ask the same question again:

- What is the exact cost gap?
- How much comes from labor cost?
- How much comes from management decisions?
- How much comes from product allocation and investment choices?
- Why is the first answer still always the workforce?

If the gap is driven by underinvestment, poor allocation, or bad strategic decisions, then the solution is not to shut plants and fire workers. The solution is to **change management choices**.

---

## To management
You say you want numbers. Fine. Then bring them — but bring the full picture, not a selective spreadsheet that pre-bakes closures as the answer.

I want to see:
- site-by-site utilization,
- fixed-cost structure,
- model allocation,
- investment commitments,
- management contribution,
- owner contribution,
- and the actual savings from non-destructive measures.

Do **not** return with a package that assumes:
- one or more German closures,
- forced redundancies as a fallback,
- and wage restraint from workers as the main adjustment.

That would not be a restructuring. It would be a transfer of risk onto the workforce.

If management insists that only destructive measures are “credible,” then I say: **prove it**. Until then, there is no basis for demanding the sacrifice of plants and jobs.

---

## To the controlling family and investors
You say long-term value matters. Then stop confusing value with short-term financial pain shifting onto labor.

Long-term value is not protected by:
- breaking trust,
- hollowing out German industrial capacity,
- and destroying the co-determined industrial relations model that has actually sustained this company for decades.

If you want workers to contribute, then owners and capital must also contribute:
- through investment,
- through product allocation,
- through management discipline,
- and through guaranteeing that the company does not use restructuring as an excuse to gut the German base.

A company that destroys its workforce’s trust may look “leaner” for one quarter. It is weaker for the next decade.

---

## To the regional state
You keep saying closures are last resort. Good — then act like it.

Your role is not to stand around waiting for management to conclude that the workforce has no alternative. You have a blocking position. Use it.  
If you are serious about protecting the industrial base, then your support must be tied to:
- no German plant closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- binding job and site security,
- and preservation of co-determination and the job-security framework.

Anything less is not stewardship. It is commentary.

---

## To the union
I agree with you on the core issue: employees can contribute, but only if the settlement is fair and reciprocal.

But let me add this: a wage contribution without absolute site and job security is not reciprocity. It is one-sided extraction.

So yes, internal flexibility, working-time change, voluntary exits, qualification, and redeployment are all discussable.  
But only inside a framework that guarantees:
- every German plant stays open,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- and no erosion of co-determination.

---

## What I am prepared to discuss
We are prepared to discuss:
- internal capacity adjustments,
- working-time solutions,
- redeployment,
- qualification,
- voluntary exit programmes,
- phased retirement,
- and limited, negotiated employee contributions.

We are **not** prepared to discuss:
- plant closures,
- forced redundancies,
- or a settlement that leaves the historic job-security framework hollowed out.

That is the line.

---

## Bottom line
The works council’s position remains unchanged and should not be misunderstood:

- **No German plant closures.**
- **No compulsory redundancies.**
- **Binding job and site guarantees.**
- **Preserve co-determination and the job-security framework.**
- **Employee contribution only in exchange for real security, not promises that disappear when convenient.**

If management, the family, investors, or the state want a real deal, they must bring a plan that proves the company can be made competitive **without sacrificing plants and jobs**.

Until then, we will not help turn a restructuring debate into a managed demolition of the German industrial base.
