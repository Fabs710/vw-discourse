I will speak plainly.

The state shares the diagnosis that this company needs a durable restructuring. We are not denying the market reality, and we are not asking management to preserve a model that no longer works. But I will not accept the implication that the only “credible” path is plant closures in Germany and compulsory redundancies. That is not the only serious option; it is simply the most destructive one.

## Where I agree
I agree with management, the family holding, and investors on one point: **we need a quantified, durable solution**. Vague assurances are not enough. The company must regain competitiveness, and it must do so in a way that can survive beyond the next bargaining round.

I also agree that:
- internal flexibility should be used,
- working-time solutions belong on the table,
- redeployment and qualification are necessary,
- and the workforce should contribute if the package is fair and future-oriented.

So I am not defending the status quo for its own sake.

## Where I disagree strongly

### 1) German plant closures are not an acceptable “default” option
Management says closures must remain on the table. The family and investors say they are not ideological about sites. I am not impressed by that formulation.

A closure is not a neutral efficiency measure. It is a permanent break in regional industrial capacity, skills, supply chains, and trust. For a company like this one, with special governance obligations and a role as a regional anchor, closures in Germany are a last-resort matter — not a bargaining chip and not the starting point.

If management believes a closure is unavoidable, then it must first prove, with hard numbers, that **no alternative package can deliver the same durable effect**. That proof has not yet been given here.

### 2) Compulsory redundancies are the wrong instrument
The same applies to forced layoffs. Saying they are “not preferred” is not enough.

Once compulsory redundancies are admitted as a normal tool, the burden is shifted onto the workforce after years of broken trust. That would be politically and socially unacceptable, and it would damage the company’s long-term standing in the regions where it operates.

I can support voluntary exits, early retirement, phased transition, redeployment, and qualification. I cannot support a restructuring that treats compulsory redundancies as an acceptable routine option.

### 3) A blanket attack on job guarantees through 2030 is not acceptable
Management says a site and job guarantee would “freeze overcapacity.” That is too simplistic.

A guarantee is not a gift; it is part of rebuilding trust. If the workforce is asked to contribute, then it must receive something binding in return:
- no German plant closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- protection of co-determination,
- and a credible investment plan for future products and technologies in Germany.

Without that, any “negotiation” becomes a demand for concessions under threat.

## On the burden-sharing question
I also want to be clear with the capital side: fairness matters. If the company needs savings, then labour should not be the only side asked to move. But neither should the answer be to hit the regions and the workforce first because that is administratively easier.

A sustainable settlement has to balance:
- competitiveness,
- social stability,
- industrial capability,
- and long-term value.

That means management must show operational changes, owners must accept that long-term value is not served by a destructive conflict, and labour must be prepared to contribute — but only within a framework that preserves jobs and sites in Germany.

## What the state would support
We could support a restructuring if it contains all of the following:
- no German plant closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- capacity reduction through internal and negotiated measures,
- a fair employee contribution,
- binding investment commitments for German sites and future products,
- and protection of co-determination and the state’s governance role.

That is not a soft position. It is a serious one.

## What I am asking management to do
Management should stop speaking in ultimatums and bring forward a **fully quantified alternative scenario** that achieves the required cost and capacity adjustment **without closures and without forced redundancies**.

If such a package cannot be designed, then say so explicitly and explain why. But do not present closures as the only adult answer before all serious alternatives have been tested.

## To the works council and union
I hear your red lines, and I share the core concern: the burden cannot be dumped on the workforce. I would add one caution, though. If we want to keep every site, we must also be willing to discuss real adjustments in working practices, capacity, and contribution. A position of pure defense will not hold.

But let me be just as clear: that contribution must be matched by genuine security. I will not support wage concessions or flexibility measures if closures and forced redundancies remain live threats.

## To the family holding and institutional investors
You speak of long-term value, and I agree that this company must be viable. But long-term value is not preserved by tearing out the company’s industrial base or by damaging labor relations in a way that makes the brand weaker, not stronger.

The state’s view is that a socially balanced settlement is not a concession to politics; it is a condition of durable competitiveness in this company.

## Bottom line
My position is unchanged:
- **No German plant closures.**
- **No compulsory redundancies.**
- **Yes to negotiated capacity adjustment, flexibility, qualification, and a fair employee contribution.**
- **Yes to binding investment and industrial commitments in Germany.**
- **No to a restructuring that sacrifices regional employment and co-determination as the price of short-term balance-sheet relief.**

If management wants the state’s support, it must bring a plan that is economically credible **and** socially sustainable. If it cannot, then I will not help force through a solution that undermines the company’s role as a regional economic anchor.
