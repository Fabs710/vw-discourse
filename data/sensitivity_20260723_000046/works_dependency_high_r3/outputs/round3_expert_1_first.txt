## Expert assessment: can a non-closure package plausibly close the cost gap?

### Bottom line
Yes, **non-closure measures can sometimes close a large share of a cost gap**, but whether they can close it **fully and durably** depends on the size and structure of the gap.

For a large German carmaker in a mature, overstaffed European footprint, a **credible non-closure package** can often deliver:
- meaningful labor-cost savings,
- some capacity reduction through shifts / product reassignment / footprint rationalization,
- lower overtime and external labor use,
- lower fixed-cost absorption through higher utilization,
- and some one-off cash savings via voluntary exits.

But if management’s gap is driven mainly by **structural plant underutilization, excess fixed manufacturing overhead, and product allocation shortfalls**, then **labor-only measures will usually not be enough**. In that case, avoiding closures may require either:
1. deeper concessions than labor usually accepts,  
2. major new product allocation and investment commitments, or  
3. owner/shareholder support bridging a residual gap.

### What determines the answer
The key question is not “closure or no closure,” but:

- **How large is the annual recurring gap?**
- Is it mostly **labor cost**, or mostly **plant fixed cost / capacity underutilization**?
- Can the company reduce labor and overhead **without losing enough volume that unit costs worsen further**?
- How much of the savings is **cash** versus **accounting**?
- How much can be achieved by **voluntary attrition**, versus requiring compulsory redundancies?
- Can the company reallocate **future EV / platform production** into the German sites to restore utilization?

Without those data, one cannot prove that closures are necessary. But one also cannot prove they are avoidable.

## Economic logic of the trade-off

### 1) Where a no-closure package can work
Non-closure measures are most effective when the problem is primarily:
- wages and benefits above benchmark,
- excessive shifts / overtime / staffing levels,
- duplicated white-collar functions,
- rigid work rules,
- or a temporary demand dip.

Then a package combining:
- wage moderation or temporary pay deferral,
- working-time reductions,
- shorter weeks,
- internal redeployment,
- attrition,
- hiring freeze,
- early retirement,
- voluntary severance,
- and process productivity improvements

can close a large part of the gap.

### 2) Where non-closure measures usually fail
They are much less effective when the problem is:
- a structurally oversupplied plant network,
- low model volume per site,
- obsolete or subscale assets,
- too many fixed-cost plants for the expected EV demand,
- or a product allocation deficit versus foreign competitors.

In that case, the cost gap is not just “too much labor”; it is **too much capacity for the volume base**. Then even aggressive labor measures may still leave:
- low utilization,
- poor absorption of depreciation and overhead,
- high logistics / complexity costs,
- and persistent margin weakness.

That is the classic situation where management pushes for closure because the fixed-cost base cannot be made competitive otherwise.

Potential annual recurring savings:

- with a possibility of additional savings later if utilization improves

This can work if:
- the company gets enough volume assurance,
- labor accepts meaningful but bounded concessions,
- and management can credibly show the residual gap is small enough to bridge.

## Employment effects in the regional economy

### Direct effects
If closures are avoided:
- direct job loss is lower,
- but jobs may still fall through attrition, voluntary exits, and outsourcing reduction.
- The reduction may be **several thousand to low tens of thousands** across a large group depending on the scale of restructuring.

If closures occur:
- direct employment losses are much larger and faster,
- and the regional multiplier effect is severe.

### Indirect effects
A large plant closure typically affects:
- suppliers,
- logistics,
- local services,
- municipal tax base,
- commuting labor markets.

The multiplier can be substantial, especially in auto-intensive regions. In practical terms, **each direct manufacturing job lost can imply additional losses across the regional supplier and service ecosystem**. The exact multiplier depends on local specialization, but the regional shock can be materially larger than the plant headcount reduction.

### Economic interpretation
From a regional employment perspective:
- **no-closure restructuring is clearly preferable** if it achieves most of the cost reduction,
- but if it leaves the company structurally uncompetitive, then it may only delay a larger employment shock later.

So the relevant comparison is not:
- “jobs now versus no jobs now,”

but:
- “how many jobs are preserved over a 3–5 year horizon under each path, after accounting for the firm’s viability.”
