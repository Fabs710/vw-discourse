## Where I agree
I agree with the expert on the core economic point: **a non-closure package can sometimes close a large share of the gap, but not always all of it**. That is the right framing. This is not an ideological closure-versus-no-closure debate; it is about whether the measures on the table actually restore competitiveness.

I also agree with management, the state, and the capital side that:
- the company needs a durable restructuring,
- the current cost base is not acceptable,
- and vague promises are worthless.

And I agree with labour that:
- internal flexibility,
- qualification,
- redeployment,
- voluntary exits,
- and working-time instruments
should be used aggressively before any irreversible step.

## Where I disagree
### 1) “No closures” cannot be an absolute governance veto
I do **not** accept the works council and union position that German plant closures are off limits as a matter of principle.

The expert has confirmed the key point: if the gap is large and fixed-cost driven, non-closure measures may not be enough. In that case, saying “no closures” in advance is not a solution; it is a constraint that may force the company into managed decline.

I am not saying closures are desirable. I am saying they cannot be ruled out if the numbers ultimately require them.

### 2) “No compulsory redundancies” is also not a universal right if the alternative fails
I understand why labour rejects forced layoffs. I would prefer to avoid them. But I will not endorse a deal that simply bans them regardless of whether the company can otherwise reach a viable cost and capacity base.

If voluntary exits, attrition, redeployment, and working-time changes are sufficient, then fine — we should use them.  
If they are not sufficient, then refusing all harder instruments is not responsible stewardship. It shifts the problem forward.

### 3) A blanket 2030 guarantee is too rigid if it locks in overcapacity
I disagree with the idea that a broad job/site guarantee through 2030 should be treated as the starting point.

Security is valuable, but only if it is tied to a viable industrial plan. A guarantee that freezes the footprint while leaving the company uncompetitive would be value-destructive. That is not long-term stewardship; that is postponement.

## What the expert changed — and what it did not
The expert did **not** prove that closures are necessary. That would require actual numbers.  
But the expert **did** prove something important: **a no-closure path is only plausible if the gap is not too large, if utilization can be improved, and if labor contributions are meaningful**.

That means the burden is on management to quantify the gap properly and on labour to show that its alternative closes it.

So I support the moderator’s call for a side-by-side comparison. That is exactly what should happen next.

## My position on each stakeholder

### To management
I support your insistence on quantified alternatives. You are right that “no closures, no forced layoffs” is not a plan by itself.

But I also expect you to stop speaking as if closures are the default truth. They are not. You need to prove:
- the size of the cost gap,
- what non-destructive measures deliver,
- what residual gap remains,
- and whether that residual gap is actually material enough to justify irreversible measures.

If the no-closure scenario is materially close, then you should prefer it. If it is not, then we have to face that honestly.

### To the works council
You are right that labour cannot be the only adjustment variable.  
You are wrong to treat closures and compulsory redundancies as inherently illegitimate in every case.

You keep asking for guarantees first. That is not enough. If you want the family holding and the supervisory board to stand behind a non-closure package, then you need to produce a credible contribution package that closes the economics, not just the principle gap.

### To the union
I agree that wage concessions must be reciprocal and time-limited. I also agree that security matters for legitimacy. But a wage framework cannot become a shield against structural reality.

If the company can be made competitive without closures or forced layoffs, I would support that path.  
If it cannot, then insisting on absolute protection is not a bargain — it is a transfer of risk to owners and the company.

### To the regional state
I appreciate your role and your instinct to protect the industrial base. That is legitimate. But protecting the industrial base means protecting a **viable** industrial base.

I do not support using your governance role to block any serious restructuring. If management comes back with hard numbers showing that a non-closure route works, I would back that. If it does not, I will not support freezing the footprint simply to avoid difficult decisions.

### To institutional investors
You are right on one thing: cosmetic compromise is not acceptable. But I would caution against treating closures as a quick cure. They carry major one-off costs and serious labour-relations and execution risks. If a non-closure path closes most of the gap, it may well be the better value-preserving route.

### To the expert
Your scenario ranges are useful, but they are only ranges. The decisive question is whether this company’s gap is in the “non-closure plausible” range or the “footprint reduction likely needed” range.

That is why I support a quantified comparison immediately.

## What I would support
I would support a settlement with these characteristics:

- a fully quantified no-closure scenario first,
- measurable cost and capacity reduction targets,
- a meaningful employee contribution,
- working-time and flexibility measures,
- voluntary exits, early retirement, redeployment, qualification,
- binding investment commitments for German sites,
- and a fixed review date with trigger points.

If that package demonstrably closes the gap, then I would support it.

## What I would not support
I will not support:
- a package that preserves uncompetitive overcapacity,
- a settlement that is merely symbolic,
- or a scheme that relies on wishful thinking instead of economics.

And I will not support a permanent governance limitation that makes closures or deeper workforce adjustments impossible even if they are ultimately required to protect the company’s value.

## Bottom line
So my position is unchanged but now more precisely grounded:

- **Yes** to a serious no-closure test.
- **Yes** to quantified alternatives.
- **Yes** to non-destructive restructuring if it is sufficient.
- **No** to treating “no closures” and “no forced redundancies” as unconditional absolutes.
- **No** to a deal that leaves the company structurally uncompetitive.

If management can prove the non-closure path works, I would prefer it. If it cannot, then we should stop pretending that principle alone can preserve value.
