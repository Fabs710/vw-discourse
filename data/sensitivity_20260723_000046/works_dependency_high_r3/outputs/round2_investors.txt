We agree on the **diagnosis**: the business needs a durable reset, not cosmetic change. We also agree that prolonged conflict and strike damage would be value-destructive. Where we do **not** agree is on a settlement that protects every current structure while leaving the cost base structurally uncompetitive.

## Where we agree
- Management is right that the margin problem is real.
- The family holding is right that vague promises are not enough.
- Labour-side flexibility tools — redeployment, qualification, working-time adjustments, voluntary exits — are part of any credible package.
- A negotiated solution is preferable to a prolonged confrontation.

## Where we disagree

### 1) “No closures, no compulsory redundancies” cannot be treated as an absolute
I understand the political and social argument, but from a capital-market perspective that is not sufficient. If those red lines prevent the company from reaching a competitive cost base, then they are not a solution — they are a constraint that protects today’s footprint at the expense of tomorrow’s value.

I am **not** attached to plant closures as a principle. But I am also **not** prepared to support a settlement that rules them out regardless of economics.

### 2) Job guarantees are only credible if they do not freeze overcapacity
A blanket guarantee through 2030 is not acceptable if it locks in an uncompetitive structure. Security has to be earned by a package that actually restores margins. Otherwise, it is just an expensive way to delay restructuring.

### 3) “Internal measures first” is fine only if they are quantitatively sufficient
I am open to alternatives to closures and compulsory redundancies. But openness is not approval. Management has to prove, with numbers, that the alternative package delivers the same durable savings and capacity reduction. So far, I have heard a lot of principle and not enough hard evidence.

## What we need to see
I want management to bring forward a **fully quantified comparison** of:
- a closure scenario,
- a no-closure scenario,
- a scenario without compulsory redundancies,
- and the effect of each on margins, capacity, headcount, and investment headroom.

If the no-closure, no-forced-layoff path works, fine — we would prefer the less disruptive route. But if it does **not** close the gap, then blocking closures and layoffs just preserves value destruction.

## Our bottom line
We will support **whatever credibly restores competitive margins**, provided it is:
- durable,
- quantified,
- enforceable,
- and not merely a delay tactic.

What we will **not** support:
- a cosmetic compromise,
- a settlement that leaves the cost base structurally impaired,
- or a package that asks shareholders to fund uncompetitive overcapacity indefinitely.

So the next step is not more rhetoric about red lines. It is hard numbers and a real comparison of options. If the alternative package can truly deliver the necessary reset without closures, we are open. If not, then the refusal to consider harder measures is just shifting the problem forward — and we will not back that.
