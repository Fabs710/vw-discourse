Management is right on one point: this cannot be solved by slogans or by pretending the cost problem does not exist. The company needs a credible restructuring, and the regional state will not deny that reality.

But I have to be equally clear: **any solution that relies on plant closures in Germany, mass job losses, or stripping away the state’s and workforce’s co-determination role is not acceptable.** That would damage not only the people affected, but the company’s role as a regional industrial anchor and its long-term social legitimacy.

### Where I agree
I agree that:
- the company needs a durable cost and capacity adjustment,
- a negotiated solution is preferable to a confrontation,
- working-time, redeployment, qualification, and internal flexibility should be used,
- and the workforce should contribute if the overall package is balanced and future-oriented.

### Where I disagree
I do **not** accept the implication that the only credible path is closures and forced job destruction.

That is too narrow and too risky. It treats the workforce as a cost block to be cut rather than as a strategic asset. It also ignores the political and economic consequences in the regions where these plants are located. A restructuring that tears out the company’s social foundation may look neat on a spreadsheet and still fail in practice.

I also reject the idea that co-determination is a constraint to be bypassed. Under the company’s governance structure, it is part of the decision-making reality. The state’s blocking minority and the board-level veto rights are not symbolic. Any plan that tries to sideline them will not get through.

### What I would support
I could support a restructuring if it meets these conditions:
- **No plant closures in Germany**
- **No compulsory redundancies**
- **A fair, negotiated employee contribution**
- **Capacity reductions through internal measures first**
- **Binding investment commitments for future products and technologies in Germany**
- **Protection of co-determination and the state’s shareholder role**
- **A socially balanced path that preserves the company’s role as a regional anchor**

That does not mean “no change.” It means change must be **managed**, not imposed.

### On management’s case
If management can bring forward a package that demonstrably restores competitiveness **without** closing plants, then I will engage constructively. But the burden of proof is on management. Saying “all options are on the table” is not enough if those options include measures we cannot accept and that would trigger a governance conflict you cannot resolve.

### On the union and works council
I take seriously their willingness to negotiate on flexibility and contribution. That is the right starting point. But I would caution them as well: if the package is framed as pure defense of the status quo, it will not hold. The workforce has to show readiness for a serious contribution — but only in exchange for genuine security and a credible industrial future.

### Bottom line
The state’s position is this: **we are open to restructuring, but not to a solution that sacrifices German plants and jobs as the price of “competitiveness.”** We want a durable settlement that protects the company’s industrial base, preserves regional employment, and keeps the decision within the framework of co-determination and shared responsibility.

If management wants our support, it must come back with a plan that is both economically credible **and** socially acceptable. Otherwise, I will not move.
