I will be equally direct.

## Where I agree
I agree with everyone on the baseline diagnosis:
- the company needs a **durable restructuring now**,
- a cosmetic deal will not work,
- internal flexibility, qualification, redeployment, and working-time tools must be used,
- and a negotiated solution is preferable to a destructive strike cycle.

I also agree that employees should contribute. I am not asking for a one-sided fix.

## Where I disagree — fundamentally

### 1) “No closures, no compulsory redundancies” is not a restructuring strategy
The works council and union keep repeating red lines as if repeating them makes the economics disappear.

It does not.

If you want me to abandon closures and compulsory redundancies, then you need to show me a package that achieves the **same durable cost and capacity reduction**. So far, I have heard refusal, not an alternative that closes the gap.

That is the central issue. I am not negotiating slogans.

### 2) A blanket 2030 job guarantee cannot override competitiveness
I understand the desire for security after trust has been damaged. But a guarantee that freezes the current footprint and prevents real adjustment is exactly how you end up with a company that cannot invest, cannot compete, and cannot protect jobs in the medium term.

Security must be earned by a viable industrial plan. It cannot be granted while the cost base remains structurally too high.

### 3) “No burden on workers” is not realistic
I have not said labour should carry everything. But I will not accept a framework where:
- labour costs stay structurally above target,
- capacity remains too large,
- and management is told to solve the crisis without touching the footprint.

That is managed decline.

### 4) Co-determination is a process, not a veto on reality
To the state and labour: yes, the governance structure matters. I respect that. But co-determination does not eliminate the need for hard choices. It means those choices must be negotiated — not that they can be ruled out in advance.

If a site is structurally uncompetitive, I am not going to pretend otherwise because it is politically uncomfortable.

## What I need to see
If the labour side wants me to move, I need a **fully quantified alternative scenario** that shows:
- how much cost comes out,
- how much capacity comes out,
- how headcount is adjusted,
- what the labour-cost contribution is,
- and how the brand gets back to target margin.

Not principles. Not moral arguments. Numbers.

## On the “trial period” idea
I am open to a time-bound, scenario-based test **only if it is serious**:
- measurable savings targets,
- measurable capacity targets,
- a fixed review date,
- and pre-agreed consequences if the targets are missed.

But I am **not** open to a trial period that simply delays the same fight while overcapacity remains in place.

A pause without a credible fallback is not a solution.

## To the works council
You say closures are destruction. I say preserving uneconomic capacity is also destruction — just slower and more expensive.

You say forced redundancies are a red line. I say then give me a plan that proves we do not need them.

If your package is genuinely strong enough, I am ready to consider it. But right now, it is not sufficient because it does not demonstrate the structural effect required.

## To the union
I hear your insistence on reciprocity. I agree with reciprocity. But reciprocity means both sides move:
- employees on working time, flexibility, and cost contribution,
- management on investment commitments and implementation discipline.

What reciprocity does **not** mean is that the company accepts unlimited job protection regardless of economics.

If you want a binding deal, bring me the numbers that make it viable.

## To the regional state
Your political constraints are real. But your responsibility is also real: protect the industrial base by supporting a viable company, not by freezing the current structure indefinitely.

If you want to use your governance role responsibly, press for a quantified plan — not for denial of the underlying problem.

## To the controlling family and investors
I agree with one thing you said: long-term value is what matters.

Exactly.

And long-term value is not served by pretending that closures and workforce reduction are never legitimate instruments. If the economics require adjustment, then we have to adjust. Otherwise we destroy value through delay.

## My position remains unchanged
I am open to alternatives to closures and compulsory redundancies.  
I am **not** open to a cosmetic compromise that leaves the company structurally uncompetitive.

So the next step is simple:

- Bring me the quantified alternative.
- Show me the durable cost reduction.
- Show me the capacity reduction.
- Show me the labour-cost contribution.
- Show me the investment and margin path to 2030.

If you cannot do that, then the refusal of harder measures is not a solution — it is a blockade.

And I will not endorse a settlement that protects the current footprint at the expense of the company’s future.
