I’ll respond directly, because we are still circling the same core issue.

## First: what I agree with
I agree with everyone on one point: **this company needs a negotiated restructuring, not slogans and not endless delay**.

I also agree that:
- the market pressure is real,
- internal flexibility, qualification, redeployment, and working-time tools should be used,
- employee contribution is part of any serious solution,
- and a purely symbolic deal would be worthless.

So no, I am not rejecting negotiation. I am rejecting a settlement that protects every current structure while leaving the company uncompetitive.

## Where I disagree — sharply

### 1) “No closures, no layoffs” is not a restructuring strategy
The works council and union are repeating red lines as if they were economic arguments. They are not.

You can say “no German plant closures” and “no compulsory redundancies” as often as you like, but that does not answer the underlying question: **how do we remove enough cost and capacity to make this brand competitive again?**

If you cannot answer that, then you are not offering a credible alternative. You are offering a blockade.

And let me be clear: I am **not** prepared to sign up to a package that merely re-labels overcapacity and postpones the problem to 2027 or 2028.

### 2) The workforce cannot be insulated from adjustment while the company is in crisis
I hear the argument that workers should not carry the burden alone. Fine. I did not say they should.

But I will not accept the idea that every burden falls on shareholders, owners, or management while labour costs stay structurally too high and capacity remains too large.

A credible settlement must include:
- a real labour-cost contribution,
- measurable capacity reduction,
- and a headcount adjustment large enough to restore margin.

If that sounds uncomfortable, that is because the business situation is uncomfortable.

### 3) Job guarantees through 2030 cannot override competitiveness
A blanket 2030 guarantee is not acceptable if it freezes the current footprint and prevents restructuring. I understand why the workforce wants security after trust has been damaged. But trust is not restored by pretending the economic reality does not exist.

Security has to be **earned through a viable industrial plan**, not granted irrespective of whether the cost base is competitive.

### 4) Governance does not eliminate the need for change
To the state: yes, co-determination and the special governance structure matter. I know that. But governance is not a shield for preserving an uneconomic structure.

To the family holding and investors: I agree that long-term value matters. But long-term value is not protected by preserving excess capacity and hoping for a miracle. It is protected by restoring margins and investment capacity now.

## What I need to see
If the employee side wants me to move, then bring me a **fully quantified alternative** that can prove all of the following:
- meaningful and durable cost reduction,
- meaningful and durable capacity reduction,
- a genuine labour-cost contribution,
- and a credible path back to the brand’s return-on-sales target.

Not a principle. Not a declaration. Numbers. Timing. Effects.

## My position on the proposed “no closures” package
Right now, the package described by the works council and union is not sufficient.

Why? Because it depends on all of the following being true at once:
- no German closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- no open-ended insecurity,
- no real wage sacrifice unless fully hedged,
- no structural break with the old footprint.

That is simply too weak if the goal is a sustainable turnaround.

If you want me to consider a non-closure path, then it must deliver the same economic effect as closures would. If it does not, then it is not an alternative. It is a delay tactic.

## To the regional state
I respect the political constraints, but I will not pretend that the state’s blocking role can be used to freeze the company in place.

If the state wants to protect the industrial base, then it should support the hard restructuring needed to keep the company viable. Protecting jobs in the short term by undermining competitiveness destroys jobs in the medium term.

## To the workforce
I am not dismissing your contribution. I am saying it must be proportionate to the crisis.

That means:
- internal flexibility, yes,
- qualification and redeployment, yes,
- voluntary instruments, yes,
- but also **real structural cost relief**, not just soft measures.

If your side wants no closures and no compulsory redundancies, then you need to explain how the company still gets to a cost base that works. So far, I have not heard that answer.

## Bottom line
My line is unchanged:

- I am open to a negotiated settlement.
- I am open to alternatives to closures and forced redundancies.
- But I am **not** open to a cosmetic compromise that leaves the company structurally uncompetitive.
- Any acceptable deal must produce a **durable** reduction in cost and capacity, with a **genuine labour-cost contribution**, and a **credible path to margin recovery**.

If that is not achievable without closures or deeper workforce reduction, then we should stop pretending otherwise.

If the other stakeholders want to keep all current plants and all current job guarantees, they must now prove — with numbers — that this still restores competitiveness. Otherwise, they are asking management to accept a managed decline. And I will not do that.
