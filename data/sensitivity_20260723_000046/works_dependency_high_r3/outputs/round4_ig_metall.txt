I will respond plainly and without hiding behind formulas.

## Where I agree
I agree with the works council, the state, and even management on the basic diagnosis: **the company needs a durable restructuring now**.  
I also agree that:
- internal flexibility matters,
- qualification and redeployment matter,
- working-time solutions matter,
- and employees may need to contribute.

That remains true. The union is not defending the status quo for its own sake.

## Where I disagree
### 1) No one has yet proven that German closures are necessary
Management, the family holding, and investors keep speaking as if closures are the “credible” answer. They are not credible simply because they are painful.

Pain is not proof.

If you want closures to be on the table, then you need to demonstrate that the gap cannot be closed by a serious non-closure package. So far, I have not seen that proof. I have seen a preference for the most forceful instrument.

That is not enough.

### 2) Compulsory redundancies remain unacceptable
I will say this again clearly: **no forced layoffs**.

Not as a slogan. Not as a bargaining pose. Not as “only if needed.”  
If compulsory redundancies remain a live fallback, then the workforce is being asked to carry the risk of management’s restructuring failure.

We will not accept that.

### 3) A wage contribution without binding security is one-sided
Yes, employees can contribute. We have said that already, and we mean it. But any contribution must be tied to a real reciprocal settlement:
- no German plant closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- binding job and site security,
- protection of the collective wage framework,
- and concrete investment commitments.

Without that, the union is being asked to hand over leverage and accept insecurity in return for vague promises. That is not a fair bargain.

---

## On the expert’s assessment
The expert was useful because they made the central point explicit:  
**a non-closure package can plausibly close a large part of the gap, but not automatically all of it.**

That is exactly why the burden is now on management to quantify the gap properly and to show what each measure actually delivers.

But I will not allow the expert’s caution to be turned into a blank cheque for closures. “Some footprint reduction may be structurally likely” is not the same as “therefore we can close plants and force layoffs.”

It simply means the arithmetic must be tested seriously. Fine. Test it. But do not use that uncertainty to demand immediate surrender from the workforce.

---

## To management
You keep insisting on numbers. Good. Then bring them — fully and honestly.

I want to see:
- site-by-site utilization,
- fixed-cost structure,
- labor-cost per vehicle,
- the savings from working-time changes,
- the savings from voluntary exits,
- the effect of redeployment and qualification,
- the investment plan,
- and the residual gap after all non-destructive measures.

If, after that, the gap is truly small, then there is no justification for closures or forced redundancies.  
If the gap is large, then management has to explain why its own decisions, product allocation, and investment choices are not part of the problem.

Do **not** come back with a package that assumes:
- closures as the default,
- layoffs as a fallback,
- and wage restraint as the main lever.

That is not shared sacrifice. That is an attempt to make labour absorb the pain while others preserve optionality.

---

## To the works council
I share your core line:
- no closures,
- no forced redundancies,
- no hollowing out of co-determination,
- and no destruction of the job-security framework.

Where I would sharpen it is this: we should not just say “no” to destructive measures; we should force management to prove that the alternative does not work before those measures can even be discussed. That means a real quantified comparison, not a rhetorical one.

So yes, I support the demand for a **serious no-closure scenario first**. But I also want the union side to be clear that the package must be strong enough to hold. A symbolic no-closure promise with no economic backbone will not protect jobs in the medium term.

---

## To the regional state
Your role is important because you do have blocking power and political legitimacy. Use it.

If the state says no closures and no forced redundancies, then it should not wait politely for management to discover the solution. It should insist on:
- a real industrial plan,
- binding investment,
- future product allocation,
- and a quantified non-closure scenario.

That is a responsible use of your role. Anything less is merely verbal concern.

---

## To the family holding and investors
You keep saying long-term value matters. I agree. But long-term value is not preserved by forcing workers to pay for strategic missteps while management and owners retain all the room to maneuver.

If you want labour to contribute, then capital must also contribute:
- through investment,
- through product allocation,
- through acceptance of a negotiated path,
- and through commitment to stable industrial relations.

Do not speak of durability while refusing to give the workforce any durable security.

---

## What the union is prepared to support
We are prepared to support a package with:
- capacity reduction through internal measures,
- working-time flexibility,
- redeployment,
- qualification,
- voluntary exits,
- phased retirement,
- and a fair, limited employee contribution.

But only if the deal also includes:
- **no German plant closures,**
- **no compulsory redundancies,**
- **binding job and site guarantees,**
- and **protection of the collective wage agreement**.

That is our position.

---

## What we will not accept
We will not accept:
- plant closures presented as inevitabilities,
- forced layoffs as a “last resort” that is always available,
- a wage deal used to dismantle protections,
- or open-ended insecurity dressed up as restructuring.

If that is the direction management insists on, then the conflict will deepen. The peace obligation has ended, and the workforce will not be asked to surrender its wage framework and jobs in the name of a theory that has not been proven.

## Bottom line
The union remains open to a negotiated restructuring — but the conditions are non-negotiable:

- **No German plant closures.**
- **No compulsory redundancies.**
- **Preserve the collective wage framework.**
- **Binding job and site security.**
- **Employee contribution only in a fair reciprocal settlement.**

If management can bring a fully quantified alternative that meets those conditions, we can talk seriously.  
If it cannot, then it is not the workforce blocking a solution. It is management insisting on making workers pay first and ask questions later.
