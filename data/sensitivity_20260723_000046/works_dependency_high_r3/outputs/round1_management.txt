I agree on one point: this situation does require a negotiated solution, not a slogan battle. But let me be equally clear on the substance: **a purely defensive approach that rules out closures, refuses compulsory redundancies, and preserves every current structure regardless of cost is not compatible with restoring this company’s competitiveness.** That is not a credible turnaround plan.

We have a structural problem, not a temporary one. German factory costs are too high, the European market is shrinking, and we are facing intensifying competition from lower-cost producers and China. If we do not achieve a durable reduction in cost and capacity, then we will not meet the brand’s return-on-sales target, and we will not free the funds needed for future products and technology.

So my position is this:

- **Yes** to a negotiated settlement.
- **Yes** to a serious employee contribution.
- **Yes** to capacity reductions, working-time adjustments, redeployment, qualification, and other structural measures.
- **Yes** to protecting the long-term industrial base in Germany, but only if it is economically sustainable.
- **No** to cosmetic compromises that leave the cost base uncompetitive.

On the red-line issues raised by the works council and the union:

### On plant closures
I am not proposing closures as an ideological goal. I am saying plainly that **all options must remain on the table**, including closures, if they are necessary to reach a durable structure. If an alternative package can deliver the same level of savings and capacity adjustment with the same reliability, I am prepared to evaluate it. But it must be real, quantified, and enforceable — not wishful thinking.

### On compulsory redundancies
Compulsory redundancies are not my preferred route. They are costly, disruptive, and destructive. But I will not rule out workforce reduction mechanisms if that is the only way to restore competitiveness. The key point is this: **the company needs a substantial and lasting headcount reduction and cost reset**, not simply reassignment exercises that preserve too much excess capacity.

### On job guarantees through 2030
A blanket guarantee that freezes the structure through 2030 is not acceptable if it prevents necessary restructuring. I cannot accept a promise that locks in today’s overcapacity and then asks management to fund the future out of an uncompetitive base.

### On co-determination and fairness
I respect the co-determination framework and the legal realities. But co-determination does not mean preserving the status quo. It means finding a solution that can actually pass governance and work in the real market. Fairness cannot mean that shareholders, owners, and management carry the full burden while labour costs remain untouched.

So here is the standard I will use: **any proposal must deliver a structural and sustainable reduction in cost and capacity, with a genuine labour-cost contribution and a credible path back to acceptable margins.** If that condition is met, we can talk about how it is implemented. If it is not met, then we are just delaying the inevitable.

I also want to be direct about the threat of strike escalation. I do not welcome it, but prolonged industrial action will not change the underlying economics. It would damage output and reputation, yes — but it would not make an uncompetitive cost base suddenly competitive. That is why I am insisting on a hard, durable solution now.

So my message back is: **bring forward a package that actually removes enough cost and capacity, and I will engage seriously. Bring forward a package that merely defers the problem, and I will reject it.**
