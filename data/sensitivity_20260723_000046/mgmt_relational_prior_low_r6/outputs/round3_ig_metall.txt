I’ll be equally direct.

## Where I agree
I agree with the Works Council, the state, and even management on the basic premise: **doing nothing is not an option**. The company needs a real restructuring, and a prolonged conflict would hurt everybody — cash flow, output, trust, and ultimately jobs.

I also agree that a durable solution has to include:
- capacity correction,
- qualification and retraining,
- redeployment,
- working-time instruments,
- and contributions from more than just labour.

So no, I am not defending the status quo.

## Where I disagree
I do **not** accept that the only “serious” restructuring is one that includes:
- plant closures,
- compulsory redundancies,
- or the erosion of the collective wage framework.

That is the central fault line, and it remains unresolved.

### 1) Closures are not my starting point
Management and the owners keep returning to closures as if they are the clean answer. They are not automatically the right answer here.

A closure may look efficient in a model, but in this company’s governance and industrial setting it would trigger:
- escalation,
- loss of trust,
- political resistance,
- and likely a much harsher conflict than the spreadsheet captures.

So I reject the idea that closures should remain the default “serious option.”

### 2) Compulsory redundancies remain unacceptable
That is still a hard line. If the adjustment only works by forcing people out, then labour is being made the sole shock absorber for a structural problem created at strategic level.

That is not a fair settlement, and I will not endorse it.

### 3) “All sites open” is not enough as a slogan — but neither is closure
I hear the Works Council’s insistence on protecting every German site. I share the goal of preserving the industrial base, but I also need to be honest: if that position is just a slogan and not backed by a workable economic plan, then it is not enough.

At the same time, management has not proven that closures are necessary. The expert assessments said something more careful:
- non-closure measures can reduce costs meaningfully,
- but may not fully close the gap if overcapacity is structurally large.

That is **not** a mandate for closures. It is a requirement for hard data.

## On the expert input
The experts were useful for one reason: they made the debate more precise.

What they said, in substance, is:
- working-time measures, redeployment, voluntary exits, and qualification **can** reduce costs,
- but they often do **not** remove fixed plant overhead,
- and if a site is subscale, it can remain expensive even after cuts.

Fine. I accept that.

But the missing conclusion is this: **management still has to prove that the remaining gap cannot be closed by a balanced package that includes labour, management, and owner contributions.**

That burden is still on the people demanding structural damage to jobs and sites.

## What I will not accept
I will not accept:
- closures as a bargaining threat to force wage restraint,
- compulsory redundancies as a fallback,
- or a “partnership” where employees give up security and management gives up nothing meaningful.

If employees are expected to contribute, then that contribution must be part of a genuinely shared package.

## What a fair package would require
I remain open to a package that includes:
- temporary wage moderation,
- reduced or foregone bonuses,
- working-time flexibility,
- voluntary exits,
- internal redeployment,
- qualification/retraining,
- and a time-limited employee contribution,

**if and only if** it is matched by:
- binding site and employment security,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- real management restraint,
- owner-funded transition and investment support,
- and a credible future-product and industrial plan.

If management wants labour to absorb pain, then ownership and leadership must also take visible pain.

## What I need before moving further
Management says the numbers justify hard measures. Then show the numbers:
- plant-by-plant utilization,
- fixed and variable cost by site,
- savings from working-time instruments,
- savings from redeployment and voluntary programs,
- what management and owners are actually contributing,
- and the exact gap that supposedly remains.

Without that, “closure” is just a threat, not a solution.

## On the state and ownership
I appreciate the state’s rejection of mass redundancies and its insistence on regional responsibility. But responsibility also means not allowing labour to be cornered into accepting one-sided pain.

And to the family holding and investors: if you want long-term value, then stop treating job cuts as the only serious discipline. A company built on industrial capability cannot be “saved” by hollowing out its workforce and hoping the rest will stand.

## My bottom line
I am prepared to negotiate a **real** restructuring:
- with temporary employee contributions,
- with capacity correction,
- with redeployment and retraining,
- and with meaningful contributions from management and owners.

I am **not** prepared to negotiate away:
- the collective wage framework,
- the principle of no compulsory redundancies,
- or the workforce’s claim to fair burden-sharing.

If the other side insists on closures and forced layoffs, then I will say plainly: **we will resist that with all collective-bargaining means available.**
