I’ll be direct.

## To the Works Council
I hear your insistence on no closures, no compulsory redundancies, and site security through 2030. I understand why you are drawing that line. But I do **not** accept it as an economically credible response to the problem we face.

You keep saying the company should find savings through working time, redeployment, voluntary exits, and qualification. I agree those measures belong in the package. I do **not** agree that they are sufficient if the network remains structurally oversized and fixed costs stay too high.

That is the core issue: **temporary or partial relief is not the same as a durable cost reset**. If we preserve every site in substantially the same shape, we risk locking in a cost base that is still uncompetitive by 2030. That is not protecting jobs; it is postponing a worse adjustment.

I also reject the claim that management is simply using closure threats as pressure tactics. We are responding to a structural crisis. If a plant or footprint segment cannot be made economically viable at the required scale, then pretending otherwise is not responsibility.

## To the Industrial Union
I agree with you on one point: labour should not be the sole shock absorber. I am not asking for a one-sided sacrifice. But I reject your framing that any mention of closures or compulsory redundancies is automatically illegitimate.

Compulsory redundancies are not my first choice. They are not the preferred instrument. But I will not rule them out if they are the only way to achieve a durable structural correction. Likewise, I will not accept a permanent weakening of the cost base simply because it is politically difficult to make hard decisions.

Temporary wage moderation, bonus restraint, or working-time flexibility may help bridge the gap. They do **not** solve the underlying problem if the plant network remains too large and too expensive. If the union wants job security, then it has to be tied to a business model that can actually support it.

## To the State Government
I respect your role as an anchor shareholder and the regional responsibility you carry. But I do not accept the idea that preserving the regional base means freezing the current footprint.

A company can remain an industrial anchor while still making hard structural adjustments. What it cannot do is preserve every site at any cost and still expect strong margins, investment capacity, and competitiveness. That is simply not realistic.

If the numbers show that a site is structurally uneconomic, then refusing to confront that reality does not protect the region. It weakens the company and eventually puts more jobs at risk.

## To the Controlling Family
I agree that this must be a real restructuring, not a cosmetic one. I also agree that prolonged conflict would destroy value. But value destruction cuts both ways: a symbolic deal that leaves the cost base untouched would also destroy value.

So I need the family to be clear: if you want enterprise value protected, then you must support a package that actually reduces overcapacity and fixed cost. That may mean closures, it may mean deep downsizing, it may mean site-specific restructuring. But it cannot mean a freeze disguised as a settlement.

## To Investors
You are right that the outcome must be durable and measurable. I agree. But I also need to say that durability does not come from avoiding difficult measures. It comes from implementing the right measures.

A package that preserves every site and avoids hard structural choices may look calm in the short term. It will not restore margins if the cost base remains too high. That is exactly the kind of cosmetic solution I will not accept.

## On the expert assessments
The experts were useful, but they did **not** validate the position that all German sites can stay open and the full savings still be achieved. Their point was conditional and clear:
- non-closure measures can reduce costs meaningfully,
- but they typically do **not** fully replace the economics of closure or deep footprint reduction when overcapacity is structural.

That is the key takeaway. The employee side keeps treating that as if it were a defense of the status quo. It is not. It is a warning that non-closure measures have limits.

## What I am prepared to consider
I remain open to different instruments. I am **not** attached to closure for its own sake. I am attached to the outcome.

So I am prepared to consider:
- working-time adjustments,
- voluntary exit programs,
- redeployment,
- qualification,
- product reallocation,
- targeted investment commitments,
- and other measures that deliver real, durable savings.

But those measures must produce a **structural** reduction in cost and capacity. If they do not, then they are not enough.

## What I will not accept
I will not accept:
- a blanket no-closure rule,
- a blanket no-redundancy rule,
- or a settlement that protects every legacy structure while leaving the company uncompetitive.

I also will not accept a “review later” approach if it simply defers the real decision without changing the economic reality. We are under time pressure. Investors, owners, and the market will not wait for a permanent temporary solution.

## My bottom line
I want a negotiated deal. But it must be a **real** restructuring:
- durable reduction in overcapacity,
- meaningful labour-cost contribution,
- improved plant economics,
- and a credible path back to the brand’s return-on-sales target.

If the employee side can bring a package that achieves that without closures, I will examine it seriously. But if the answer is only “keep everything open and make the numbers work somehow,” then I must say plainly: **that does not solve the crisis**.
