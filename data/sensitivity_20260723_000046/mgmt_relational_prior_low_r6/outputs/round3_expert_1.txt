From an industrial restructuring and capacity-planning perspective, the discussion has now converged on the real technical question:

**Can the German footprint be rebalanced enough without full plant closures to close the cost gap by 2030?**

My assessment, based on the facts provided, is:

## 1) What is technically plausible
A package built around:
- working-time flexibility,
- voluntary exits,
- redeployment,
- qualification/retraining,
- product allocation changes,
- overhead reduction,
- and temporary labour-cost concessions

**can produce meaningful savings**. In a large automotive footprint, those measures can lower labor cost, improve utilization, and reduce some fixed overhead through consolidation of shifts, functions, and support work.

But they have limits:
- they do **not** eliminate plant-level fixed costs,
- they do **not** fully remove underabsorbed overhead in a subscale plant,
- and they usually do **not** substitute one-for-one for the economics of a closure when structural overcapacity is large.

So the expert framing is correct: **non-closure measures can narrow the gap, but may not fully close it**.

## 2) What cannot be concluded yet
There is **not enough data** in the scenario to determine whether:
- one site,
- several sites,
- or the full German network

is economically sustainable without closures.

To reach that conclusion, you would need a plant-by-plant model with at least:
- current and forecast utilization,
- fixed and variable cost by site,
- product mix and margin contribution,
- logistics and capex implications,
- labor flexibility assumptions,
- and the volume required for each plant to remain above a minimum efficient scale.

Without that, “no closures” is a political position, not yet a verified operating plan.

## 3) Operational reality of the options
### Option A: Full closure(s)
This is the strongest lever for removing fixed cost and excess capacity. It gives the clearest structural reset, but also creates:
- major industrial conflict risk,
- transition complexity,
- know-how loss,
- and potentially long execution delays if blocked by governance or labor action.

### Option B: Protected footprint reduction
This is often the most realistic compromise in heavily constrained governance settings. It can include:
- line removal,
- repurposing,
- partial site specialization,
- and volume concentration.

It may preserve the site “shell” while materially shrinking its economic footprint. Technically, this can work if:
- the plant retains a viable product assignment,
- its remaining scale is above minimum efficient volume,
- and the residual fixed cost burden is not too high.

But there is a danger of **hollowing out**: the site remains open formally while becoming structurally weak and expensive.

### Option C: No closures, negotiated burden-sharing
This is feasible only if the savings gap is moderate and if the company can also secure:
- material management/owner contributions,
- strong product volume commitments,
- and enough labor flexibility to lift utilization and reduce cost per unit.

If the overcapacity is large, this option usually leaves some combination of:
- margins still below target,
- future capex constrained,
- or a deferred restructuring problem.

## 4) Key technical trade-off
The central trade-off is between:

- **short-term industrial peace and site preservation**
versus
- **durable fixed-cost removal and footprint simplification**

In automotive manufacturing, if the network has multiple subscale sites, the cost penalty is often persistent. You can reduce headcount and variable cost, but if the base load of fixed cost remains spread across too many plants, competitiveness can still lag peers.

## 5) My practical judgment
Based on the scenario alone:

- **A purely non-closure solution is possible only if the required savings are at the lower end of what management claims, or if ownership/state/labor jointly provide large additional contributions.**
- **If overcapacity is structurally large, then some form of plant-level downsizing, repurposing, or closure is likely necessary to achieve a durable reset.**

That said, a full closure is not the only way to achieve structural correction. A **protected footprint reduction** with product reallocation and concentrated volume can sometimes deliver much of the same economic effect, especially if combined with workforce measures.

## 6) What should be requested next
The next round should not debate slogans. It should request a **plant-by-plant comparison** of:

1. **Status quo**
2. **Non-closure labor-concession package**
3. **Downsizing / repurposing package**
4. **Closure package**

For each site:
- utilization,
- fixed-cost absorption,
- headcount path,
- annual savings,
- capex needs,
- logistics impacts,
- and 2030 margin effect.

That is the only way to determine whether the company can preserve all German sites in a materially viable form, or whether one or more sites require structural reduction.

## Bottom line
My technical view is:

**Non-closure measures can contribute materially, but they are unlikely to fully replace plant closure or deep footprint reduction if the overcapacity is truly structural.**  
The most credible compromise is likely **downsizing/repurposing with binding protections**, not an unchanged all-sites-open model and not necessarily immediate full closure either.
