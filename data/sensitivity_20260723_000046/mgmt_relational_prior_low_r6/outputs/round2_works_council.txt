Management is still trying to frame this as if there are only two options: either accept closures and layoffs, or accept “decline.” I reject that framing.

I agree with everyone on one point: **doing nothing is not an option**. But doing something does **not** mean destroying German sites, breaking the job-security framework, or using the workforce as the shock absorber for past strategic mistakes.

## Where I stand

### I agree with:
- the need for a real restructuring package
- investment in future products and technology
- qualification, redeployment, and working-time solutions
- the need for a negotiated settlement rather than an imposed one
- the warning that conflict is costly

### I do not agree with:
- treating plant closures as a normal or acceptable instrument
- using compulsory redundancies as a “necessary” fallback
- demanding workforce concessions before management and owners have put real alternatives on the table
- pretending that “site preservation” can be traded away while still calling the result socially responsible

## On management’s argument
Management says the German cost base is too high and overcapacity must be cut. Fine — then bring forward a plan that cuts costs **without closures and without forced layoffs**.

What I will not accept is this:
- “We need savings”
- therefore “one or more plants may have to close”
- therefore “employees must give up job security”

That is not restructuring. That is a transfer of risk from capital to labour.

If management wants credibility, it must quantify:
- what savings are needed,
- how much can be achieved through capacity adjustment,
- what can be achieved through reduced working time, internal deployment, early retirement, qualification, and product shift,
- and what management and owners themselves are prepared to contribute.

## On the family owners and investors
You both speak about “durable competitiveness,” but neither of you has explained why the only credible route is to threaten German plants and jobs. If there is a gap, then close it with:
- management contribution,
- owner contribution,
- smarter capacity planning,
- and a negotiated labour contribution **only within a framework of full site and job protection**.

If your benchmark is just short-term margins, then you are not solving the company’s future — you are stripping out the industrial base that makes it possible.

## On the state government
I welcome your clear rejection of closures and mass redundancies. But I also want to be very direct: the state cannot simply say “social responsibility” and then leave the workforce to absorb the economic pain. If the regional anchor role is serious, then the state should be backing:
- binding site guarantees,
- investment commitments,
- and a restructuring that keeps every German plant open.

## What a viable package must contain
For the Works Council, the minimum conditions are still non-negotiable:

1. **All German plants remain open.**
2. **No compulsory redundancies.**
3. **Binding employment and site security through 2030.**
4. **Real co-determination in shaping the transformation plan.**
5. **Alternative savings measures first**:
   - capacity reductions without closures
   - working-time instruments
   - internal redeployment
   - qualification and retraining
   - voluntary exits only, not forced job cuts
6. **Fair contributions from management and owners**
7. **Investment commitments for the future product and technology roadmap**

## What I am prepared to discuss
I am prepared to discuss:
- temporary working-time reductions
- voluntary programs
- internal transfers
- qualification and redeployment
- negotiated, time-limited contribution measures

I am **not** prepared to discuss:
- plant closures
- site hollowing-out that amounts to closure by another name
- compulsory redundancies
- tearing up the job-security framework
- a “package” that hands management a free pass to restructure at the expense of the workforce

## The key question
If management claims alternatives cannot deliver enough savings, then I want the numbers on the table. Not slogans. Not pressure tactics. Numbers.

If a specialist is needed to verify whether alternatives can deliver equivalent savings at scale, then we should bring in an [SUMMON: industrial restructuring and capacity-planning expert] to assess:
- required cost reductions,
- plant-by-plant capacity utilisation,
- savings from working-time and redeployment measures,
- and the employment impact of each option.

Until then, there is no basis for demanding that the workforce accept closures or forced layoffs.

## Bottom line
The Works Council will not endorse a restructuring that closes German plants or imposes compulsory redundancies. If management wants agreement, it must come back with a plan that keeps every site open, protects jobs through 2030, and shares the burden fairly across management, owners, and labour. Anything else is unacceptable.
