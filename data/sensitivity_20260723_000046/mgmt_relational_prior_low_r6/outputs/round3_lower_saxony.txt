As the regional state shareholder, I will be clear and consistent: I support a real restructuring, but I will not support a solution that guts regional employment, sidelines the state’s co-determination role, or turns plant closures into the default answer.

## Where I agree
I agree with management, owners, investors, the works council, and the union on the basic diagnosis: **doing nothing is not an option**.  
The company needs a durable improvement in competitiveness, and a prolonged conflict would damage output, cash flow, trust, and the brand.

I also agree with the expert assessments on one important point: **working-time tools, redeployment, voluntary exits, and qualification can reduce costs meaningfully, but they are not automatically enough to solve a large structural overcapacity problem**.

## Where I disagree
I do **not** accept the conclusion that this necessarily points to plant closures or compulsory redundancies.

That is where management, the family holding, and investors keep overreaching. They are treating the closure option as if it were the benchmark of seriousness. It is not. It is one possible instrument, and it is politically and industrially the most damaging one. If the company wants my support, it must first show that a serious restructuring can be achieved without destroying the regional industrial base.

I also do **not** accept the works council’s assumption that “all sites open” is self-evidently the only legitimate outcome. I understand and share the instinct to protect jobs and sites, but I need more than a political position. I need a credible industrial plan. If a plant cannot remain viable in its current form, then we have to discuss **downsizing, repurposing, and capacity correction** before talking about closure.

So my line is not “everything stays exactly as it is.”  
My line is: **no closure-driven solution unless every serious alternative has been quantified and shown to fail.**

## To management
You keep saying that the company needs a durable structural reset. I am not contesting that. But you still have not shown, plant by plant, that non-closure measures cannot do the job.

If you want to move this discussion forward, then bring:
- plant-by-plant utilization data,
- fixed and variable cost structure by site,
- savings from working-time changes,
- savings from redeployment and voluntary exits,
- the capex and logistics costs of any footprint change,
- and the exact residual gap after those measures.

Without that, “closures may be necessary” remains a claim, not a justified conclusion.

## To the works council and union
I support your rejection of compulsory redundancies and mass layoffs as the first response. That is a legitimate line. But I also need honesty: if the network is structurally oversized, then a package of only temporary labor measures will not be enough.

That means you should be prepared to discuss:
- capacity reductions without closures,
- working-time flexibility,
- voluntary programs,
- redeployment,
- retraining,
- and temporary, negotiated employee contributions,

provided that the package includes:
- binding site protection,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- fair burden-sharing,
- and real investment commitments for the future product portfolio.

What I cannot accept is a settlement that simply freezes the current structure and hopes the numbers improve on their own.

## To the controlling family and investors
You are right that the company must remain viable and competitive. But viability is not the same as forcing the harshest possible restructuring. If you push closures too quickly, you risk triggering industrial conflict, political escalation, and long-term damage to the company’s social legitimacy.

A “serious” package is not one that maximizes immediate cuts. It is one that restores competitiveness **without** destroying the company’s role as a regional anchor.

## On the expert input
The experts were useful, but the key point is still unresolved. They said:
- non-closure measures can reduce costs,
- but may not fully replace the economics of a closure or deep downsizing if overcapacity is structurally large.

That is a conditional finding. It does **not** authorize closure as the default. It means the burden of proof is still on those who want to move to closures.

## What I could support
I could support a package that includes:
- capacity correction,
- working-time adjustments,
- voluntary exits,
- redeployment,
- qualification and retraining,
- targeted investment commitments,
- temporary and limited workforce contributions,
- and a jointly verified review of site economics,

**if and only if** it protects jobs and preserves the company’s regional footprint as far as economically possible.

## What I will not support
I will not support:
- plant closures as the starting point,
- compulsory redundancies,
- a one-sided transfer of adjustment costs onto labour,
- or any outcome that weakens the state’s co-determination/veto role.

That is not an opening offer. It is the boundary of responsible ownership.

## My position going forward
I am open to a hard but fair restructuring. I am **not** open to a closure-led solution unless it is proven unavoidable and socially contained. If management wants my support, it must come with a credible, data-backed package that preserves the company as a regional economic anchor and protects the workforce as far as possible.
