I’ll be direct.

## Where I agree
I agree with management, investors, and the expert on the central economic point: **this company has a structural competitiveness problem, and doing nothing would destroy value**. If the German footprint remains too large for the market, margins will stay under pressure, investment capacity will erode, and the long-term value of the core asset will suffer.

I also agree with the employee side and the state on one equally important point: **a prolonged, destructive conflict would be value-destructive**. A strike war, political escalation, and brand damage would help no one, including the owners.

## Where I disagree
I do **not** accept the Works Council’s and union’s attempt to make:
- **no plant closures**
- **no compulsory redundancies**
- **all sites open**
- **employment security through 2030**

into absolute starting points.

That is not a serious answer to a structural overcapacity problem. It may be politically understandable, but it does not yet demonstrate that the company can restore competitiveness at the scale required.

I also do **not** accept the state government’s framing that preserving the regional anchor role means effectively freezing the current site structure. The company is not being run for sentiment. It has to remain viable. If we preserve every site regardless of economics, we risk preserving a weaker company and fewer jobs over time.

## On the expert input
The expert’s assessment was useful, and it matters.

My reading is straightforward:
- **Non-closure alternatives can meaningfully reduce costs**
- But **they are unlikely, on the facts given, to fully close a large structural gap plant by plant**
- Capacity correction without closure may work in some cases, but only if the remaining footprint is still efficient

That means the employee side has **not proven** that all German sites can stay open and the full savings still be achieved. The burden of proof remains on those who claim that closures are unnecessary.

## What I would support
I am open to a negotiated package, but it must satisfy three conditions:

1. **It materially improves margins and competitiveness**
2. **It delivers durable, not cosmetic, capacity and cost correction**
3. **It avoids value-destructive instability**

That can include:
- working-time adjustments
- redeployment
- qualification and retraining
- voluntary exit programs
- capacity reductions
- site-specific restructuring where necessary
- investment commitments tied to future products

I am not fixated on a specific instrument. I am fixated on the outcome.

## What I will not support
I will not support:
- a symbolic deal that keeps every structure intact and calls it restructuring
- a package that rules out closures in principle even if the economics require them
- a settlement that leaves the company structurally uncompetitive
- a conflict strategy that damages the enterprise and then pretends the owners should simply absorb it

## On labour contributions
I do agree that if employees are asked to contribute, they must do so within a balanced package. But “balanced” does not mean “no hard decisions.” It means:
- labour contributes,
- management contributes,
- owners support investment and transition,
- and the company gets a viable cost base.

What I will not accept is a deal where the workforce contributes only after management is forced to promise away every restructuring instrument that would actually make the company competitive.

## On governance and control
Let me be clear on one point: **the family holding will not support any arrangement that permanently impairs enterprise value or weakens governance control**. Our job is long-term stewardship, not short-term appeasement.

That does **not** mean automatic endorsement of the harshest restructuring proposal. It means we need a credible, enforceable solution that restores competitiveness **without triggering lasting instability**.

## My bottom line
I am prepared to back a real restructuring. I am **not** prepared to back:
- a cosmetic compromise,
- a freeze of the current site structure,
- or a settlement that sacrifices long-term value for short-term peace.

If the employee side wants our support, it must engage with the scale of the economic problem rather than demanding that it be wished away. If management can present a package that achieves real savings with limited disruption, I will consider it. If not, then stronger structural measures remain on the table.
