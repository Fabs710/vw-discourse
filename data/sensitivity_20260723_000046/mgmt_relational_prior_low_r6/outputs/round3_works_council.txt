Management, owners, investors — I hear the same message again: “We need a durable reset, so closures must remain on the table.” My answer is still no.

## Where I agree
I agree that:
- the company is in a serious structural crisis,
- doing nothing is not an option,
- the package must be negotiated,
- qualification, redeployment, working-time tools, and investment commitments belong in the solution,
- and a prolonged conflict would be costly for everyone.

That is not the dispute.

## Where I disagree
I reject the idea that a “serious” restructuring must include:
- plant closures,
- compulsory redundancies,
- or tearing up the job-security framework.

That is not the only credible path. It is the path management prefers because it shifts the burden onto labour and the regions.

The expert interventions did not prove your case. They said alternatives may not fully match the savings of closure **if** overcapacity is large and persistent. That is a conditional assessment — not a green light for closures. It means management still has to prove, with real plant-by-plant numbers, that all non-closure options are exhausted and insufficient.

Until then, your closure narrative is still just a threat.

## To management
You say you want facts, so here is the fact that matters to us: German plants are not variables on a spreadsheet. They are livelihoods, skills, and industrial capability. If you want the workforce to accept concessions, then show a plan that keeps every German plant open and avoids compulsory layoffs.

You have not done that.

You keep saying working-time measures and voluntary exits are not enough. Maybe not — but that does **not** automatically justify closures. It means you have to reduce the burden elsewhere:
- management compensation,
- owner contributions,
- product allocation,
- overhead reduction outside the plants,
- and a more balanced restructuring path.

If the economics only work by destroying sites, then the economics are incomplete.

## To the controlling family and investors
You both talk about long-term value. Then act like long-term stewards.

A closure-driven solution may look efficient now, but it creates:
- strike risk,
- political escalation,
- loss of know-how,
- weaker trust,
- and a permanently damaged labour relationship.

That is not “durable competitiveness.” That is industrial conflict with a balance-sheet explanation.

If you truly want long-term value, then support a package that preserves the site network and uses the workforce’s willingness to contribute only within a framework of job and site security.

## To the state government
I welcome your rejection of closures and mass redundancies. But I am also going to be blunt: “preserve the regional anchor role” is not enough if the company’s own restructuring plan is still built around site threats.

The state should use its influence to back:
- binding guarantees that all German plants stay open,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- investment commitments,
- and a negotiated transformation that keeps industrial capacity in the region.

If the state starts treating partial closures as an acceptable compromise, then the red line moves — and once it moves, jobs and bargaining power disappear site by site.

## To the union
We are aligned on the essentials:
- no closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- fair burden-sharing,
- no one-sided sacrifice.

If management insists on a cost contribution from labour, it must be temporary, limited, and matched by real contributions from capital and management — not a permanent weakening of the collective framework.

## On the expert’s “protected footprint reduction” idea
I do **not** accept “downsizing without closure” as a backdoor to hollowing out sites until they are closures in all but name.

A plant that is stripped of volume, product, and future investment is not preserved. It is being prepared for exit.

So no, I will not accept a model that:
- keeps the sign above the gate,
- but removes the operational basis for the site,
- and then calls it a compromise.

## On the proposed review trigger
A review trigger is only meaningful if the initial package does **not** pre-authorize closure logic.

I could discuss data verification, yes. But I will not accept a mechanism that says:
- “We keep sites open now,”
- while secretly building a countdown to closure later if targets are missed.

That is not protection. That is deferred conflict.

## What is actually non-negotiable for us
Let me be explicit:

1. **All German plants remain open.**
2. **No compulsory redundancies.**
3. **Binding job and site security through 2030.**
4. **Co-determination remains fully intact.**
5. **Any contribution from labour is temporary, negotiated, and matched by management and ownership contributions.**

Those are not opening demands. Those are the conditions under which we can even talk.

## What I would consider
I am prepared to discuss:
- working-time flexibility,
- voluntary programs,
- internal redeployment,
- retraining,
- product changes,
- temporary contribution measures,
- and investment packages.

I am **not** prepared to discuss:
- plant closures,
- forced layoffs,
- or so-called “protected” downsizing that simply delays the closure decision.

## On the need for an expert
If management insists that no-closure alternatives cannot deliver the full savings, then bring the hard data and let an independent specialist test your assumptions.

[SUMMON: independent plant-network and employment-impact expert to verify whether plant-by-plant savings from working-time, redeployment, voluntary exits, product shifts, and capacity adjustment can meet the target without closures or compulsory redundancies]

But let me be clear: I do not summon an expert because I doubt our position. I summon one because management has not earned the right to demand closures.

## Bottom line
We are not blocking restructuring. We are blocking an attempt to make workers pay for management’s strategic failures by closing plants and threatening jobs.

If management wants agreement, it must come back with a plan that:
- keeps every German plant open,
- rules out compulsory redundancies,
- protects jobs through 2030,
- and shares the burden fairly.

Anything else is unacceptable.
