I will be direct.

## Where I agree
I agree with management, investors, and the experts on the central diagnosis: this is a **structural competitiveness problem**, not a temporary wobble. The German footprint cannot remain insulated from economics forever, and the company needs a durable improvement in margins, capacity utilization, and cost discipline.

I also agree with the employee side and the state on one crucial point: a **prolonged conflict would destroy value**. That is bad for the workforce, bad for the region, and bad for the family holding as long-term owner. A negotiated settlement is clearly preferable.

## Where I disagree
I do **not** accept the repeated attempt to turn:
- “no plant closures,”
- “no compulsory redundancies,”
- “all sites open,”
- “job security through 2030”

into fixed preconditions.

That is not a credible response to structural overcapacity unless it is backed by hard, plant-by-plant proof. So far, I have not seen that proof.

### To the Works Council
I understand your concern that closures can become a backdoor to hollowing out. That concern is legitimate. But your position goes too far when it treats every structural adjustment as an attack on the company’s industrial base.

A site is not automatically “preserved” because the gate stays open. If a plant loses:
- volume,
- product allocation,
- fixed-cost absorption,
- and future investment,

then it is being weakened, even if nobody uses the word “closure.” So I do **not** accept a package that preserves the appearance of all sites while leaving the economics unrepaired.

You say management has not earned the right to demand closures. Fair enough. But the reverse is also true: you have not yet shown that all German sites can remain open **and** the company can still restore competitiveness by 2030.

### To the Industrial Union
I agree that labour should not be the sole shock absorber. I am not asking for a one-sided sacrifice. But I do **not** accept a blanket refusal to contemplate compulsory measures in any circumstance.

If voluntary exits, redeployment, working-time flexibility, and wage moderation can close the gap, that is obviously preferable. But if they cannot, then pretending that forced reductions are off the table regardless of economics is not responsible bargaining. It simply postpones the adjustment.

### To the Regional State Government
I respect your co-determination role and your responsibility for the region. But regional stewardship does **not** mean freezing the footprint. It means ensuring the company remains a viable industrial anchor for the region in the long run.

That requires realism:
- viable sites,
- investable products,
- competitive costs,
- and a structure that can support future capital allocation.

I cannot support a solution that protects the symbolism of regional presence while undermining the company’s ability to earn its future.

### To Institutional Investors
You are right that the outcome must be durable and measurable. I agree. But durability does not come from avoiding hard decisions; it comes from making the right ones.

If the package simply preserves every legacy structure and calls that restructuring, then it will not restore margins. That would be deferred decline, not discipline.

## On the expert input
The experts have been useful, but they have **not** proven that all sites can remain open and the company can still hit the required economics.

What they actually said is narrower:
- non-closure tools can reduce costs meaningfully,
- but they usually do **not** fully remove fixed plant cost or underabsorbed overhead,
- and if overcapacity is structural, deeper footprint reduction may be needed.

That is not a green light for closures in every case. It is a warning that partial measures may fall short.

## What I would support
I remain open to a negotiated package that delivers all three of these:
1. **durable cost reduction**
2. **real capacity correction**
3. **no value-destructive instability**

That package can include:
- working-time adjustments,
- redeployment,
- voluntary exit programs,
- qualification and retraining,
- product reallocation,
- overhead reduction,
- targeted investment commitments,
- and site-specific restructuring where the numbers justify it.

I am not attached to one instrument. I am attached to the outcome.

## What I will not support
I will not support:
- a frozen footprint,
- a cosmetic compromise,
- a blanket no-closure rule,
- a blanket no-redundancy rule,
- or a deal that leaves the company structurally uncompetitive.

I also will not support an open-ended delay strategy. We are under time pressure, and the market will not wait for a permanent temporary solution.

## What is still missing
What I need next is not another declaration of principle. I need a **plant-by-plant quantified comparison** of:
1. status quo,
2. non-closure labor-concession package,
3. protected downsizing / repurposing,
4. and closure,

including:
- fixed-cost absorption,
- utilization,
- capex implications,
- logistics effects,
- and 2030 margin impact.

Without that, we are debating slogans instead of strategy.

## Bottom line
The family holding is prepared to support a **real restructuring**. We are **not** prepared to support:
- a symbolic deal,
- a frozen cost base,
- or a settlement that sacrifices long-term value for short-term peace.

If the employee side and the state can demonstrate, with hard numbers, that the required savings can be achieved without closures and without permanent damage to competitiveness, we will examine that seriously. If not, stronger structural measures remain on the table.
