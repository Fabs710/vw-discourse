## Expert assessment: can non-closure alternatives fully close the cost gap plant by plant?

**Short answer: probably not, unless the required savings are modest or the plants are far more flexible and underutilized than management is implying.**  
In a structurally overcapacity automotive network, **working-time changes, redeployment, voluntary exits, and qualification can close part of the gap, but they usually do not fully replace the economics of a targeted closure or deep footprint reduction** on a plant-by-plant basis.

## The technical test
To conclude that non-closure alternatives can fully close the gap, you would need to show, for **each plant**, that the proposed package removes enough of the following to match the closure case:

1. **Fixed labor and overhead**
2. **Production support and logistics cost**
3. **Maintenance, utilities, and site overhead**
4. **Depreciation / underutilization burden**
5. **Complexity cost from subscale operation**
6. **Capex required to keep the site competitive**
7. **Any residual product-transfer and ramp-up costs elsewhere**

If the plant remains open, some of these costs often remain even after headcount reductions.

## What non-closure measures can realistically do
### 1) Working-time reduction / shorter schedules
- Can reduce labor expense quickly.
- Can bridge a short-term demand dip.
- **Does not remove plant fixed costs**.
- Typically insufficient as a permanent substitute for a closure case.

### 2) Redeployment across the network
- Useful only if there is genuine spare demand elsewhere.
- It shifts labor cost, but does not necessarily reduce total enterprise cost unless it eliminates otherwise needed capacity.
- Often constrained by skills, tooling, and location.

### 3) Voluntary exits / early retirement
- Can reduce headcount without forced layoffs.
- Usually **more expensive per exit** than planned restructuring redundancies.
- Often too slow and too small if the required savings are large.

### 4) Qualification / retraining
- Important for transition, but it is not itself a major savings source.
- Can enable productivity gains over time, but not usually enough to bridge a large gap.

### 5) Capacity reduction without closure
- This is the only non-closure tool that can approach a closure case.
- But only if the plant can be resized to a **truly efficient minimum scale**.
- If the remaining footprint is still subscale, the site becomes a **half-solution**: lower volume, nearly the same overhead.

## Plant-by-plant feasibility depends on four variables
Without these, no one can conclude the alternatives fully close the gap:

- **Current and projected utilization by plant**
- **Fixed versus variable cost structure by plant**
- **Ability to reallocate products without major capex or logistics penalties**
- **Magnitude and permanence of the market decline / overcapacity**

If a plant is running materially below minimum efficient scale, then keeping it open usually leaves a structural cost penalty.

## My judgment on the scenario as stated
Based on the information provided:

- A **negotiated non-closure package is feasible**
- A **package with no compulsory redundancies is feasible**
- A **package that preserves all plants and fully closes a large structural cost gap is not yet demonstrated**
- If management’s overcapacity diagnosis is correct, then **alternatives alone are unlikely to match the full savings of closure or deep downsizing**

So the works council’s position is **not disproven**, but it is also **not proven operationally**. The burden of proof is on those claiming that all plants can remain open and the cost target still be met.
