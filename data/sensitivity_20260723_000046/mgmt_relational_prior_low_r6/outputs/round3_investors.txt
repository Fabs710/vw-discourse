We are aligned on the diagnosis, but not on the outcome.

## Where we agree
- The company has a **structural competitiveness problem**.
- **Doing nothing is not an option**.
- A **prolonged conflict** would destroy value.
- A durable settlement must include **capacity correction, redeployment, qualification, working-time flexibility, and some form of burden-sharing**.
- The expert interventions have been helpful in one key respect: they show that **non-closure tools can reduce costs meaningfully**, but they are not automatically enough.

## Where we disagree
### To the Works Council and union
Your insistence on:
- no plant closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- all sites open,
- job security through 2030,

is understandable, but as a shareholder we cannot support it **if it leaves the company structurally uncompetitive**.

The experts did **not** prove your position. They said something narrower: alternatives *may* offset a substantial part of the gap, but likely **not all of it** if overcapacity is structurally large. That is exactly why we cannot accept a package that protects every site and simply hopes the margins recover.

If the result is a deal that preserves jobs in the short term but leaves the cost base above peer levels, then it is not a solution from our perspective; it is deferred decline.

### To the state government
We respect the regional and political constraints, but preserving the company’s role as an anchor does **not** mean freezing the current footprint. If the economics of a site do not work, then insisting on unchanged structures risks weaker margins, less investment, and ultimately more damage to the regional base.

### To management
We agree that the restructuring must be real, not cosmetic. But you still need to show, with hard numbers, that the chosen package is:
- durable,
- executable,
- and sufficient to restore return on sales.

Threatening closures is not enough. You need a **plant-by-plant case** showing the savings from:
- closure,
- downsizing,
- and non-closure labor-concession scenarios.

Without that, we cannot tell whether the proposed path is disciplined restructuring or just leverage in negotiations.

### To the controlling family
We agree that value must be protected and that symbolic compromise is unacceptable. But value protection does **not** mean maximizing harshness. A prolonged industrial conflict, a damaged brand, or an execution-risk-heavy settlement would also destroy value. We want disciplined cost action, not a headline-grabbing cut for its own sake.

## What would move us
We would support a settlement if it credibly delivers:
- **material, lasting cost reduction**
- **real capacity correction**
- **a clear path back to competitive margins**
- **measurable 2030 return-on-sales improvement**
- **execution certainty**
- and **no cosmetic substitution of temporary measures for structural change**

That can include working-time changes, voluntary exits, redeployment, retraining, and targeted site restructuring. But the package must be judged by its effect on margins and competitiveness, not by how politically comfortable it is.

## What would not move us
We would not support:
- a settlement that preserves all legacy structures and calls it restructuring,
- a package that leaves fixed costs too high,
- a deal that avoids hard decisions but merely postpones them,
- or a long conflict that burns cash and credibility.

## What we need next
Before any final position, we need a **quantified comparison** of:
1. closure,
2. downsizing without full closure,
3. and negotiated labor-concession scenarios,

including:
- plant-by-plant fixed-cost absorption,
- utilization,
- capex requirements,
- and 2030 margin impact.

## Bottom line
We are prepared to back **whatever credibly restores competitiveness**. We are **not** prepared to back a package that leaves the cost base structurally impaired, margins below target, or the restructuring non-credible.
