From an industrial finance and plant-network perspective, the expert answer is directionally correct: **non-closure measures can narrow the gap, but they usually do not fully replace the cost relief from removing a plant or a substantial chunk of fixed capacity**.

### Technical conclusion
A **full no-closure / no-compulsory-redundancy package** can only be judged sufficient if, plant by plant, it still delivers:
- competitive unit costs,
- adequate utilization,
- acceptable capex per unit of output,
- and sustainable 2030 margins.

If any plant remains:
- structurally underutilized,
- reliant on permanent internal cross-subsidy,
- or requiring repeated rescue investment,

then the package has **not** fully closed the gap.

### What is needed to prove sufficiency
To decide this properly, the minimum data set must be plant-specific and include:
1. forecast volumes by model/platform through 2030,
2. installed capacity and realistic utilization,
3. fixed plant overhead,
4. direct labour cost per vehicle,
5. logistics and network cost impact,
6. capex required to keep each site competitive,
7. workforce and shift structure,
8. quantified savings from each non-closure lever:
   - wage moderation,
   - working-time changes,
   - voluntary exits,
   - redeployment,
   - overhead reduction,
   - management/owner contributions.

### Decision rule
A non-closure package is financially equivalent to closure only if it recovers most of the closure economics, namely:
- fixed-cost removal,
- indirect labour reduction,
- logistics simplification,
- capex avoidance,
- and planning complexity reduction.

That is a high threshold. In most mature automotive networks, **working-time changes and labour concessions help, but they do not fully eliminate subscale plant economics**.

### Bottom line
So, on the evidence available in this scenario:

- **Non-closure measures are meaningful**
- **They may be enough for a smaller or more flexible gap**
- **But if overcapacity is structurally large, they are unlikely to fully close the cost gap without some footprint reduction**
