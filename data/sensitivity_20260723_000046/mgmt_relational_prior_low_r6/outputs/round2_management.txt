I’ll respond directly.

## First, what I agree with
I agree with everyone on one point: **doing nothing is not an option**.

I also agree that any settlement must be **negotiated**, not imposed, and that a prolonged conflict would be damaging for output, cash flow, and the brand. I am not seeking confrontation for its own sake.

And I accept the expert’s core point: **alternatives to closures can deliver meaningful savings, but only up to a point**. That is exactly why we need a serious restructuring discussion, not a slogan-based “keep everything open” demand.

## Where I disagree — fundamentally
I do **not** accept the idea that the company can restore competitiveness while preserving every German site, avoiding compulsory job reductions entirely, and leaving the current cost base largely intact.

That is not a restructuring. That is postponement.

The Works Council and union keep saying:
- no plant closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- all sites open,
- job security through 2030.

I understand why you are drawing that line. But from management’s side, that is **not yet a credible economic answer** to a structural overcapacity and cost problem.

## To the Works Council
You asked for numbers, and that is fair. Here they are in principle: the company needs a **durable, structural reduction in cost and capacity**, not temporary relief.

What I will not accept is a package that relies only on:
- working-time cuts,
- voluntary departures,
- redeployment,
- and qualification,

while leaving the underlying plant economics unchanged. Those tools can help, but they do **not** solve a large fixed-cost burden if the network remains oversized.

If we are serious, then we must examine all instruments, including:
- capacity reductions,
- site-specific restructuring,
- and, if necessary, plant adjustments.

I am **not** committing today to a plant closure as the only option. But I am also **not** accepting a categorical ban on closures if that is the only way to restore viable economics.

## To the trade union
You say compulsory redundancies are unacceptable. I hear that. But I need to be equally clear: if the only red line is “no closures, no layoffs, no structural change,” then there is no path to the savings we need.

A temporary wage moderation or bonus restraint is not enough if the network still carries too much capacity and too much fixed cost.

If the workforce wants job security through 2030, then that security must rest on a business model that can actually support it. Security cannot be declared into existence while the company remains uncompetitive.

## To the state government
I appreciate your concern for the regional anchor role, but I cannot accept the idea that political stewardship means freezing industrial structure. The company is not a museum.

Yes, we must preserve industrial capability in Germany. But preserving capability is **not the same as preserving every site in its current form**. If a site is structurally uneconomic, then refusing any adjustment simply pushes the burden into weaker margins, lower investment, and ultimately a worse future for the region as well.

## To the controlling family and investors
I agree with the principle that the company needs a **real** restructuring, not a symbolic one. A cosmetic deal would be unacceptable.

But I also need support for a package that is not paralyzed by veto politics. If we are to restore competitiveness, we need an outcome that is durable, enforceable, and financially meaningful. That means real savings from labour, capacity, and structure — not just rhetoric about long-term value.

## On the expert’s contribution
The expert was useful, but the conclusion is not what the employee side is implying.

Yes, alternatives to closure can reduce costs.  
No, they do **not automatically** replace the economics of closing or deeply downsizing a structurally underutilized plant.

That is the critical point. The expert did **not** validate the claim that all German sites can remain open and still deliver the scale of savings management needs. The expert said the opposite risk is real: if overcapacity is structural and persistent, then avoiding closures may leave us with a costly half-solution.

That is the reality we have to face.

## What I am prepared to discuss
I remain open on the **instrument**. I am not fixated on closures as a fetish. I am focused on outcomes.

So I am prepared to discuss:
- working-time adjustments,
- redeployment,
- qualification,
- voluntary exit programs,
- product and capacity reallocation,
- targeted investment commitments,
- and other measures that deliver real, durable savings.

But I need a package that meets three tests:
1. **It materially reduces the cost base**
2. **It reduces overcapacity in a durable way**
3. **It restores the brand’s return-on-sales path and investment capacity**

If a proposal does not do that, it is not acceptable.

## What I am not prepared to accept
I will not accept:
- a blanket no-closure rule,
- a blanket no-redundancy rule,
- or a settlement that protects every legacy structure while the company remains uncompetitive.

I will also not accept prolonged strikes as a negotiating tactic. That would damage the company, the workforce, and the credibility of all sides. But if others insist on blocking real structural change, then they should be clear that they are choosing delay over viability.

## My bottom line
I want a negotiated settlement. But it must be a **real restructuring**, not a symbolic one.

That means:
- a substantial and lasting reduction in overcapacity,
- a genuine labour-cost contribution,
- structural improvement in plant economics,
- and a credible path to 2030 investment and competitiveness.

If the employee side can bring forward a package that delivers that without closures, I will examine it seriously. But if the answer is simply “keep everything open and make the numbers work somehow,” then I have to say plainly: **that does not solve the crisis**.
