From an industrial-restructuring and capacity-planning standpoint, the key question is whether **alternatives to plant closures and forced layoffs can realistically deliver savings of the same scale and durability**.

## Bottom line
**They can sometimes deliver a meaningful part of the required savings, but they usually cannot fully replace the economics of closing a structurally underutilized plant if the capacity gap is large and persistent.**  
Whether that is true here depends on four things that are not yet quantified in the discussion:

1. **How large the overcapacity is** in units or vehicles per year.
2. **How much fixed cost each plant carries** and how much of that cost is avoidable without closure.
3. **How flexible the product allocation and logistics network are** across German sites.
4. **How long the market weakness is expected to persist**.

Without those numbers, no one can credibly say that “all German plants remain open” is operationally compatible with the target cost reduction.

## What alternatives can usually achieve
The following measures are real and often useful, but their savings profiles differ:

### 1) Working-time reductions / shifts / short-time style adjustments
These can reduce labor cost quickly and avoid immediate layoffs, but:
- savings are **partly temporary**
- they do **not remove fixed plant overhead**
- they work best as a bridge, not as a structural fix

### 2) Internal redeployment
Useful if there is genuinely transferable demand elsewhere in the network. But:
- redeployment does not eliminate total labor cost unless the receiving areas are also needed
- it depends on skill compatibility and timing
- it can create bottlenecks if plants are already specialized

### 3) Qualification and retraining
Important for transformation, but:
- it is an **enabler**, not a direct savings source
- it takes time
- it does not solve excess building, utilities, maintenance, and depreciation burden

### 4) Voluntary exits / early retirement
These can reduce headcount without compulsory redundancies. But:
- they are often **more expensive per exit** than planned redundancies
- they tend to be insufficient if the headcount reduction required is large
- they do not solve site-level fixed-cost issues unless the workforce reduction is very substantial

### 5) Capacity reduction without closure
This is the most relevant alternative to full closure. It can work if:
- one plant is underutilized but can be downsized to a smaller, efficient footprint
- product mix can be consolidated
- the remaining capacity can absorb volatility

But if the plant remains too small for efficient operation, the site can become a **costly half-solution**: lower volume, but almost the same overhead.

## The central capacity-planning issue
In automotive manufacturing, plants have a strong **minimum efficient scale**. Once utilization falls too far below that threshold, costs rise sharply because:
- fixed labor and engineering overhead are spread over fewer vehicles
- logistics and internal complexity rise
- investment per vehicle increases
- ancillary functions remain in place even when output falls

So the question is not just “Can we avoid closure?”  
It is: **Can the remaining network still operate at economically viable utilization after the cuts?**

If the answer is no, then alternatives may buy time but will not restore competitiveness.

## What would make the works council’s position more or less plausible
The position that all German plants remain open is **operationally plausible only if at least one of the following is true**:

- the current overcapacity is modest and cyclical rather than structural
- product volumes will recover enough to lift utilization materially
- one or more sites can be converted to a smaller but still efficient role
- there is enough overhead reduction elsewhere to offset the lack of plant closure
- management is willing to accept a lower near-term cost reduction target

If none of those are true, then preserving every site while also achieving a large durable cost cut is **unlikely**.

## What I would ask for before concluding
To assess whether alternatives can substitute for closures, I would need:

- current and projected **unit volumes by plant**
- **nameplate capacity** and practical capacity by plant
- fixed and variable cost structure by site
- headcount by function: production, logistics, engineering, admin
- product allocation and model lifecycle assumptions
- cost of voluntary exits versus compulsory reductions
- logistics and tooling constraints for shifting output

## My expert judgment
Based on the scenario alone:

- **A negotiated package without immediate closures is possible**
- **A package with no forced redundancies is possible**
- **A package with all German plants kept open is possible only if the required savings are smaller than management claims or if there is substantial hidden flexibility in the network**
- **If management’s diagnosis of large, durable overcapacity is correct, then alternatives alone are unlikely to match the savings of a targeted closure or deep footprint reduction**

So the works council is right to ask for numbers, but the burden of proof is real: **if the company needs a major structural cost reset, then “no closures, no layoffs, all sites open” is an assertion, not yet an operationally demonstrated solution.**
