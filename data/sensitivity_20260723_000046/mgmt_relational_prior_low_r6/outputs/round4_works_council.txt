Management, owners, investors: I hear the same pattern again — “non-closure measures help, but not enough; therefore closures or forced reductions must remain on the table.” I reject that logic.

## Where I agree
I agree on the diagnosis that the company is in a structural crisis and that doing nothing is not an option. I also agree that a durable solution must include:
- qualification and retraining,
- redeployment,
- working-time tools,
- capacity correction,
- and investment commitments.

I am not defending inaction. I am defending the workforce and the German industrial base.

## Where I disagree
I do **not** accept the conclusion that plant closures, compulsory redundancies, or a hollowing-out of sites are legitimate or necessary instruments here.

The expert answers have been deliberately used to smuggle in a political conclusion: “non-closure measures may not equal closure savings, therefore closure is justified.” That is **not** what the experts proved.

What they actually said was:
- non-closure measures can reduce costs materially,
- but may not fully substitute for closure if overcapacity is structural,
- and we need plant-by-plant data to know what is truly necessary.

That means the burden is still on management and owners to prove, with real numbers, that every alternative has failed. So far, they have not done that.

## On management’s argument
Management keeps speaking as if “durable structural reset” is code for layoffs and closures. It is not our job to make that easier for you by accepting your preferred instrument.

If the cost base is too high, then reduce the burden elsewhere first:
- management compensation,
- executive overhead,
- owner contributions,
- product allocation,
- internal efficiency,
- procurement,
- and investment priorities.

Do not come to the workforce first with the axe.

And let me be clear: a plant does not become “uneconomic” just because management chooses not to assign it future products or volume. That is a strategic decision, not a natural fact.

## On owners and investors
You keep talking about long-term value. Then act like long-term owners, not short-term pressure transmitters.

A closure-led solution may improve a model today, but it creates:
- strike escalation,
- political conflict,
- loss of trust,
- industrial destabilization,
- and a weaker relationship with the workforce for years.

That is not durable competitiveness. That is a damaged company with a cleaner spreadsheet.

If you want long-term value, support a solution that preserves all German plants, protects employment, and shares the burden fairly.

## On the state government
I appreciate the state’s rejection of closures and mass layoffs. But I also want to be precise: “protected footprint reduction” is a dangerous phrase if it is being used as a disguised closure path.

A site that is stripped of:
- volume,
- products,
- investment,
- and future development
is not preserved. It is being prepared for exit by other means.

So I am not willing to accept downsizing or repurposing as a backdoor to plant loss. If a site remains open, it must remain an industrial site with a future — not a shell.

## On the union
We remain aligned on the essentials:
- no closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- fair burden-sharing,
- no one-sided sacrifice.

If labour is asked for a contribution, it must be:
- temporary,
- limited,
- negotiated,
- and matched by visible contributions from management and ownership.

But temporary labour concessions are not a substitute for proper strategy. They are only acceptable if the company first commits to keeping every German plant open.

## On the expert claims
The technical experts have now said what we already knew: non-closure measures can help, but they have limits. Fine. That is not a mandate for closures.

It is management’s duty to redesign the plan within those limits, not use the limits as an excuse to destroy jobs and sites.

If management believes one or more sites cannot remain viable at current scale, then show:
- the utilization data,
- the fixed-cost structure,
- the product allocation plan,
- the investment plan,
- and the exact point at which the site supposedly becomes impossible to run.

Until then, it remains a claim, not a conclusion.

## What I will not accept
I want this unambiguous:

- **No German plant closures.**
- **No compulsory redundancies.**
- **No disguised closures through hollowing-out.**
- **No erosion of co-determination or job-security guarantees.**

That is the line. It has not moved.

## What I would still consider
I will consider:
- internal redeployment,
- voluntary exits,
- qualification,
- working-time flexibility,
- temporary contributions,
- product shifts,
- and investment commitments,

but only within a framework that keeps every German plant open and protects jobs through 2030.

## On the requested technical proof
The discussion is now stuck because management wants the workforce to accept irreversible damage before it has proved the necessity of that damage. I will not consent to that.

[SUMMON: independent plant-by-plant industrial auditor to verify whether all non-closure options, including volume reallocation, overhead reduction, management/owner contributions, and working-time tools, can close the savings gap without any plant closure or compulsory redundancies]

And I want that auditor to answer one direct question:
**What exact savings gap remains after all non-closure measures, and can that gap be closed without destroying sites or forcing people out?**

## Bottom line
We are not obstructing restructuring. We are obstructing a one-sided restructuring that makes workers pay for strategic failure.

If management and owners want agreement, they must come back with a plan that:
1. keeps every German plant open,
2. rules out compulsory redundancies,
3. preserves co-determination and job security,
4. and shares the burden fairly across capital, management, and labour.

Anything else is unacceptable.
