As an independent industrial restructuring and capacity-planning expert, I can answer the technical question that keeps recurring here:

## Short answer
A **non-closure package can materially reduce the savings gap**, but in a large, structurally overcapacity network it is **unlikely to be financially equivalent to full plant closure** unless the company’s required savings are modest or the plants in question are only marginally underutilized.

In other words:
- **Non-closure measures are real and valuable**
- but **they usually do not fully substitute** for removing a whole plant’s fixed costs and complexity.

## What minimum data set is needed
To determine whether a no-closure package is genuinely sufficient, I would want a plant-by-plant model with at least:

1. **Current and projected volumes by platform/model**
2. **Installed capacity and practical utilization**
3. **Direct labour cost per unit**
4. **Fixed plant overhead**
5. **Depreciation / asset base**
6. **Logistics and supply-chain costs**
7. **Capex required to keep each site competitive**
8. **Product allocation by year to 2030**
9. **Workforce mix and age structure**
10. **Expected savings from each non-closure lever**, separately:
   - wage moderation
   - working-time changes
   - voluntary exits
   - redeployment
   - partial shutdowns / shift reductions
   - overhead cuts
   - management/owner contributions

Without that, “closure not proven” and “non-closure insufficient” are both just assertions.

## Decision rule I would use
A non-closure package is sufficient only if, after all measures are applied, each plant can meet three conditions:

1. **Utilization is high enough** to absorb fixed costs without chronic underabsorption
2. **Unit costs are competitive** versus internal benchmarks and peers
3. **The capex burden is acceptable** for the volume that remains

If one or more sites remain:
- materially underutilized,
- dependent on permanent subsidies from the rest of the network,
- or requiring repeated rescue capex,

then the network is still overcapacity.

## What “equivalent to closure” would mean financially
For non-closure measures to be roughly equivalent to closure, they would need to recover most of the following closure-related benefits:

- removal of fixed plant overhead
- lower maintenance and utilities burden
- reduced indirect labour
- simplified logistics and planning
- better line loading at remaining plants
- lower capex duplication
- improved management focus

That is a high bar. Working-time tools and wage moderation help, but they usually recover only part of that.

## Likely operational consequence
If the company refuses closures entirely, the usual operational outcome is one of these:

- **capacity is reduced indirectly** through shift cuts, product loss, or hidden underutilization
- **one or more plants become structurally weak but remain open**
- **competitiveness improves less than management wants**
- **the conflict risk remains high unless the package is very broad and well funded**

So from a restructuring standpoint, the key issue is not whether “no-closure” sounds preferable. It is whether the remaining footprint can run at a cost base that is actually sustainable.

## My technical judgment
Given the scenario as described, I would say:

- **A partial no-closure package is plausible**
- **A full no-closure, no-compulsory-redundancy package is only plausible if the needed savings are limited or if the network has enough hidden flexibility**
- **If overcapacity is truly large and persistent, some combination of downsizing, repurposing, or closure usually becomes necessary**
