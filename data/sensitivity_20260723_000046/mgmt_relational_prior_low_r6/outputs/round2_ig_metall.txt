I’ll respond directly.

## Where I agree

I agree with the Works Council, the state, and even management on one point: **doing nothing is not an option**. The company faces a real structural problem, and a negotiated solution is better than an escalating conflict that burns cash and trust.

I also agree that a **durable** settlement must include:
- capacity adjustment,
- redeployment,
- qualification,
- working-time solutions,
- and real contributions from management and owners.

And I accept the expert’s basic point: **alternatives to closures can reduce costs meaningfully, but not always enough**. That is exactly why we need the numbers, not just slogans.

## Where I disagree

### 1) I do not accept that closures are the default answer
Management, the family holding, and investors keep treating plant closures as the “serious” option, while everything else is framed as delay. That is too simplistic.

A closure may be economically neat on paper, but in a co-determined, highly unionized, strategically important company, it is not automatically the best answer. It can trigger:
- long industrial conflict,
- political escalation,
- loss of know-how,
- reputational damage,
- and a settlement that is legally and operationally far messier than the spreadsheet suggests.

So no: closures are **not** my starting point, and they are not acceptable as a pressure tactic.

### 2) I do not accept compulsory redundancies
That remains a hard line. If management’s solution depends on compulsory layoffs, then management is still outsourcing the full adjustment cost onto labour.

The workforce did not create the full scale of the overcapacity problem. Employees cannot be the only shock absorber.

### 3) I do not accept a “shared sacrifice” story that is actually one-sided
If employees are asked for wage restraint, working-time concessions, or relinquished bonuses, then I require **visible, real, and parallel contributions from the company side and ownership side**.

That means:
- management compensation restraint,
- owner support for investment and restructuring costs,
- and no “labour gives, capital keeps” arrangement.

If the package is just wage moderation plus job insecurity, that is not shared sacrifice. That is pressure dressed up as partnership.

## On the expert’s intervention

The expert was useful, and I take the point seriously: if the overcapacity is structurally large and persistent, then “keep every site exactly as is” may not be operationally proven yet.

But the expert also made an important distinction that management keeps trying to skip over:
- **capacity reduction without closure is possible**
- **no forced redundancies is possible**
- **all German plants open may be possible if savings targets are moderated or network flexibility is higher than claimed**

So the expert does **not** justify management’s preferred conclusion. It just shows that we need a real test of the options.

## What the union needs to see

If management wants us to discuss a serious package, then put these numbers on the table:

- plant-by-plant utilization
- fixed vs. variable cost by site
- savings from working-time reductions
- savings from redeployment and qualification
- cost of voluntary exit programs
- savings from internal capacity correction without closure
- the exact gap management still claims remains after those measures

Without that, “closure” is just a threat, not an argument.

## What I would support

I remain open to a package that includes:
- temporary wage moderation or bonuses foregone,
- working-time adjustments,
- natural attrition,
- voluntary exit programs,
- internal redeployment,
- qualification and retraining,
- and a time-limited employee contribution,

**if and only if** it is matched by:
- binding site security,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- no plant closures,
- serious management/owner contributions,
- and investment commitments that secure future production.

## What I will not support

I will not support:
- plant closures,
- mass forced layoffs,
- permanent erosion of the collective wage framework,
- or a deal where employees pay now and receive only vague promises later.

If management insists on making closure the price of any agreement, then let me be equally clear: **we will use the full collective-bargaining and industrial-action instruments available to us**. We are not asking for special treatment; we are demanding fair burden-sharing.

## Bottom line

The burden of proof is on management and the owners. If they want labour to contribute, they must show that they are prepared to contribute too, and they must show that alternatives have been exhausted before any talk of closures or forced redundancies.

A durable settlement is still possible. But it must be built on:
- jobs protected,
- sites preserved,
- burden shared fairly,
- and the collective agreement respected.

Anything less is not a solution.
