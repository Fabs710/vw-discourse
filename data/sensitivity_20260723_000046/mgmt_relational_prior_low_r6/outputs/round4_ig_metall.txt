I’ll respond plainly.

## Where I agree
I agree with everyone on one point: **this crisis is real and doing nothing is not an option**.  
I also agree that a durable settlement will likely need some combination of:
- capacity correction,
- working-time measures,
- redeployment,
- retraining,
- product allocation changes,
- and contributions from more than one side.

And I agree that a prolonged conflict would damage the company and everyone around this table.

## Where I disagree
What I do **not** accept is the repeated attempt to turn “durable restructuring” into a synonym for:
- plant closures,
- compulsory redundancies,
- and permanent weakening of the collective wage framework.

That is not the only serious option. It is simply the option management, owners, and parts of the capital side prefer because it shifts the adjustment burden onto labour.

### To management
You keep saying the non-closure measures “help, but not enough.” That may be true in the abstract. It is **not yet proven** for this company, plant by plant, under real negotiated conditions.

You have not shown:
- the exact savings gap,
- what management and owners will contribute,
- what volume/product reallocations are possible,
- and whether the remaining gap truly cannot be closed without destroying sites or forcing people out.

So no, “closure remains on the table” is not a sufficient argument. It is a threat, not proof.

### To the controlling family and investors
You talk about long-term value, but you are still treating workers as the primary adjustment variable. That is not stewardship. If you want durable competitiveness, then put real capital and governance responsibility into the package:
- investment,
- transition funding,
- product commitments,
- overhead reduction outside the shop floor,
- and visible restraint on the ownership side.

If workers are expected to contribute, the burden must be shared. Not symbolically — materially.

### To the state government
I share your rejection of mass layoffs as a first response. But I need to be equally clear: “protected footprint reduction” cannot become a euphemism for hollowing out plants until they are closures in all but name. If a site loses its volume, products, and future, then it has not been protected.

### To the works council
We are aligned on the essentials:
- no compulsory redundancies,
- no one-sided sacrifice,
- no erosion of the collective framework,
- fair burden-sharing.

Where I want to sharpen the line is this: if the company needs capacity correction, then we should discuss **how** to do it without forced layoffs or plant closures — not whether labour alone should absorb the pain.

## What I am prepared to consider
I remain open to a serious package that includes:
- temporary wage moderation,
- bonus restraint,
- working-time flexibility,
- voluntary exit programs,
- redeployment,
- qualification/retraining,
- and targeted employee contributions,

**if and only if** it is matched by:
- binding job and site security,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- owner and management contributions,
- and a credible product and investment plan.

That is the condition for labour to contribute fairly. Not more, not less.

## What I am not prepared to accept
I will not accept:
- plant closures as the default tool,
- compulsory redundancies as a fallback,
- or a “protected downsizing” model that is really just staged site liquidation.

I also will not accept a settlement that weakens the collective wage framework permanently. Any labour contribution must be temporary, negotiated, and reversible if the company recovers.

## On the technical question
The debate is stuck because the parties are arguing from different assumptions. So let’s test those assumptions properly.

[SUMMON: independent plant-by-plant industrial and financial auditor to verify the savings gap under three scenarios — non-closure concessions, protected downsizing, and closure — including fixed-cost absorption, utilization, product allocation, management/owner contributions, and 2030 margin impact]

I want that auditor to answer one direct question:
**Can the required savings be achieved without closures and without compulsory redundancies, if management and ownership also contribute fairly?**

That is the only question that matters right now.

## Bottom line
The union is ready to negotiate a **real** restructuring.  
We are **not** ready to sign off on a restructuring that:
- closes plants,
- forces people out,
- and asks labour to carry most of the pain.

If management and owners want an agreement, they need to come back with a package that:
1. protects jobs,
2. rules out compulsory redundancies,
3. preserves the collective wage framework,
4. and shares the burden fairly.

Anything else will meet resistance.
