## To the Works Council
I agree with you on one point: this is a structural crisis, and doing nothing is not an option. I also agree that a prolonged conflict would damage output, trust, and value.

But I do **not** agree that “all German plants open” can be the starting and ending point of a credible restructuring. You keep treating plant preservation as the objective, when the real objective has to be a **competitive cost base by 2030**.

You say closures are not proven necessary. That is exactly why we asked for plant-by-plant data. But until the numbers show that non-closure measures can close the gap, your position remains a political red line, not an economic solution.

And I need to be clear: I will not accept a settlement that leaves us with:
- structurally excessive capacity,
- fixed costs that remain above target,
- and a network that is still too expensive to support the brand’s return-on-sales target.

That is not “protecting jobs.” That is postponing a harsher correction later.

## To the Industrial Union
I agree that labour should not be the sole shock absorber. I am not asking for a one-sided sacrifice. But I do **not** accept your refusal to contemplate compulsory measures in any circumstance.

If the company can be saved through voluntary exits, redeployment, working-time flexibility, and wage moderation, then that is preferable. But I will not rule out forced reductions if that is what a durable structural reset requires. We are under time pressure, and we cannot build a future on wishful thinking.

You also keep framing any mention of closures as a threat rather than a response to economics. That’s not persuasive. If a site is structurally uneconomic, then pretending otherwise does not protect the workforce in the long run.

## To the Regional State Government
I respect your role and your responsibility to the region. But I do **not** accept the idea that regional stewardship means preserving every site in its current form.

A strong industrial base is not maintained by freezing the footprint. It is maintained by keeping the company viable, profitable, and investable. If the current structure prevents that, then defending it is not stewardship — it is delay.

I am open to capacity reduction, repurposing, and phased adjustment. I am **not** open to a solution that makes the company structurally uncompetitive in order to avoid hard choices.

## To the Controlling Family Holding
I agree with you on the central point: we need a real restructuring, not a cosmetic one. I also agree that value destruction through endless conflict would be unacceptable.

But value is also destroyed by a settlement that leaves the cost base untouched. So I need you to be consistent: if you want enterprise value protected, then you have to support a package that actually reduces overcapacity and fixed cost.

I am not fixated on closures for their own sake. I am fixated on the outcome:
- a durable cost reset,
- a leaner footprint,
- and a path back to competitive margins.

If that can be achieved without closures, fine. But it has to be **proven**, not asserted.

## To Institutional Investors
I agree with you that the outcome must be durable, measurable, and execution-safe. I also agree that symbolic compromise is unacceptable.

Where I disagree is your repeated implication that because non-closure tools do not perfectly replicate closure economics, we should assume closures are therefore justified. That leap is too fast.

The right question is not whether non-closure measures are ideal in theory. The question is whether, with the right package, they can deliver enough structural savings to restore the economics. If they cannot, then more severe measures remain necessary. But that decision must rest on a credible plant-by-plant assessment.

## On the summoned expert findings
The expert input was useful, but it did **not** answer the question the employee side keeps pretending it answered.

What it said was:
- non-closure tools can materially reduce costs,
- but they usually do not fully eliminate plant fixed costs or subscale overhead,
- and if overcapacity is structural, deeper footprint reduction may be needed.

That is not a validation of the status quo. It is a warning that partial measures may fall short.

And that is exactly why I requested a plant-by-plant financial model. I need to know:
- where the fixed-cost problem sits,
- which sites can be made viable,
- where capacity must come out,
- and what the 2030 margin impact is under each scenario.

Without that, we are debating slogans, not strategy.

## My position, plainly
I remain open to a negotiated solution. But it must satisfy three conditions:

1. **Durable structural cost reduction**
2. **Meaningful labour-cost contribution**
3. **A footprint that is economically viable by 2030**

I will consider different instruments to get there:
- working-time changes,
- redeployment,
- voluntary exits,
- qualification,
- product reallocation,
- targeted investment commitments,
- and site-specific restructuring.

But I will **not** accept:
- a blanket no-closure rule,
- a blanket no-redundancy rule,
- or a package that leaves the company with an uncompetitive cost base.

If the employee side wants to avoid closures, then bring a package that genuinely closes the savings gap. If it does not, then we will not pretend that a cosmetic compromise is a solution.
