From the perspective of dispersed capital-market shareholders, the key issue is not who “wins” the conflict, but whether the final outcome credibly restores **competitive margins, cash flow, and investability**. On that standard, I see some useful realism in the positions so far — but also a lot of language that risks producing another expensive, ambiguous settlement.

## Where we agree

### With management
We agree with the basic diagnosis: this is not just a cyclical dip. If German costs remain structurally above peer levels, the company will keep missing return-on-sales targets and the equity will continue to be discounted. A cosmetic adjustment is not enough.

We also agree that:
- voluntary exits, redeployment, working-time flexibility, and qualification should be part of the toolbox;
- the company needs durable savings, not a temporary truce;
- future investment in products and technology matters.

### With the family holding
We agree that a solution must be **durable** and must materially improve competitiveness. A deal that preserves the current cost base and simply defers the pain would destroy value.

### With the state government and workforce
We agree that the workforce should not be treated as the only adjustment variable. If employees are asked to contribute, management and owners must also show discipline and commitment.

## Where we disagree

### To the works council and union
Your “no plant closures, no compulsory redundancies, no dismantling of protections” line is understandable politically, but as a capital provider it is not enough on its own.

If that position effectively prevents real capacity adjustment, then it risks locking in the very cost structure that is destroying margins. I would not support an outcome that preserves all sites and all guarantees while leaving the company structurally uncompetitive.

I am also not interested in a prolonged industrial conflict that burns cash, damages output, and creates uncertainty for quarters on end. Strike risk is value-destructive. But so is pretending that the existing footprint can simply be frozen and financed through goodwill.

### To management
I agree with the diagnosis, but I am not signing up for a strategy that is all threat and no executable settlement. If management pushes closures and compulsion without a credible route through co-determination and governance, it may be right economically but still fail operationally.

In other words: a “hard” plan that cannot be implemented is just as bad as a soft one that does nothing.

### To the family holding and state
I share the concern about governance reality, but I do not want the outcome to become a lowest-common-denominator compromise designed to avoid confrontation rather than restore returns. Shareholders will not reward symbolism. They will reward a visible, measurable improvement in margins, capacity utilization, and free cash flow.

## What would move us
We would support a settlement only if it includes all of the following:

1. **A quantified, enforceable cost-out package**
   - not aspirational language, but clear numbers;
   - savings large enough to restore competitiveness.

2. **Real capacity reduction**
   - if demand and economics require it, then some combination of plant rationalization, line consolidation, or material footprint reduction must be on the table;
   - if plant closures are ruled out, then the alternative must still achieve the same economic effect.

3. **Durable labour-cost improvement**
   - temporary moderation is not enough if the structural gap remains;
   - savings must be sufficient and lasting.

4. **No open-ended delay**
   - the market cannot wait through a long standoff;
   - a quick, credible agreement is worth more than an elaborate process that drags on.

5. **Protection of long-term value**
   - investment in future products and technology is necessary;
   - but only if it is funded by a leaner operating base.

## Our limit
What we will not support is any outcome that:
- keeps the cost base uncompetitive,
- leaves margins structurally impaired,
- or substitutes a political compromise for an economically credible restructuring.

That is the line for capital markets. We are not asking for maximal pain. We are asking for **credible discipline** and a path back to peer-level returns.

## Bottom line
We are open to a negotiated settlement, but only if it produces **hard, durable, measurable competitiveness gains**. If the final package is merely a face-saving arrangement that avoids closures while not delivering equivalent savings, we will view that as value destruction — and we will respond accordingly through capital allocation and market pressure.
