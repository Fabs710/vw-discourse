We are at the point where slogans are no longer enough. So let me respond directly.

## First: what I agree with
I agree with several points raised by management, the family holding, and the state:

- The company has a **real structural competitiveness problem**.
- A **durable** solution is required; a short patch will not do.
- **Voluntary instruments**, redeployment, qualification, working-time flexibility, and retirement corridors must be part of the package.
- Future products, EV investment, and capacity utilization matter.
- The workforce should **not** bear the adjustment alone.

On those points, there is common ground.

## Second: where I firmly disagree
### 1) Plant closures are not the baseline
To management, the family holding, and investors: you keep describing closures as if they were a neutral technical option. They are not. They are a direct attack on jobs, know-how, and trust.

We do **not** accept “equivalent footprint reduction” as code for shutting down German plants. If you want a serious settlement, then say clearly how you will solve overcapacity **without** closures.

### 2) Compulsory redundancies are not acceptable
You keep insisting on keeping forced layoffs “available if needed.” That is not a good-faith settlement offer. It is leverage against the workforce.

Our position is clear: **no compulsory redundancies**. Adjustment has to happen through negotiated, reversible, and socially responsible measures. If management cannot design a package within that framework, then the package is not acceptable.

### 3) The collective bargaining framework cannot be the casualty
We are willing to talk about employee contributions. We are **not** willing to dismantle the collective wage framework or turn a temporary crisis into a permanent lowering of labour standards.

If labour is asked to contribute, then that contribution must be:
- **temporary**,
- **proportionate**,
- **shared fairly**,
- and tied to **binding guarantees** on jobs, sites, and investment.

## Where I disagree with each party

### To management
Your argument is too one-sided. You talk about competitiveness, but you are not putting enough on the table from management and capital. Workers did not create the market shift alone, and they will not accept being the only adjustment variable.

If you want serious cooperation, we need:
- a quantified savings gap,
- a transparent explanation of why voluntary measures are allegedly insufficient,
- and a concrete package of management/owner contributions.

Without that, “necessary restructuring” sounds like “workers pay, others decide.”

### To the family holding
You are right that the company must remain competitive. But you are wrong if you think the answer is to keep closures and forced measures in reserve and wait for labour to concede.

If you want a durable settlement, use your governance influence to push management toward a **no-closure, no-forced-layoff** solution. If that cannot be done, then you are effectively shifting the burden downward onto the workforce.

### To the regional state
We appreciate the political and industrial responsibility you are carrying, and we note your rejection of rushed closures. But “balanced” cannot mean that workers give up protections while management keeps maximum flexibility.

If the state wants to protect the industrial base, it should back a package that secures:
- domestic production,
- investments,
- qualifications,
- and employment stability.

### To the investors
You are right that a deal must be credible and measurable. But if your “credibility” depends on the threat of closures and compulsory redundancies, then you are asking for a deal that may be economically tidy on paper and socially explosive in reality.

A strike, a governance conflict, or a collapse in trust would also destroy value. So do not treat labour rights as an obstacle to be brushed aside.

## What we are prepared to discuss
We are prepared to negotiate a package that includes:

- working-time flexibility,
- temporary wage moderation or waiver of certain extras,
- voluntary exit and transfer programs,
- internal redeployment,
- qualification and retraining,
- productivity measures,
- and a negotiated employee contribution.

But those talks only make sense if they are matched by:

- **binding site guarantees**,
- **binding commitments on future products and investment**,
- **no compulsory redundancies**,
- **no plant closures**,
- and a **clear role for the union and works council in monitoring implementation**.

## What would have to be on the table from management and owners
If you want our members to make sacrifices, then we need to see sacrifices from the other side too:

1. **Management contribution**
   - overhead reduction at top levels,
   - compensation restraint,
   - a visible contribution from leadership.

2. **Owner contribution**
   - willingness to support the transformation with real capital and strategic commitment,
   - not just demands for labour to absorb the shock.

3. **Investment commitment**
   - future products,
   - EV allocation,
   - utilization of German sites,
   - and guarantees that the restructuring is not a prelude to gradual hollowing-out.

## My bottom line
We are not blocking change. We are blocking a **one-sided restructuring**.

If the proposal is:
- no closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- fair contributions from management, owners, and workers,
- and a credible industrial future,

then we can negotiate seriously.

If the proposal remains:
- closures,
- forced layoffs,
- and permanent erosion of labour standards,

then there is no agreement from us, and members will understand exactly why.

If management wants to move this forward, it should come back with:
- the quantified savings gap,
- the exact capacity measures it wants,
- and the concrete guarantees it is prepared to sign.

Until then, the union will not soften its line.
