I will speak plainly as the regional state government and anchor shareholder.

## First: what I agree with

I agree with management, the family holding, and investors on one point: **this company has a structural competitiveness problem**. This is not a temporary dip, and a durable solution is needed.

I also agree with the expert that a no-closure solution is **not cheap** and not symbolic. If we want to avoid plant closures, then the package must be deep: capacity reduction, headcount reduction, complexity cuts, overhead cuts, and a real contribution from labour, management, and owners.

And I agree with the workforce side that **workers must not carry the whole burden alone**. A settlement that asks the state to protect industrial stability while management and capital simply demand sacrifice would be neither fair nor stable.

## Where I disagree

### To management
I do **not** accept your repeated implication that plant closures and compulsory redundancies are the natural default and that everything else is merely “soft.”

That is not a neutral economic fact. It is a choice of instrument.

The expert has already made clear that a **no-closure path is possible if it is deep enough**. So before talking about closures as the serious option, management needs to put a fully quantified no-closure package on the table.

Right now, what I hear is:
- “we need savings,”
- “we need capacity cuts,”
- “we need labour contribution,”

but not yet a package that shows why German closures are genuinely unavoidable.

If you want the state to consider harsher structural measures, then show us:
- the exact annual savings gap,
- the required FTE reduction,
- the exact capacity reduction,
- the management and owner contribution,
- and the investment commitments that justify such pain.

Without that, closures remain a preferred management instrument, not a demonstrated necessity.

### To the works council and union
I support your refusal to accept a one-sided sacrifice plan. That is legitimate.

But I will also be direct: **“no closures, no compulsory redundancies, no losses, no downside” is not enough as a restructuring answer**. It is a boundary, not a solution.

If we want to preserve plants and jobs in the state, then the alternative has to be operationally serious:
- substantial voluntary exits,
- meaningful redeployment,
- working-time flexibility,
- labour-cost moderation,
- simplification of production,
- and measurable capacity reduction.

So I support your red lines on closures and forced layoffs **only if** the alternative is genuinely strong enough to preserve competitiveness. If not, the risk is simply delayed decline.

### To the controlling family holding
I appreciate that you want durability and value protection. But I do **not** accept the logic that closures should stay as the benchmark of seriousness from the outset.

The benchmark is whether the company can restore competitiveness **while preserving its regional anchor role as far as possible**.

That means the family should use its governance role to force management to test the no-closure route seriously, not merely keep closure threats alive as leverage.

If the no-closure package is truly closure-equivalent, then it deserves support. If it is not, then it is not because labour is “unreasonable”; it is because management has not yet made the case.

### To the institutional investors
You are right to reject a cosmetic deal. So do I.

But I do **not** accept the idea that only closures prove seriousness. That is too crude. The real test is:
- durable savings,
- better margins,
- lower fixed cost,
- and a credible future for the company.

If those can be achieved without plant closures, that is not weakness. It is better risk management and better long-term value creation.

## What would move me

I will support a negotiated restructuring if it contains all of the following:

1. **A quantified savings plan**
   - with the savings gap stated clearly.

2. **A real capacity and FTE reduction plan**
   - not just attrition rhetoric.

3. **A binding no-closure commitment for a defined period**
   - paired with measurable milestones.

4. **No compulsory redundancies during that period**
   - adjustment through voluntary and negotiated tools first.

5. **Visible management and owner contribution**
   - overhead restraint, capital support, and strategic commitment.

6. **Binding investment and product commitments**
   - especially for German sites and future technologies.

7. **A real co-determination role**
   - not post-facto consultation.

## What I cannot accept

I cannot accept:
- a closure-first strategy presented as inevitability,
- a restructuring that gutts regional employment,
- a deal where workers bear the main pain while others merely demand “discipline,”
- or a settlement that sidelines the state’s co-determination and veto role.

That last point is not negotiable. The governance structure is part of the company’s reality. It must be respected, not treated as an inconvenience.

## On the “trigger” idea
I am open to hard review points, but only if they are designed as **control mechanisms**, not as a back door to plant closures and forced layoffs later.

If trigger-based review means:
- verify whether the package is delivering,
- adjust specific measures if targets are missed,
- and protect the agreed site and job framework,

then that is acceptable.

If it means:
- “agree now, reopen closures later,”

then no. That is not a safeguard. That is delayed pressure.

## My position, plainly
As the regional state government and anchor shareholder, I support:

- a **serious negotiated restructuring**,
- a **deep no-closure test**,
- real burden-sharing,
- and a solution that protects employment, plants, and the region’s industrial base.

I do **not** support:
- plant closures as the default answer,
- compulsory redundancies as a normal instrument,
- or a settlement that weakens the company’s role as a regional economic anchor.

So the next step should be simple: management puts forward a **fully quantified no-closure package**. Then we test it against the economic gap. If it works, we have a path forward. If it does not, then the burden is on management to prove why harsher measures are unavoidable.

Until that is done, I will continue to oppose any premature closure logic.
