I appreciate the clarity from all sides. From the perspective of the controlling family holding, I want to be equally clear: **we will support a restructuring only if it restores competitiveness in a durable way without creating permanent value destruction or a governance crisis.**

That means I see merit in parts of what each of you have said, but I do **not** support a process that simply preserves the current cost structure and then calls that a compromise.

## Where I agree

### With management
I agree with the core diagnosis: the company has a **structural cost and capacity problem**, not just a temporary earnings dip. If German operations remain materially above target cost levels, then the company will keep underperforming, and that hurts the long-term value of the whole group.

I also agree that:
- voluntary exits, redeployment, qualification, and working-time flexibility should be used,
- a durable solution is needed, not a quarter-to-quarter patch,
- and future product investment must remain fundable.

### With the works council and union
I agree that the workforce did not create this situation alone and that **fair burden-sharing** matters. A restructuring that asks employees to absorb all the pain while management and capital do nothing would be neither sustainable nor acceptable.

I also agree that:
- a negotiated approach is preferable to a prolonged open conflict,
- site security and credible investment commitments are important,
- and the company should not treat co-determination as a formality.

### With the regional state government
I agree that this company has a major industrial and regional responsibility, and that a purely financial “cut and move on” logic would be short-sighted. The asset is too important for that.

## Where I disagree

### On “no plant closures” as an absolute
I do **not** share the view that every German site must be protected regardless of economics.

If capacity is structurally too high, then the company cannot rule out rationalization in advance. A promise that all plants stay open no matter what may sound stabilizing, but it can also become a promise of **ongoing underperformance**. That would erode the company’s value and ultimately threaten more jobs than it protects.

So I do not accept an unconditional no-closure line.

### On “no compulsory redundancies” as an absolute
I understand the preference, and I would also prefer that we avoid forced measures. But I will not support a framework that precludes compulsion under all circumstances.

If negotiated instruments and voluntary measures are enough, then good. If they are not, the company must retain the ability to act. Otherwise, management is asked to carry the restructuring burden with one hand tied behind its back.

### On job-security guarantees through 2030
I do not think a long guarantee can be the starting point unless there is a credible economic package behind it.

A guarantee without structural adjustment just delays the problem. We should not mistake postponement for protection.

### On co-determination and veto rights
Co-determination is a fact of governance, and it must be respected. But it cannot be turned into a mechanism that blocks any meaningful adjustment. The company needs a path that can actually be executed.

At the same time, I would say to management: a confrontation strategy that ignores the board reality and the special statutory constraints is equally unrealistic. Governance is not an inconvenience; it is the structure within which a viable solution must be built.

## What I think is necessary

The family holding will only support a solution if it contains **all three** of the following elements:

1. **Real competitiveness gains**
   - substantial and durable cost reduction,
   - productivity improvement,
   - capacity alignment with demand.

2. **Socially and governance-wise executable change**
   - use of voluntary measures first,
   - negotiated solutions where possible,
   - no unnecessary escalation into a drawn-out conflict.

3. **A credible future for the core asset**
   - investment in future products and technology,
   - clear utilization logic for the remaining German footprint,
   - a framework that protects long-term value, not just short-term peace.

## My position on the current lines being drawn

### To management
If your package depends on presenting closures and compulsory redundancies as the default answer, you should not expect automatic backing from the controlling owners. I will not endorse a path that risks a destructive governance fight unless you can show that alternatives truly fail.

But I also will not endorse a cosmetic compromise that leaves the company uncompetitive. If the final package does not materially close the cost gap, it will not have my support.

### To works council and union
Your insistence on fairness is legitimate. Your absolute rejection of closures and compulsory redundancy is understandable, but I do **not** accept it as the decisive framework for the entire company.

If labour wants to avoid harsher measures, it needs to come forward with a package that actually delivers enough savings and flexibility. Saying “no” to the hard parts is not a complete strategy.

### To the regional state
I understand your political constraints, and I recognize your role as an anchor shareholder. But the state should not mistake its responsibility for a license to freeze the industrial structure. Protecting the company sometimes requires allowing difficult decisions.

## Bottom line

The controlling family holding supports **deep restructuring if necessary**, but only as part of a credible plan that:
- restores margins,
- preserves long-term value,
- respects governance realities,
- and minimizes instability.

I am **not** prepared to back a plan that:
- permanently protects all sites regardless of economics,
- rules out compulsory measures in all cases,
- and leaves the structural cost problem unsolved.

At the same time, I am also **not** asking for a reckless confrontation. I want a durable settlement, but one that actually changes the company’s trajectory.

If the next proposal can show how to achieve the needed savings while preserving execution stability, then we can engage seriously. If not, I will not support a settlement that simply delays the crisis.
