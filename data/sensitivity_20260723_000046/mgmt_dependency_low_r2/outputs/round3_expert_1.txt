From an operations and restructuring perspective, the key issue is now clear:

## 1) The real decision test
A **no-closure restructuring** can work **only if it delivers closure-equivalent savings** in a durable way.

That means the package must close the gap on three dimensions:
- **fixed-cost reduction**
- **capacity reduction**
- **headcount reduction**

If it does not, then it is not an equivalent alternative; it is a delay.

---

## 2) What “closure-equivalent” usually means in numbers
For a large automotive plant, a medium site closure often produces roughly:

- **€100m to €300m per year** in recurring savings, depending on size, shift structure, and degree of local overhead eliminated
- plus one-time restructuring costs that can be substantial:
  - severance
  - decommissioning
  - transfer costs
  - write-downs
  - logistics reconfiguration

A no-closure package can match that only if it combines several measures at scale:

### Typical savings levers
- **Shift elimination / utilization cuts:**  
  Often worth **€20m–€80m per eliminated shift line**, depending on site structure.

- **Headcount reduction via voluntary exits / attrition / retirement corridors:**  
  A fully loaded automotive manufacturing employee often costs roughly **€70k–€100k per year** all-in, sometimes more with overhead allocation.  
  So:
  - **1,000 FTE** reduction ≈ **€70m–€100m/year**
  - **2,000 FTE** reduction ≈ **€140m–€200m/year**

- **Working-time moderation / temporary wage concessions:**  
  Useful, but usually not enough alone unless very large.  
  A 5% labour-cost moderation on a €2bn wage base would be about **€100m/year**, but that assumes broad coverage and real permanence.

- **Overhead and complexity cuts:**  
  Often **€30m–€100m/year** if management layers, indirect functions, and product complexity are materially reduced.

So to replace one medium closure, the company typically needs some combination like:
- **1,500–3,000 FTE reduction**
- **one or more shift eliminations**
- **material overhead reduction**
- **product simplification**
- **some labour-cost moderation**

That is a serious restructuring, not a symbolic one.

---

## 3) Comparative trade-offs

### Option A: Plant closure / footprint reduction
**Pros**
- Fastest path to structural savings
- Clear fixed-cost elimination
- Strong signal to capital markets
- Usually strongest in economic certainty

**Cons**
- High one-time cost
- Very high execution and governance risk
- Industrial conflict risk
- Knowledge loss and supply-chain disruption
- Potentially impossible under co-determination veto structure

**Operational conclusion:**  
Best on pure economics, worst on executability if governance blocks it.

---

### Option B: No closures, but deep internal downsizing
**Pros**
- More executable under co-determination
- Lower political and social disruption
- Preserves plant assets and know-how
- Can be phased and reversible

**Cons**
- Requires very large employee contribution
- Savings are harder to lock in
- Risk of underdelivery if measures are voluntary only
- Can preserve structural inefficiencies if not deep enough

**Operational conclusion:**  
Viable only if it includes hard capacity and FTE reductions, not just flexibility.

---

### Option C: Hybrid package
This is often the most realistic in a governance-constrained environment.

Typical elements:
- no immediate closures
- one or two shift eliminations
- concentrated footprint rationalization inside plants
- voluntary exit corridor plus retirement wave
- limited temporary labour-cost moderation
- management overhead cuts
- product complexity reduction
- binding review triggers

**Pros**
- More executable than full closure plan
- Can generate meaningful recurring savings
- Allows time for redeployment and investment

**Cons**
- Requires strict monitoring
- May still fall short if too many protections are absolute
- Can become a postponement mechanism if targets are vague

**Operational conclusion:**  
This is usually the best compromise if and only if targets are quantified and binding.

---

## 4) What would make a no-closure package credible
If I were assessing feasibility, I would look for these minimum features:

### A. Quantified savings target
A deal needs a stated annual run-rate savings target, for example:
- **€200m/year**
- **€300m/year**
- or whatever the gap is versus target margin

Without this, there is no way to judge equivalence.

### B. FTE target
A credible package should specify:
- gross headcount reduction
- net headcount reduction
- timing by year
- share achieved through voluntary exits

A vague promise of “efficiency” is not enough.

### C. Capacity target
There should be a measurable footprint reduction:
- fewer shifts
- lower planned output
- plant specialization
- line consolidation

If capacity stays materially unchanged, savings often leak away.

### D. Overhead target
Management should also commit to:
- indirect headcount cuts
- organizational flattening
- central overhead reduction

Workforce measures alone rarely close the full gap.

### E. Timing
Savings must arrive fast enough to matter.  
A package that delivers only in year 4 or 5 is often too slow for a balance-sheet and margin repair.

---

## 5) The main risks in the current posture
From a restructuring mechanics standpoint, the risks are:

### Risk 1: Both sides overstate their position
- Management may overstate the necessity of closures as leverage.
- Labour may overstate what can be achieved without any hard structural change.

### Risk 2: Voluntary-only measures underdeliver
Attrition and voluntary exits are useful, but usually insufficient alone unless the age profile is favorable and the time horizon is long.

### Risk 3: Temporary labour concessions expire too early
If the core cost problem is structural, temporary moderation does not solve it unless matched with permanent productivity and capacity gains.

### Risk 4: Governance delay destroys value
If the company waits too long for a perfect agreement, it can lose output, talent, and market share while still not getting the savings.

---

## 6) Practical sequencing that would make sense
A workable restructuring sequence in this type of environment is usually:

1. **Quantify the gap transparently**
   - savings required
   - FTE required
   - capacity required
   - timing required

2. **Offer voluntary and negotiated measures first**
   - retirement corridors
   - internal mobility
   - qualification
   - working-time adjustments
   - voluntary exits

3. **Bundle with structural operational change**
   - shift elimination
   - line consolidation
   - complexity reduction
   - overhead restructuring

4. **Tie concessions to investment commitments**
   - product allocation
   - EV/next-gen platform investment
   - site utilization plan

5. **Use hard checkpoints**
   - if savings miss targets, predefined measures are activated
   - this is much better than reopening the entire agreement

That sequencing is more executable than a pure closure-first or pure guarantee-only model.

---

## 7) Bottom-line assessment
If the question is:

**Can the company achieve closure-level savings without closures and forced layoffs?**

My answer is:

**Yes, but only under strict conditions.**

Those conditions are:
- substantial FTE reduction
- real shift and capacity cuts
- permanent cost relief, not just temporary concessions
- management overhead reduction
- quantified targets and monitoring
- a credible implementation timetable

If those are not in the package, then a no-closure approach will almost certainly fall short of what management and shareholders are asking for.
