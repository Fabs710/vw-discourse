[Works Council (Betriebsrat) (Plant-level employee representation under co-determination) — Round 1]
As the Works Council, our position is clear and non-negotiable on the essentials:

- **No plant closures in Germany.**
- **No compulsory redundancies.**
- **No unilateral dismantling of the historic job-security framework.**

The workforce did not create this crisis alone, and it cannot be made to pay for it alone. We are prepared to discuss a restructuring that secures the company’s future, but only if it is based on **fair burden-sharing**, **binding site guarantees**, and a **credible industrial strategy** rather than a short-term cost-cutting exercise.

What we will argue for:
1. **All German plants remain open.**
2. **Job guarantees are extended through 2030.**
3. **Adjustments are handled through natural attrition, internal redeployment, qualification, working-time measures, and voluntary instruments — not forced layoffs.**
4. **Any cost contribution from labour must be matched by real commitments from management and owners on investment, future products, and site security.**
5. **The works council and employee representatives must have a real role in shaping the restructuring, not just being informed after decisions are taken.**

What we oppose:
- Plant closures or relocation of production out of Germany.
- Mass layoffs or operational dismissals.
- Breaking existing protections while asking the workforce for sacrifices.
- A fait accompli presented as “there is no alternative.”

We are ready to negotiate seriously, and we understand the competitive pressure. But if management comes with closures, forced redundancies, or the destruction of hard-won guarantees, then it will meet firm resistance — including industrial action and use of all co-determination rights available to us.

I would prefer to avoid forced redundancies, but I will not promise that nothing compulsory can happen regardless of the size of the gap. If the savings required cannot be achieved through negotiated instruments alone, then we must retain the ability to act.

#### 3) A job-security guarantee through 2030 without structural cost relief is not realistic
A guarantee can only exist if the economics support it. A blanket extension of protections while leaving the cost base untouched would simply lock in uncompetitiveness.

### What I need to see

- **A substantial, durable reduction in labour costs**
- **A real reduction in capacity and overheads**
- **A meaningful contribution from the workforce**
- **Clear productivity improvements**
- **Credible room for investment in future products and technology**

That means we need to discuss:
- wage moderation or other labour-cost concessions,
- longer working-time flexibility,
- reduction of legacy extras,
- voluntary exit and transfer programs at scale,
- and, where necessary, site rationalization.

We respect co-determination, but it cannot be used as a veto over necessary transformation. The supervisory board structure means no one side can impose terms unilaterally. That cuts both ways. If labour wants to avoid sharper interventions, it needs to engage with the scale of the problem rather than declare core outcomes off limits.

### Bottom line
I am not prepared to endorse a cosmetic compromise that keeps every plant open, preserves every protection, and leaves the cost base fundamentally unchanged. That would fail the company and, in the end, fail the workforce as well.

If the employee side is serious about securing German jobs long term, then it must help deliver a **smaller, leaner, competitive footprint**. I am willing to negotiate on the shape of that adjustment. I am **not** willing to negotiate away the need for deep structural change.

[Expert: industrial restructuring/operations expe — Round 2]
Below is the operational restructuring assessment, focused narrowly on **what savings can be achieved without plant closures**, and what it would typically take for those savings to be **economically equivalent** to a closure-based restructuring.

## 1) Core point: “No closure” is possible only if the savings gap is closed elsewhere

In large automotive manufacturing networks, a plant closure is usually the **fastest and cleanest way** to remove fixed-cost overcapacity. If closures are ruled out, the equivalent savings must come from a combination of:

- **capacity reduction inside remaining plants**,
- **shift elimination / working-time reduction**,
- **headcount reduction via attrition and voluntary exits**,
- **outsourcing or insourcing changes**,
- **product-mix concentration onto fewer variants/platforms**,
- **overhead simplification**.

In practice, a credible no-closure package usually has to generate **most of the same annual run-rate savings** as a closure package, because the fixed-cost problem does not disappear just because the footprint remains intact.

## 2) What a plant closure typically saves

For a modern car plant, the annual run-rate savings from closure depend on size and whether the site is fully eliminated or partially repurposed, but a reasonable order of magnitude is:

- **Direct labour and shift cost avoided:** often €80m–€200m/year for a medium-sized plant
- **Maintenance, energy, indirect site costs, logistics simplification:** €20m–€60m/year
- **Overhead allocation and support-function reduction:** €10m–€40m/year

So a meaningful German plant closure can easily produce roughly:

- **€100m–€300m per year** in recurring savings, sometimes more for a large site  
- plus **one-time restructuring costs** that can be very large: redundancy, decommissioning, remediation, contract termination, relocation, and supply-chain transition.

A multi-plant footprint reduction can therefore require **several hundred million euros per year** in offsetting savings if closures are not used.

## 3) Equivalent savings from alternative measures

### A. Shift reduction / working-time changes
If you remove one shift from a heavily loaded plant or reduce planned production time materially, the savings can be large, but only if volumes do not simply reappear elsewhere.

Typical annual savings:
- **One shift removed at a plant:** about **€30m–€100m/year**
- **Shorter weekly working time / seasonal flexibility:** often **€10m–€40m/year**, depending on staffing structure and overtime reliance

Important: the savings are only durable if staffing is actually resized. If headcount stays the same, much of the “saving” becomes temporary or merely accounting-based.

### B. Headcount reduction
In German automotive production and supporting functions, a fully loaded employment cost can roughly range from:

- **€60k–€100k per employee/year** in production/technical functions
- **€90k–€140k+ per employee/year** in higher-skill indirect roles, once overhead and benefits are included

So the annual run-rate savings from headcount reduction are approximately:

- **1,000 FTE removed:** roughly **€60m–€120m/year**
- **2,000 FTE removed:** roughly **€120m–€240m/year**

This is the single most important offset to no-closure restructuring. If management needs “closure-equivalent” savings, headcount reduction is usually the biggest lever.

### C. Voluntary exits / retirement corridors
These are operationally attractive but slower and less certain.

Typical effects:
- Lower annual savings in year 1 because many exits happen gradually
- Stronger savings once the cohort fully rolls through

A corridor of, say, **1,500–3,000 exits** over 2–4 years can ultimately yield:
- **€90m–€300m/year** in steady-state savings, depending on cost per employee and replacement rate

But the timing is critical: the company often needs cash relief **before** the full savings arrive.

### D. Product-mix and platform concentration
Reducing complexity can save money without closing plants, but only if the product architecture really changes.

Typical mechanisms:
- fewer variants
- fewer option combinations
- higher commonality
- fewer low-volume derivatives
- shifting volume to fewer lines/sites

Potential savings:
- **€20m–€80m/year** in direct manufacturing and logistics savings
- plus larger indirect savings in engineering, planning, purchasing, and quality systems over time

These savings are real, but they are usually **not enough alone** to replace a plant closure unless the network is also overstaffed.

### E. Outsourcing or supplier substitution
This can lower fixed costs, but often raises variable cost and risk.

Savings range:
- **€10m–€50m/year** in selected functions or components
- sometimes more, but with quality, supply security, or labor-relations risk

It is usually a supporting measure, not the main answer.

## 4) What “equivalent” usually means in practice

If management is targeting a closure package worth, for example, **€150m–€250m/year** in recurring savings, then a non-closure package generally has to combine measures roughly like this:

- **1,500–2,500 FTE reduction**  
  = about **€90m–€240m/year**
- **one shift elimination or equivalent working-time reduction**  
  = about **€30m–€80m/year**
- **product/complexity reductions and overhead cuts**  
  = about **€20m–€60m/year**

That yields an approximate combined run-rate of:
- **€140m–€380m/year**

So yes, a non-closure package can be economically equivalent, **but only if it is deep**. A “light” voluntary package will not replace closure economics.

## 5) The usual problem: non-closure packages are often slower and less certain

The main tradeoffs are:

### Direct savings
- Closures are immediate and durable.
- Voluntary measures are slower and may not fully realize.

### One-time restructuring costs
- Closures have high one-time costs.
- Voluntary packages also cost money: severance, buyouts, bridge payments, retraining, transfer support, and possible wage concessions.

### Timing
- Closures usually improve the P&L faster.
- Headcount attrition and working-time measures often take **12–36 months** to fully show up.

### Execution risk
- If the no-closure package depends on voluntary exits, there is a risk the take-up rate is too low.
- If it depends on labor-cost concessions, there is a risk the savings expire too early.
- If it depends on productivity improvements, there is a risk the savings are overstated.

## 6) What a credible no-closure package would need

For a large German automotive operation, a credible substitute for closure generally needs:

1. **Hard headcount reduction target**
   - not just “natural attrition”
   - but a measurable FTE reduction with dates

2. **Shift and capacity reset**
   - less scheduled capacity, not just more flexible scheduling

3. **Variant and complexity reduction**
   - fewer derivatives, fewer special builds, fewer low-volume lines

4. **Workforce cost contribution**
   - temporary or structural wage moderation, reduced extras, or deferred increases

5. **Management and overhead cuts**
   - visible reductions in indirect cost and executive overhead

6. **Clear savings governance**
   - monthly tracking against run-rate targets
   - trigger points if exits or savings underdeliver

## 7) Practical benchmark

As a rule of thumb:

- If the issue is **one medium plant**, a no-closure solution must usually find at least **€100m–€200m/year** in offsetting savings.
- If the issue is **multiple sites / a large footprint excess**, the required offset can rise to **€300m/year or more**.
- Without **material headcount reduction**, the package is unlikely to be equivalent.
- Without **some capacity reduction**, the package is usually not durable.

## 8) Bottom-line assessment

From an operations and restructuring perspective:

- **A no-closure solution is possible.**
- **It is not cheap.**
- It must be **deep enough to remove the same fixed costs** that a closure would remove.
- The main levers are:
  - **headcount reduction**
  - **shift reduction**
  - **working-time reduction**
  - **product-mix simplification**
  - **overhead cuts**
- If those do not add up to the closure-equivalent savings target, then the company will still carry structural overcapacity.

1. **closure-based**
2. **no-closure / high labour contribution**
3. **hybrid phased package**

including estimated annual savings, one-time costs, and execution risk.

[Expert: industrial restructuring/operations expe — Round 3]
From an operations and restructuring perspective, the key issue is now clear:

## 1) The real decision test
A **no-closure restructuring** can work **only if it delivers closure-equivalent savings** in a durable way.

That means the package must close the gap on three dimensions:
- **fixed-cost reduction**
- **capacity reduction**
- **headcount reduction**

If it does not, then it is not an equivalent alternative; it is a delay.

---

## 2) What “closure-equivalent” usually means in numbers
For a large automotive plant, a medium site closure often produces roughly:

- **€100m to €300m per year** in recurring savings, depending on size, shift structure, and degree of local overhead eliminated
- plus one-time restructuring costs that can be substantial:
  - severance
  - decommissioning
  - transfer costs
  - write-downs
  - logistics reconfiguration

A no-closure package can match that only if it combines several measures at scale:

### Typical savings levers
- **Shift elimination / utilization cuts:**  
  Often worth **€20m–€80m per eliminated shift line**, depending on site structure.

- **Headcount reduction via voluntary exits / attrition / retirement corridors:**  
  A fully loaded automotive manufacturing employee often costs roughly **€70k–€100k per year** all-in, sometimes more with overhead allocation.  
  So:
  - **1,000 FTE** reduction ≈ **€70m–€100m/year**
  - **2,000 FTE** reduction ≈ **€140m–€200m/year**

- **Working-time moderation / temporary wage concessions:**  
  Useful, but usually not enough alone unless very large.  
  A 5% labour-cost moderation on a €2bn wage base would be about **€100m/year**, but that assumes broad coverage and real permanence.

- **Overhead and complexity cuts:**  
  Often **€30m–€100m/year** if management layers, indirect functions, and product complexity are materially reduced.

So to replace one medium closure, the company typically needs some combination like:
- **1,500–3,000 FTE reduction**
- **one or more shift eliminations**
- **material overhead reduction**
- **product simplification**
- **some labour-cost moderation**

That is a serious restructuring, not a symbolic one.

---

## 3) Comparative trade-offs

### Option A: Plant closure / footprint reduction
**Pros**
- Fastest path to structural savings
- Clear fixed-cost elimination
- Strong signal to capital markets
- Usually strongest in economic certainty

**Cons**
- High one-time cost
- Very high execution and governance risk
- Industrial conflict risk
- Knowledge loss and supply-chain disruption
- Potentially impossible under co-determination veto structure

**Operational conclusion:**  
Best on pure economics, worst on executability if governance blocks it.

---

### Option B: No closures, but deep internal downsizing
**Pros**
- More executable under co-determination
- Lower political and social disruption
- Preserves plant assets and know-how
- Can be phased and reversible

**Cons**
- Requires very large employee contribution
- Savings are harder to lock in
- Risk of underdelivery if measures are voluntary only
- Can preserve structural inefficiencies if not deep enough

**Operational conclusion:**  
Viable only if it includes hard capacity and FTE reductions, not just flexibility.

---

### Option C: Hybrid package
This is often the most realistic in a governance-constrained environment.

Typical elements:
- no immediate closures
- one or two shift eliminations
- concentrated footprint rationalization inside plants
- voluntary exit corridor plus retirement wave
- limited temporary labour-cost moderation
- management overhead cuts
- product complexity reduction
- binding review triggers

**Pros**
- More executable than full closure plan
- Can generate meaningful recurring savings
- Allows time for redeployment and investment

**Cons**
- Requires strict monitoring
- May still fall short if too many protections are absolute
- Can become a postponement mechanism if targets are vague

**Operational conclusion:**  
This is usually the best compromise if and only if targets are quantified and binding.

---

## 4) What would make a no-closure package credible
If I were assessing feasibility, I would look for these minimum features:

### A. Quantified savings target
A deal needs a stated annual run-rate savings target, for example:
- **€200m/year**
- **€300m/year**
- or whatever the gap is versus target margin

Without this, there is no way to judge equivalence.

### B. FTE target
A credible package should specify:
- gross headcount reduction
- net headcount reduction
- timing by year
- share achieved through voluntary exits

A vague promise of “efficiency” is not enough.

### C. Capacity target
There should be a measurable footprint reduction:
- fewer shifts
- lower planned output
- plant specialization
- line consolidation

If capacity stays materially unchanged, savings often leak away.

### D. Overhead target
Management should also commit to:
- indirect headcount cuts
- organizational flattening
- central overhead reduction

Workforce measures alone rarely close the full gap.

### E. Timing
Savings must arrive fast enough to matter.  
A package that delivers only in year 4 or 5 is often too slow for a balance-sheet and margin repair.

---

## 5) The main risks in the current posture
From a restructuring mechanics standpoint, the risks are:

### Risk 1: Both sides overstate their position
- Management may overstate the necessity of closures as leverage.
- Labour may overstate what can be achieved without any hard structural change.

### Risk 2: Voluntary-only measures underdeliver
Attrition and voluntary exits are useful, but usually insufficient alone unless the age profile is favorable and the time horizon is long.

### Risk 3: Temporary labour concessions expire too early
If the core cost problem is structural, temporary moderation does not solve it unless matched with permanent productivity and capacity gains.

### Risk 4: Governance delay destroys value
If the company waits too long for a perfect agreement, it can lose output, talent, and market share while still not getting the savings.

---

## 6) Practical sequencing that would make sense
A workable restructuring sequence in this type of environment is usually:

1. **Quantify the gap transparently**
   - savings required
   - FTE required
   - capacity required
   - timing required

2. **Offer voluntary and negotiated measures first**
   - retirement corridors
   - internal mobility
   - qualification
   - working-time adjustments
   - voluntary exits

3. **Bundle with structural operational change**
   - shift elimination
   - line consolidation
   - complexity reduction
   - overhead restructuring

4. **Tie concessions to investment commitments**
   - product allocation
   - EV/next-gen platform investment
   - site utilization plan

5. **Use hard checkpoints**
   - if savings miss targets, predefined measures are activated
   - this is much better than reopening the entire agreement

That sequencing is more executable than a pure closure-first or pure guarantee-only model.

The works council rejects plant closures and compulsory redundancies as starting points and treats them as threats rather than necessities. It will discuss working-time flexibility, redeployment, voluntary exits, and productivity measures, but only alongside binding no-closure, no-forced-redundancy, and job-security guarantees through 2030.

### Industrial Union
The union agrees the crisis is structural and supports a serious negotiated restructuring, but only if burden-sharing is real and workers are not the sole adjustment variable. It is open to temporary concessions and operational change, but not to a cosmetic deal or a closure-first logic.

### Regional State Government
The state accepts the competitiveness problem and the need for deep restructuring, but wants to preserve industrial capacity and employment in the region. It supports a serious no-closure route if it is quantified and executable, and resists premature closure logic or a one-sided sacrifice of the workforce.

### Controlling Family Holding
The family supports a durable, margin-accretive restructuring and rejects arrangements that merely postpone the problem. It is open to testing a no-closure package, but only if it is closure-equivalent in substance and capable of restoring competitiveness.

### Institutional Investors
Investors strongly favor verified savings, lower fixed costs, and a credible path back to target margins. They are open to no-closure restructuring, but only if it is demonstrably equivalent to closure-led savings; they will not support symbolic compromise or prolonged delay.

### Expert
The expert says a no-closure solution is operationally possible in principle, but only if it delivers real capacity reduction, shift reduction, headcount reduction, and overhead reset. In short: no closures can work, but only as a hard-numbers restructuring, not as a slogan.

## 4. Genuine conflicts
1. **Plant closures vs. no closures**: the workforce and state reject closures; management, family, and investors keep them as fallback or benchmark if the no-closure route underdelivers.
2. **Compulsory redundancies vs. job protection**: labor demands no forced layoffs; management and owners refuse to rule them out if voluntary tools fail.
3. **Who moves first**: labor wants binding protection before concessions; capital wants quantified sacrifice before granting guarantees.
4. **Definition of “equivalent” savings**: labor sees internal measures as sufficient if deep enough; capital demands proof that they match closure-led economics.
5. **Risk allocation**: labor wants the company to bear the downside of adjustment; management and capital want the workforce to share structural risk.
6. **Time horizon**: labor wants protections through 2030; management and investors want faster, more permanent cost relief.

## 5. Areas of convergence
- All sides accept the problem is structural, not cyclical.
- All sides want a durable solution, not a short-term patch.
- There is shared support for voluntary exits, redeployment, qualification, and working-time flexibility.
- Everyone recognizes future product and technology investment is required.
- There is broad acceptance that burden-sharing should include management, owners, and capital, not only labor.
- A no-closure package is no longer dismissed outright; it is being tested against economic equivalence.

## 6. Key trade-offs for the decision-maker
- **Job preservation vs. competitiveness**: preserving sites and jobs reduces social and political damage, but may leave excess cost unresolved.
- **Closure-led certainty vs. negotiated feasibility**: closures remove capacity more decisively, but are hard to execute under governance and likely trigger conflict.
- **Temporary concessions vs. permanent restructuring**: temporary labor moderation may buy time, but investors and management doubt it closes the structural gap.
- **Governance stability vs. managerial flexibility**: co-determination and blocking rights require negotiated packages, but reduce unilateral options.
- **Short-term industrial peace vs. long-term execution risk**: a no-closure pact may lower immediate conflict, but only if trigger mechanisms and metrics are credible.

## 7. Integrative pathways
1. **Time-limited no-closure pact with hard triggers**  
   No closures or compulsory redundancies for a defined period, paired with quantified savings, FTE, and capacity targets; if milestones are missed, specific measures are reopened.

2. **Hybrid footprint reduction inside sites**  
   Avoid full plant closures while eliminating shifts, consolidating lines, reducing variants, and cutting indirect overhead; combine with voluntary exits and redeployment.

3. **Protection-for-performance bargain**  
   Bind site and job guarantees to measurable labor-cost moderation, productivity gains, management/owner contributions, and investment commitments through 2030.

4. **Phased sequencing**  
   Phase 1: voluntary exits, redeployment, working-time flexibility, complexity reduction, overhead cuts.  
   Phase 2: governance review against verified savings.  
   Phase 3: only if needed, targeted fallback measures short of immediate closures.

## 8. Open questions
- What is the exact annual savings gap in euros?
- How many FTEs and how much capacity must be removed to be closure-equivalent?
- What mix of shifts, lines, indirect labor, and overhead can realistically be cut without closures?
- What management and owner contributions are concrete and measurable?
- What binding job, site, and investment guarantees are acceptable to the workforce?
- What triggers would reopen specific measures without collapsing the whole agreement?
- Can the supervisory board and co-determination structure approve the package as designed?

## 9. Recommended next step
Require management to table a **fully quantified three-scenario package** for board and bargaining review:
1. closure-led,
2. no-closure,
3. hybrid.  
Each scenario should specify annual savings, one-time costs, FTE impact, capacity impact, implementation timing, and execution risk. Negotiation should then focus only on the gap between the no-closure/hybrid scenario and the closure benchmark, with explicit trigger points and reciprocal guarantees.

## 10. Emergent insights that only appeared through multi-turn interaction
- The debate shifted from **“whether restructuring is needed”** to **“which instruments are allowed and who carries the downside if the no-closure path underdelivers.”**
- “No closures” is not a complete position; through repeated questioning, it became clear it must be translated into **a hard operating model** with quantified savings and capacity reduction.
- The expert clarified that closures are **not the only technically valid mechanism** for fixed-cost reduction, but also that internal measures must be much deeper than superficial concessions to be credible.
- A key political insight emerged: the state and workforce are not simply anti-restructuring; they are demanding that management **prove necessity before invoking closures**.
- Another governance insight: because of co-determination and blocking rights, a technically optimal plan that cannot pass institutional veto points is not truly viable.
- The most plausible compromise is not a binary choice, but a **phased, trigger-based hybrid** that preserves sites initially while forcing real structural adjustment.
