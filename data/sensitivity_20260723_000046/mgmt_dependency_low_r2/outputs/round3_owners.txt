We are at the point where the discussion has to be made concrete. I will be equally direct.

## Where I agree

I agree with all sides on the basic diagnosis:
- this is a **structural competitiveness problem**,
- the solution must be **durable**,
- voluntary tools, redeployment, qualification, and working-time flexibility should be part of the package,
- and future investment is essential.

I also agree that the company should not be pushed into a prolonged conflict that destroys value.

## Where I disagree

### To the Works Council
I do **not** accept the position that “no closures, no compulsory redundancies, no job-security erosion” can stand as a complete answer by itself.

That is a boundary, not a restructuring plan.

The expert assessment confirmed what many of us suspected: a no-closure route can work **only if it is deep enough**. That means real FTE reduction, shift reduction, lower capacity, lower complexity, and meaningful labour-cost contribution. If those elements are not large enough, then keeping every site open simply preserves overcapacity under a different label.

So I disagree with the idea that preserving all German plants is automatically the better economic outcome. If a no-closure package truly closes the savings gap, fine. If not, it is not stewardship; it is deferral.

### To the Union
I agree that labour should not bear the burden alone. I also agree that temporary employee concessions by themselves are insufficient.

But I do **not** accept a blanket refusal of compulsory redundancies in all circumstances. Nor do I accept that any structural adjustment must be temporary by definition.

If the cost gap is structural, the response must also be structural. A temporary fix that expires while the business remains overburdened is not a solution.

### To the Regional State Government
I understand and respect your role as anchor shareholder and regional stakeholder. I also accept that closures are socially and politically serious.

But I do **not** accept the idea that plant closures should be pushed out of the discussion before a real test of alternatives has been done. The expert has made clear that a no-closure solution is possible only if it is closure-equivalent in substance. That is the real standard.

So I agree with you on the need for a negotiated, serious alternative. I do not agree with treating closures as unacceptable by default if the economics ultimately require them.

### To the Institutional Investors
I agree with your core point: a cosmetic compromise would destroy value.

But I also think it is too narrow to treat closures as the only sign of seriousness. The real question is whether the package delivers:
- durable savings,
- lower complexity,
- lower capacity,
- and better margins.

If a no-closure package can do that, it should be judged on results, not symbolism.

## What I want to see next

This discussion now needs numbers, not just positions.

Management should present:
1. the **savings gap**,
2. the **required capacity reduction**,
3. the **FTE reduction target**,
4. the **expected labour contribution**,
5. the **management and owner contribution**,
6. the **investment commitments** tied to the restructuring.

If the employee side believes a no-closure package can be closure-equivalent, then prove it in those terms.

## Our line as controlling shareholder

We will support a restructuring that:
- materially restores competitiveness,
- improves margins,
- is executable under governance realities,
- and avoids unnecessary instability.

We will **not** support:
- a deal that preserves an uncompetitive structure,
- a purely symbolic compromise,
- or a drawn-out conflict that destroys value.

So my position is straightforward:
- **Yes** to a serious negotiated restructuring.
- **Yes** to testing a no-closure package.
- **No** to a package that cannot credibly close the cost and capacity gap.
- **No** to a settlement that leaves the company structurally weak and the owners carrying the cost of underperformance.

If the no-closure route truly matches the economics, then show it. If it does not, then we should stop pretending that soft language can substitute for hard restructuring.
