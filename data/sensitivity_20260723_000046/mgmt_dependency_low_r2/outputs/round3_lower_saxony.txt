I will respond plainly.

## My position as regional state government and anchor shareholder

I support a **serious restructuring**. I do **not** support a restructuring that begins with, or relies on, **German plant closures** or **compulsory redundancies** as the default answer.

That is not sentimentality. It is a recognition of the company’s role as a regional economic anchor, the importance of skilled employment in the state, and the fact that the governance structure gives us a real responsibility — and real leverage — to insist on a better solution.

At the same time, I am not pretending there is no economic problem. There is one. And the expert assessment makes clear that if we want to avoid closures, the alternative must be **deep, quantified, and credible**. I accept that.

## Where I agree

### To management
I agree that:
- the company has a structural competitiveness problem,
- the solution must be durable,
- voluntary tools alone may not be enough unless they are scaled up,
- and future investment has to be secured.

You are right that a cosmetic deal will not work. A package that only delays the problem would be irresponsible.

### To the works council and union
I agree that:
- the workforce did not create this crisis alone,
- burden-sharing must be fair,
- voluntary exits, redeployment, qualification, and working-time flexibility belong in the package,
- and workers should not be the sole adjustment variable.

I also agree that the company’s industrial future depends on trust. A forced, one-sided approach would damage that trust badly.

### To the controlling family and investors
I agree that the solution must be:
- economically real,
- executable under the governance structure,
- and durable.

I do not support a deal that preserves every inefficiency forever. But I also do not support a deal that destroys sites and regional value in the name of short-term discipline.

## Where I disagree

### 1) Management’s closure-first logic
I do **not** accept the idea that closures must remain the default instrument simply because overcapacity exists.

The expert has said clearly: a no-closure solution is possible if it is deep enough. That means the closure question is not settled. It means the burden is on management to present a **serious alternative package** before reaching for closures.

I reject the framing that “if you do not accept closures, you are denying reality.” That is too simplistic. Reality also includes:
- co-determination,
- social partnership,
- regional responsibility,
- and the long-term costs of conflict and know-how loss.

### 2) The insistence on keeping compulsory redundancies “available”
I do not accept compulsory redundancies as a normal bargaining lever.

If the company wants a durable agreement, it should build one around:
- voluntary exits,
- retirement corridors,
- internal transfers,
- and negotiated capacity reduction.

Keeping forced layoffs on the table may feel like leverage, but it also makes any agreement unstable and escalatory. I am not prepared to endorse that as the primary route.

### 3) A solution that shifts the burden primarily onto labour
I agree labour must contribute. But I do **not** accept a package where the workforce carries the bulk of the pain while management and owners mainly demand sacrifice.

If workers are asked for wage moderation, flexibility, or reduced extras, then management and owners must also make visible contributions:
- overhead reduction,
- executive restraint,
- strategic commitment,
- and capital support for the transition.

Without that, there is no fairness — and without fairness, there is no stable implementation.

## What I think the expert assessment means in practice

The expert has been helpful, because it shows this is not a binary choice between “closures” and “doing nothing.”

A no-closure solution can work, but only if it is **deep enough**. That means:

- meaningful headcount reduction,
- shift reduction or footprint-equivalent capacity reduction,
- simplification of product complexity,
- overhead cuts,
- and a real labour-cost contribution.

So I am not asking for a soft deal. I am asking for a **serious no-closure restructuring** that can actually close the savings gap.

That is the standard.

## What would move me

I would support a negotiated package if it includes all of the following:

1. **No German plant closures**
   - not as a temporary slogan, but as a real commitment in the restructuring path.

2. **No compulsory redundancies**
   - adjustment through voluntary and negotiated instruments first.

3. **A quantified savings plan**
   - with annual run-rate savings and FTE reductions spelled out.

4. **A real capacity reset**
   - fewer shifts, lower planned output, leaner operations, and complexity reduction.

5. **Binding investment and product commitments**
   - especially for future technologies and German site utilization.

6. **Management and owner contribution**
   - visible restraint at the top and real financial backing.

7. **A genuine co-determination role**
   - works council and union must be part of design and monitoring, not just informed after the fact.

## Where I draw the line

I will not support:
- a closure-first strategy,
- a package that treats forced redundancies as the normal tool,
- or a settlement that leaves the company’s German industrial base weaker than necessary.

I also will not support a symbolic no-closure position if it cannot deliver closure-equivalent savings. That would be just as irresponsible, because it would postpone the crisis rather than solve it.

## On the bridge proposals

A phased package can be useful **only if it is genuinely binding**.

I am open to a time-limited arrangement if it contains:
- clear savings targets,
- a monitored implementation schedule,
- a hard no-closure commitment for the agreed period,
- no compulsory redundancies in that period,
- and trigger-based review if targets are missed.

What I will not accept is a “phase one” that is really just a delayed path to the same closure logic.

## My current line

So my position is:

- **Yes** to a deep negotiated restructuring.
- **Yes** to voluntary exits, redeployment, qualification, flexibility, and productivity measures.
- **Yes** to measurable contributions from management, owners, and labour.
- **Yes** to a serious no-closure package if it is truly closure-equivalent.

But:
- **No** to plant closures as the default,
- **No** to compulsory redundancies as the normal instrument,
- **No** to a solution that guts regional employment or weakens the company’s role as a regional anchor.

If management wants this to move forward, it should stop speaking in generalities and put the numbers on the table:
- savings gap,
- FTE reduction,
- capacity reduction,
- management contribution,
- and investment commitments.

Then we can judge whether the no-closure route is serious. Until then, I will continue to oppose a closure-driven approach.
