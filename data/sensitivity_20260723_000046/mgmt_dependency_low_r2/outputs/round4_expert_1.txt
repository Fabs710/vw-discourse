## What must be quantified
For each scenario, the board needs at least:

1. **Annual run-rate savings**  
   - € per year, fully ramped

2. **Net FTE reduction**
   - total and by function/site

3. **Capacity reduction**
   - shifts removed, line capacity cut, output foregone

4. **One-time restructuring costs**
   - severance, transfers, retraining, decommissioning, write-downs

5. **Payback period**
   - one-time cost / annual savings

6. **Execution risk**
   - delivery, quality, launch, labor-relations, supply-chain

7. **Timing**
   - when savings start, when they peak, and when they are durable

## A practical comparison framework

### Scenario A: Closure-led restructuring
Typical effect:
- highest and fastest fixed-cost removal
- strongest capacity reset
- highest one-time cost and highest execution friction

### Scenario B: No-closure restructuring
Must compensate with:
- shift reductions
- line consolidation
- lower complexity/variant mix
- headcount reduction through voluntary exits and retirement corridors
- overhead reset
- labor-cost moderation

This can work, but only if it removes enough **fixed cost and excess capacity**.

### Scenario C: Hybrid
Usually the most executable:
- no immediate plant closures
- selective footprint reduction inside plants
- voluntary exits first
- trigger-based fallback if targets are missed

This often gives the best balance of savings credibility and implementation risk.

## Indicative savings logic
Without company-specific data, only directional ranges are possible:

- **Shift elimination / capacity reduction inside plants:**  
  meaningful savings, but often not enough alone unless utilization is currently low

- **Indirect labor / overhead reset:**  
  usually material, but slower to realize than direct production labor cuts

- **Voluntary exits / retirement corridors:**  
  lower immediate friction, but savings depend on replacement rules and staffing discipline

- **Wage moderation / deferred compensation / lower extras:**  
  helps, but by itself rarely closes a large structural gap

- **Plant closure:**  
  largest fixed-cost removal per unit of capacity taken out, but with the highest one-time cost and social/industrial disruption

In automotive manufacturing, a no-closure deal is credible only if it produces something close to the same **net annual savings** as the closure scenario, not just headline job reductions.

---

## What makes a no-closure package credible
A no-closure package becomes realistic if it includes all of the following:

- a **clear savings target**
- a **clear FTE target**
- a **clear capacity/shift target**
- **permanent** cost removal, not only temporary concessions
- a **documented payback period**
- a **fallback mechanism** if milestones are missed

If any one of those is missing, the plan is usually not closure-equivalent.

---

## My operational judgment
- If the company’s problem is mainly **fixed-cost and capacity overhang**, then **closure-equivalent savings without closures are possible only through very deep footprint rationalization inside the remaining plants**.
- If utilization is already weak and product volumes continue to fall, then the no-closure route becomes harder, because you are trying to preserve sites while still taking out excess capacity.
- In that case, the hybrid scenario is usually the most plausible compromise.
