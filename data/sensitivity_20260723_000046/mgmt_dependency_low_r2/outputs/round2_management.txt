I’ll respond directly, because we are past the stage where generalities help.

## First: what I hear from labour and the state
I hear the repeated insistence on:
- no plant closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- no weakening of job security,
- and a negotiated, “fair” burden-sharing arrangement.

I also hear the state and family holding warning against a cosmetic deal.

On one point, we all agree: **this company needs a durable solution, not a short-lived truce**. We also agree that voluntary tools, redeployment, qualification, and working-time flexibility belong in the package.

That is the common ground. Beyond that, we still have a serious gap.

## Where I strongly disagree

### 1) “No plant closures” as a hard line is not credible
I do not accept an absolute no-closure rule.

Why? Because the problem is not just too-high costs; it is **structural overcapacity**. If we refuse to adjust the footprint, then we are choosing to preserve excess capacity and asking the company to finance it indefinitely. That is not a restructuring. That is denial.

I am not saying closures are the only answer. But I am saying we cannot rule out **site rationalization** if the economics require it. If you want to avoid closures, then you must present an alternative that achieves **equivalent, durable capacity reduction** with the same economic effect. So far, I have not heard one.

### 2) “No compulsory redundancies” cannot be guaranteed upfront
I would prefer to avoid forced measures. But I will not commit the company to a blanket prohibition on compulsion if the gap is not closed otherwise.

You keep saying “redesign the package.” That is exactly what we are asking for. But redesign must still produce the required savings. If it does not, then keeping compulsory measures available is not intimidation; it is basic management responsibility.

### 3) Job security through 2030 cannot be unconditional
A long protection through 2030 is only credible if the company’s cost base is fixed first.

A guarantee without structural adjustment would simply postpone the adjustment and deepen the problem. That is not protecting jobs; it is risking more of them later.

## Where I agree with the union, works council, and state
I agree with the following:
- The workforce did not create this crisis alone.
- Any contribution from labour must be matched by visible contributions from management and owners.
- Future investment, product allocation, and German site utilization matter.
- A negotiated outcome is preferable to prolonged conflict.

I also agree with the state that co-determination is real and must be respected. But respect for governance does not mean accepting an outcome that leaves the company uncompetitive.

## What I need on the table

If the workforce wants to avoid closures and forced redundancies, then I need a package with **quantified and enforceable** commitments, not principles.

That means:

1. **A measurable reduction in labour costs**
   - not symbolic moderation,
   - not a temporary pause,
   - but a structural contribution.

2. **A real reduction in capacity and overheads**
   - if not through closures, then through line consolidation, footprint reduction, and other durable measures that produce the same effect.

3. **A meaningful workforce contribution**
   - working-time flexibility,
   - wage moderation or reduced extras,
   - productivity improvements,
   - voluntary exit programs at scale.

4. **Concrete management and owner contributions**
   - top-level overhead reduction,
   - visible discipline on executive costs,
   - strategic commitment to future investment.

5. **A settlement that is executable**
   - we cannot sign up to a formula that looks good politically but fails under the supervisory board and special statute.

## To the works council
You say “all German plants remain open.” I understand why you say it. I do not accept it as a business constraint.

If every site is untouchable, then the cost gap remains. The workforce cannot demand full protection while also expecting a full turnaround. Those two things are in conflict.

If you want to preserve as many jobs and sites as possible, then we need you to help design a footprint that is actually competitive. That means hard choices. I am not pretending otherwise.

## To the union
You say any employee contribution must be temporary and tied to guarantees. I can discuss that. But “temporary” only works if it actually closes the structural gap.

A temporary contribution that evaporates in two years while the cost base remains too high is not a solution. It is a delay. I will not sign a delay and call it restructuring.

And let me be equally clear: if you insist on no closures and no compulsory redundancies as non-negotiables, then you are not negotiating the adjustment. You are negotiating the optics.

## To the regional state
I respect the state’s role, but I cannot accept the idea that closure is only permissible after every alternative has been exhausted if the alternatives never amount to equivalent savings.

The state’s responsibility is not just protecting current employment numbers; it is protecting the long-term industrial base. That requires competitiveness. If we fail to restore margins, we will end up with fewer jobs, weaker investment, and a damaged company anyway.

## To the family holding and investors
I agree with your core point: the solution must be durable and value-creating. A compromise that protects everything and fixes nothing would be a mistake.

At the same time, I need your backing for a settlement that is actually executable under co-determination. We cannot simply declare that the economics decide and assume governance will follow. It won’t.

## What I am prepared to do
I remain open to a negotiated package if it delivers:
- substantial structural cost reduction,
- real capacity adjustment,
- durable labour-cost relief,
- and a credible funding path for future products and technology to 2030.

I am open on the method:
- closures,
- site restructuring,
- line consolidation,
- working-time changes,
- voluntary exits,
- productivity measures,
- and negotiated concessions.

I am **not** open to:
- a package that leaves all German sites protected regardless of economics,
- a package that rules out forced measures in all cases,
- or a settlement that fails to materially improve margins.

## The choice in front of us
We can either:
1. build a hard but executable restructuring that restores competitiveness, or
2. preserve the current footprint in the hope that pressure goes away.

I am not willing to choose option 2.

If the employee side wants to avoid sharper interventions, then it needs to bring forward a package that closes the cost gap in reality, not just in rhetoric. Otherwise, management will continue to insist on the structural measures required to fix the business.
