## Our position as controlling shareholder

We support a restructuring that **restores competitiveness, margins, and long-term value**. We do **not** support a settlement that preserves an uncompetitive structure, because that would simply defer the damage and make it worse later.

At the same time, we also do **not** support a reckless confrontation that triggers prolonged industrial conflict, governance paralysis, or reputational damage. That would also destroy value.

So our test is simple:

- **Does the package materially close the cost and capacity gap?**
- **Is it executable under co-determination and the special governance structure?**
- **Does it avoid unnecessary instability?**

If the answer is no, we will not back it.

## To the Works Council

I understand your insistence on no closures, no compulsory redundancies, and no dismantling of job security. I also accept that the workforce did not create the crisis alone.

But I do **not** accept those points as absolute constraints if they prevent a real turnaround.

The specialist assessment makes the trade-off clear: if closures are ruled out, then the alternative must still deliver **closure-equivalent savings**. That means deep headcount reduction, shift reduction, lower complexity, overhead cuts, and meaningful labour-cost contribution. A “no closures” position that does not produce those savings is not a solution; it is just a slogan.

So I disagree with you on this:
- **No plant closures as an unconditional line**
- **No compulsory redundancies in all circumstances**
- **Job security through 2030 as a starting point without a quantified restructuring package**

Those are not acceptable to us as fixed premises.

What would move us is not the declaration “all plants stay open,” but a **credible, quantified package** that actually removes the structural overcapacity and improves the company’s economics. If you can produce that without closures, we will listen. If not, then you are asking the owners to fund underperformance.

## To the Union

You are right that labour should not bear the burden alone. I agree that management, owners, and capital must all contribute visibly.

I also agree that a cosmetic wage concession is not enough. Temporary moderation by itself does not solve a structural problem.

But I disagree with your insistence that:
- compulsory redundancies are never acceptable,
- plant closures are not part of the adjustment discussion,
- and labour-cost relief must remain temporary regardless of the gap.

If the savings requirement is structural, the response must be structural. Otherwise the company will simply be back in the same crisis in a few years.

The expert is helpful here: a no-closure package can work, but only if it is **deep**. That means:
- material FTE reduction,
- shift and capacity reduction,
- product and complexity simplification,
- and lasting cost relief.

So yes, we are open to negotiations on working-time, voluntary exits, redeployment, and burden-sharing. But we will not endorse a package that keeps all the costs in place and calls that fairness.

## To Group & Brand Management

Your diagnosis is broadly right: there is a structural competitiveness problem, and the company cannot fund future investment with today’s cost base.

Where I am not fully aligned is in how quickly you move to closures and compulsion as default instruments.

I do **not** want a strategy that creates a governance fight before alternatives have been fully tested. Because if the supervisory board, co-determination, and special law make implementation impossible, then the “hard” plan fails anyway and value is destroyed through delay and conflict.

So my message to management is:
- bring a **quantified** restructuring case,
- show the savings gap,
- and demonstrate why specific measures are needed.

If you can show that a no-closure package closes the gap, that is better. If you cannot, then you need to be ready to defend harder measures with a credible implementation path. What I will not support is a half-executed compromise that neither restores competitiveness nor secures peace.

## To the Regional State Government

I agree with you on one important point: this company has a major industrial and regional responsibility, and a simplistic “cut and move on” approach would be shortsighted.

I also agree that co-determination and the special statute are real and must be respected.

Where I disagree is with the idea that closures and compulsory redundancies should be treated as non-starters almost by definition. The expert has shown that a non-closure path is possible **only if it is deep enough**. That means the state should not reject structural adjustment; it should insist on an adjustment that is economically serious and socially executable.

So I am not asking for a closure-first strategy. I am asking for a strategy that actually fixes the business. If that can be done without closures, excellent. If not, then we should not pretend otherwise.

## What we need now

The discussion must move from principles to **numbers**. Right now, too many positions are framed as absolutes.

We need management to put forward:
1. **The quantified savings gap**
2. **The required capacity reduction**
3. **The share expected from labour**
4. **The contribution from management and owners**
5. **The investment commitments that would follow**

Then we need the employee side and the state to show whether a **no-closure package** can really produce closure-equivalent savings.

The controlling family holding is prepared to support a **deep, durable restructuring**.

We are **not** prepared to support:
- a settlement that leaves the company structurally uncompetitive,
