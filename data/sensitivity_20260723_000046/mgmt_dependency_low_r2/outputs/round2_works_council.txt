Management, the family holding, and capital markets keep repeating the same thing: “deep restructuring,” “durable savings,” “competitiveness.” We do not dispute the challenge. We dispute the method being pushed.

Let me be explicit.

## Where we agree
- Yes, the company has a serious structural problem.
- Yes, the solution must be durable, not cosmetic.
- Yes, voluntary measures, redeployment, qualification, working-time tools, and attrition belong on the table.
- Yes, future investment and product allocation matter.

That is where agreement ends unless the package protects jobs and sites.

## Where we strongly disagree

### 1) Plant closures in Germany are not an acceptable adjustment tool
Management keeps saying closures “may be necessary.” For us, that is not a neutral statement — it is a direct attack on the workforce and on the company’s social contract.

We are not here to negotiate which German plant gets sacrificed.  
We are here to prevent closures altogether.

The company is not being asked to choose between “competitiveness” and “sentiment.” It is being asked to choose between:
- a socially responsible restructuring that preserves industrial capacity in Germany, or
- a destructive cut-and-move strategy that may improve spreadsheets in the short term but breaks trust, destroys livelihoods, and weakens the company long-term.

We reject the second path.

### 2) Compulsory redundancies are a hard red line
I am not hearing a credible case for forced layoffs — only a desire to keep that weapon available.

That is not a solution. That is intimidation.

If management cannot achieve adjustment through voluntary exits, internal transfers, qualification, retirement corridors, and working-time measures, then the answer is **to redesign the package**, not to threaten operational dismissals.

We will not accept a restructuring that treats workers as disposable.

### 3) A “blanket” weakening of job security is unacceptable
Management and investors are framing protections as an obstacle. For us, those protections are the reason the company has social peace and skilled, committed employees.

A job-security framework cannot be dismantled just because the balance sheet is under pressure. If you want the workforce to contribute, then you must offer something real in return:
- binding site guarantees,
- binding investment commitments,
- and binding protection against forced job losses.

Without that, there is no fair deal.

## On the arguments from the capital side
The family holding says it will not support “permanent value destruction.” Fine. Neither will we. But closing plants and forcing layoffs also destroys value:
- it triggers conflict,
- delays implementation,
- damages morale,
- risks know-how loss,
- and undermines future capacity.

So do not pretend that only labour-side resistance creates risk. Unilateral restructuring creates its own destruction.

The institutional investors demand “equivalent savings” if closures are off the table. Then state clearly what you mean in operational terms. We are willing to discuss savings from:
- working-time flexibility,
- temporary labour-cost moderation,
- internal productivity measures,
- overhead reductions,
- and voluntary instruments.

But we are **not** willing to exchange those for closures or forced redundancies. The savings must be achieved through a different mix, not by breaking the workforce.

## On the state government’s point
We appreciate the state’s rejection of rushed closures and its insistence on co-determination. But realism does not mean accepting management’s preferred logic as the baseline.

If the state wants a “balanced” solution, then it should use its influence to back:
- a German footprint that remains intact,
- investment in future products,
- and a negotiated restructuring without forced layoffs.

Not a compromise that simply delays the inevitable closure logic.

## What we will support
We are prepared to discuss a serious package only if it includes all of the following:

1. **No plant closures in Germany**
2. **No compulsory redundancies**
3. **Extension of job security through 2030**
4. **Adjustment through voluntary and reversible measures**
5. **Binding commitments on future products, investment, and German site utilization**
6. **A real co-determination role in designing and monitoring the restructuring**

## What we will not support
- any “equivalent footprint reduction” that is just closures in disguise,
- any package that leaves management free to impose forced layoffs later,
- any erosion of the historic job-security framework without strong reciprocal guarantees,
- any fait accompli presented as unavoidable.

## To management
If you want a real negotiation, put numbers on the table for:
- the savings gap,
- the expected contribution from management and owners,
- the capacity measures short of closures,
- and the binding investment commitment you are willing to make.

Do not come back with general claims that “something must give” while expecting the workforce to surrender the most.

## To the family holding and investors
If you want a durable turnaround, then stop treating closures and compulsory redundancies as the default benchmark. You have governance power too. Use it to force management to present a credible no-closure solution. If you cannot do that, then you are not demanding discipline — you are demanding that workers carry the cost of strategic failure.

## Our minimum line
There will be **no agreement** that includes:
- German plant closures,
- compulsory redundancies,
- or the dismantling of job security as the price of adjustment.

That is not a bargaining preference. That is the boundary.

If management wants a workable path, it must bring a plan that preserves every German plant and protects jobs while achieving savings through other means. Anything else will meet resistance — in the boardroom, in the workplaces, and in the public arena.
