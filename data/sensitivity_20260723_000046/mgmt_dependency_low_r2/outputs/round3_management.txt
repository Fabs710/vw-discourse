I’ll respond directly, because we are at the point where slogans need to be replaced by hard operational reality.

## Where I agree

I agree with everyone on one essential point: **this company needs a durable solution, not a cosmetic truce**.

I also agree that:
- the workforce did not create this market shift alone,
- voluntary tools, redeployment, qualification, and retirement corridors belong in the package,
- future investment and product allocation must be part of the settlement,
- and governance constraints are real.

So let’s not pretend there is no common ground. There is. But it does **not** extend to preserving an uncompetitive structure.

## Where I disagree

### To the Works Council
You keep repeating “no closures, no compulsory redundancies, no job-security erosion” as if those were operational solutions.

They are not. They are **boundaries**. And I understand why you set them. But a boundary is not a restructuring plan.

The expert assessment is clear: a no-closure path is **possible only if it is deep enough**. That means:
- meaningful FTE reduction,
- shift reduction,
- lower planned capacity,
- product and complexity simplification,
- overhead cuts,
- and a real labour-cost contribution.

What I have **not** heard from you is a package that actually closes the cost gap on that basis. Saying “keep every plant open” is not enough if the company still carries excess fixed cost.

If you want us to avoid closures, then we need:
- quantified savings,
- a measurable capacity reset,
- and a structurally lower cost base.

Otherwise, you are asking management to accept the same uncompetitive footprint under a different label.

### To the Union
You are right that a temporary wage concession alone is insufficient. I agree.

But I do **not** accept your insistence that no compulsory redundancies can ever be part of the solution, regardless of the savings gap. That is not a sustainable bargaining position; it is a unilateral veto over adjustment.

If voluntary exits, redeployment, and flexibility do not produce the needed savings, then the company cannot simply pretend the gap is gone. We either solve the problem structurally or we don’t.

And I will be equally direct: if labour contribution is only temporary and reversible, while the cost problem is permanent, then the package is too weak. A time-limited contribution that expires while overcapacity remains is not a turnaround.

### To the Regional State Government
I appreciate your support for a negotiated route and your reluctance to start with closures. But I think you are underweighting the scale of the issue.

You say a no-closure solution is possible if deep enough. Fine. I agree with that **in principle**. But then the question becomes: where is that deep package?

A political preference for preserving sites does not close the cost gap. The state should not confuse social stability with industrial competitiveness. We need both. If we fail on competitiveness, we lose jobs anyway.

So yes, I’m prepared to test a no-closure package. But it must be **closure-equivalent in substance**, not just politically easier to sell.

### To the Controlling Family Holding
You are right that the solution must be durable and executable. But I disagree with the idea that the workforce can be asked for deep concessions while closures remain the benchmark of seriousness.

The benchmark is not whether a plant is shut. The benchmark is whether the company reaches a structurally competitive cost base and frees cash for investment.

If a no-closure package can do that, I support it. If it cannot, then keeping closures off the table by principle means preserving overcapacity and underperformance.

I also want to be blunt: a package that leaves the current footprint largely intact, protects every site, and relies on temporary relief will not restore investor confidence or brand margins. That is exactly the kind of cosmetic compromise I will not sign up for.

### To the Institutional Investors
You are right to reject cosmetic compromise. I agree with that completely.

Where I disagree is the implication that closures are the only serious signal. They are not. What matters is the **run-rate savings**, the **capacity reduction**, and the **margin impact**.

But let me be just as clear: if the no-closure route does not deliver comparable savings, then it is not credible. I am not interested in “soft” restructuring. I am interested in a restructuring that actually changes the economics.

## The operational reality

The expert already laid this out:
- a medium plant closure can generate roughly **€100m–€300m per year** in recurring savings, depending on scale;
- a no-closure package can be equivalent, **but only if it is deep**;
- that means meaningful FTE reduction, shift reduction, complexity reduction, and overhead cuts.

That is the standard now.

So here is the straightforward conclusion:  
**If you want no closures, then you must deliver closure-level savings elsewhere.**

That is not a threat. That is arithmetic.

I am not asking for rhetoric. I am asking for a package with numbers.

Bring me:

2. **The required FTE reduction**
3. **The shift and capacity reduction**
4. **The management and owner contribution**

## My line, plainly

I remain open to **either**:
- closures, if they are necessary to restore competitiveness and can be executed under the governance structure, **or**
- a genuine no-closure package that delivers equivalent, durable savings.

What I am **not** open to is:
- a symbolic deal,
- a package that preserves excess capacity,
- a temporary labor concession without structural change,
- or an agreement that protects every site while leaving the company uncompetitive.

That would not be stewardship. It would be deferral.

- **Yes** to a negotiated settlement.
- **Yes** to voluntary instruments, redeployment, training, and flexibility.

- **Yes** to a test of a serious no-closure package.

- **No** to a settlement that leaves the cost base structurally too high.
- **No** to a deal that depends on temporary concessions without lasting savings.
- **No** to an arrangement that cannot credibly restore margins and fund future investment.

Bring the numbers. If the no-closure route truly closes the gap, then prove it. If it doesn’t, then we have to stop pretending otherwise.
