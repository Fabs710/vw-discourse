From an operations and restructuring perspective, the central question is not whether “no closures” is politically desirable, but whether it can be translated into a **credible, executable operating model** that produces the same financial and industrial effect as footprint reduction.

## Expert assessment

### 1) A no-closure solution is operationally possible in principle
Yes — **if** the company can generate savings through a combination of:
- **capacity reduction inside plants**,
- **shift elimination / shift compression**,
- **variant and complexity reduction**,
- **line consolidation**,
- **headcount reduction through voluntary exits, retirement corridors, and redeployment**,
- **overhead and support-function reset**,
- and **labor-cost moderation**.

That is a valid restructuring route. In manufacturing, plant closure is only one mechanism for taking out fixed cost and excess capacity. You can often achieve a meaningful portion of the savings through internal footprint rationalization.

### 2) But “no closures” is only credible if it is closure-equivalent on the numbers
If management wants to compare a no-closure package to closure scenarios, the package must show, at minimum:

- **annual run-rate savings** in euro,
- **net FTE reduction** by site and function,
- **capacity reduction** by plant/line/shift,
- **fixed-cost reduction** in production and overhead,
- **implementation timing** and ramp profile,
- **one-time restructuring costs** and payback period,
- and **execution dependencies**.

Without those, “no closures” is just a position, not a restructuring model.

### 3) The hardest operational issue is not job cuts; it is eliminating fixed cost and unused capacity
In automotive manufacturing, the biggest value leak is usually not just labor hours. It is:
- underutilized plant capacity,
- duplicated support functions,
- too many variants,
- poor line balance,
- inefficient logistics,
- and a cost structure that stays high even after volume declines.

So if the company keeps all sites open, it still must remove enough:
- **shifts**,
- **headcount**,
- **indirect labor**,
- **production complexity**,
- and **support overhead**

to bring the cost base down materially. If those elements are not large enough, a no-closure plan will underdeliver.

### 4) The labor-side line is operationally understandable, but it is not sufficient by itself
A refusal of closures and compulsory redundancies sets a boundary, but it does **not** answer:
- where the savings come from,
- how much capacity is removed,
- which products/lines are consolidated,
- and what happens if voluntary measures fall short.

To be executable, a no-closure deal needs **fallback mechanics**:
- checkpoint reviews,
- target thresholds,
- and pre-agreed escalation on specific measures if savings lag.

Otherwise the company carries all the downside of delay.

### 5) In practice, a no-closure package usually requires stronger concessions elsewhere
If closures are off the table, then the operational burden shifts to:
- larger FTE reductions,
- more aggressive shift reduction,
- longer or more flexible working-time arrangements,
- greater labor-cost moderation,
- stricter redeployment,
- and tighter product/variant rationalization.

That is the trade-off. You do not get the savings from closures without replacing them with something equally real.

## What a hard-numbers model should contain
If I were preparing this for board comparison, I would require three scenarios:

### Scenario A: Closure-led restructuring
- plant/site closures or partial closures,
- associated headcount reduction,
- fixed-cost removal,
- one-time restructuring cost,
- expected savings ramp.

### Scenario B: No-closure restructuring
- no German plant closures,
- no compulsory redundancies for a defined period,
- but:
  - shift cuts,
  - line consolidation,
  - reduced volume assumptions,
  - indirect-function reductions,
  - voluntary exit/retirement corridors,
  - labor-cost concessions,
  - and overhead resets.

### Scenario C: Hybrid
- no immediate closures,
- but selected lines, shifts, or functions are removed,
- with trigger-based review if targets are missed.

For each scenario, the board needs:
- **gross savings**
- **net savings**
- **one-time costs**
- **payback period**
- **risk to delivery / quality / launch timing**
- **industrial relations risk**
- **execution feasibility under co-determination**

## My operational judgment
A no-closure solution can be acceptable **only if** it delivers:
- a materially lower cost base,
- a real reduction in capacity,
- a credible FTE reduction,
- and a clear path to margin improvement.

If the package cannot show that on hard numbers, then it is **not a restructuring solution**; it is a delay mechanism.
