Below is the operational restructuring assessment, focused narrowly on **what savings can be achieved without plant closures**, and what it would typically take for those savings to be **economically equivalent** to a closure-based restructuring.

## 1) Core point: “No closure” is possible only if the savings gap is closed elsewhere

In large automotive manufacturing networks, a plant closure is usually the **fastest and cleanest way** to remove fixed-cost overcapacity. If closures are ruled out, the equivalent savings must come from a combination of:

- **capacity reduction inside remaining plants**,
- **shift elimination / working-time reduction**,
- **headcount reduction via attrition and voluntary exits**,
- **outsourcing or insourcing changes**,
- **product-mix concentration onto fewer variants/platforms**,
- **overhead simplification**.

In practice, a credible no-closure package usually has to generate **most of the same annual run-rate savings** as a closure package, because the fixed-cost problem does not disappear just because the footprint remains intact.

## 2) What a plant closure typically saves

For a modern car plant, the annual run-rate savings from closure depend on size and whether the site is fully eliminated or partially repurposed, but a reasonable order of magnitude is:

- **Direct labour and shift cost avoided:** often €80m–€200m/year for a medium-sized plant
- **Maintenance, energy, indirect site costs, logistics simplification:** €20m–€60m/year
- **Overhead allocation and support-function reduction:** €10m–€40m/year

So a meaningful German plant closure can easily produce roughly:

- **€100m–€300m per year** in recurring savings, sometimes more for a large site  
- plus **one-time restructuring costs** that can be very large: redundancy, decommissioning, remediation, contract termination, relocation, and supply-chain transition.

A multi-plant footprint reduction can therefore require **several hundred million euros per year** in offsetting savings if closures are not used.

## 3) Equivalent savings from alternative measures

### A. Shift reduction / working-time changes
If you remove one shift from a heavily loaded plant or reduce planned production time materially, the savings can be large, but only if volumes do not simply reappear elsewhere.

Typical annual savings:
- **One shift removed at a plant:** about **€30m–€100m/year**
- **Shorter weekly working time / seasonal flexibility:** often **€10m–€40m/year**, depending on staffing structure and overtime reliance

Important: the savings are only durable if staffing is actually resized. If headcount stays the same, much of the “saving” becomes temporary or merely accounting-based.

### B. Headcount reduction
In German automotive production and supporting functions, a fully loaded employment cost can roughly range from:

- **€60k–€100k per employee/year** in production/technical functions
- **€90k–€140k+ per employee/year** in higher-skill indirect roles, once overhead and benefits are included

So the annual run-rate savings from headcount reduction are approximately:

- **1,000 FTE removed:** roughly **€60m–€120m/year**
- **2,000 FTE removed:** roughly **€120m–€240m/year**

This is the single most important offset to no-closure restructuring. If management needs “closure-equivalent” savings, headcount reduction is usually the biggest lever.

### C. Voluntary exits / retirement corridors
These are operationally attractive but slower and less certain.

Typical effects:
- Lower annual savings in year 1 because many exits happen gradually
- Stronger savings once the cohort fully rolls through

A corridor of, say, **1,500–3,000 exits** over 2–4 years can ultimately yield:
- **€90m–€300m/year** in steady-state savings, depending on cost per employee and replacement rate

But the timing is critical: the company often needs cash relief **before** the full savings arrive.

### D. Product-mix and platform concentration
Reducing complexity can save money without closing plants, but only if the product architecture really changes.

Typical mechanisms:
- fewer variants
- fewer option combinations
- higher commonality
- fewer low-volume derivatives
- shifting volume to fewer lines/sites

Potential savings:
- **€20m–€80m/year** in direct manufacturing and logistics savings
- plus larger indirect savings in engineering, planning, purchasing, and quality systems over time

These savings are real, but they are usually **not enough alone** to replace a plant closure unless the network is also overstaffed.

### E. Outsourcing or supplier substitution
This can lower fixed costs, but often raises variable cost and risk.

Savings range:
- **€10m–€50m/year** in selected functions or components
- sometimes more, but with quality, supply security, or labor-relations risk

It is usually a supporting measure, not the main answer.

## 4) What “equivalent” usually means in practice

If management is targeting a closure package worth, for example, **€150m–€250m/year** in recurring savings, then a non-closure package generally has to combine measures roughly like this:

- **1,500–2,500 FTE reduction**  
  = about **€90m–€240m/year**
- **one shift elimination or equivalent working-time reduction**  
  = about **€30m–€80m/year**
- **product/complexity reductions and overhead cuts**  
  = about **€20m–€60m/year**

That yields an approximate combined run-rate of:
- **€140m–€380m/year**

So yes, a non-closure package can be economically equivalent, **but only if it is deep**. A “light” voluntary package will not replace closure economics.

## 5) The usual problem: non-closure packages are often slower and less certain

The main tradeoffs are:

### Direct savings
- Closures are immediate and durable.
- Voluntary measures are slower and may not fully realize.

### One-time restructuring costs
- Closures have high one-time costs.
- Voluntary packages also cost money: severance, buyouts, bridge payments, retraining, transfer support, and possible wage concessions.

### Timing
- Closures usually improve the P&L faster.
- Headcount attrition and working-time measures often take **12–36 months** to fully show up.

### Execution risk
- If the no-closure package depends on voluntary exits, there is a risk the take-up rate is too low.
- If it depends on labor-cost concessions, there is a risk the savings expire too early.
- If it depends on productivity improvements, there is a risk the savings are overstated.

## 6) What a credible no-closure package would need

For a large German automotive operation, a credible substitute for closure generally needs:

1. **Hard headcount reduction target**
   - not just “natural attrition”
   - but a measurable FTE reduction with dates

2. **Shift and capacity reset**
   - less scheduled capacity, not just more flexible scheduling

3. **Variant and complexity reduction**
   - fewer derivatives, fewer special builds, fewer low-volume lines

4. **Workforce cost contribution**
   - temporary or structural wage moderation, reduced extras, or deferred increases

5. **Management and overhead cuts**
   - visible reductions in indirect cost and executive overhead

6. **Clear savings governance**
   - monthly tracking against run-rate targets
   - trigger points if exits or savings underdeliver

## 7) Practical benchmark

As a rule of thumb:

- If the issue is **one medium plant**, a no-closure solution must usually find at least **€100m–€200m/year** in offsetting savings.
- If the issue is **multiple sites / a large footprint excess**, the required offset can rise to **€300m/year or more**.
- Without **material headcount reduction**, the package is unlikely to be equivalent.
- Without **some capacity reduction**, the package is usually not durable.

## 8) Bottom-line assessment

From an operations and restructuring perspective:

- **A no-closure solution is possible.**
- **It is not cheap.**
- It must be **deep enough to remove the same fixed costs** that a closure would remove.
- The main levers are:
  - **headcount reduction**
  - **shift reduction**
  - **working-time reduction**
  - **product-mix simplification**
  - **overhead cuts**
- If those do not add up to the closure-equivalent savings target, then the company will still carry structural overcapacity.
