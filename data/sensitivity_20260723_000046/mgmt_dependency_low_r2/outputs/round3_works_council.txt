We are now at the point where everyone is pretending the only remaining question is technical. It is not. It is a power and responsibility question.

Let me respond directly to each side.

## To management
You still have not answered the central issue: **why the company must start with closures or the threat of forced layoffs** when a no-closure restructuring is still available.

Yes, the expert has shown that a no-closure solution must be deep. We never denied that. But “deep” does **not** mean “therefore close plants.” It means:

- real shift and capacity reductions,
- real FTE reduction through voluntary exits and internal transfers,
- real productivity and complexity cuts,
- real management and owner contributions,
- and real investment commitments.

That is the standard now. Not “closures unless labour surrenders.”

You keep saying the economics must be solved. Agreed. But you are trying to make the workforce pay for management’s strategic missteps by forcing us to accept the destruction of sites and the threat of compulsory redundancies. That is not restructuring. That is blackmail dressed up as business logic.

If you want our cooperation, bring a quantified plan that **keeps every German plant open**, **excludes compulsory redundancies**, and **shows the savings gap is closed by other means**. Until then, you are asking us to sign away the company’s social contract for nothing.

## To the controlling family holding
You say you want durable value and no prolonged conflict. Then stop backing a line that guarantees both.

You now know from the expert that the alternatives exist. So the question is not whether there is a no-closure path. The question is whether you are willing to force management to take it seriously.

If the controlling shareholder supports closures as the default, then you are not acting as a responsible anchor; you are escalating a conflict that will hit output, trust, and implementation.

So let me be clear: I do **not** accept your framing that “all sites open regardless of economics” is the problem. The problem is that management has not yet produced a credible no-closure solution with management and owner sacrifices large enough to match the workforce’s ask. That burden is on you too.

## To the regional state government
You are right on one point: the adjustment must be economically serious. But you are wrong if you think that means we must first accept closures and then see what happens.

The state should be using its political and governance weight to insist on:
- domestic production,
- future product allocation,
- job protection,
- and negotiated restructuring without forced measures.

If you are serious about protecting the industrial base, then use your leverage now. Do not wait until management has engineered a fait accompli and call that “realism.”

## To the institutional investors
You say you will only support closure-equivalent savings. Fine. Then be honest about what that means in operational terms: not just headcount reduction, but **who carries the risk** and **what is being preserved**.

If the package is deep enough to deliver the savings, then closures are not economically necessary. That is exactly what the expert confirmed. So stop treating plant closures as the benchmark of seriousness. The benchmark is whether the company can achieve durable savings **without destroying sites and trust**.

And let me also be blunt: a deal that triggers strikes, litigation, governance deadlock, and loss of know-how will destroy value too. That is not an employee side fantasy — it is an industrial reality.

## On the expert assessment
The expert has given you the operational answer, and it cuts against the management default. The assessment says clearly:

- a no-closure solution is possible,
- it can be economically equivalent,
- but only if it is deep enough.

That is exactly why the works council and union are insisting on a serious package rather than surrendering on closures and forced layoffs.

What does “deep enough” mean in practice? It means the company must put numbers on:

- the savings gap,
- the FTE reduction,
- the shift reduction,
- the overhead cuts,
- the management contribution,
- and the investment commitment.

Management has not yet done that transparently. Until they do, the call for closures remains a political preference, not a proven necessity.

## Our position
I will say it plainly, again, because there is no reason to hide behind mediator language:

- **No German plant closures**
- **No compulsory redundancies**
- **No dismantling of the job-security framework**
- **No weakening of co-determination into a post-facto consultation role**

Those are not bargaining chips. They are boundaries.

We are willing to discuss:
- working-time flexibility,
- voluntary exit programs,
- internal redeployment,
- qualification,
- temporary and proportionate labour contributions,
- productivity measures,
- and a monitored restructuring process.

But only on the condition that:
- every German plant remains open,
- no one is forced out,
- and the company commits to future products, investment, and site utilization.

## On the “bridge” proposals
I reject any “phased no-closure” plan that is really just a delayed closure trap.

If a proposal says:
- “no closures for now,”
- “no forced layoffs for now,”
- and then triggers later reopen the door to exactly what we rejected,

then it is not a guarantee. It is a postponement of pressure.

We do not need a grace period before being asked to surrender. We need binding protection.

## What would actually be acceptable
Not “acceptable” in the loose sense — acceptable to negotiate seriously.

That requires:
1. **A binding no-closure commitment for all German plants**
2. **A binding no-compulsory-redundancies commitment**
3. **Site and job guarantees through 2030**
4. **A quantified savings plan based on voluntary and negotiated measures**
5. **Concrete management and owner contributions**
6. **A real co-determination role in design and monitoring**

If those conditions are not met, there is no basis for agreement.

## Final point
The workforce has already shown it will mobilize. The warning strikes were not symbolic. They were a signal that management cannot simply push through a restructuring against the workforce and expect peace.

If you want stability, then respect the red lines.  
If you want a fight, then keep talking about closures and forced redundancies as if they were inevitable.

We are not moving off our core position.
