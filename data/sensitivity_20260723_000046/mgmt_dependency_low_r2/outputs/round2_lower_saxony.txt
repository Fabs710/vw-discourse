I will respond plainly.

## My position

The regional state government is **not prepared to support a restructuring that relies on German plant closures as the default answer, or that treats compulsory redundancies as the normal instrument of adjustment**.

That is not because we deny the economic pressure. We do not. The specialist assessment confirms what we already knew: this is a structural cost and capacity problem, and a real turnaround requires material savings. But the same assessment also shows something important: **a no-closure solution is possible if it is deep enough**. That is the route we should exhaust first.

## Where I agree

I agree with management, the family holding, and investors on one point:  
- **the company needs measurable, durable competitiveness gains**.

I also agree with the union and works council that:
- the workforce did not create this crisis alone,
- burden-sharing must be fair,
- and any solution must include future products, investment, and a credible industrial strategy.

I also accept the expert’s core operational conclusion:
- if closures are off the table, the alternative must be **serious**, not symbolic;
- it must include **real headcount reduction, shift reduction, complexity reduction, and overhead cuts**.

## Where I disagree

### 1) Plant closures are not the starting point
Management says closures may be necessary because there is overcapacity. That may be true in the abstract, but it does **not** follow that closures must be chosen first.

For a company of this importance, with the special statute, co-determination, and regional responsibilities, you do not begin by sacrificing plants and communities. You begin by demanding a package that achieves the required savings through a combination of:
- voluntary exits,
- retirement corridors,
- internal transfers,
- working-time changes,
- product and complexity reduction,
- and management/overhead contribution.

If that package proves insufficient, then we can discuss what further steps are truly unavoidable. But we are not there yet.

### 2) Compulsory redundancies are not an acceptable default
I do not accept management’s insistence on keeping forced layoffs “available” as a normal bargaining lever.

That may be convenient for management, but it is not a fair or stable basis for settlement. It poisons trust and makes the whole process more conflictual. If the company wants a durable agreement, it should build one on negotiated instruments first.

### 3) A “temporary” labour contribution is not enough by itself
To the union: I understand your insistence on no forced redundancies and no dismantling of protections. But I also need to be candid: if the contribution from labour is only symbolic or very short-lived, it will not close the structural gap.

The expert is right that a no-closure package needs to be deep. That means labour may have to contribute through:
- temporary wage moderation,
- reduced extras,
- longer working-time flexibility,
- and lower planned capacity.

I am open to that. But I will only support it if there are **binding reciprocal commitments** on:
- sites,
- jobs,
- investment,
- and product allocation.

## What would actually move the state

A viable package for us would need these elements:

1. **No plant closures in Germany**
   - not just “not yet,” but a real commitment that closures are not the adjustment tool of first resort.

2. **No compulsory redundancies**
   - adjustment through voluntary exits, redeployment, qualification, and retirement corridors.

3. **A quantified and executable savings plan**
   - not generalities, but numbers that close the gap.

4. **A real capacity reset**
   - yes, this means fewer shifts, lower planned volume, and leaner operations;
   - but done through a negotiated footprint adjustment, not closures.

5. **Binding site and investment guarantees**
   - if workers make concessions, they must be matched by commitments to German production, future products, and EV investment.

6. **Management and owner contribution**
   - top-level overhead restraint,
   - visible discipline at executive level,
   - and willingness from owners to support the transformation financially.

7. **A genuine co-determination role**
   - the works council and union must not be informed after the fact;
   - they must help design and monitor the restructuring.

## To management

I hear your point that the economics must be solved. That is true. But you have not convinced me that closures are required before a serious non-closure package is tested.

The expert’s numbers point to what is needed:
- substantial FTE reduction,
- shift elimination,
- complexity reduction,
- overhead cuts.

So bring us that package in concrete form. Show us:
- how many jobs can be reduced through voluntary and negotiated means,
- which shifts can be cut,
- how product complexity will be reduced,
- what management will contribute,
- and what the savings target is.

If the numbers truly do not add up, then we can discuss the next step. But not before.

## To the works council and union

Your line that there must be no closures and no compulsory redundancies is politically and socially understandable. I support that as a **negotiating starting point**, not as an excuse to avoid all hard adjustments.

The expert has made clear that a no-closure solution has to be deep. That means:
- real capacity reduction,
- real workforce adjustment through voluntary instruments,
- and real concessions on flexibility and cost.

If you want me to stand with you against closures, I need to see that you are also prepared to support a serious restructuring that makes the remaining German footprint viable.

## To the family holding and investors

You are right that symbolic compromise will not work. But I am not going to endorse a value-destructive closure program just to satisfy a theoretical purity test on returns.

You have governance power too. Use it to support a negotiated package that is hard, measurable, and executable **without** breaking the regional anchor role of the company.

If the final package contains:
- deep savings,
- leaner operations,
- structural cost relief,
- and future investment,

then it can be supported. But if you insist that only closures count as “real” restructuring, then you are narrowing the field unnecessarily and escalating the conflict.

## Bottom line

The regional state government is prepared to support a **deep negotiated restructuring without German plant closures or compulsory redundancies**, provided it is genuinely capable of closing the savings gap.

What we will not support is:
- plant closures as the first resort,
- forced layoffs as the default tool,
- or a settlement that shifts the burden almost entirely onto the workforce.

So my message is simple:  
**Bring a quantified no-closure package that is as serious as the expert says it must be.**  
If it is serious, we can engage. If it is just a softer version of the same closure logic, then we will oppose it.
