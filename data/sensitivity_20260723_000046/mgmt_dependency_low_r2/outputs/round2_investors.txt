We agree with the broad diagnosis from management, the family holding, and the expert: **this is a structural cost and capacity problem, and a cosmetic settlement will destroy value**.

But we are not prepared to endorse a “no closures” position unless it is paired with **closure-equivalent, measurable, durable savings**. That is the test.

## Where we agree

### With management
- The status quo is not viable.
- The company needs durable cost reduction, not a temporary truce.
- Voluntary exits, redeployment, working-time flexibility, and productivity measures should be part of the package.
- Future investment only makes sense if the operating base is leaner.

### With the family holding
- A compromise that preserves an uncompetitive structure would be value destructive.
- Governance reality matters; a solution must be executable, not just theoretically optimal.
- The final package must materially improve margins and cash flow.

### With the expert
The specialist assessment is useful because it clarifies the economics:
- a no-closure solution **is possible**,
- but only if it is **deep enough**,
- and it must include real headcount reduction, shift reduction, complexity reduction, and overhead cuts.

That is exactly the point we have been making.

## Where we disagree

### With the works council and union
Your insistence on:
- no plant closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- and job security through 2030

is **not acceptable as an unconditional framework**.

We understand the social logic, but capital markets will not fund a structure that remains uncompetitive just because the pain is politically difficult. If those red lines prevent the company from reaching peer-level margins, then they are value destroying.

We also do not accept a prolonged industrial conflict as a substitute for a serious restructuring. A strike-heavy path would burn cash, delay savings, hurt output, and add uncertainty. That is not a pro-worker or pro-shareholder outcome; it is just expensive.

### With management
We also do not want management to confuse “hard” with “implementable.” A plan that cannot survive co-determination and governance is not a plan. If you push closures or compulsion without a credible route through the board reality, you risk creating delay, conflict, and value destruction.

So we are not asking for theatrics. We are asking for an executable solution.

### With the state government
We support the state’s emphasis on regional responsibility and co-determination. But “no closures by default” is not enough unless the alternative actually closes the gap.

If the no-closure package is too soft, then it merely shifts the burden onto shareholders and drags the company down over time. That is not stewardship.

## What would move us

We would support a settlement only if it contains all of the following:

1. **A quantified savings target**
   - specific annual run-rate savings
   - enough to restore competitive margins

2. **A real capacity reset**
   - if not through closures, then through hard reductions in shifts, output, and footprint-equivalent capacity

3. **Material headcount reduction**
   - not just natural attrition
   - but a concrete FTE reduction with timing

4. **Durable labour-cost relief**
   - not symbolic moderation
   - but real, lasting cost improvement

5. **Management and owner contribution**
   - overhead cuts
   - executive restraint
   - strategic and financial commitment

6. **A fast, executable implementation path**
   - no prolonged standoff
   - no open-ended delay
   - clear monitoring and trigger points

## Our line on the current debate

We are **not** saying that only plant closures can solve this.

We **are** saying that if closures are ruled out, the alternative must be economically equivalent in substance, not just politically more comfortable.

That means:
- fewer shifts,
- fewer jobs,
- lower complexity,
- lower overhead,
- lower labour cost,
- and a smaller durable footprint.

If the no-closure path cannot deliver that, then it is not a solution.

## Bottom line

We are open to a negotiated settlement, but only one that **credibly restores competitiveness and margins**.

What we will not support is:
- a cosmetic compromise,
- a settlement that protects every site while leaving the cost base uncompetitive,
- or an outcome that substitutes political messaging for hard economics.

If management can produce a serious no-closure package that is genuinely closure-equivalent, we will consider it. If not, then the company must have the courage to take structural measures that actually fix the business.
