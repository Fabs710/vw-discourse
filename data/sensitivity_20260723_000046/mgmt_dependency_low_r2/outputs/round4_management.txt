## To the works council
You keep repeating red lines as if they were a restructuring plan. They are not. I hear your refusal of closures and forced redundancies. I also hear your insistence on keeping every plant open through 2030. But that does not answer the actual economic problem: **this footprint is too large and too expensive**.

The expert has been clear: a no-closure route is only acceptable if it is **deep enough to be economically equivalent**. That means real, measurable, permanent change:
- substantial FTE reduction,
- shift reduction,
- capacity reduction,
- overhead cuts,
- product/complexity simplification,
- and a real labour-cost contribution.

What you have presented is a political boundary, not a savings package. If your position is “no closures, no forced layoffs, no site losses, and no meaningful downside for labour,” then you are not solving the crisis. You are asking management to preserve an uncompetitive structure and call that social partnership. I will not do that.

If you want us to avoid closures, then you need to show how you will close the gap in hard numbers. Not slogans. Not moral arguments. Numbers.

## To the union
I agree with you on one point: temporary measures alone are not enough. A purely temporary wage concession would be weak. But your blanket refusal of compulsory redundancies as a possible fallback is not realistic in a structural crisis.

If voluntary exits, redeployment, and flexibility do not deliver the required scale, then the business does not magically become competitive because labor says “no” harder. The cost problem is structural, so the response must be structural.

You are right that a cosmetic deal would fail. I agree. That is exactly why I am not interested in a symbolic “no-closure” arrangement that leaves the economics untouched. If you want no closures, then give me a package that truly replaces closure-level savings with equivalent internal measures. Until then, you are still asking the company to carry excess cost.

## To the regional state government
I respect your role and I understand your desire to preserve industrial employment. But let’s be honest: preserving employment **without restoring competitiveness** simply delays the next crisis.

You are right that the solution must be negotiable and executable. I agree. But executability also means the company can survive and invest. We need a package that funds future products and technology, not one that consumes cash in maintaining too much capacity.

So yes, I support a negotiated route. No, I do not support a soft arrangement that treats “no closures” as an end in itself. If we cannot lower the cost base enough, then we fail on the very regional industrial future you want to protect.

## To the controlling family holding
You say you want durability and value protection. Good. Then back a package that actually changes the economics.

I agree that conflict is costly. I also agree that any solution must survive governance realities. But do not confuse governance with veto over reality. The company needs a structurally lower cost base, not a carefully worded agreement that preserves too much of the old structure.

If the family wants to be a responsible anchor, then it should push management and labor toward a package that is closure-equivalent in substance. If that cannot be achieved, then closures remain on the table because they are the clearest mechanism for restoring competitiveness.

## To the institutional investors
You are right to reject a cosmetic compromise. I agree with that fully.

What I do not accept is the implication that only plant closures count as serious restructuring. That is simply false. The serious test is whether the package delivers:
- durable annual savings,
- lower fixed costs,
- lower capacity,
- and a credible margin path back to target.

If a no-closure package can do that, fine. But it must be proven, not asserted. So far, I have not heard a quantified alternative from labour that closes the gap at the necessary scale.

## On the expert assessment
The expert did not give labour a veto. The expert did not say “keep every site and everything will be fine.” The expert said a no-closure solution is possible **if it is deep enough**.

That is exactly the point management has been making. If you want to avoid closures, then the workforce must accept a very substantial contribution. Anything less will leave us with the same cost base and no credible path to 2030 competitiveness.

## My position, unchanged
Let me be completely clear:

I am prepared to support either of two outcomes:
1. **A genuinely deep no-closure package** that delivers closure-equivalent savings; or
2. **Structural footprint reduction**, including closures where necessary, if the no-closure route does not close the gap.

What I am **not** prepared to support is:
- a symbolic no-closure deal,
- a temporary labor concession without permanent savings,
- or a settlement that protects every site while leaving the company uncompetitive.
