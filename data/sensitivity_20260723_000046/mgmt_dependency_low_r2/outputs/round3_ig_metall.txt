## To management
I agree with you on one point: this company has a **real structural competitiveness problem** and it needs a durable solution. I also agree that voluntary measures, qualification, redeployment, working-time flexibility, and complexity reduction all belong in the package.

Where I disagree is your insistence that **closures or compulsory redundancies must remain the default threat**.

That is not a neutral “management option.” It is a way of shifting the burden onto the workforce before the alternatives have been seriously exhausted.

The expert assessment made something very clear:
- a **no-closure solution is possible**,
- but it must be **deep enough**,
- and it must include **real capacity reduction, headcount reduction, and cost relief**.

So the real question is not whether adjustment is necessary.  
The real question is whether management is prepared to pursue a **serious negotiated restructuring** instead of using closures as leverage.

If you want our cooperation, then put numbers on the table:
- the exact savings gap,
- the FTE reduction target,
- the shift reduction target,
- the management/owner contribution,
- and the investment commitments you are prepared to sign.

Until then, “closures may be necessary” is not a solution. It is pressure.

## To the works council
We are aligned on the essentials:
- no plant closures as the starting point,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- no unilateral dismantling of job security,
- and a real industrial future for the German sites.

I fully support your refusal to let this become a sacrifice plan for workers alone.

Where I would sharpen the point is this: a no-closure settlement cannot be **symbolic**. If we want to hold the line on closures and forced layoffs, then the alternative has to be operationally serious.

That means we must be ready to discuss:
- temporary wage moderation or deferred extras,
- longer working-time flexibility,
- substantial voluntary exit corridors,
- internal redeployment on a meaningful scale,
- and product/complexity simplification.

But I am saying this clearly: those talks only make sense if they are matched by binding guarantees:
- on sites,
- on jobs,
- on investment,
- and on no compulsory redundancies.

If management wants workers to contribute, then the contribution must be shared, not one-sided.

That means your governance role should be used to force management to bring forward the **deep no-closure package**, not to keep plant closure as the benchmark of seriousness.

- preserves German sites,
- avoids compulsory redundancies,
- and still achieves the savings target through negotiated means.

## To the institutional investors
You are right about one thing: a cosmetic deal would destroy value. I do not disagree with that.

But your benchmark must not be “closures prove seriousness.” That is too simplistic.

The operational assessment already showed the path:
- if the no-closure package is deep enough, it can be closure-equivalent.

So the test is not whether a plant is shut.  
The test is whether the company gets:
- durable savings,
- lower complexity,
- lower capacity,
- and a credible future investment base.

If the savings can be achieved without closures, then that is not weakness. It is actually a better outcome because it avoids the damage of conflict, know-how loss, and long-term morale collapse.

## My position, plainly
I am not softening on the essentials.

1. **No German plant closures**
2. **No compulsory redundancies**
3. **Real burden-sharing from management and owners**
4. **Temporary and proportionate workforce contributions**
5. **Binding investment and product commitments**
6. **A durable collective-bargaining framework**
7. **A real role for co-determination in monitoring the deal**

What we will not accept:
- closures as a bargaining starting point,
- forced layoffs as a normal adjustment tool,
- or a restructuring where workers carry the pain while management and owners mainly issue demands.
