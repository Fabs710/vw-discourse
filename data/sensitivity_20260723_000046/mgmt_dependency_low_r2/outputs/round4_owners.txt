## My position as controlling shareholder

We support a restructuring that **restores competitiveness, margins, and investment capacity**. We do **not** support a settlement that merely postpones the problem or preserves an uncompetitive cost base under a more comfortable label.

I agree with all sides on one point: this is a **structural** issue, not a temporary dip. The company needs a durable solution, not a truce.

But I am **not** prepared to endorse an arrangement that rules out all hard structural measures in advance while offering only general promises of “voluntary” adjustment. That would not protect long-term value. It would erode it.

---

## To the works council

I understand why you insist on protecting jobs and sites. I also agree that the workforce should not carry the burden alone.

Where I disagree is your attempt to turn that into a blanket rule of:
- no closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- no real downside for labour,
- and no fallback if the economics do not close.

That is not a restructuring strategy. It is a red line list.

The expert assessment has been useful because it clarified the real question: **a no-closure path can work, but only if it is deep enough to be economically equivalent**. That means:
- material FTE reduction,
- shift reduction,
- lower capacity,
- overhead cuts,
- and genuine labour-cost contribution.

So I do **not** accept the claim that closures are automatically unnecessary just because they are unpopular. If a no-closure package truly closes the gap, then fine — show it. If it does not, then keeping every site open becomes a way of preserving overcapacity at the expense of competitiveness.

That is not acceptable to us.

---

## To the union

I agree that temporary concessions alone are insufficient. A short-lived wage moderation that expires while the structural problem remains would be weak and ineffective.

But I do **not** accept a blanket refusal of compulsory redundancies in all circumstances. If voluntary exits, redeployment, working-time flexibility, and internal productivity measures do not close the gap, then the company cannot pretend the gap has vanished.

You are right that a cosmetic compromise would fail. I agree. That is precisely why I am not interested in a symbolic no-closure deal. If you want to keep closures off the table, then the alternative must be hard, quantified, and durable.

So my answer is: **I will test a no-closure package, but only if it is closure-equivalent in substance.**

## To management

You are right about the economic problem. But I want to be equally clear: **“closures may be necessary” is not a plan**. It is leverage.

If you want governance support, bring a package that can survive co-determination and still deliver the savings. That means:
- the savings gap,
- the required FTE reduction,
- the required capacity reduction,
- the management and owner contribution,
- and the investment commitments.

What I will not back is a plan that is arithmetically neat but politically and institutionally impossible to execute. And I will also not back a plan that destroys labour peace without producing a clear competitiveness gain.

So management must stop speaking in abstractions and show us the operational path.

## To the regional state government

I agree with you that the company has a regional responsibility, and I respect your role as anchor shareholder.

Where I disagree is your tendency to treat no closures as the preferred answer regardless of the scale of the required adjustment. That is too soft if it leaves the company structurally impaired.

You are right that closures have social costs. But prolonged underperformance also has social costs:
- lost market share,
- weaker investment,
- margin erosion,
- and eventual job losses anyway.

So I support your insistence on a negotiated solution, but only if it is economically real. A political preference for site preservation is not enough by itself.

## To the institutional investors

I agree with your main concern: a cosmetic deal would destroy value.

But I would caution against treating plant closures as the only credible signal of seriousness. The real question is whether the package delivers:
- durable savings,
- lower fixed cost,
- lower capacity,
- and a credible margin path.

If a no-closure package can do that, it should be judged on its economics, not on the symbolism of whether a site is shut. If it cannot, then it is not serious enough.

---

## My view of the expert assessment

The expert has confirmed a very important point: **the no-closure route is not impossible**.

But the expert has also made clear that it is not easy or cheap. It must be:
- deep,
- quantified,
- and structurally meaningful.

That is the standard. Not “no closures at any price,” and not “closures first no matter what,” but **closure-equivalent restructuring or a hard alternative**.

## What would move us

We would support a serious no-closure package if it includes all of the following:

1. **A quantified savings target**
   - annual run-rate savings stated clearly.

2. **A concrete FTE reduction target**
   - not attrition slogans, but real net reduction.

3. **A measurable capacity reset**
   - shift reduction, line consolidation, specialization, or equivalent footprint reduction.

4. **Permanent cost relief**
   - not just temporary employee concessions.

5. **Visible management and owner contribution**
   - overhead restraint and strategic commitment.

6. **Binding investment and product commitments**
   - especially for future technologies and German sites.

7. **A governance-executable implementation path**
   - something that can actually pass supervisory-board realities.

---

## What we will not support

We will not support:
- a symbolic no-closure arrangement,
- a deal that preserves excess capacity,
- a temporary labor concession without durable savings,
- or a settlement that leaves the company structurally weak while claiming victory.

We will also not support a prolonged conflict that destroys output, trust, and value. That is exactly the risk we are trying to avoid.

---

## On the “bridge” proposals

A phased no-closure pact can only work if it is **genuinely binding**.

I would consider a time-limited arrangement if it contains:
- hard savings targets,
- verified milestones,
- a real no-closure commitment for the agreed period,
- and a clear monitoring mechanism.

But I do **not** accept a “phase one” that is simply a delayed route to the same closure debate later. If the agreement is only a pause before reopening the same fight, it does not solve anything.

## Bottom line

Our position is straightforward:

- **Yes** to a serious restructuring.
- **Yes** to voluntary exits, redeployment, qualification, and flexibility.
- **Yes** to burden-sharing from labour, management, and owners.
- **Yes** to testing a no-closure package.

But:

- **No** to a deal that preserves an uncompetitive structure.
- **No** to a cosmetic compromise.
- **No** to a package that cannot credibly restore margins and fund future investment.
- **No** to governance paralysis created by refusing all hard choices in advance.

If the no-closure route truly matches the economics, prove it.  
If it does not, then we should stop pretending that sentiment can substitute for restructuring.
