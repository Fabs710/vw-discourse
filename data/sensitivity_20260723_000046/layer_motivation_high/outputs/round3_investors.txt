We appreciate the restructuring assessment. It clarifies the central point: **there is a viable non-closure path only if it delivers real, permanent capacity reduction and not just temporary concessions**.

That is also where our position becomes very clear.

## Where we agree
- **Management is right** that the current cost base is not competitive.
- **The expert is right** that this is not “closures or nothing.”
- **The family owners are right** that prolonged conflict would destroy value.
- **The labor and state side are right** that one-sided employee sacrifice would be unstable and likely to trigger costly disruption.

So we are not aligned with any ideological closure-first position. We are aligned with **durable margin repair**.

## Where we disagree
### To the works council and union
Your insistence on:
- no plant closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- no weakening of job-security frameworks,

is understandable politically, but from a capital-market perspective it is only acceptable **if you can show a quantified alternative that fully closes the competitiveness gap**.

So far, the discussion has not produced that.

Temporary wage restraint, bonus waivers, working-time flexibility, redeployment, and attrition are useful tools. But by themselves they do not necessarily create the **permanent fixed-cost reset** the company needs. If they are not enough, then ruling out closures in advance becomes a way of preserving structural overcapacity.

That is not acceptable to us.

We will say this plainly: **we cannot support a settlement that leaves the company with a structurally impaired cost base and weak margins.**

### To management
We also do not accept a blunt-force posture that treats closures as the default answer before the alternatives are fully quantified.

The expert is right on this point: the decision must be plant-by-plant and measure-by-measure. Management still needs to show:
- which savings are achievable without closures,
- which are not,
- what the net savings are after transition costs,
- and how investment in future products is protected.

If management cannot prove that closures are required, then pushing them too early would be value-destructive.

### To the state government
We support your insistence that labor should not bear the whole burden. But a moratorium cannot become a permanent freeze.

A blocking position is legitimate to force negotiation discipline. It is **not** legitimate if it is used to prevent all meaningful network rationalization indefinitely.

### To the controlling family holding
We agree that stewardship means real restructuring, not optics. But stewardship also means being willing to make the case with actual economics, not just broad statements about “hard choices.”

You need to support a quantified plan. Otherwise markets will assume the governance structure is just delaying the inevitable.

## Our position on the expert’s conclusion
The expert’s conclusion is basically right:
- **A no-closure path is plausible**
- **but only if it includes genuine capacity reduction**
- **and only if it materially improves fixed costs and margins**

That is our standard too.

What we will not support is:
- a cosmetic compromise,
- a deal that preserves every site by default,
- or a package where the burden is mostly on employees while the cost base remains uncompetitive.

## What would move us
We would support a non-closure restructuring if it included all of the following:

1. **Quantified permanent savings**
   - enough to credibly restore competitive margins.

2. **Real capacity reduction**
   - not just temporary underutilization or flexible working-time language.

3. **Shared sacrifice**
   - employees, management, owners, and the company all contributing.

4. **Binding investment commitments**
   - future products and technology in Germany.

5. **Clear milestones and enforceability**
   - not aspirational statements.

6. **A credible fallback**
   - if the package fails to deliver, the company must retain the option to act.

That last point matters. If there is no credible consequence for failure, then there is no discipline.

## Our bottom line
We are open to a negotiated solution that avoids closures **if and only if** it produces the same economic effect.

But we will not back:
- a symbolic settlement,
- a permanent closure taboo,
- or a restructuring that leaves structural overcapacity untouched.

If the parties can build a package that actually resets the cost base, we will support it and defend it in the market. If they cannot, then we will not pretend a weak compromise is a turnaround.
