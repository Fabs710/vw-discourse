## Where I agree

I agree with everyone on three things:

1. **This company needs a durable restructuring.**  
   A cosmetic deal, a temporary truce, or a package that leaves the cost base untouched will fail.

2. **The burden cannot fall only on employees.**  
   Management, ownership, and the company itself must contribute visibly.

3. **Investment in future products and technology through 2030 is essential.**  
   If we cut costs without freeing up capital for the future, we lose twice.

So on the diagnosis, there is broad agreement.

## Where I disagree — and strongly

### To the works council
You keep saying “no closures, no compulsory redundancies, no weakening of guarantees” as if that were a restructuring answer. It is not. It is a boundary line.

I respect your duty to the workforce. But you have still not explained how you will solve **permanent overcapacity** and **costs far above target** without touching the footprint in a material way.

You ask for proof that closures are necessary. Fine. But the burden is not on management alone to produce a legal brief while the company bleeds margin. The burden is on all of us to produce a package that actually works.

If your alternative is:
- temporary wage restraint,
- bonus waivers,
- working-time flexibility,
- attrition,
- redeployment,
- and modest internal reallocation,

then I ask directly: **what is the quantified permanent savings effect?**  
If it is not enough, then it is not enough. Naming it “fair” does not make it structurally sufficient.

I also reject your claim that closures are merely a strategic failure on management’s part. The reality is more blunt: the European market is shrinking, German factory costs are too high, and the brand is missing its return-on-sales target. That is not solved by moral arguments.

### To the union
You say closures and forced redundancies are coercion. In a narrow sense, every serious restructuring is coercive to someone. The question is whether it is **economically necessary and proportionate**.

I do not want conflict for its own sake. But I will not pretend that temporary concessions alone are a structural solution. If concessions expire and the cost base remains uncompetitive, then we have merely delayed the crisis and made it more expensive.

I also need to say this clearly: if the workforce uses the end of bargaining peace to launch prolonged strikes, it may win short-term leverage but it will damage output, reputation, and confidence. That is not a threat; it is a fact.

### To the regional state government
You are right that this is not a purely technical decision. But your blocking position cannot function as a permanent shield against structural change. If the state’s role becomes “protect every site at any cost,” then the company will lose competitiveness and future investment will move elsewhere.

A moratorium is only useful if it produces a real result. If it simply postpones the same deadlock, then it is not a solution.

### To the controlling family holding
You say you support stewardship and long-term value. Then stewardship requires more than saying closures are “not preferred.” It means supporting an actual restructuring that lowers the cost base materially.

If the family wants credibility, it should support:
- real capacity reduction,
- real overhead reduction,
- real labour-cost contribution,
- and a settlement that is enforceable.

Not just a general endorsement of “hard choices.”

### To institutional investors
You are right to insist on quantification and durability. I agree with that entirely.

But you also need to be honest about this: a package that rules out any meaningful footprint adjustment while keeping structural overcapacity in place will not restore margins. It will just re-label the problem.

## My position on the moderator’s proposals

### Proposal 1: “No closure now, but binding trigger-based review”
I do **not** accept a trigger mechanism that becomes a disguised veto over future restructuring.

A review can be useful if it measures delivery against a serious package. But it cannot become a permanent promise that closures will never happen regardless of performance. That would remove the last credible lever for fixing structural underperformance.

So: **a limited moratorium may be negotiable; a permanent closure taboo is not.**

### Proposal 2: “Shared-sacrifice package with explicit contributions”
This is the right direction in principle. But it must be real. That means:

- employee contributions with measurable cost impact,
- management cost restraint,
- owner payout discipline,
- and binding investment commitments.

What it does **not** mean is:
- employees carry the major burden,
- management preserves full flexibility,
- and ownership keeps all strategic options except those that affect itself.

Shared sacrifice must be comparable sacrifice.

### Proposal 3: “Site-specific restructuring with protected strategic sites”
This is the most realistic proposal on the table, but let’s not romanticize it.

Not every site can be preserved in its current form. Some sites will be strategic; others will need to shrink, specialize, or change materially. That is not ideology. It is industrial reality.

The question is whether we do that through:
- negotiated specialization,
- redeployment,
- attrition,
- and targeted capacity reduction,

or whether we wait until the company is forced into a harsher outcome later.
