I’ll respond directly.

## First: what I agree with
I agree with all of you on one point: **this company needs a negotiated solution, not a theatrical standoff for its own sake**. A prolonged conflict would damage output, reputation, and value.

I also agree that the burden cannot fall only on employees. There must be contributions from management, owners, and the company itself.

And I agree that future investment in Germany matters. A restructuring that cuts costs but starves the business of product and technology investment would be self-defeating.

## But I need to be equally clear: your red lines do not solve the problem
### To the works council
You say:
- no plant closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- no weakening of job security,
- no changes to co-determination.

That is a political position, not an economic answer.

If you rule out closures in advance, you are asking management to absorb structural overcapacity without using the tools needed to fix it. That is not a sustainable offer. It leaves the cost base where it is, just with nicer language.

I respect the workforce’s contribution, but I do **not** accept the idea that every German site is automatically protected regardless of economics. If a site is structurally underutilized and too expensive, then preserving it unchanged is not responsible stewardship.

### To the union
You say closures and forced redundancies are coercion. I disagree.

What is coercive is asking the company to remain trapped in an uncompetitive structure while competition intensifies and margins stay below target. That would eventually destroy more jobs, more investment, and more security than a difficult restructuring now.

You propose temporary wage restraint, bonus waivers, working-time flexibility, and voluntary exits. Those can help, but they are not enough **unless** they deliver a durable reduction in the cost base and capacity. Temporary measures alone do not fix a structural problem.

### To the state government
You are right that this is not just a technical cost issue. But your blocking position cannot become a guarantee that the company never changes its footprint. If the state uses its influence to freeze structural adjustment, it will protect short-term appearances while weakening the enterprise long term.

### To the family owners
I appreciate your recognition that the status quo is not sustainable. But I also need clarity: if all you do is endorse “hard decisions” in principle while opposing every concrete measure that creates real savings, then we will not get a solution. We need ownership support for an actual restructuring, not just a diagnosis.

### To institutional investors
You are right to demand quantified, durable savings. That is exactly what we are trying to achieve. But your support for capacity reduction has to be real, not conditional on a fantasy that every site can be retained while the economics magically improve.

## The minimum economic reality
Let me put this plainly:

- German factory costs are far above target.
- European demand is shrinking.
- The brand is missing its return-on-sales target.
- We need investment capacity for future products and technology through 2030.

That means we need **permanent** savings in:
1. labour cost,
2. capacity,
3. and fixed overhead.

Anything else is a delay mechanism.

---

## What is negotiable
I am prepared to discuss a package that avoids the most disruptive path if it delivers the same economic effect.

That means I am open to:
- capacity reduction through redeployment, working-time changes, and attrition,
- voluntary departure programs,
- temporary or targeted wage restraint,
- waiver of bonuses or special payments,
- internal efficiency measures,
- investment commitments for future products,
- and site-specific industrial planning.

## What is not negotiable
But I will not sign up to a package that:
- preserves every site in current form,
- protects all employment levels regardless of utilization,
- and relies only on temporary employee concessions.

Nor will I accept a settlement that leaves us unable to restore the cost base structurally.

---

## The package I need to see
If you want a real agreement, then I need a package with these elements:

### 1. Structural capacity reduction
Not cosmetic adjustments. Real reduction in overcapacity, with clear site-level impact.

### 2. Material labour-cost contribution
A genuine contribution from employees, not just symbolic restraint. That means pay and cost flexibility with measurable effect.

### 3. Management and owner contribution
Visible restraint on top-level cost items, governance discipline, and support for investment.

### 4. Investment and product commitment
Binding commitments for future vehicles and technologies in Germany, so that the restructuring is linked to a future, not just a cutback.

### 5. A timetable and enforceability
No vague promises. I want milestones, quantified savings, and a mechanism to verify delivery.

---

## On closures and redundancies
I’ll say this as directly as possible:

- **I prefer not to close plants.**
- **I prefer not to impose compulsory redundancies.**
- But I will not rule them out if the alternative is an uncompetitive structure that the company cannot sustain.

That is not a threat; it is economic reality.

If the labor side brings me a package that achieves the required savings and capacity reduction without closures, I will take it seriously. If not, then closures remain on the table because I have a fiduciary duty to restore competitiveness.

---

## My bottom line
I am prepared to negotiate a serious, durable restructuring.

I am **not** prepared to negotiate a deal that merely preserves the current footprint and cost base while calling it transformation.

If you want a settlement, bring:
- structural capacity reduction,
- real labour-cost savings,
- shared sacrifice,
- and binding investment for the future.

If you do not, then we are not talking about a restructuring. We are talking about postponing the crisis.
