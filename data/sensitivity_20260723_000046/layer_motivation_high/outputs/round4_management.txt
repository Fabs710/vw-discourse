## Round 4 — Group & Brand Management

I’ll be direct.

We are now at the point where the company needs **decisions**, not repeated assertions of principle. I have listened carefully to the works council, the union, the state, the owners, and the investors. I agree with parts of several arguments — but the core problem remains unresolved:

**We still do not have a package that removes enough permanent cost and capacity to restore competitiveness.**

That is the standard. Everything else is secondary.

---

## Where I agree

### 1) A cosmetic deal would be useless
I agree with the expert, investors, and even the labor side on this point: a temporary truce that leaves the structural cost base untouched would fail. We cannot afford that.

### 2) The burden cannot fall only on employees
I have said this already, and I repeat it: management, ownership, and the company itself must contribute visibly. Executive restraint, overhead cuts, payout discipline, and investment support all matter.

### 3) Investment in future products and technology to 2030 is non-negotiable
If we only cut costs but do not free funds for the next cycle, we lose the company twice. So any restructuring must create room for future investment.

---

## Where I disagree — strongly

### To the works council
You keep saying: “No closures, no compulsory redundancies, no weakening of guarantees.”  
That is a boundary line. It is **not** a restructuring answer.

The expert has confirmed that a non-closure solution is **possible in principle** only if it includes **real permanent capacity reduction and fixed-cost removal**. That is not the same as saying closures are unnecessary under all circumstances. It means we should test the alternatives seriously — and that is exactly what I am demanding.

But you have still not produced a quantified alternative that closes the gap. “Attrition, redeployment, working-time flexibility, and management cuts” are instruments, not outcomes. I need the numbers:
- how much permanent cost reduction,
- how much capacity removal,
- by when,
- and with what enforceability?

Without that, your position amounts to preserving the current footprint by assertion. That is not sustainable.

And let me be blunt: if you insist on ruling out closures in advance, then you are asking management to absorb all the downside while keeping the company structurally uncompetitive. I will not sign up to that.

### To the union
You are right that temporary concessions must be limited and matched by other contributions. Agreed.

But you are wrong if you think temporary concessions, by themselves, can do the job. They cannot. If the labor cost base remains structurally too high after the agreement expires, then we have delayed the problem and made it more expensive.

That is why I insist on **durable structural savings**, not just a short-term wage truce.

Also, let’s be honest about strike escalation. Prolonged strikes will damage output, reputation, supplier confidence, and investor trust. That is not a threat; it is the reality of industrial conflict. If we go that route, the company and everyone in it will pay a price.

### To the regional state government
I respect your role and your political responsibility. But your blocking position cannot become a permanent shield against necessary restructuring.

A moratorium is only useful if it leads to a credible result. If it merely freezes the problem, then it becomes a mechanism for delay. And delay is exactly what this company cannot afford.

I will not accept a settlement that turns the state’s role into a veto over any meaningful footprint adjustment. That would lock in overcapacity and weaken future investment in Germany.

### To the controlling family holding
You speak of stewardship and long-term value. Good. Then stewardship requires support for real restructuring, not just general concern about conflict.

If ownership wants long-term value, it must accept:
- real capacity reduction,
- real overhead reduction,
- a genuine labour-cost contribution,
- and a settlement that is enforceable.

Saying “closures are not preferred” is not enough. If closures are not preferred, fine — but then bring me a better package that actually delivers the same effect. So far, I have not seen one.

### To institutional investors
You are right to insist on durability and quantification. I agree with that.

But you also need to be honest: a package that preserves every site in substantially the same form while offering only temporary concessions will not restore margins. It will re-label the problem. I will not accept that either.

---

## On the expert input

The expert’s conclusion is important, and I take it seriously:

- **A serious restructuring can plausibly be delivered without immediate closures**
- **but only if it includes real, permanent capacity reduction and fixed-cost removal**
- **otherwise it is just postponement**

That is precisely why I am pushing for a quantified, site-by-site package. I am not asking for closure for its own sake. I am asking for a restructuring that actually works.

But I also want to be equally clear: the expert did **not** say closures are off the table. The expert said they may still become necessary if one or more sites remain uneconomic after the alternatives are fully tested. That aligns with my position.

So let us not pretend the expert has endorsed a permanent no-closure doctrine. They have not.

---

## My position on the compromise proposals

### 1) Time-bound moratorium + quantified network review
I can accept a **limited moratorium** on plant closures and compulsory redundancies **during a defined negotiation period**, **if** it is paired with a serious, plant-by-plant review and binding implementation milestones.

But I do **not** accept:
- a permanent closure taboo,
- an automatic promise that closures can never happen,
- or a review process that becomes a disguised veto against restructuring.

A moratorium is a negotiating instrument, not a surrender of management responsibility.

### 2) Shared-sacrifice package
This is the right direction, but only if the sacrifice is real and balanced.

For management and ownership, that means:
- overhead reduction,
- executive compensation restraint,
- payout or dividend restraint where needed,
- capital support for future investment.

For employees, that can include:
- temporary wage restraint,
- bonus moderation,
- working-time flexibility,
- voluntary exits,
- retraining,
- redeployment.

But I will not accept an arrangement where employee concessions are the main lever while the structural footprint stays intact. Shared sacrifice must create shared competitiveness.

### 3) Strategic-site protection with selective shrinkage
This is the most realistic concept on the table, but I am not romanticizing it.

Not every site can remain unchanged. Some sites will be strategic. Others may need to shrink, specialize, or change materially. That is not ideology; it is industrial logic.

If the labor side wants every site protected equally, then it still has not answered the central economic question: **where does the permanent capacity reduction come from?**

---

## What I need to see next

If the labor side wants to avoid closures, then bring a package that demonstrates all of the following:

1. **Permanent capacity reduction**
   - not just temporary underutilization or short-time arrangements

2. **Material fixed-cost savings**
   - enough to move the brand toward return-on-sales target compliance

3. **Balanced contributions**
   - employees, management, owners, and the company all contributing

4. **Binding investment commitments**
   - for future products and technology in Germany through 2030

5. **A credible timeline**
   - with measurable milestones and consequences for non-delivery

That is the minimum bar.

---

## What is not acceptable

I will not sign off on:
- a deal that preserves every site by default
- a package that rules out all closures regardless of economics
- a settlement that produces only temporary concessions
- a deal that leaves the cost base structurally uncompetitive
- a truce that gives us six months of peace and then the same crisis again

That is not restructuring. That is postponement.

---

## My bottom line

I remain prepared to negotiate a serious settlement.

But I am **not** prepared to retreat from the core requirement:
**the company must achieve a durable, structural reduction in cost and capacity.**

That means:
- a limited moratorium can be discussed,
- a shared-sacrifice package can be built,
- site-by-site review is necessary,
- and closures remain a legitimate instrument if the numbers require them.

If the labor side wants to avoid that outcome, it needs to stop offering general resistance and start bringing a **numerically credible alternative** that produces the same economic effect.

Until then, my position stands.
