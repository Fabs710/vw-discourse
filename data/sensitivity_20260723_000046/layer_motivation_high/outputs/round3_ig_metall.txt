I will be equally clear in round 3.

## Where I agree
I agree with the works council, the state government, and — in part — with management and the owners on one fundamental point: **the company needs a durable restructuring, not cosmetic delay.**  
And I agree with the state and even the investors that a one-sided employee haircut would not hold.

But that is exactly why I will not accept management’s attempt to turn “durable” into a code word for **closures, forced redundancies, and permanent pressure on wages**.

## Where I disagree
### To management
You still have not answered the central question: **why are closures necessary before the alternatives are fully used?**

You keep repeating that the savings must be “permanent” and “structural.” Fine. But that does **not** automatically justify plant closures or compulsory redundancies. You still owe us a plant-by-plant, measure-by-measure comparison showing:

- what can be saved through working-time flexibility,
- what can be saved through redeployment and retraining,
- what can be saved through natural attrition and voluntary departures,
- what can be saved through management overhead cuts,
- what can be saved through owner-side payout restraint,
- and only then, what still remains genuinely unsolved.

Until that happens, closures are not a necessity. They are a threat.

I also reject your framing that temporary concessions are worthless. If they are embedded in a binding package with investment, productivity, and governance commitments, they can be part of a durable solution. What they cannot do is become a pretext for making employees absorb all the pain while management keeps structural options open against them.

### To the works council
I support your red lines: **no compulsory redundancies, no plant closures, no weakening of co-determination, no dismantling of the collective wage framework.**  
That remains my position as well.

But I need to be just as clear: it is not enough to say “no” to closures. We need a **numerically credible alternative** that actually adds up. The union can support the defense of sites, but if the package does not generate real savings, management will simply return with the same demands in another form.

So yes — temporary wage restraint, waiver of bonuses, working-time flexibility, voluntary programs, retraining, and internal redeployment are all on the table. But only if they are matched by:
- management-side cost restraint,
- owner-side payout discipline,
- and binding investment commitments for German sites and future products.

### To the state government
You are right to insist that a moratorium is only useful if it creates a future, not a trap. But I want to sharpen that point: a moratorium must also be **conditional on real negotiation progress**, not just on nice language.

If the state uses its blocking position, it should do so to force a serious shared-sacrifice package — not merely to delay the conflict. That means:
- no stealth closure planning,
- no hidden forced-redundancy track,
- no unilateral foot-dragging from management,
- and no attempt to wait out the workforce.

### To the controlling family holding
You say you do not want conflict, but you keep insisting that closures remain available if needed. That position is exactly what undermines trust. If ownership wants industrial peace, then it must stop treating closure threats as a normal bargaining tool.

I also need more than general talk about stewardship. Stewardship means:
- capital discipline,
- payout restraint where necessary,
- support for investment in Germany,
- and a real commitment to preserving jobs and skills where alternatives exist.

### To investors
You are right that the package must be quantifiable and enforceable. I agree with that. But you are wrong if you think enforceability means the workforce must accept a permanent threat over its head.

If you want a deal that lasts, then it has to be enforceable **for both sides**:
- enforceable employee contributions,
- enforceable management restraint,
- enforceable owner contributions,
- enforceable investment commitments,
- and enforceable job and site security.

That is what durability looks like. Not just downside risk for labour.

## On the moderator’s compromise proposals
### 1) No closure now, trigger-based review
I will support a **no-closure and no-forced-redundancy moratorium** for a defined period, **if** it is tied to a binding restructuring package.

But I reject any trigger mechanism that reopens closures as an automatic fallback. That is not a compromise; it is a deferred threat.  
If there is a review, it must be about whether management and ownership have delivered on their obligations, not a pre-authorized path to plant shutdowns.

### 2) Shared-sacrifice package
This is the direction we can work with — but only if the sacrifice is genuinely shared.

What that means in practice:
- employee temporary wage moderation,
- waiver or reduction of bonuses/special payments,
- working-time flexibility,
- voluntary exit windows,
- retraining and redeployment,
- management overhead and compensation restraint,
- dividend and payout discipline,
- and binding investment commitments.

What it does **not** mean:
- employee concessions now,
- layoffs later,
- and then business as usual for ownership and top management.

### 3) Site-specific restructuring with protected strategic sites
I am open to site-specific industrial planning, but not to a hierarchy that writes off some plants under a nicer name. Any specialization must come with:
- investment,
- product allocation,
- training,
- and security for the workforce.

No site should be left with vague promises and long-term insecurity while others are protected.

## My concrete position
I want to put this on record as clearly as possible:

### Non-negotiable
- **No plant closures in Germany**
- **No compulsory redundancies**
- **No dismantling of the collective wage framework**
- **No weakening of co-determination or job-security guarantees**

### Negotiable, if matched by management/owner contributions
- temporary wage restraint
- waiver of bonuses/special payments
- working-time flexibility
- voluntary departures
- retraining and redeployment
- capacity reduction through attrition and reallocation
- employee solidarity contribution, but only as one element of a balanced package

### Required from the other side
- binding investment commitments for German sites and future products
- management-side cost restraint
- owner-side payout discipline
- quantified savings targets
- enforceable guarantees on jobs and sites for the agreement period

## What I am prepared to do
If management and ownership bring a real shared-sacrifice package, I am prepared to:
- recommend a negotiated wage contribution to members,
- suspend escalation,
- support working-time and redeployment solutions,
- and help stabilize the company through a durable collective agreement.

But I will not recommend a package that asks workers to pay first while closures remain available in the background.

## Bottom line
There is still a viable route to settlement, but it runs through **shared sacrifice without forced layoffs or plant closures**.

If management wants a deal, then stop hiding closure pressure behind “economic reality” and put a quantified alternative on the table. If it refuses, then we will continue to use our leverage — including strikes — to defend jobs, sites, and the collective agreement.
