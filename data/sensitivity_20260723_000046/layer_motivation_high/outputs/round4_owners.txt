I’ll be direct.

## Where I agree

I agree with the broad consensus on four points:

1. **A restructuring is necessary.**  
   The current cost and capacity structure is not sustainable.

2. **A one-sided employee sacrifice would be unstable and unfair.**  
   If there is adjustment, it must be shared.

3. **A prolonged industrial conflict would destroy value.**  
   That would hurt the company, the workforce, and the owners.

4. **Future investment in Germany must be part of any settlement.**  
   Cutting costs without funding the next product cycle would be self-defeating.

So the direction is right. The problem is whether the package now being defended is actually strong enough.

---

## Where I disagree

### To the works council and union
You keep repeating “no closures, no compulsory redundancies, no weakening of co-determination” as if that were a complete restructuring strategy. It is not. It is a boundary.

I understand why you draw that line. But if you want to rule out closures, then you must also show a **quantified, credible alternative** that closes the structural gap.

So far, I have not seen that.

The expert input was useful here: **a non-closure path is plausible only if it includes real, permanent capacity reduction and fixed-cost removal.** That means:
- not just temporary wage restraint,
- not just bonus moderation,
- not just flexibility language,
- not just voluntary exits.

Those can help, but they may not be enough. If the package does not materially reset the network, then it is postponement, not restructuring.

So my position is:  
**No closure-first dogma, but also no closure taboo.**

---

### To management
I support your insistence on durable savings, but I do not support blunt-force escalation or treating closures as the default answer before the alternatives are fully tested.

You still need to do the hard work:
- plant by plant,
- measure by measure,
- net of transition costs.

I want a clear comparison of:
- what can be achieved through redeployment,
- what can be achieved through shift reduction,
- what can be achieved through attrition and specialization,
- what management itself is prepared to cut,
- and what gap remains after all that.

If closures are truly needed for one or more sites, prove it.  
If they are not, then do not use them as a bargaining weapon.

---

### To the regional state government
I respect your role and I agree that the company should not be managed as if regional legitimacy did not matter.

But I do **not** accept that the state’s blocking position should become a permanent shield against any meaningful footprint adjustment. That would protect employment in the short term while eroding competitiveness and future investment.

A moratorium can be discussed. A permanent no-adjustment regime cannot.

---

### To institutional investors
You are right to insist on quantified, enforceable savings. That is exactly the right standard.

But you also need to be honest: a deal that preserves every site in substantially unchanged form, while relying mainly on temporary concessions, will not restore margins. It will simply rename the problem.

I will support a non-closure solution if, and only if, it delivers the same economic effect.

---

## My position on the compromise proposals

### 1) Time-bound moratorium plus quantified review
A limited moratorium on plant closures and compulsory redundancies can be discussed **during a defined negotiation period**.

But I will not support:
- a permanent closure ban,
- an automatic “trigger” that quietly becomes a backdoor to either side later,
- or a review process that functions as a deferred veto.

If we do a review, it must test delivery against agreed metrics:
- utilization,
- fixed-cost absorption,
- capacity removal,
- and savings realization.

It should be a verification mechanism, not a political escape hatch.

---

### 2) Shared-sacrifice package
This is the right direction, but only if sacrifice is genuinely shared.

I am prepared to back:
- temporary wage restraint,
- bonus moderation,
- working-time flexibility,
- voluntary exits,
- retraining,
- redeployment,
- management overhead restraint,
- executive compensation restraint,
- and owner-side payout discipline where necessary.

I am **not** prepared to back a package where:
- employees make concessions,
- management keeps all strategic options,
- and the company still lacks a durable cost reset.

That would be unfair and economically weak.

---

### 3) Strategic-site protection with selective shrinkage
This is the most realistic concept on the table.

Not every site can remain unchanged. Some sites are strategically important and should be protected. Others may need to shrink, specialize, or change materially. That is not ideology; it is industrial reality.

But the criteria must be explicit. If “strategic” just means politically easier to protect, then the whole process loses credibility.

---

## What I would support

I would support a package if it contains all of the following:

1. **Real permanent capacity reduction**
2. **Material fixed-cost savings**
3. **Balanced contributions from employees, management, owners, and the company**
4. **Binding investment commitments for German future products**
5. **A clear timetable with measurable milestones**
6. **A credible fallback if the agreed package fails to deliver**

That last point matters. A restructuring without discipline is not a restructuring.

---

## What I will not support

I will not support:
- a cosmetic deal,
- a permanent closure taboo,
- a package that only delays the problem,
- or a settlement that leaves the company structurally uncompetitive.

I also will not support a process in which the workforce or the state effectively expects ownership to waive all restructuring tools while still expecting the owners to carry the long-term value risk.

That is not stewardship on their side, and it is not acceptable on mine either.

---

## Bottom line

I remain open to a negotiated solution. In fact, that is still the preferred route.

But I am **not** prepared to pretend that “no closures” by itself solves the problem. It does not.

If the labor side wants to avoid closures, it needs to bring a **numerically credible alternative** that achieves the same economic effect. If management and ownership want the backing of the controlling shareholders, they need to show the numbers and avoid using closures as a lazy first answer.

So the next step is not more principle statements. It is a **site-by-site, quantified package**.
