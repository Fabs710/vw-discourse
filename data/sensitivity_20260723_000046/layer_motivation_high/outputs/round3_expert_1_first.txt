## Bottom line

The central technical question is not whether the company needs restructuring — it clearly does — but **whether the same economic effect can be achieved without plant closures and forced redundancies**.

My assessment is:

- **Yes, meaningful savings can be achieved without immediate closures**, through capacity reduction, shift-line rationalization, attrition, redeployment, working-time flexibility, and temporary employee contributions.
- **No, those measures are unlikely to fully substitute for closure-level savings** if management’s target is a large, durable, structural cost reset.
- Therefore, the real decision is **not “closures or nothing,”** but **how much of the gap can be closed by negotiated internal measures before site exit becomes economically necessary**.

## What matters analytically

To compare the options properly, you need to separate four different things:

1. **Gross savings**
   - wage moderation, bonus cuts, overhead reductions, shorter working time, fewer shifts.

2. **Structural savings**
   - permanent capacity removal, fixed-cost elimination, asset write-down avoidance of future losses.

3. **Transition costs**
   - severance, retraining, transfer, tooling changes, logistics disruption, supplier requalification.

4. **Strategic value effects**
   - whether a site carries future product allocation, battery/EV relevance, scale efficiency, or regional political weight.

A proposal can look large in headline job terms but deliver only modest net savings after transition costs and replacement investments.

## Scenario comparison

### 1) Closure-led restructuring
This is the strongest tool for permanent fixed-cost reduction.

**Advantages**
- Largest and most durable reduction in fixed manufacturing overhead.
- Removes chronic underutilization.
- Can improve plant network utilization and simplify logistics.
- Sends a strong signal to capital markets and rating agencies.

**Disadvantages**
- High one-time cost: severance, transfer packages, decommissioning, site remediation, tooling relocation.
- Industrial conflict risk is very high in Germany given co-determination and political stakes.
- Loss of skills, supplier disruption, and reputational damage.
- If too aggressive, it can reduce future flexibility exactly when EV and model-cycle uncertainty remains high.

**Assessment**
- Economically effective, but only if management can truly absorb the industrial and political cost.
- In a German governance setting with a blocking workforce and state stake, closure is often a **last-resort instrument**, not a first-best negotiating outcome.

---

### 2) No-closure restructuring with capacity reduction and negotiated employee contribution
This is the most plausible negotiated path.

Typical measures:
- voluntary exits and retirement windows,
- reduced hours or shift losses,
- internal redeployment across plants,
- lower temporary pay components,
- bonus restraint,
- longer product-life utilization at selected sites,
- specialization of plants rather than full closure.

**Advantages**
- Lower conflict intensity than closure.
- Preserves workforce legitimacy and co-determination stability.
- Can achieve meaningful fixed-cost savings if capacity is genuinely removed through shift eliminations and line consolidation.
- Better preserves strategic flexibility.

**Disadvantages**
- Usually slower to deliver.
- Savings are often partly temporary unless capacity is truly taken out.
- If management keeps excess sites open “on paper,” fixed-cost relief is limited.
- Risk of deferred conflict if the package only buys time.

**Assessment**
- This can be credible if it includes **real capacity reduction**, not just wage moderation.
- It is the best option if the objective is to avoid a rupture, but it must be quantified carefully.

---

### 3) Hybrid: protected strategic sites plus selective shrinkage/conversion elsewhere
This is often the most realistic industrial compromise.

Likely pattern:
- Core strategic plants receive product commitments and investment.
- Weaker or less utilized sites are reduced, specialized, or converted.
- Employment declines occur mostly through attrition, voluntary exits, and reallocations.
- Limited closure may still remain available for the least viable site(s) if the numbers do not work.

**Advantages**
- Aligns capacity with product strategy.
- Protects the most competitive assets.
- Politically more saleable than blanket closures.
- Usually the best balance between competitiveness and industrial peace.

**Disadvantages**
- Creates winners and losers across sites.
- If criteria are vague, it becomes a political dispute rather than a restructuring.
- Requires disciplined capital allocation and clear product-roadmap commitments.

**Assessment**
- From a restructuring standpoint, this is often the most credible compromise.
- It is more sustainable than a purely defensive no-change approach and less destructive than large-scale closure as the first step.

## What is likely true in this case

Given the scenario:
- shrinking European demand,
- high German cost base,
- EV transition investment needs,
- and a brand below target profitability,

the company likely has **a real fixed-cost and capacity problem**, not merely a temporary wage issue.

That means:

- but **plant closures are not automatically required everywhere**.

The likely economically efficient approach is:
1. remove excess shifts and underutilized capacity,
2. consolidate production onto fewer lines,
3. secure a temporary labour contribution,
4. reduce management and overhead costs,
5. protect only those sites with clear strategic or scale advantages,
6. retain closure as a fallback only if the numbers still do not close.

## What a credible non-closure package would need

If management wants to avoid closures, the package must be judged by whether it achieves at least five things:

1. **Permanent capacity reduction**
   - not just overtime cuts or temporary short-time work.

2. **Material fixed-cost savings**
   - enough to move the return-on-sales trajectory, not merely smooth the optics.

3. **Balanced burden-sharing**
   - management, owners, and workforce all contributing.

4. **Investment commitments**
   - especially for future platforms, EV-related production, and tooling.

5. **Enforceability**
   - milestones and review mechanisms that can’t be ignored.

If any of these are missing, the package risks being a delay mechanism rather than a restructuring.

## Employment impact

A few distinctions are important:

- **Job cuts do not have to equal closures.**
  You can reduce headcount materially through attrition, natural retirement, and selective redeployment.
- **Capacity cuts do not have to mean mass layoffs immediately.**
  But they must reduce labour demand over time.
- **Closures create the largest one-time employment shock**, but sometimes lower long-term employment loss if they prevent a wider company failure.
- In a German setting, the social cost of closures is much higher because redeployment across sites is often only partial and regionally constrained.

So the employment question is not simply “how many jobs disappear,” but **how fast, where, and whether the company remains investable afterward**.
