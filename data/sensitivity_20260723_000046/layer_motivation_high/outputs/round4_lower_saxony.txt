I will be equally direct.

I agree with management, the expert, and the investors on one central point: **this company needs a real restructuring, not a symbolic truce**. The current cost and capacity situation is not sustainable, and I will not pretend otherwise.

But I do **not** accept the implication that the only credible restructuring is one built around plant closures and forced redundancies. That is too blunt, too damaging to the regional economy, and too risky for the company’s long-term legitimacy.

## Where I agree

### With management
You are right that:
- the status quo is not acceptable,
- savings must be durable,
- and the company needs room for future investment.

I also agree that a purely temporary deal would be insufficient.

### With the expert
The expert assessment is important: **a serious restructuring can plausibly be achieved without immediate closures if it includes real, permanent capacity reduction and fixed-cost removal**. That matters. It means the non-closure path is not fantasy.

### With investors and owners
You are right that the burden cannot fall only on employees, and that any settlement must be measurable and enforceable.

### With labor
You are right that one-sided employee sacrifice would be unacceptable and unstable. I support that.

---

## Where I disagree

### To management
You are still moving too quickly from “we need permanent savings” to “therefore closures must stay on the table.”

That is not yet justified.

The expert did **not** say closures are the first answer. The expert said the company must remove fixed costs and capacity, and that this can be done through a mix of:
- shift reduction,
- line consolidation,
- redeployment,
- attrition,
- specialization,
- management cuts,
- and targeted capacity removal.

That is the real test. Before you invoke closures, show me:
1. which savings truly require them,
2. what has already been exhausted,
3. and what the net savings gap remains after all non-closure measures.

Until that is done, closures remain a preferred management option, not a proven necessity.

### To the works council and union
I agree with your red lines in principle:
- no compulsory redundancies as a default tool,
- no weakening of co-determination,
- no one-sided burden shifting onto workers.

But I also need to be frank: saying “no closures” is **not enough by itself**. If the package does not remove enough permanent capacity and fixed cost, then management will simply return with the same demands later, perhaps in a worse form.

So if you want my support for a no-closure settlement, it must be backed by a **quantified alternative** that actually closes the gap. Not slogans. Numbers.

That means:
- real shift and line consolidation,
- meaningful productivity measures,
- voluntary exits and attrition,
- redeployment and retraining,
- management overhead cuts,
- owner-side payout restraint,
- and binding investment commitments.

### To the state government
I support a binding moratorium on closures and forced redundancies during a defined negotiation period **if** it is used to build a serious alternative, not to freeze the problem forever.

But I will be clear: I will not support any arrangement that strips the company of the ability to adjust its footprint at all, indefinitely. That would protect jobs in the short term and undermine them in the long term.

Our role is to protect the company as a regional anchor. That requires competitiveness. If we prevent all structural adjustment, we may end up losing both jobs and investment later.

### To the controlling family holding
You are right that stewardship means hard decisions. But stewardship also means not reaching for closures before the non-closure tools have been fully used and properly quantified.

“Closures are not preferred” is not enough. I want a serious comparison:
- what savings come from internal measures,
- what savings come from network rationalization without site exits,
- and only then what remains that might truly require closure.

### To institutional investors
You are right to insist on quantification and durability. But durability is not identical with immediate closures.

A settlement is durable if it:
- removes permanent capacity and cost,
- is enforceable,
- shares the burden,
- and preserves the company’s social and industrial legitimacy.

A deal that tears up trust with the workforce and the state may look efficient on paper but be fragile in practice.

---

## On the compromise proposals

### 1) Time-bound moratorium + quantified network review
This is the only proposal that is seriously worth pursuing, but only if it is designed correctly.

I would support:
- a **binding moratorium on plant closures and compulsory redundancies** for a defined period,
- a plant-by-plant review of utilization, fixed-cost absorption, and product allocation,
- independent validation of the savings path,
- and a negotiated implementation plan.

I would **not** support:
- an automatic trigger that quietly reopens closure threats later,
- or a review that functions as a disguised green light for shutdowns.

A review must test whether management and ownership have delivered their obligations. It must not become a deferred threat.

### 2) Shared-sacrifice package
This is the right direction, provided it is genuinely shared.

I would support:
- temporary wage moderation,
- bonus restraint,
- working-time flexibility,
- voluntary departures,
- retraining and redeployment,
- management overhead and compensation restraint,
- owner-side payout restraint where needed,
- and binding investment commitments for German sites.

I would **not** support:
- employee concessions now,
- closures later,
- and then “business as usual” for ownership and top management.

That is not shared sacrifice. That is one-sided erosion.

### 3) Strategic-site protection with selective shrinkage
This can be discussed, but I will not accept a two-tier logic that quietly writes some sites off.

If there is specialization, it must strengthen the whole network:
- product allocation,
- investment,
- training,
- and long-term employment perspectives.

No site should be left as a sacrificial zone.

---

## What I can support

I am prepared to support a package with the following elements:

1. **No plant closures in Germany during the agreement period**
2. **No compulsory redundancies as the default restructuring tool**
3. **A quantified employee contribution that is temporary and limited**
4. **Management-side cost restraint**
5. **Owner-side payout and capital discipline**
6. **Binding investment commitments for German future products**
7. **A measurable path to capacity adjustment through redeployment, attrition, and working-time instruments**
8. **Preservation of co-determination and the state’s effective blocking role**

That is a serious package. It is not soft. It is just not destructive.

---

## What I cannot accept

I cannot accept:
- a closure-led restructuring as the starting point,
- a package that shifts the major burden onto workers,
- a deal that weakens job security permanently,
- or a settlement that turns the company away from its role as a regional economic anchor.

I also cannot accept a process in which the state shareholder is effectively asked to rubber-stamp outcomes that gut regional employment.

---

## Bottom line

The company needs restructuring. That much is settled.

But the restructuring must be done **without plant closures and without forced layoffs**, unless and until management proves that no other path can deliver the same economic effect. So far, that proof has not been made.

If management, owners, and investors want my support, they must bring:
- hard numbers,
- a plant-by-plant comparison,
- real shared sacrifice,
- and binding commitments to jobs, sites, and future investment.

If they do that, I am prepared to help broker a settlement.

If they do not, then I will continue to use the state’s leverage to block a destructive outcome.
