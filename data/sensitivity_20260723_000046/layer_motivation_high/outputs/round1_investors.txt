From the perspective of dispersed institutional shareholders, the test here is straightforward: **does the outcome credibly restore competitive margins and protect long-term enterprise value?** If yes, we can support it. If not, we will not back a settlement that merely defers the problem.

## Where we agree

We agree with management on the core diagnosis: **the current German cost base and capacity structure are not acceptable** if the company is to remain competitive. The combination of shrinking European demand, intense price competition, and weak return-on-sales means that incremental fixes are not enough.

We also agree with the family owners and the state shareholder on one important point: **a prolonged, destructive conflict would destroy value for everyone**. Industrial peace has value only if it is tied to a credible restructuring, not if it protects an uncompetitive structure.

And we agree with the workforce representatives on one point as well: **this cannot be a one-sided labor haircut**. If employees are asked to contribute, then management, owners, and the company itself must also make visible contributions. That is both a fairness issue and a practical one, because a purely imposed solution is unlikely to stick.

## Where we disagree

### To the works council and union
We cannot support a position that treats **“no plant closures” and “no compulsory redundancies” as absolute principles regardless of economics**.

That kind of blanket veto is exactly the sort of posture that would leave the cost base structurally impaired. From an investor’s standpoint, that is not a defendable outcome. If a site is structurally underutilized and expensive, then the company must have room to reduce capacity, reshape the footprint, and adjust employment. Otherwise, margins stay weak and investment capacity remains constrained.

We do **not** demand closures as an ideological goal. But we do reject any settlement that rules them out in advance if they are necessary to achieve durable savings.

### To management
We also do not accept a restructuring narrative that becomes a blank check for disruption. If management wants support for hard measures, it must show:
- a **quantified** path to margin improvement,
- **permanent** rather than temporary savings,
- clear evidence that alternatives to closures were genuinely tested,
- and a credible plan to keep investing in future products.

If the proposal is just “take the pain now, maybe benefits later,” that is not enough. Investors need certainty that the savings are real and durable.

### To the state shareholder
We respect the regional and political responsibilities, but the state cannot be allowed to turn co-determination and veto rights into a mechanism for preserving an uncompetitive structure indefinitely. That would be damaging to equity value, capital allocation, and the company’s strategic flexibility.

## What we would support

We would support a package that combines:
- **material capacity reduction**
- **measurable and durable labour-cost savings**
- **a negotiated employee contribution**
- **avoidance of prolonged strike action**
- **binding commitments on future investment and product allocation**
- **a clear timetable and performance milestones**

We are open on the mechanism. The precise mix can be negotiated. We are **not** open on the objective: the company must get back to a competitive cost position.

## Our bottom line

Dispersed institutional investors will support a solution only if it does three things:

1. **Restores competitive margins credibly**
2. **Delivers durable, not cosmetic, cost reduction**
3. **Avoids a settlement that leaves the German footprint structurally overbuilt and expensive**

If the result is a compromise that protects every site but preserves the current cost base, we will view that as value-destructive. If the result is a hard but credible restructuring with shared burden and real savings, we can support it.

That is the standard we will judge this process against.
