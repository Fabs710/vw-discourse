I hear the positions clearly, and I want to respond just as clearly from the perspective of the controlling family owners.

First: **I agree that the company cannot solve this crisis by permanently damaging its German industrial base or by provoking a prolonged conflict**. That would destroy value, damage the brand, and weaken the long-term asset we are responsible for stewarding. Stability matters.

Second: **I also agree with management on the core diagnosis**: the status quo is not sustainable. If the company keeps an uncompetitive cost base and excess capacity, then we will lose margin, lose investment headroom, and ultimately lose competitiveness in the transition. That is not acceptable either.

Where I draw the line is this: **I will not support a restructuring that creates lasting value destruction through uncontrolled conflict, or one that undermines governance control and the company’s long-term strategic freedom.** So I am not signing up to a cosmetic compromise that postpones the problem, but I am equally not endorsing a forced rupture that weakens the enterprise for years.

To the works council and union:
- I respect the insistence on social responsibility.
- I do not dismiss the workforce’s contribution to the company’s success.
- But I do **not** accept a blanket veto on structural change.

“No plant closures ever” is not a sustainable governing principle if a site is structurally unviable. I am not saying closures are the preferred route. I am saying they cannot be ruled out as a matter of principle before the economics are tested. The same applies to compulsory redundancies: I prefer to avoid them, but I will not treat them as illegitimate if the alternative is a permanent competitive handicap.

To management:
- I support a credible competitiveness agenda.
- But I will not back a plan that looks like escalation for its own sake.
- If you want support from the ownership side, you must show that you have seriously exhausted alternatives and that any hard measures are tied to a durable industrial plan.
- You need to present this as a value-restoring strategy, not just a cost-cutting exercise.

To the regional state government:
- I understand the political and regional responsibility.
- I also note your own point that the status quo is not sustainable.
- But we should be careful not to turn the blocking rights of governance into a guarantee that structural problems can never be addressed.
- A solution that preserves every site at any cost is not stewardship; it is delay.

My position, therefore, is this:

1. **The objective must be durable competitiveness and margin repair.**
2. **All structural options remain on the table**, including plant closures, but they should only be used if less destructive alternatives fail to achieve the needed savings.
3. **A negotiated employee contribution is reasonable and necessary**, but it must be fair, time-limited where possible, and matched by contributions from management and the owners.
4. **Any agreement must protect long-term investment in Germany and the future product pipeline.**
5. **We must avoid a prolonged industrial confrontation** that would erode value faster than any restructuring benefit.

So I am open to a serious social-partnership solution, but only if it delivers real and durable improvements in cost, capacity, and competitiveness. If it does not, then we will have to face harder decisions.

That is not a threat. It is the requirement of responsible ownership.
