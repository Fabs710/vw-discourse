From a plant-economics and network-optimization standpoint, the key issue is whether the company can achieve the required **durable fixed-cost absorption improvement** without closing sites.

## My assessment

### 1) A no-closure solution is possible in principle
Yes — **if** it includes real structural changes, not just temporary concessions.

The measures most likely to deliver durable savings without closures are:
- **shift reduction / line consolidation**
- **specialization of sites**
- **permanent reallocation of product volume**
- **redeployment across the network**
- **natural attrition and voluntary exits**
- **permanent indirect-cost reduction**
- **overhead and management simplification**
- **procurement, logistics, and energy-cost improvements**

These can lower the cost base materially. But they only work if they change the **steady-state footprint**.

### 2) Temporary measures are not enough
Temporary wage restraint, bonus moderation, or working-time flexibility can help cash flow and buy time, but they do **not** solve a structural overcapacity problem on their own.

If the plants remain underutilized, then:
- fixed costs remain spread over too few units,
- unit costs stay high,
- and the competitiveness problem returns once temporary concessions expire.

### 3) The central test is permanent capacity reduction
A closure-free package can work only if it achieves one or more of the following:
- **permanent line or shift elimination**
- **lower total network capacity**
- **higher utilization at the remaining sites**
- **lower indirect and overhead costs**
- **a more efficient product/site allocation**

In other words, the company does not necessarily need to close buildings to reduce capacity, but it does need to **remove real capacity from the system**.

## What would make a no-closure path credible
A credible package would need:
- site-by-site utilization data
- a quantified savings target
- headcount reduction plan based mainly on attrition/voluntary exits if forced layoffs are excluded
- product allocation by plant
- investment commitments for retained sites
- an implementation timeline with measurable milestones

Without those, “no closures” is not an industrial plan; it is just a political position.

## Where closure becomes hard to avoid
Closure or major footprint reduction becomes more likely when:
- network utilization is persistently too low,
- product allocation cannot be rationalized enough,
- there are too many overlapping plants with similar functions,
- and the required savings are large relative to what internal measures can deliver.

In that case, non-closure measures may still help, but they may not be sufficient to close the gap.

## Bottom line
My technical judgment is:

**A non-closure restructuring is economically feasible only if it still delivers meaningful permanent capacity reduction and fixed-cost removal.**  
If it relies mainly on temporary concessions while leaving the network largely unchanged, it will not be durable.
