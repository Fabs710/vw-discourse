### Independent restructuring assessment

From an automotive plant-economics perspective, the core issue is straightforward:

**A no-closure solution is possible in principle, but only if it removes enough permanent capacity and fixed cost to close the structural gap.**  
Temporary concessions alone do not solve an overcapacity problem.

## What can realistically deliver savings without closures

The main non-closure instruments are:

- **Shift reduction / line consolidation**
- **Redeployment across plants or functions**
- **Natural attrition and voluntary exits**
- **Working-time flexibility**
- **Product or model specialization**
- **Overhead and management-cost reduction**
- **Procurement, logistics, and energy-cost improvements**
- **Temporary wage moderation / bonus restraint**

These can be significant, but they have different profiles:

### 1) Temporary measures
Examples:
- wage restraint
- bonus waivers
- working-time flexibility

These help near-term cash flow, but **do not on their own fix a structurally uncompetitive footprint**.

### 2) Structural measures
Examples:
- shift elimination
- permanent line consolidation
- specialization of sites
- permanent reduction in indirect headcount
- product allocation changes

These can generate the kind of durable savings management is asking for.

## What is usually required to match a closure-led savings case

If management is serious that one or more German plants are structurally uncompetitive, then a non-closure package must usually include:

- **real capacity reduction**, not just lower utilization
- **permanent headcount reduction**, mainly through attrition and voluntary exits if forced redundancies are off the table
- **a substantial fixed-cost reset**
- **simplification of site/product assignments**
- **investment reallocation to the retained sites**

In practical terms, a non-closure agreement can work only if it achieves **the same economic effect as footprint reduction would have produced**, even if the buildings stay open.

## Where the economics tend to break

The main risk in a “no closures, no layoffs” approach is that it can leave three problems unresolved:

1. **Excess capacity remains**
   - Plants stay open but underfilled.
   - Fixed costs are spread over too few units.

2. **Savings are diluted by transition costs**
   - Redeployment, retraining, parallel operations, and severance for voluntary exits all cost money.

3. **The residual network still needs investment**
   - If the retained plants are not assigned enough volume and product mix, the company loses competitiveness again.

## What would count as a credible non-closure alternative

A credible package would need all of the following:

- **site-by-site volumes and utilization forecasts**
- **permanent line or shift removals**
- **quantified indirect-cost reduction**
- **a clear headcount path**
- **product allocation by plant**
- **investment commitments tied to the retained footprint**
- **a verified savings target with timing**

Without that, “no closure” becomes a political slogan rather than an industrial solution.

## My judgment on the likely trade-off

- **If the company’s required savings are modest to moderate:**  
  a negotiated no-closure restructuring is often feasible.

- **If the required savings are large and the current network is heavily underutilized:**  
  then avoiding closures becomes much harder, and the package must rely on deeper specialization, permanent capacity cuts, and stronger workforce reductions through attrition and voluntary exits.

- **If management is asking for closure-level savings but the labor side refuses any footprint change at all:**  
  the gap is likely too large for a purely internal solution.

## Bottom line

The expert answer is:

**Yes, a closure-free restructuring can work — but only if it still achieves meaningful permanent capacity reduction and fixed-cost removal.**  
If the measures are only temporary, the restructuring will not be durable.
