Here is my expert assessment, strictly on the plant-economics and network-optimization question.

## Short answer

**A serious restructuring can plausibly be delivered without immediate plant closures, but only if it includes real, permanent capacity reduction and fixed-cost removal.**  
If the package consists mainly of temporary wage restraint, bonus waivers, and flexible working time, it will likely **delay** the problem rather than solve it.

So the technical question is not:

- “closures or no closures?”

It is:

- **How much fixed-cost and capacity relief can be achieved through internal network changes before closure becomes economically necessary at one or more sites?**

## What the economics usually require

In an automotive manufacturing network, the main sources of cost pressure are:

1. **Underutilized fixed assets**
   - plant overhead, maintenance, utilities, depreciation, plant-level salaried labor, and support functions

2. **Low line or shift utilization**
   - if a plant runs below efficient volumes, unit fixed cost rises sharply

3. **Excess complexity**
   - too many models, variants, and logistics flows across too many sites

4. **High labor cost per unit**
   - especially where volumes are weak and labor absorption is poor

5. **Network inefficiency**
   - duplicated processes, long transport legs, duplicated changeover/tooling costs

A restructuring works when it reduces these permanently.

## What can be achieved without closures

A non-closure package can still produce meaningful savings if it includes:

- **shift elimination or line consolidation**
- **capacity rationalization** within plants
- **volume transfer to fewer sites**
- **attrition and voluntary exits**
- **redeployment across the network**
- **working-time flexibility**
- **site specialization**
- **overhead reduction**
- **supplier and logistics simplification**

These measures can reduce labor demand and improve absorption. In a large carmaker, they can be substantial.

### But there is an important limit
If the company keeps the same number of plants substantially intact, then:

- plant-level fixed costs remain,
- support functions remain,
- utilities and maintenance remain,
- management layers remain,
- and underabsorption persists.

That means temporary concessions from workers help cash flow, but they do **not** fully cure structural overcapacity.

## When closures become economically justified

Closure becomes rational when a site has all or most of the following:

- chronically low utilization
- weak or declining product allocation
- poor logistics position
- high conversion cost to future platforms
- limited strategic role in the EV transition
- high fixed cost per unit even after optimization
- no credible path to scale recovery

In that case, the site is not just expensive; it is **structurally misfit** to the future network.

## How I would test the sites

A disciplined site review would compare each plant on these dimensions:

1. **Utilization**
   - current and forecast output versus efficient capacity

2. **Fixed-cost absorption**
   - fixed cost per vehicle at current volume and at planned volume

3. **Contribution margin**
   - whether the site contributes enough after direct and allocated costs

4. **Product fit**
   - future platform allocation, EV/ICE mix, model lifecycle timing

5. **Logistics cost**
   - inbound/outbound transport, supplier proximity, export access

6. **Capex requirement**
   - cost to keep the site competitive over the next product cycle

7. **Alternative use**
   - whether the site can be specialized, downsized, or converted instead of closed

## Likely outcome in the scenario as described

Based on the facts you gave:
- shrinking European market
- cost base above target and above competitors
- return-on-sales target missed
- major EV investment needs
- strong governance constraints

my view is:

- **The company almost certainly needs network rationalization.**
- **Some capacity should be removed permanently.**
- **Not every plant must be closed to achieve that.**
- But **one or more least-competitive sites may still end up being uneconomic to retain** if the numbers do not close after internal measures.

So the credible answer is a **hybrid**:
- preserve strategic sites,
- reduce capacity materially,
- use attrition/redeployment first,
- and keep closure as a fallback for the weakest residual case.

## What would make a no-closure solution credible

To be credible, a no-closure package would need to show:

- permanent capacity reduction, not just temporary cost relief
- materially lower fixed-cost absorption
- a clear product roadmap for protected sites
- quantified management and owner contributions
- investment commitments tied to the new footprint
- enforceable milestones

If those elements are present, the non-closure route can be economically sound.

If they are absent, it is likely just a negotiated pause.
