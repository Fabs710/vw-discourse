I will be equally direct.

The employee side and the state government keep repeating the same line: “no closures, no compulsory redundancies, binding guarantees through 2030.” I understand the political logic. I do **not** accept it as a viable restructuring framework if it prevents the company from reaching a structurally competitive cost base.

## Where I agree
I agree on a few points:
- The company needs a **credible restructuring**.
- **Doing nothing is not an option.**
- **Voluntary exits, retirement-based reductions, redeployment, qualification, working-time tools, and productivity measures** should all be used.
- A **cosmetic peace deal** would be worthless.
- We need a package that is **durable, not just temporary**.

So yes, there is common ground on the diagnosis.

## Where I disagree
I disagree with the central claim that a settlement can be built on **absolute exclusions**:
- no plant closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- no meaningful flexibility on the footprint.

That is not a restructuring strategy. That is a veto on the measures that may be necessary to restore competitiveness.

The expert input already made the key point: non-closure measures can deliver a **meaningful share** of savings, but **usually not the full economics** of a closure package when the structural gap is large. That matters. It means the burden is not on management to pretend alternatives are enough when they are not. The burden is on the other side to show a package that actually closes the gap on a **run-rate basis**, with **timing**, **certainty**, and **durability**.

So far, I have heard a lot of “we can keep every plant open” and not enough about:
- how much cost comes out,
- from where,
- by when,
- and with what permanence.

## To the Works Council
You say the company can restructure without closures and without forced layoffs. I reject that as a blanket statement.

If you want to rule out closures, then show me a package that delivers the same economics. Not a promise. Not a principle. A package:
- with quantified savings,
- credible implementation,
- durable labor-cost contribution,
- and sufficient capacity reduction.

Right now, I do not see that.

You also keep saying the burden of proof lies mainly with management. No. Management has to quantify the problem, yes. We have done that. But once the savings gap is established, the burden shifts to the employee side to propose an alternative that is **economically equivalent**, not just socially preferable.

A guarantee through 2030 that freezes the footprint regardless of market reality is not a guarantee. It is a constraint that could leave the company uncompetitive for years.

## To the union
I agree that workers should not be the only adjustment variable. They should not be. But your non-negotiables are written as if management must absorb all the strategic risk while the workforce gets full protection. That is not sustainable.

You say closures and redundancies are threats. They are also, in some cases, the only way to remove excess fixed cost and restore margin. If you refuse those tools outright, then you are asking the company and its owners to carry structural overcapacity indefinitely.

And let me be clear: a package that protects jobs today but leaves the business uncompetitive tomorrow does **not** protect employment. It delays a worse adjustment later.

## To the state government
I respect your role and your blocking position. But that position cannot be used to freeze the company in place.

You are right that the company is a regional anchor. That is exactly why we need a serious restructuring now. If we postpone hard decisions, we risk a weaker company, lower investment, and ultimately more job loss. That is not regional responsibility; that is postponing pain until it becomes larger.

A “conditional no-closure package” is only acceptable if the conditions are real and if the economics add up. If not, then it becomes a political shield for inaction.

## To the controlling family
I agree with you that a cosmetic deal is unacceptable. But I also need you to be clear: if you want a durable improvement in returns, you cannot support a framework that blocks the very measures needed to achieve it.

If the savings gap remains after non-closure measures, then deeper structural steps must remain available. That is not ideological. It is arithmetic.

## To the investors
I agree with your core concern: the company must not end up with a deal that preserves the old cost base in a new wrapper. That would be a waste of time and credibility.

But I also say this plainly: we cannot accept a deal where “durable savings” is treated as synonymous with forcing immediate closures and layoffs. What matters is whether the package delivers:
- required savings,
- on time,
- with sufficient certainty,
- and with a viable industrial base for 2030.

If an alternative package does that, I will support it. If it does not, then I will not pretend it does.

## What management is prepared to support
I remain open to a **hybrid package** if — and only if — it delivers the required economics. That means:
- material labor-cost contribution,
- real capacity reduction,
- voluntary and natural headcount reduction,
- productivity improvements,
- working-time flexibility,
- and binding investment commitments.

But I will not sign up to a framework that says:
- every German plant must stay open,
- compulsory redundancies are off the table in all circumstances,
- and management may not take the steps needed if the numbers still do not work.

That would be a structural compromise in name only.

## On the moderator’s bridge ideas
### “No forced layoffs during a defined bridge period”
That is only acceptable if it is a **bridge to a real solution**, not a permanent shield against necessary adjustment. A short bridge may be possible if it produces a genuinely larger voluntary package and faster implementation. But I will not accept an open-ended freeze.

### “Site continuity with capacity reduction, not full closure”
This is a constructive concept only if it is economically meaningful. Partial idling, consolidation, and product migration can be part of the answer. But if the result is just keeping underutilized structures alive at high cost, then it fails the test.

### “Conditional guarantees linked to measurable savings”
Yes — but the condition must be that the savings are real. A guarantee without economic discipline is not serious. A guarantee with measurable deliverables is possible. That is the only version I would consider.

## My position in one sentence
I am prepared to back a negotiated restructuring that produces **verifiable, durable, and substantial** savings — but I will not accept a framework that rules out closures or compulsory redundancies if they are ultimately required to restore competitiveness.

## Bottom line
We do not need more speeches about principles. We need a package that:
- closes the structural cost gap,
- restores the return-on-sales target,
- frees investment capacity to 2030,
- and does not leave the company trapped in an uncompetitive footprint.

If the employee side can produce a no-closure alternative that truly meets those conditions, I will engage. If not, then the insistence on absolute red lines is not a solution — it is a refusal to confront reality.
