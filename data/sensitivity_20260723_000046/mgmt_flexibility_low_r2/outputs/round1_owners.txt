I agree with one central point from management: **the current cost and capacity structure cannot simply be left as it is**. If the company’s German operations remain structurally uncompetitive, that will erode margins, investment capacity, and ultimately the industrial base we are all trying to protect.

But I do **not** accept the implication that plant closures and forced redundancies are the natural or preferred answer. From the perspective of long-term ownership, that is not a trivial detail. Once you close a plant or break the trust architecture of this company, you do not just cut costs — you can also destroy know-how, morale, labour peace, and the company’s reputation as an industrial employer. That can become value-destructive very quickly.

So my position is:

- **Yes** to a durable competitiveness package
- **Yes** to real capacity reduction
- **Yes** to labour-cost savings if they are substantial and enforceable
- **Yes** to reallocation, qualification, working-time tools, and retirement-based exits
- **No** to a restructuring that permanently weakens the company’s value base or governance stability
- **No** to any plan that makes ownership control or co-determination a casualty of the crisis

To the Works Council and union: I hear the insistence on **no closures, no compulsory redundancies, and no dismantling of job security**. I understand why those red lines exist. But I would be misleading everyone if I pretended that a pure status-quo defense is enough. The company needs a package that actually delivers savings at scale, not just a postponement.

What I would regard as credible is an arrangement that does all of the following:

1. **Material cost reduction**
   - through a negotiated labour contribution
   - through working-time and staffing adjustments
   - through productivity improvements and internal redeployment

2. **Capacity rationalization**
   - without automatic resort to closures
   - but with a real reduction in output where demand does not justify it

3. **Binding stability on the sites that remain**
   - site and employment guarantees for a defined period
   - if and only if the savings package is real enough to justify that commitment

4. **A clear future investment commitment**
   - especially on next-generation products and technologies
   - because savings without investment simply degrade the asset

To the state government: I agree that the regional and political consequences matter, and I also agree that the burden of proof lies with management to show it has exhausted alternatives. But I would caution against turning that into an absolute veto on any meaningful restructuring. The company must be able to adapt. If every adjustment is treated as intolerable, then the result is not protection — it is slow decline.

So my test is straightforward:

- If the employee side can help deliver a **credible, durable savings package** that restores competitiveness **without** plant closures, I will support that route.
- If not, then I will not support arrangements that merely preserve the appearance of stability while leaving the underlying economics broken.
- But equally, I will not support a blunt solution that creates lasting damage to value, labour peace, and the company’s social legitimacy.

In short: **I support restructuring, not destruction; competitiveness, not symbolism; and a settlement that restores margins without permanently impairing the enterprise.**
