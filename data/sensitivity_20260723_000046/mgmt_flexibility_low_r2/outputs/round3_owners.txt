I will be equally direct.

I agree with the broad diagnosis from management and the investors: the company’s German cost base is too high, capacity is too large for the market, and the business needs a durable restructuring, not a symbolic truce. I also agree with the state government, works council, and union on one essential point: a prolonged, open conflict would destroy value fast. That is in nobody’s interest, least of all the family holding.

Where I disagree is with the attempt to turn “no closures” and “no compulsory redundancies” into absolute starting points. That is not a restructuring framework; it is a political red line. If those lines make the economics impossible, then they are not protecting the company — they are preserving inefficiency and delaying the bill.

## Where I agree

### With management
- The current structure is not sustainable.
- Real savings must be durable and measurable.
- Capacity must come down.
- The company needs room to invest in future products and technology.

### With the union and works council
- A disorderly confrontation would be damaging.
- Working-time tools, redeployment, qualification, retirement-based exits, and internal transfers should all be used.
- The workforce should not be treated as the only adjustment variable.
- A settlement needs legitimacy, not just board arithmetic.

### With the state government
- The company is a regional anchor, and that matters.
- Social fallout cannot be ignored.
- Co-determination and the statutory governance structure are part of reality and must be respected.

## Where I disagree

### 1) “No plant closures” cannot be an unconditional rule
If a plant is structurally uncompetitive and cannot be absorbed through alternatives, then refusing closure in principle is value-destructive. I am not saying closures should be the first move. I am saying they cannot be ruled out a priori if they are needed to restore margins and investment capacity.

### 2) “No compulsory redundancies” cannot be a hard veto if the numbers fail
I prefer voluntary exits, retirement-based reduction, and negotiated attrition. But if the savings target cannot be met otherwise, then the company cannot be forced to carry excess labour indefinitely. That would shift the burden onto owners, creditors, and future investment.

### 3) Site guarantees must be tied to real economics
A guarantee through 2030 is only acceptable if it is backed by a package that genuinely closes the gap. A promise that freezes the footprint while preserving the old cost structure is not a guarantee; it is deferred decline.

### 4) The burden of proof is not one-sided
Management must quantify the savings requirement and test alternatives. That is fair. But once the gap is established, the employee side must also show how its alternative package reaches the same economic outcome. A proposal that protects every site and rejects hard measures is not sufficient unless it actually delivers the savings.

## On the expert input
The specialist’s contribution was useful. It did not move me toward accepting a no-closure settlement. If anything, it reinforced the core point:

- non-closure measures can materially reduce the burden;
- they may be enough when the savings gap is moderate;
- but they rarely fully replicate closure economics when the structural gap is large;
- and avoiding compulsory redundancies is feasible only if the company has time, attrition, and meaningful labor flexibility.

That is exactly why I will not accept a blanket rule that rules out closures or involuntary reductions regardless of economics.

## What I would support

I would support a credible hybrid package if it meets three conditions:

1. **Material and durable savings**
   - not symbolic concessions
   - not temporary peace
   - not savings that evaporate when the agreement expires

2. **Real capacity rationalization**
   - through consolidation, site adjustments, and, if necessary, closures
   - with preference for the least destructive route that still works economically

3. **A binding future investment commitment**
   - because cost reduction without investment is just shrinkage

That means I am open to a settlement that avoids closures if and only if the non-closure package truly closes the savings gap. But I will not endorse a deal that preserves every historic protection while leaving the company structurally uncompetitive.

## To the Works Council
You say the company can restructure without closures and without forced layoffs. I reject that as a blanket statement.

If you want to rule out closures, then show a package that delivers the same economics:
- quantified savings,
- credible implementation,
- durable labor-cost contribution,
- and sufficient capacity reduction.

Right now, I do not see that.

You also keep saying the burden of proof lies mainly with management. No. Management has to quantify the problem, yes. But once the savings gap is established, the burden shifts to the employee side to propose an alternative that is economically equivalent, not just socially preferable.

A guarantee through 2030 that freezes the footprint regardless of market reality is not a guarantee; it is a constraint that could leave the company uncompetitive for years.

## To the union
I agree that workers should not be the only adjustment variable. They should not be. But your non-negotiables are written as if management must absorb all strategic risk while the workforce gets full protection. That is not sustainable.

You say closures and redundancies are threats. They are also, in some cases, the only way to remove excess fixed cost and restore margin. If you refuse those tools outright, then you are asking the company and its owners to carry structural overcapacity indefinitely.

And let me be clear: a package that protects jobs today but leaves the business uncompetitive tomorrow does not protect employment. It postpones a worse adjustment later.

## To the state government
I respect the regional responsibility and the blocking role under the special law. But that role cannot be used to freeze the company in place.

You are right that the company is an anchor employer. That is exactly why we need a serious restructuring now. If we postpone hard decisions, we risk a weaker company, lower investment, and ultimately more job losses. That is not regional stewardship; it is deferring pain until it becomes larger.

A “conditional no-closure package” is only acceptable if the conditions are real and the economics add up. If not, it becomes a political shield for inaction.

## To management
You must avoid turning necessary restructuring into unnecessary provocation. If you present closures as the default answer before exhausting negotiated alternatives, you will harden resistance and create avoidable instability.

What I need from management is not rhetoric, but a clear, site-by-site economic case:
- required savings,
- current and target cost base,
- which measures close which part of the gap,
- and what remains if closures are removed from the table.

Only then can governance judge whether the employee-side alternative is sufficient or whether deeper measures are unavoidable.

## On the bridge ideas from the moderator
### No forced layoffs during a defined bridge period
That can be considered only if it is a bridge to a real solution, not an open-ended freeze that preserves every inefficiency.

### Site continuity with capacity reduction, not full closure
This is the right direction if it produces meaningful fixed-cost reduction. Partial idling, consolidation, and product migration can be part of the answer.

### Conditional guarantees linked to measurable savings
Yes, but the condition must be that the savings are real. A guarantee without economic discipline is not serious. A guarantee with measurable deliverables is possible.

## My bottom line
I am prepared to support a negotiated restructuring that produces verifiable, durable, and substantial savings — but I will not accept a framework that rules out closures or compulsory redundancies if they are ultimately required to restore competitiveness.

So I am not asking for consensus. I am asking for a package that restores competitiveness without permanently impairing the enterprise. If the employee side can deliver that without closures, I am open. If not, then governance must retain the freedom to support deeper structural measures.
