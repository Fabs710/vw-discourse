I’ll be equally clear.

## First: what I agree on
I agree with everyone on three points:
1. **The company needs restructuring.**
2. **Doing nothing is not an option.**
3. **A cosmetic deal would be worthless.**

I also agree that non-closure measures can deliver real savings:
- voluntary exits,
- retirement-based reductions,
- redeployment,
- working-time tools,
- productivity improvements,
- and targeted cost discipline.

So let’s not pretend we are debating whether action is needed. We are not.

## Where I disagree — strongly
I do **not** accept the attempt to convert “no closures” and “no compulsory redundancies” into absolute red lines.

That position may be politically attractive, but it is not a restructuring strategy if it prevents the company from reaching a structurally competitive cost base.

The reality is simple:
- German factory costs are too high,
- the European market is shrinking,
- competition is brutal,
- and the brand is missing its return-on-sales target.

If we do not cut the structural cost base, then we will not free the funds needed for future products and technology to 2030. That is the central issue. Everything else is secondary.

## To the Works Council
You keep saying management must prove closures are necessary rather than convenient. We have done more than enough to show the problem:
- overcapacity exists,
- the cost base is uncompetitive,
- and the current structure does not fit the market.

What you have **not** shown is an alternative package that closes the gap **quantitatively, durably, and on time**.

Saying “keep every German plant open” is not an answer to a cost crisis. It is a demand to preserve the status quo while expecting the business to somehow become competitive anyway.

That does not work.

You also say non-closure measures can cover a substantial share of the gap. Fine. A substantial share is not the full job. If the residual gap remains material, then the company must retain the ability to take harder structural measures. I will not sign up to a framework that removes that option in advance.

## To the Industrial Union
I respect the fact that you are willing to discuss labour-cost and flexibility contributions. But your limit remains the same as the Works Council’s:
- no compulsory redundancies,
- no plant closures,
- no real fallback.

That is not enough.

If the savings target is large, then voluntary-only instruments may simply be insufficient on timing and scale. The question is not whether you dislike closures. The question is whether your package can actually deliver the necessary run-rate savings. If it cannot, then I will not pretend otherwise.

A package that protects jobs today but leaves the company uncompetitive tomorrow does **not** protect employment. It postpones a worse adjustment later.

## To the Regional State Government
I respect your role and your concern for the regional base. But your blocking power cannot be used to freeze the company in place.

This company is a regional anchor precisely because it must remain viable. If we preserve capacity that no longer fits demand, we are not protecting the region — we are delaying the reckoning until it becomes larger.

A bridge can make sense only if it is a bridge to real savings, not a bridge to permanent avoidance of hard decisions.

## To the Controlling Family
You say you support long-term value. Then I need you to be consistent.

Long-term value is not protected by preserving every historic arrangement regardless of market reality. It is protected by:
- restoring competitiveness,
- cutting excess fixed cost,
- and ensuring the company can invest.

If you want returns and resilience, then you cannot support a framework that removes closures or involuntary reductions from the table before the economics are satisfied.

## To the institutional investors
You are right to focus on durable savings. I agree. But durable savings are not the same as simply demanding pain from the workforce.

Durability means:
- the cost base comes down structurally,
- the savings hold beyond the next bargaining round,
- and the business has room to invest.

If the employee-side proposal cannot deliver that, then it is not sufficient. I will not endorse a deal that preserves the old footprint under a new label.

## On the expert input
The expert confirmed exactly what management has been saying:
- non-closure measures can deliver a meaningful share of savings,
- sometimes even a large share,
- but they do **not always replicate closure economics** when the structural gap is large,
- and timing matters.

That is the key point.

So yes, a no-closure package can help. But it is not automatically enough. It must be tested against the actual savings requirement. Until then, closures and compulsory reductions must remain available as fallback options.

## What management is prepared to support
I remain open to a **hybrid settlement**, but only one that delivers real economics:
- material labour-cost contribution,
- capacity reduction,
- voluntary and natural headcount reduction,
- productivity gains,
- working-time flexibility,
- and binding investment commitments.

What I will **not** accept is a framework that says:
- every German plant must stay open,
- compulsory redundancies are off the table under all circumstances,
- and management is barred from taking deeper structural steps even if the numbers still do not work.

That would not be restructuring. That would be institutionalizing the problem.

## My bottom line
We can support a negotiated package if it closes the cost gap in a way that is:
- **quantified**
- **durable**
- **on time**
- and **credible for investors, owners, and the company’s future**

If the employee side can produce a no-closure alternative that truly meets those conditions, I will engage seriously. If not, then I will not retreat from the position that closures and involuntary reductions must remain available.

We need a company that is competitive in 2030 — not one that protects every historic guarantee and then discovers too late that it cannot fund the future.
