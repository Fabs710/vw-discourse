I will be equally direct.

## Where I agree
I agree with the Works Council, the state government, and even parts of management’s and investors’ diagnosis on one point: **the company needs a durable restructuring**. Costs must come down, capacity must be aligned to demand, and the EV transition needs a credible industrial plan. I also agree that **retraining, redeployment, working-time adjustments, and retirement-based exits** belong on the table.

And I agree with the principle that a purely cosmetic deal is worthless. If a package does not actually improve competitiveness, it will only delay the next round of conflict.

## Where I disagree
I disagree with the framing that the only serious route is to threaten **plant closures** or keep **compulsory redundancies** as a live option.

That is not a neutral “toolbox.” It is a threat to force the workforce to absorb the biggest share of the pain. I will not endorse a restructuring that turns workers into the adjustment buffer while management, owners, and capital preserve their freedom of action.

I also disagree with the idea that the burden of proof sits mainly with the employee side. If management wants to break with the historic job-security framework, then **management must prove that it has exhausted every non-destructive alternative** and that the savings truly require such a step. So far, I have not heard that proof.

## Our non-negotiables
Let me be explicit:

- **No compulsory redundancies**
- **No plant closures in Germany**
- **No dismantling of the collective wage framework**
- **No open-ended insecurity for the workforce**

Those are not rhetorical preferences; they are the boundary of what we can accept.

## What we are prepared to do
We have already shown willingness to contribute. That remains the basis:

- **labour-cost contribution**
- **capacity reduction**
- **employee financial participation**
- **working-time solutions**
- **internal redeployment and qualification**
- **retirement-based exits**

But that only works if it is part of a **shared-sacrifice package**. Workers cannot give up wages, security, and guarantees while management and shareholders do not make comparable contributions.

## What a serious settlement must include
If you want our cooperation, then the package must include:

1. **Binding site and employment guarantees through 2030**
2. **No forced layoffs**
3. **Capacity reduction through negotiated, reversible measures**
4. **A fair contribution from management, owners, and capital**
5. **Clear investment commitments for future products and technologies**
6. **Preservation of co-determination and the collective wage structure**

That is the minimum basis for durable peace.

## To management
If you claim closures are necessary, then show the quantified case:
- what exact savings are required,
- what measures were tested first,
- why internal restructuring is insufficient,
- and what management and owners are contributing themselves.

Until then, closures look like a choice, not a necessity.

## To the investors
You say you want durable competitiveness. Fine. But a deal that destroys labour peace and trust is not durable. It may improve margins on paper for a quarter or two, but it increases industrial risk and undermines the company’s social base. That is not value creation; it is short-termism.

## To the Works Council
We are aligned on the essentials: no closures, no compulsory redundancies, no dismantling of protections. I support that line. But I also want us to keep the discussion focused on an **alternative package that actually delivers measurable savings**. If we want to hold the line, we need to show that the alternative is not only socially better, but economically real.

## My bottom line
We are ready for a tough settlement. We are **not** ready to sign off on downsizing by fear. If the company wants peace, it must come back with a credible package that protects jobs and sites, preserves the wage framework, and shares the burden fairly. If it does not, then conflict will continue — and we have the means to make that costly.
