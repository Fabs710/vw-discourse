I will be equally clear in this round: **the central issue is not whether the company needs restructuring. It does. The issue is whether restructuring is allowed to become a pretext for plant closures, compulsory redundancies, and the dismantling of the job-security framework. On that, my answer remains no.**

## Where I agree
I agree with management, the family holding, the state government, and the investors on one point: **the company needs a credible, durable restructuring**. I also agree with the expert that:
- non-closure measures can deliver substantial savings,
- voluntary exits, retirement-based reductions, working-time tools, redeployment, and productivity measures matter,
- and a cosmetic deal would be worthless.

So yes, we all know the company must act. That is not the disagreement.

## Where I disagree — and strongly
What I reject is the attempt to turn the expert’s range into an argument for keeping closures and forced layoffs “on the table” as if that were the neutral, responsible default.

The expert did **not** say closures are required. The expert said:
- non-closure measures often cover a meaningful share of the gap,
- they may be enough if time, attrition, and flexibility exist,
- and compulsory redundancies are avoidable in some cases.

That is not a mandate for plant closures. It is a mandate to **seriously exhaust alternatives first**.

And let me be explicit:

- **No German plant closures.**
- **No compulsory redundancies.**
- **No erosion of co-determination.**
- **No breaking of the job-security framework through the back door.**

Those are not bargaining slogans. They are the boundary of what is acceptable.

## To management
Management keeps saying the burden is on the employee side to prove an alternative. I reject that framing.

If you want to break with historic guarantees, then **you** must show:
- the precise savings required,
- the exact measures already tested,
- the savings achieved by each,
- and why internal restructuring cannot close the gap.

So far, what I hear is: “we need flexibility.” That is not a business case. That is a preference for managerial freedom.

You say non-closure measures may not fully replace closure economics. Fine. But “may not fully” is not the same as “therefore closures are necessary.” If the remaining gap exists, then management must look at:
- deeper management and overhead cuts,
- capital contributions,
- product and investment commitments,
- redistribution of work inside Germany,
- and real burden sharing above the shop floor.

We are not prepared to accept that workers carry the adjustment while ownership keeps strategic freedom untouched.

## To the investors
You keep repeating that only “durable savings” matter. I agree. But durable savings are not the same as **permanent social damage**.

A settlement that blows up trust, industrial peace, and the social foundation of production is not durable. It may look neat in a spreadsheet for one quarter. It creates risk for years.

Your argument is always: if the gap is large, closures may be necessary. That is a theoretical possibility. But theory is not a justification for doing it now. If the non-closure route can close a substantial majority of the gap, then you do not get to jump straight to closures because they are cleaner on paper.

I also reject the idea that site guarantees are “not credible” if they protect workers through 2030. That is exactly the point of a guarantee: to give the workforce security while transformation is implemented. If management cannot plan within that framework, then management is asking for a blank cheque, not restructuring.

## To the controlling family
You speak about long-term value and avoiding value destruction. Then act accordingly.

Value is not protected by threatening the workforce with plant closures first and asking for trust later. Value is protected by:
- preserving productive capacity,
- retaining skilled labour,
- preventing industrial conflict,
- and securing the company’s social licence to operate.

If the family truly wants stability, it should back a package that:
- keeps every German plant open,
- rules out compulsory redundancies,
- and commits to investment and future products in Germany.

Anything less means the old promises only held until the next downturn.

## To the state government
You are right that regional responsibility matters. But then use your blocking role to defend it in practice, not just in speeches.

A “conditional no-closure package” is only acceptable if the condition is **job and site security first**, not “we accept closures later if the numbers are inconvenient.” The state should not help create a process that normalizes closures as the fallback after the workforce has already conceded.

## On the moderator’s bridge proposals
I need to be very clear: some of those suggestions are not acceptable starting points.

### “No forced layoffs during a defined bridge period”
A bridge period can only be relevant if it is **paired with a binding no-closure and no-forced-layoff framework through 2030**, not as a temporary pause before the same threat returns later.

If the proposal simply means “we postpone layoffs while everyone gets used to the idea,” then no, that is not a compromise.

### “Site continuity with capacity reduction, not full closure”
This is closer to the only sensible route — and it is exactly why full closure talk is unnecessary. Keep the plants open, reduce capacity, reassign volumes, use working time and internal reallocation. That is restructuring without destroying sites.

### “Conditional guarantees linked to measurable savings”
A guarantee that can be withdrawn when management decides the savings are insufficient is not a guarantee. It is conditional insecurity. We do not accept that as the new normal.

## What we can support
We can support a serious package only if it contains all of the following:
1. **Binding site guarantees through 2030**
2. **No compulsory redundancies**
3. **No plant closures**
4. **Capacity reduction through reversible, negotiated measures**
5. **Material contributions from management and owners**
6. **Investment commitments for future products and technologies**
7. **A real co-determination role in implementation**

That is the minimum. Not a wish list — a floor.

## What we will not accept
We will not accept:
- closures disguised as “site adjustments,”
- redundancies dressed up as “flexibility,”
- guarantees that evaporate when management gets impatient,
- or a restructuring that treats workers as the sole shock absorber.

If the other sides want peace, they need to come back with a package that **keeps every German plant open and prevents forced layoffs**. If they cannot do that, then they are choosing confrontation, not compromise.

## Bottom line
I am not moving from the core position. The company can restructure without closing German plants and without compulsory redundancies. If management says otherwise, then it is not presenting necessity — it is presenting a choice.

And if the choice is between a fair, negotiated transformation and breaking the historic protections of the workforce, then I say again: **we will use every right we have to stop that.**
