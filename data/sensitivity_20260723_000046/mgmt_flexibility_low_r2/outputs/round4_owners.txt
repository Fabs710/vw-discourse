I’ll be direct.

## Where I agree
I agree with the basic diagnosis shared by management, investors, the state, and even the employee side:

- the company needs a **credible restructuring**
- the German cost base is too high
- capacity has to be adjusted
- a cosmetic peace deal would destroy value
- prolonged conflict would be damaging for everyone, including the family holding

I also agree that **non-closure tools matter**:
- voluntary exits
- retirement-based reductions
- redeployment
- working-time changes
- productivity improvements
- overhead cuts
- management restraint

Those are not decorative measures. They can and should do real work.

## Where I disagree
I do **not** accept the employee-side attempt to make **“no closures”** and **“no compulsory redundancies”** absolute starting points.

That is not a restructuring strategy. It is a red line that may preserve comfort, but it does not by itself restore competitiveness. And competitiveness is what protects long-term value.

### To the Works Council
I respect your role, but I reject your core framing.

You keep saying management must prove closures are necessary rather than convenient. Fair enough — management must quantify the gap and test alternatives. But once the gap is established, you cannot simply declare every hard measure illegitimate and expect the economics to disappear.

Your position amounts to:
- keep every plant open
- rule out involuntary reductions
- preserve guarantees through 2030
- and still expect a structurally lower cost base

That may be politically attractive, but it is not automatically credible.

### To the union
I agree that workers should not be the only adjustment variable. They should not be. But your absolute limits — no closures, no forced layoffs, no real fallback — are too rigid if the savings gap proves large.

If voluntary exits and labor flexibility can close the gap, fine. If they cannot, then the company must retain the ability to take deeper steps. Otherwise you are asking owners and future investment to absorb permanent inefficiency.

### To management
I agree with you on one important point: the burden is not only on management to wish for a better outcome. We need numbers, site by site, measure by measure.

But I also need management to be careful not to turn necessary restructuring into self-defeating provocation. If you present closures as the default answer before exhausting negotiated alternatives, you will harden resistance and create avoidable instability.

So: quantify the gap, show what non-closure measures deliver, and only then judge what remains.

### To the state government
I respect your blocking role and your regional responsibility. That role matters.

But it cannot be used to freeze the company in place. If the company remains structurally uncompetitive, the region loses anyway — through weaker investment, weaker margins, and ultimately a weaker industrial base.

A bridge is only acceptable if it is a bridge to real savings, not a bridge to permanent avoidance of difficult decisions.

### To investors
I agree with your concern about durable savings. That is the right concern.

But durability is not the same as maximizing short-term flexibility at any social cost. A settlement that triggers prolonged industrial conflict is not durable. It is expensive, slow, and reputationally damaging.

## On the expert input
The expert’s contribution is helpful. It does **not** support the idea that closures are automatically necessary. What it does support is this:

- non-closure measures can cover a **substantial share** of the gap
- sometimes even most of it
- but not always all of it
- and timing matters

That is precisely why I am not prepared to accept a blanket “no closure ever” framework, but I also reject the idea that closures are the default solution from the start.

## My position
I will support a negotiated restructuring if it produces:

1. **durable savings**
2. **real capacity reduction**
3. **a credible path back to target margins**
4. **continued investment in future products and technology**
5. **industrial peace**

I will **not** support a framework that permanently impairs the enterprise by locking in uncompetitive structures.

## What I can support
I am open to a package that is:
- heavily weighted toward non-closure measures
- tested by site and function
- monitored on savings delivery
- and includes real contributions from management, owners, and capital

I am also open to a **time-limited bridge** if it is tied to measurable outcomes, not open-ended comfort.

## What I cannot support
I cannot support:
- an unconditional ban on closures regardless of economics
- an unconditional ban on compulsory redundancies if the numbers fail
- guarantees that freeze the footprint even if the business case collapses
- a process that treats the family’s ownership control as secondary to the need for political comfort

Ownership control and long-term value are not to be traded away for the sake of a short-lived truce.

## On the decision rule
The right test is not “can we avoid all pain?”

The right test is:
- can a no-closure package close the savings gap **quantitatively, durably, and on time**?
- if yes, then it deserves support
- if not, then deeper structural measures must remain available

That is the standard I will hold.

## Bottom line
I am not shifting to the employee side’s absolute red lines.

I support restructuring, cost discipline, and capacity reduction. I support exhausting non-closure measures first. But I will not endorse a settlement that rules out closures or involuntary reductions in all circumstances if they are ultimately required to restore competitiveness and protect long-term value.

If the employee side wants my support for a no-closure solution, bring a package that actually closes the gap on a run-rate basis. If it cannot do that, then insisting on absolute exclusions is not stewardship — it is delaying the bill.
