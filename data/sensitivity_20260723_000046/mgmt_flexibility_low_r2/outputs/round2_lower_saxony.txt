I will be direct.

I agree with everyone that this company needs a **credible restructuring**. The cost base must come down, capacity has to match demand, and the EV transition needs a viable industrial plan. I also accept the expert’s point that **non-closure measures alone may not fully replicate closure economics**.

But I do **not** accept the leap from that observation to “therefore closures and compulsory redundancies must remain on the table as the default answer.” That is too simplistic, and for a company with this regional importance, it is economically and politically reckless.

## Where I agree
- **Management is right** that doing nothing is not an option.
- **The union and works council are right** that a purely top-down downsizing would destroy trust and industrial peace.
- **The state government is right** that the company’s role as a regional anchor matters and that the burden cannot fall only on workers.
- **The expert is right** that a hybrid package, combining voluntary exits, working-time tools, productivity measures, and capacity reduction, can materially reduce the restructuring gap.

## Where I disagree
### 1) I reject plant closures as a first resort
The expert did **not** say closures are necessary in this case. The expert said non-closure measures can often close a large share of the gap, and that avoiding compulsory redundancies is feasible if the company has time, flexibility, and negotiated concessions.

That is exactly why I cannot support management’s attempt to keep closures as an always-available threat. For us, a closure is not just a line item. It means:
- loss of industrial employment in the state,
- damage to the regional supplier base,
- political and social fallout,
- and a major blow to the company’s identity as an anchor employer.

So no: I do not accept “closure economics” as an argument for going straight to closures while reversible alternatives remain available.

### 2) I reject compulsory redundancies as a planning assumption
If management is saying it wants flexibility “just in case,” then I have to say plainly: that is not a neutral request. It shifts all risk onto the workforce and gives management too much leverage.

A serious restructuring should start from:
- voluntary exits,
- retirement-based measures,
- internal redeployment,
- working-time adjustment,
- pay and premium moderation,
- and capacity reduction through negotiated means.

Compulsory redundancies are the **last resort**, not the starting point.

### 3) I reject the idea that site guarantees are “not credible”
The works council and union are right that employees need binding protection if they are asked to contribute. I do not support freezing every business decision forever, but I **do** support a **binding site and employment framework through 2030**, provided it is paired with measurable restructuring measures and investment commitments.

That is not a fantasy. It is the condition for social peace and a durable transformation.

## What I need to see
If management wants my support, it must come forward with a package that is serious on economics **and** serious on regional responsibility.

That means:
1. **No German plant closures as part of the current package**
2. **No compulsory redundancies**
3. **Capacity reduction through negotiated, reversible measures**
4. **Material labour-cost contribution**
5. **Management and shareholder contribution as well**
6. **Binding investment commitments for future products**
7. **Protection of co-determination and the state’s veto role**

## On the expert’s 40%–80% range
That range actually strengthens my position more than management’s.

Why? Because it shows there is **room for a negotiated solution**. If non-closure measures can deliver a substantial majority of the savings in the right circumstances, then management cannot credibly claim that closures are inevitable at the outset. It must show:
- the precise savings gap,
- the exact measures it has tested,
- and why a hybrid package is insufficient.

Until then, closures remain a **choice**, not a necessity.

## To management
You say the burden is on others to show an alternative. I reverse that part:
- The burden is on **management** to prove that it has exhausted non-destructive options before demanding closures or forcing people out.
- And management must also show what it is contributing itself, rather than presenting labour as the sole adjustment variable.

If your plan depends on retaining maximum flexibility to close plants later, then you are not offering a restructuring. You are demanding a blank cheque.

## To the union and works council
I support your core red lines:
- no closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- no dismantling of the social partnership model.

But I also urge you to keep your alternative concrete and quantified. We need a package that not only protects jobs but also genuinely improves competitiveness. If we are going to defend sites, we must be able to show that the alternative is economically real, not just politically appealing.

## To the controlling family and investors
I hear your concern about competitiveness and value. That concern is legitimate. But long-term value is not created by blowing up trust, employment, and regional legitimacy. A company like this cannot be run as if social stability were a luxury.

If you want a durable settlement, then back a package that:
- preserves the industrial base,
- delivers real savings,
- and avoids a conflict that could damage the company for years.

## My bottom line
I am prepared to support a **negotiated restructuring** that includes:
- capacity reduction,
- labour-cost contributions,
- productivity improvements,
- redeployment and retraining,
- retirement-based exits,
- and binding investment commitments.

But I will **not** support a package that:
- closes German plants,
- relies on compulsory redundancies,
- weakens co-determination,
- or guts regional employment.

If management wants to test my resolve, it should understand this: I have a blocking minority, board representation, and a clear political mandate. I will use those rights to defend the company’s role as a regional economic anchor.

What I want next is not another warning that “something must give.” I want a **quantified, non-destructive restructuring package** that preserves jobs and plants while restoring competitiveness. If that is not on the table, then we have not reached a serious solution yet.
