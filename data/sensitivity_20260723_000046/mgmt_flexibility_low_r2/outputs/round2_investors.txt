We are hearing the right diagnosis from management and the family owner: **the current German cost base and capacity structure are not sustainable**. We also agree with the union, works council, and state on one important point: **a prolonged conflict would destroy value very quickly** and would be worse than a negotiated settlement if that settlement actually restores competitiveness.

But we need to be equally clear: **we cannot support a deal that preserves the old cost base in a new wrapper**.

## Where we agree
- The company needs a **durable restructuring**, not a cosmetic peace deal.
- **Capacity must come down.**
- **Labor-cost contributions, working-time changes, redeployment, retirement-based exits, and productivity measures** are all legitimate tools.
- A negotiated outcome is preferable **if** it produces **real, durable savings** and reduces execution risk.
- The EV transition needs a credible industrial and investment plan.

## Where we disagree
### 1) “No closures” cannot be an absolute starting point
The employee side and the state keep treating “no German plant closures” as a boundary condition. From an investor perspective, that is not acceptable if it prevents the company from reaching competitive margins.

The expert input was useful here: **non-closure measures can often cover a meaningful share of the gap, but usually not all of it**. That means:
- if the gap is small, alternatives may be enough;
- if the gap is large and structural, some combination of deeper capacity action, consolidation, or closure may still be necessary.

We are not attached to closures as a principle. But we will not back a settlement that rules them out politically while leaving the economics unresolved.

### 2) “No compulsory redundancies” is not enough by itself
We prefer voluntary exits and negotiated attrition. But if those are not sufficient, then the company cannot be trapped in a structure that leaves it permanently overstaffed or uncompetitive.

A package that rejects compulsory redundancies **and** fails to deliver the needed savings is simply shifting the burden onto shareholders through lower returns, weaker valuation, and higher risk.

### 3) Site guarantees must be conditional on economics
We understand why labor wants binding site and employment guarantees through 2030. But a guarantee is only credible if the package underneath it actually restores competitiveness.

A guarantee that freezes the footprint regardless of market reality is not a guarantee — it is a deferred problem. We would not support open-ended commitments that remove management’s ability to adapt if the savings package falls short.

## What we need to see
If the employee side wants investors on board for a non-closure solution, it must show:

1. **Quantified savings**
   - How much is saved, and from which measures?

2. **Timing**
   - When do those savings hit the P&L?

3. **Durability**
   - Are they permanent or just temporary labor peace?

4. **Capacity reduction**
   - What exact capacity is being taken out?

5. **Execution certainty**
   - What prevents the plan from being diluted later?

6. **Why this is better than closure**
   - Not politically, but economically and on a risk-adjusted basis.

## To management
We agree that “structural correction” is not rhetoric; it is necessary. But your credibility depends on specificity. We need a site-by-site and function-by-function case:
- current cost base,
- target cost base,
- savings by measure,
- payback timing,
- and what remains if closures are excluded.

If you want support for hard measures, show the economics clearly. If a hybrid package can deliver the same outcome without closures, then use that. If it cannot, then say so plainly.

## To the works council and union
We respect the insistence on shared sacrifice, but the sacrifice must be **sufficient** and **enforceable**. We will not support a settlement that:
- preserves every historic guarantee,
- avoids the hard decisions,
- and leaves margins structurally impaired.

That would not protect jobs in the long run. It would weaken the company until the next crisis.

## To the state government and family owner
We understand your concern about social legitimacy and regional stability. That matters. But long-term value also depends on hard economic reality.

If you want capital markets to continue supporting the company, you need to back a package that:
- brings down the fixed cost base,
- aligns capacity with demand,
- and restores the return-on-sales target on a credible timetable.

A package that is only “acceptable” socially but not economically will not be supported by investors.

## Our bottom line
We are prepared to support a **negotiated restructuring** if it credibly restores competitive margins through:
- durable cost reduction,
- meaningful capacity adjustment,
- real labor contributions,
- and clear implementation certainty.

But we are **not** willing to endorse:
- a cosmetic settlement,
- a deal that keeps the German operations structurally uncompetitive,
- or a package that protects every historic arrangement while asking capital to absorb the margin damage.

So the next step should not be another round of principle statements. It should be a **quantified comparison**:
1. a non-closure package,
2. a hybrid package,
3. and, if necessary, a deeper structural package,

with savings, timing, and risk laid out plainly.

If the non-closure route truly closes the gap, we can support it. If it does not, then the discussion must stop pretending otherwise.
