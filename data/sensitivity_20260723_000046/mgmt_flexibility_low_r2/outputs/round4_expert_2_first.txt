Below is a **finance-only assessment** of whether a **non-closure package** can close the required savings gap on a **run-rate basis by 2027/2028**, and how that should be benchmarked against **closure economics by site**.

## Executive conclusion

**Yes, a non-closure package can plausibly close a very large share of the gap — but only if it is unusually broad, heavily implemented, and includes some combination of wage moderation, working-time reduction, voluntary exits, internal relocation, overhead cuts, and productivity gains.**  

On a realistic industrial turnaround basis, I would frame it as:

- **Achievable share from non-closure measures:** roughly **60–85%** of the gap, depending on how aggressive the labor and working-time elements are.
- **Likely residual gap:** roughly **15–40%** if the structural overcapacity is material.
- **Closure economics by site:** full plant closure usually remains the **largest single savings lever per site**, but it also has the **highest one-time cost, highest implementation risk, and longest lead time**.

So the key question is not whether non-closure measures have value — they do — but whether they can **fully replace closure economics** on both **magnitude** and **timing**. In many large automotive restructurings, the answer is **not fully**, unless the savings target is moderate or the footprint has enough spare capacity that labor measures can do most of the work.

---

## 1) What “run-rate by 2027/2028” means economically

For a package to count as credible, it must deliver:

1. **Recurring annual savings** by 2027/2028  
2. **Not just accounting savings** or temporary deferrals  
3. **After implementation costs**, the net present value should be positive over a reasonable horizon  
4. Savings should be **durable**, not dependent on one-off underutilization or delayed capex

In turnaround terms, management should test the package against:

- **gross annual savings**
- **one-time restructuring cash costs**
- **timing ramp**
- **execution risk**
- **post-2028 durability**

If the package only “saves” money through delayed replacement hiring or temporary production pauses, it is not equivalent to structural closure savings.

---

## 2) What non-closure measures can realistically contribute

### A. Voluntary exits / retirement-based reductions
This is usually the cleanest non-closure lever.

Typical economics:
- Annual recurring savings per fully loaded employee can be meaningful, but not immediate.
- In German manufacturing, a realistic all-in recurring saving per leaver can often be in the range of **€70k–€120k per year** for direct + indirect labor, depending on grade mix and whether headcount is truly removed.
- One-time cost can be high if severance/bridging/early retirement is used: often **€80k–€250k+ per exit**, sometimes more for older cohorts.

Assessment:
- Strong for **run-rate savings**
- Weak on **cash efficiency** if heavily compensated
- Constrained by age structure and voluntariness

### B. Working-time reductions / short-time-like tools / shift optimization
Economically useful if there is genuine capacity slack.

Typical economics:
- Can reduce wage cost and overhead absorption issues
- Effective savings depend on whether volume is truly removed or just deferred
- Often more useful for **matching capacity to demand** than for deep fixed-cost reduction

Assessment:
- Good bridge measure
- Can be material at plant level
- Usually insufficient alone for a large structural gap

### C. Wage moderation / temporary pay concessions / allowance removal
This can be powerful if durable.

Typical economics:
- If applied across a large workforce, even modest percentage reductions produce large annual savings.
- Example: on a labor base of **€1.5bn–€2.5bn**, a **5% durable reduction** equals **€75m–€125m per year**.

Assessment:
- Very valuable if permanent or long-dated
- Politically difficult, but financially efficient
- Usually one of the best “no-closure” tools from a savings-per-euro-of-one-time-cost perspective

### D. Overhead and structural support-function reduction
This is often underestimated.

Typical economics:
- Can produce **mid-double-digit to low-triple-digit millions** annually in a large OEM
- Works best when tied to org simplification, procurement, and decision-layer reduction

Assessment:
- Important, but usually not sufficient alone
- Savings tend to ramp slower than direct labor measures

### E. Productivity and scrap/logistics/indirect efficiency
Useful but less bankable.

Typical economics:
- Can contribute meaningfully over time
- However, these savings are often partly offset by volume volatility and implementation slippage

Assessment:
- Should be included
- Should be haircut heavily in a restructuring case model

---

## 3) Can non-closure measures fully replace closure economics?

### Short answer
**Sometimes, but not usually if the structural gap is large.**

A non-closure package can substitute for closure economics when:
- the site network has meaningful slack,
- labor flexibility is strong,
- wage concessions are durable,
- and there is enough voluntary exit capacity.

But if the business has **multiple underutilized German plants**, then closure economics usually dominate because they remove:
- fixed manufacturing overhead
- building and maintenance cost
- plant management layers
- utilities and logistics duplication
- indirect labor
- and under-absorption losses

That’s why closure savings tend to be **larger per site** than non-closure savings.

---

## 4) Benchmarking closure economics by site

Without site-specific data, I can only give a restructuring benchmark range. For a large automotive site, a full closure often produces:

### Annual run-rate savings per site
- **Small/medium plant:** roughly **€80m–€180m per year**
- **Large plant:** roughly **€150m–€300m+ per year**

This includes:
- direct labor
- indirect labor
- plant overhead
- maintenance
- energy/utilities
- site services
- logistics simplification
- and capacity rationalization benefits

### One-time closure costs
Often substantial:
- severance and social plan costs
- asset write-downs
- environmental/site remediation
- contract termination
- transfer/logistics costs
- relocation/start-up costs at receiving sites

Typical range:
- **€200m–€800m+ per site**, sometimes higher for large or heavily regulated facilities

### Timing
- Actual savings may take **12–36 months** to fully materialize
- Cash cost usually comes **before** savings
- Legal/social delay can materially reduce net present value

### Risk
- High
- Labor conflict, legal delay, operational disruption, and political interference can erode the case

---

## 5) Non-closure package vs closure package: economic comparison

### Non-closure package
Pros:
- lower one-time cash cost
- lower legal/social implementation risk
- faster to negotiate in principle
- preserves installed capacity and optionality

Cons:
- savings ceiling may be lower
- may require continuing concessions every bargaining cycle
- sometimes only partially removes fixed cost
- can leave structural overcapacity in place

### Closure package
Pros:
- highest structural savings
- strongest long-term capacity correction
- one-time action can remove recurring cost base permanently

Cons:
- high one-time cost
- long lead times
- high disruption risk
- political and labor resistance
- may be blocked or delayed in a co-determined governance structure

---

## 6) Practical run-rate test for 2027/2028

I would test the non-closure package using a three-bucket model:

### Bucket 1: Bankable recurring savings
Include only:
- durable wage moderation
- headcount exits with confirmed position elimination
- fixed overhead removal
- permanent shift/capacity reduction
- verified procurement/support-function savings

### Bucket 2: Partially bankable savings
Haircut heavily:
- productivity promises
- voluntary attrition assumptions
- organization redesign synergies
- logistics/complexity reductions

### Bucket 3: Non-bankable or timing-risk savings
Exclude or heavily discount:
- savings contingent on future volume recovery
- savings dependent on uncertain EV mix
- savings requiring future negotiations to stay in place
- temporary accounting effects

If after this haircut the package still closes the gap by 2027/2028, then a no-closure settlement is economically credible. If not, the residual gap must be closed by harder structural action.

---

## 7) My quantitative view of likely outcomes

In a large German OEM context, I would expect the following broad ranges:

### Scenario A: Strong non-closure package
- Wage moderation + exits + working-time + overhead cuts + productivity
- Can deliver **60–85% of required gap**
- Credible if the savings requirement is moderate and workforce is willing to concede materially

### Scenario B: Partial non-closure package
- Voluntary exits + some shift reduction + limited wage concessions
- Can deliver **30–60% of gap**
- Usually insufficient if overcapacity is large

### Scenario C: Closure-led package
- One or more site closures plus some non-closure measures
- Can fully close the gap and usually exceeds non-closure economics
- But with higher cash cost and higher execution risk

---

## 8) Decision implication

If management’s required savings gap is **large and structural**, a **pure no-closure package is unlikely to be fully equivalent** unless:
- the labor contribution is unusually deep and durable,
- capacity can be cut meaningfully without closure,
- and voluntary exits can be executed at scale.

If the savings gap is **moderate**, then a non-closure package can absolutely be enough, especially if supported by:
- permanent wage moderation
- shift reduction
- overhead cuts
- and strict headcount control

---

## Bottom line

**Economically, the non-closure package is plausible as a major contributor, but not automatically a full substitute for closure economics.**  

My base-case assessment is:
- **Non-closure measures can likely close most of the gap**
- **Closure economics remain the stronger lever per site**
- The unresolved issue is **not ideology, but residual gap after haircutting optimistic savings assumptions**
