We are aligned on the diagnosis, but not yet on the standard for action.

## Where we agree
We agree with management, the family holding, and the expert that:
- the current German cost base is too high,
- capacity has to come down,
- and a cosmetic peace deal would destroy value.
We also agree with the works council, union, and state that a prolonged conflict would be value-destructive and would damage execution.

So the real issue is not whether restructuring is needed. It is whether the package actually restores **competitive margins on a durable basis**.

## Where we disagree with the employee side and state
You keep trying to make **“no closures”** and **“no compulsory redundancies”** absolute starting points. From our perspective, that is not a credible restructuring discipline. It is a preferred outcome.

The expert did not say closures are required. But the expert did say something management and investors need to hear clearly: **non-closure measures usually do not fully replicate closure economics when the structural gap is large**. That is the key point. If the savings gap is real and material, then we cannot support a framework that rules out the tools needed to close it.

We are not attached to closures for their own sake. We are attached to:
- durable savings,
- lower fixed costs,
- and a credible return to target margins.

If the alternatives can achieve that, fine. If they cannot, then the company must retain the ability to use deeper structural measures.

## What we need to see
A settlement is acceptable only if it answers four questions convincingly:

1. **How large is the savings gap?**
2. **Which measures close it, by site and function?**
3. **When do the savings hit the P&L?**
4. **Why is the package durable, not just a temporary truce?**

So far, the employee side has been strong on red lines, but weak on quantified equivalence. Saying “keep every German plant open” is not a savings plan.

## On the “no forced layoffs” claim
We prefer voluntary exits, retirement-based reductions, and redeployment. But if those tools do not generate enough permanent savings, then we will not support a structure that leaves the company overstaffed or uncompetitive in order to avoid discomfort. That would simply shift the pain into lower returns, weaker valuation, and future underinvestment.

We are moderately risk-tolerant, but not tolerant of prolonged margin erosion.

## On site guarantees through 2030
A guarantee can be acceptable only if it is **conditional on real economics**. A guarantee that freezes footprint and flexibility regardless of demand and cost reality is not protection; it is deferred decline.

We would support:
- a time-limited bridge period,
- with measurable milestones,
- a credible voluntary-package push,
- and a clear fallback if the target savings are not reached.

We would not support a blank cheque that locks in the old cost structure.

## On management and owners
We agree with management that flexibility matters. But flexibility must be used to achieve a numerically credible outcome, not as an excuse to provoke confrontation.

We also agree with the family and state that the company has social and regional responsibilities. But those responsibilities are not served by preserving uncompetitive structures indefinitely. Long-term value requires hard cost discipline.

## Our bottom line
Our support goes to the package, not to a side.

We can back a hybrid solution if it delivers:
- material and durable cost reduction,
- real capacity rationalization,
- measurable labor contributions,
- and clear investment commitments.

We cannot back:
- a cosmetic settlement,
- a deal that keeps the German footprint intact while margins stay impaired,
- or a package that rejects hard decisions regardless of economics.

So if the employee side wants investor support for a no-closure solution, bring the numbers. Show that it closes the gap on a run-rate basis, with enough certainty and durability to restore competitiveness. If it cannot do that, then we should stop pretending that “no closures” is a financially sufficient answer.
