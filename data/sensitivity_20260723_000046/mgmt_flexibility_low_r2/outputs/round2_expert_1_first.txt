From a turnaround-finance perspective, the key question is not whether non-closure measures can contribute materially — they can — but **whether they can usually replace the full economic effect of plant closure when the restructuring gap is large**. In most mature automotive restructurings, the answer is:

- **Non-closure measures can often close a meaningful share of the gap**
- **They rarely replicate 100% of closure economics**
- **The residual gap can sometimes be closed without compulsory redundancies, but only if the company has enough time, enough natural attrition, and enough labor-cost flexibility**

## 1) How the economics usually break down

A plant closure creates savings in several layers:

1. **Direct fixed-cost elimination**
   - plant overhead
   - utilities and maintenance
   - indirect labor
   - local logistics and support functions

2. **Labor cost removal**
   - wages and social costs for the affected workforce
   - management and indirect staff tied to the site

3. **Capacity rationalization**
   - better utilization of remaining plants
   - lower depreciation burden per unit if volume consolidates

4. **Avoided future capex**
   - less need to modernize a structurally excess site

But closures also create costs:

- severance / social plan costs
- asset write-downs
- decommissioning and remediation
- transfer and ramp-up costs at surviving sites
- possible supply-chain disruption

So the **net present value** of a closure is not simply “headcount removed × average cost.” The real question is whether the company can achieve comparable **run-rate savings** through alternatives fast enough and with enough certainty.

## 2) What non-closure measures can typically deliver

In automotive restructuring, non-closure measures usually come from five buckets:

### A. Headcount reduction without forced layoffs
- early retirement
- natural attrition
- hiring freeze
- internal transfers
- voluntary exit programs

**Typical effect:** meaningful, but slower  
**Strength:** lower industrial conflict and lower cash severance than mass involuntary cuts  
**Weakness:** uncertain volume and timing

### B. Working-time changes
- reduced hours
- temporary short-time work
- shift redesign
- overtime elimination
- flex-account use

**Typical effect:** useful for cyclical compression, weaker for permanent overcapacity  
**Strength:** fast cash relief  
**Weakness:** often temporary unless permanently embedded in agreements

### C. Pay and allowance measures
- base-pay moderation
- removal of special premiums
- bonus reductions
- suspension of future increases
- conversion of cash into deferred benefits

**Typical effect:** material if the workforce is large and savings are durable  
**Strength:** immediate run-rate impact  
**Weakness:** often politically difficult and may be partially offset by later restoration demands

### D. Productivity / process improvement
- lean measures
- line balancing
- automation
- procurement savings
- fewer layers of management
- overhead reduction

**Typical effect:** can be large, but execution risk is high  
**Strength:** structurally positive if real  
**Weakness:** usually slower than headcount-based savings

### E. Capacity reduction without closure
- line consolidation
- product migration to fewer sites
- reduced shifts
- partial idling of lines

**Typical effect:** can remove a lot of the economic pain of excess capacity while preserving the site  
**Strength:** flexible compromise  
**Weakness:** may still leave structural fixed costs in place

## 3) Can non-closure measures match closure savings?

### Short answer
**Usually not fully, unless the savings target is moderate or the company is willing to combine several levers aggressively.**

A plant closure tends to remove an entire fixed-cost base. A non-closure package often removes:
- part of labor cost,
- part of overhead,
- part of capacity cost,

but not all of it.

### Rule-of-thumb range
Without company-specific data, I would expect:

- **Non-closure package alone:** can often deliver about **40%–80% of the savings of a closure package**
- **Upper end** if there is a large voluntary exit pool, major working-time concessions, and real overhead rationalization
- **Lower end** if the site is highly integrated and fixed costs are hard to strip out

That is not a precise forecast; it is a realistic restructuring range based on comparable industrial cases.

## 4) Can the gap be closed without compulsory redundancies?

### Yes, sometimes — but only under certain conditions

The gap can be closed **without compulsory redundancies** if all of the following are available:

1. **Enough time**
   - usually 18–36 months, sometimes longer

2. **Enough natural attrition**
   - retirements, resignations, internal moves

3. **A credible voluntary exit program**
   - financially attractive enough to generate uptake

4. **Labor-cost flexibility**
   - wage moderation, allowance cuts, or benefit changes

5. **Product / volume consolidation**
   - enough work is moved out of the excess-cost structure

6. **Management discipline**
   - no offsetting rehiring or hidden growth in overhead

If those conditions are not present, then avoiding compulsory redundancies becomes difficult **unless the company accepts a smaller savings target or a longer implementation period**.

## 5) The biggest financial distinction: cash savings vs. accounting savings

This matters a lot.

A package can look good on paper but fail on cash flow.

### Closure often gives:
- bigger long-run fixed-cost savings
- but higher near-term cash costs for severance, decommissioning, and transition

### Non-closure measures often give:
- lower up-front cash cost
- slower or smaller run-rate savings

For a company under earnings pressure, the relevant test is:
- **How much cash is saved in the next 12–24 months?**
- **How much recurring EBIT improvement follows?**
- **How certain is the delivery?**

If the company’s problem is immediate liquidity, non-closure measures are often attractive.  
If the problem is structural competitiveness over 5–10 years, closures or equivalent capacity exits often become more compelling.

## 6) What I would want to see to judge this properly

To say whether the gap can be closed without compulsory redundancies, I would need:

- plant-by-plant fixed cost base
- direct labor and indirect labor at each site
- volume, mix, and utilization by plant
- marginal cost of each unit of capacity removed
- severance assumptions for voluntary versus involuntary exits
- retirement eligibility profile
- pension and social plan liabilities
- capex required to keep each site competitive
- transfer costs to other plants
- timing of product changes and model cycles

Without that, any answer must be a range, not a certainty.
