I will be equally direct.

## Where I agree
I agree with the Works Council, the state government, and even parts of management’s diagnosis on one central point: **the company needs a credible restructuring**. Costs must come down, capacity must match demand, and the EV transition needs a real industrial plan, not slogans. I also agree that **qualification, redeployment, working-time adjustments, retirement-based exits, and productivity measures** belong on the table.

And I agree with the expert already heard: **non-closure measures can deliver substantial savings**. They are not cosmetic if they are properly designed and enforced.

## Where I disagree
I do **not** accept the repeated attempt to turn “closures are possible” into a default bargaining threat. That is not a neutral restructuring tool; it is pressure to shift the burden onto the workforce.

Let me be explicit about our boundary:

- **No compulsory redundancies**
- **No German plant closures**
- **No dismantling of the collective wage framework**
- **No open-ended insecurity for the workforce**

That is not rhetoric. That is the limit of what we can accept.

## To management
You keep saying the gap must be closed “quantitatively, durably, and on time.” Fine. But you still have not shown that closures or forced layoffs are necessary rather than chosen for flexibility.

The burden is **not** on the workforce to prove that a no-closure plan is perfect. The burden is on management to prove that it has exhausted:
- working-time reductions,
- internal transfers,
- retirement-based exits,
- voluntary exit programs,
- bonus and allowance moderation,
- management overhead cuts,
- and real owner/shareholder contributions.

So far, I have heard a lot of “there may still be a gap,” but not a convincing case that the gap cannot be closed without destroying plants or imposing forced exits.

If management wants serious negotiations, then come with numbers by site and function:
- current cost base,
- target cost base,
- savings by measure,
- timing,
- and what remains after non-closure measures.

## To the investors
You are right about one thing: a cosmetic peace deal is worthless. We do not support that either.

But you are wrong if you believe “durable savings” only mean plant closures and layoffs. Durable savings also come from:
- fair labor-cost contributions,
- capacity reduction without closure,
- permanent overhead cuts,
- management restraint,
- and product/investment commitments that secure the volume base.

If you push for solutions that provoke labour conflict and destroy trust, you are not creating value — you are increasing industrial risk. That risk will come back in lower productivity, weaker commitment, and higher execution cost.

## To the controlling family
You speak about long-term value. Then behave like long-term owners.

Long-term value is not protected by forcing the workforce into a corner. It is protected by:
- keeping skilled labor,
- preserving industrial peace,
- securing a stable bargaining framework,
- and committing to production in Germany.

If you want workers to contribute, then owners and management must contribute too. That means real burden sharing, not just asking the shop floor to absorb the pain.

## To the state government
You have a blocking position and a political responsibility. Use both for a genuine settlement, not a temporary pause before the same conflict returns.

I appreciate your support for binding protection through 2030. That is the right direction. But it has to be **binding and real**, not a conditional promise that evaporates the moment management gets impatient.

## On the moderator’s bridge ideas
### “No forced layoffs during a defined bridge period”
A bridge period can only be part of an acceptable deal if it is embedded in a **binding no-forced-layoff framework** and paired with a serious industrial plan. It cannot be a grace period before the same threat returns.

### “Site continuity with capacity reduction, not full closure”
Yes — this is the only credible direction. Keep the plants open, reduce capacity, consolidate production, and use internal reallocation. That is restructuring without breaking the company’s social base.

### “Conditional guarantees linked to measurable savings”
A guarantee that can be pulled whenever management says the savings are insufficient is not a guarantee. It is insecurity in formal dress. We will not sign up to that.

## What we can support
We are prepared to support a tough but fair package if it includes all of the following:

1. **Binding site and employment guarantees through 2030**
2. **No forced layoffs**
3. **No plant closures**
4. **Capacity reduction through negotiated, reversible measures**
5. **A fair contribution from management, owners, and capital**
6. **Preservation of the collective wage framework**
7. **Clear investment commitments for future products and technologies**
8. **Real co-determination in implementation**

That is the minimum basis for a durable settlement.

## What we will not accept
We will not accept:
- closures disguised as “necessary adjustments,”
- redundancies dressed up as “flexibility,”
- workers carrying the full burden while others keep strategic freedom,
- or a restructuring that weakens the collective bargaining framework.

## Bottom line
We are not here to administer downsizing. We are here to prevent restructuring from becoming a one-sided demolition of jobs, wages, and site security.

If management and owners want peace, they need to come back with a **quantified, non-destructive package** that keeps German plants open, avoids compulsory redundancies, and shares the burden fairly. If they cannot do that, then they should be honest that they are choosing confrontation — and we have the means to make that costly.
