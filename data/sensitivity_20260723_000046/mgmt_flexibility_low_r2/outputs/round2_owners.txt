I will be direct.

I agree with the broad diagnosis from management, investors, and the expert: **the German cost base is too high, capacity is too large for the market, and the company needs a durable restructuring, not a cosmetic truce**. I also agree with the employee side and the state that a purely top-down shock would be damaging. A prolonged conflict would destroy value faster than almost any restructuring measure.

Where I disagree is on the attempt by the Works Council, union, and state to make **“no plant closures” and “no compulsory redundancies” absolute starting points**. That is not a restructuring plan; it is a political red line. If those lines make the economics impossible, then they are not protecting the company — they are preserving inefficiency.

The expert’s input is useful, but it should not be misread. Yes, non-closure measures can cover a meaningful share of the gap. That is important. But the expert also said what management is saying: **non-closure measures rarely replace closure economics in full when the structural gap is large**. That is the core issue here. We cannot pretend otherwise.

## Where I agree

### With management
- The current structure is not sustainable.
- Real savings must be durable and measurable.
- Capacity must come down.
- The company needs room to invest in future products.

### With the union and works council
- A disorderly confrontation would be bad for everyone.
- Working-time tools, redeployment, retirement-based exits, and qualification should be used.
- Workers should not be treated as the only adjustment variable.
- A settlement needs legitimacy, not just board arithmetic.

### With the state government
- The company remains an industrial anchor, and that matters.
- Social and regional fallout cannot be ignored.
- Co-determination and statutory governance rights are part of the reality and must be respected.

## Where I disagree

### 1) “No closures” cannot be an unconditional rule
If a plant is structurally uncompetitive and cannot be absorbed through alternative measures, then refusing closure in principle is value-destructive. I am not saying closures should be the first move. I am saying they cannot be ruled out a priori if they are needed to restore margins and investment capacity.

### 2) “No compulsory redundancies” cannot be a hard veto if the math fails
I prefer voluntary exits, retirement-based reduction, and negotiated attrition. But if the savings target cannot be met otherwise, then the company cannot be forced to carry excess labour indefinitely. That would simply shift the burden onto owners, creditors, and future investment.

### 3) Site guarantees must be tied to real economics
A binding guarantee through 2030 is only acceptable if it is backed by a package that truly closes the gap. A promise that freezes the footprint while preserving the old cost structure is not a guarantee; it is deferred decline.

### 4) The burden of proof is not one-sided
Management must quantify the savings requirement and test alternatives. That is fair. But once the gap is established, the employee side also has to show how its alternative package reaches the same economic outcome. A proposal that protects every site and rejects hard measures is not sufficient unless it actually delivers the savings.

## What I would support

I would support a **credible hybrid package** if it meets three conditions:

1. **Material and durable savings**
   - not symbolic concessions
   - not a temporary bridge
   - not savings that vanish when labour peace expires

2. **Real capacity rationalization**
   - through a mix of consolidation, site adjustments, and, if necessary, closures
   - with preference for the least destructive route that still works economically

3. **A binding future investment commitment**
   - because cost reduction without investment is just asset stripping

That means I am open to a settlement that **avoids closures if and only if** the non-closure package truly closes the savings gap. But I will not endorse a deal that preserves every historic protection while leaving the company structurally uncompetitive.

## To the Works Council and union
You say closures are unacceptable. I understand the position, but you have not yet shown that your preferred alternative can close the gap at scale, with certainty, and on time.

If you want to avoid closures, then bring a package that is:
- quantified,
- enforceable,
- durable,
- and sufficient.

That means:
- real labour-cost contribution,
- significant capacity reduction,
- meaningful staffing reduction through voluntary and natural exits,
- working-time flexibility,
- productivity improvements,
- and a credible implementation schedule.

If you can do that, I will support it. If you cannot, then you are asking the shareholders to subsidize a cost structure that the market no longer supports.

## To management
You must be careful not to turn necessary restructuring into unnecessary provocation. If you present closures as the default answer before exhausting negotiated alternatives, you will harden resistance and create avoidable instability.

What I need from management is not rhetoric about structural correction, but a **clear, site-by-site economic case**:
- required savings,
- current and target cost base,
- which measures close which part of the gap,
- and what remains if closures are removed from the table.

Only then can governance judge whether the employee-side alternative is sufficient or whether deeper measures are unavoidable.

## To the state government
I respect the concern for jobs and the regional base. But a blocking position is only legitimate if it helps produce a solution, not if it simply prevents one. If the alternative package does not add up, then insisting on no closures becomes a recipe for drift. And drift will eventually cost jobs anyway — just more slowly and less transparently.

## My bottom line
My position remains:

- **Yes** to a durable restructuring.
- **Yes** to major non-closure measures where they genuinely deliver savings.
- **Yes** to negotiated labour contributions and capacity reductions.
- **Yes** to binding investment commitments.
- **No** to a settlement that permanently locks in uncompetitive structures.
- **No** to a blanket rule that excludes closures or redundancies regardless of economics.
- **No** to value-destroying instability.

So I am not asking for consensus. I am asking for a **package that restores competitiveness without permanently impairing the enterprise**. If the employee side can deliver that without closures, I am open. If not, then governance must retain the freedom to support deeper structural measures.
