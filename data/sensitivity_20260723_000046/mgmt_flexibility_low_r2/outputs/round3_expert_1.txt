From a restructuring finance perspective, the key question is not whether the company needs action — it does — but whether a **no-closure / no-compulsory-redundancy package can close the gap quantitatively, durably, and in time**.

## My expert assessment

### 1) Non-closure measures can produce meaningful savings
Measures such as:
- voluntary exits,
- retirement-based reductions,
- redeployment,
- working-time cuts,
- temporary or permanent wage concessions,
- allowance/bonus moderation,
- management overhead cuts,
- productivity improvements,
- consolidation of shifts and product lines,

can often deliver **a substantial share of needed savings**, especially over a 12–36 month bridge period.

In large industrial restructurings, a **material savings contribution from non-closure measures is realistic** if:
- the workforce is aging enough to allow natural attrition,
- there is spare internal demand for redeployment,
- management is willing to cut its own cost base,
- and the labor side accepts real, measurable concessions.

A reasonable broad range, in the abstract, is that non-closure measures can cover roughly **30–70% of the savings gap**, depending on the starting point and the urgency.

### 2) They do not always fully replicate closure economics
Plant closures typically remove:
- fixed overhead,
- maintenance and utilities,
- duplicate logistics,
- underutilized equipment,
- site-level supervision,
- and often some embedded labor cost.

That means closure economics are usually **more structural** than a package based only on labor flexibility.

So if the company’s structural gap is large, non-closure tools may be enough to stabilize the business, but **not always enough to fully restore target margins**. In that case, you often need one or more of:
- deeper labor-cost concessions,
- permanent capacity reduction without closure,
- product migration,
- capex reallocation,
- or ultimately a site exit.

### 3) The savings gap can sometimes be closed without compulsory redundancies
Yes — **if** the following are true:
- enough employees are near retirement or eligible for voluntary exit,
- the company can afford financed exit programs,
- redeployment opportunities exist across plants/functions,
- working-time tools are large enough in scale,
- and the labor side accepts a real contribution on pay/flexibility.

But if the required headcount reduction is large relative to natural turnover, then avoiding compulsory redundancies becomes much harder.

As a rule of thumb, if the company needs to remove a very large number of positions quickly — especially where the workforce is not near retirement and alternative openings are limited — then **voluntary-only solutions may not close the gap on time**.

### 4) The economics of “no closure” depend on timing
This is critical.

A package may be acceptable in **run-rate terms**, but still fail if the savings arrive too slowly. Management and investors will focus on:
- cash impact in the next 12 months,
- EBITDA/run-rate savings by 24–36 months,
- and whether the cost base is locked in long enough to fund EV investment.

A deal that is “good eventually” but weak in the first 18 months may still be rejected if liquidity and covenant pressure are tight.

### 5) What would make a no-closure package credible?
To be credible, it would need:
- a **quantified savings target** by site and function,
- a **voluntary headcount plan** with realistic uptake assumptions,
- **measurable wage and flexibility concessions**,
- **productivity KPIs**,
- **management and overhead cuts**,
- and a **fallback mechanism** if savings underdeliver.

Without those elements, “no closure” is just a political position, not a financially proven restructuring plan.

## Bottom line
My neutral view is:

- **Yes**, a non-closure restructuring can produce substantial savings.
- **Yes**, in some cases it can avoid compulsory redundancies.
- **No**, it cannot be assumed to fully replace closure economics in every case.
- Whether the gap can be closed without forced layoffs depends on the **size of the required savings, the workforce age profile, the amount of voluntary turnover available, and the scale of labor-cost concessions**.
