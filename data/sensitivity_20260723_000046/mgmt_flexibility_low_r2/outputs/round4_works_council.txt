I will speak plainly.

## My response to the room

I agree with everyone on one thing: **the company needs restructuring**. I do not dispute that. What I dispute is the attempt to smuggle in plant closures and compulsory redundancies as if they were the only serious answer.

They are not.

## Where I agree
I agree with management, investors, the family holding, the state government, and the expert that:
- costs must come down,
- capacity must be adjusted,
- the transformation needs a durable industrial plan,
- and a cosmetic settlement would be worthless.

I also accept the expert’s point that non-closure tools alone may not always replicate closure economics perfectly. Fine. But that does **not** justify making closures the default or the threat that drives the whole process.

## Where I disagree
### To management
You keep saying the burden shifts to us to prove an “economically equivalent” no-closure package. I reject that framing.

You are the party proposing to break with historic protections. Then **you** must prove, site by site and measure by measure:
- what the exact savings gap is,
- what non-closure measures have already been exhausted,
- what they deliver,
- and why closures or forced layoffs are truly necessary rather than simply more convenient.

So far, I hear a lot of “flexibility,” but not a convincing case that the workforce has to pay with plant closures and involuntary redundancies.

And let me be clear: **we will not accept a framework that treats every German plant as conditional.**

### To investors
You argue for “durable savings.” I agree with durability. I disagree with your conclusion that durability requires keeping closures and involuntary reductions on the table.

Durable savings also come from:
- working-time changes,
- voluntary exit programs,
- retirement-based reductions,
- redeployment,
- productivity improvements,
- overhead cuts,
- and management/shareholder contributions.

A settlement that destroys trust, provokes strikes, and breaks the job-security framework is **not durable**. It is unstable and costly.

### To the controlling family
You talk about long-term value. Then stop treating the historic job guarantees like disposable paper.

Long-term value is protected by:
- preserving skilled labor,
- keeping plants open,
- avoiding industrial conflict,
- and maintaining co-determination as a real power, not a decorative one.

If the family wants stability, it should back a package that keeps German sites open and avoids forced layoffs. Anything less is a decision to put short-term restructuring convenience above the company’s social foundation.

### To the regional state government
Your blocking role matters. Use it.

Do not help normalize a process where closures become the fallback after the workforce has already conceded enough. A regional anchor company should not be asked to solve its problems by cutting away its industrial base.

A “bridge” can only be acceptable if it is a bridge to:
- binding site security,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- and real reallocation of work inside Germany.

Not a bridge to closures later.

## On the expert’s input
The expert was useful, but I want to be precise: the expert did **not** say closures are required. The expert said non-closure measures can often cover a substantial share of the gap, and in some cases avoid compulsory redundancies entirely.

That supports our position, not management’s threat politics.

Yes, the expert also said timing matters. We know that. That is exactly why management should have come with a serious negotiated plan earlier instead of escalating toward closures.

## On the moderator’s three compromise tracks
### 1) Time-limited no-closure bridge
I will only consider this if it is not a Trojan horse.

A bridge period is acceptable only if it is explicitly tied to:
- **no German plant closures**
- **no compulsory redundancies**
- **binding site and employment guarantees through 2030**
- **measurable management and owner contributions**
- **joint monitoring with real co-determination**

If the bridge is just a pause before the same threat returns, then it is not a compromise. It is delay.

### 2) Site continuity plus capacity reduction
This is the only sensible direction.

We can reduce shifts, consolidate product lines, reallocate volumes, use working-time tools, and adjust internal flows. That is restructuring **without destroying sites**.

So yes to capacity reduction.  
No to closure.  
Yes to internal reallocation.  
No to forced redundancies.

### 3) Conditional protection with a fallback
Absolutely not, if the fallback is closures or involuntary layoffs.

A guarantee that disappears when management says the numbers are inconvenient is not a guarantee. It is insecurity with a deadline.

We do **not** accept that logic.

## What we can support
We can support a serious package only if it includes all of the following:

1. **No German plant closures**
2. **No compulsory redundancies**
3. **Binding site and job guarantees through 2030**
4. **Capacity reduction through negotiated, reversible measures**
5. **Real burden sharing from management, owners, and capital**
6. **Investment commitments for future products and technologies**
7. **Preservation of co-determination and the collective job-security framework**

That is the floor. Not an opening bid — the minimum acceptable basis.

## What we reject
We reject:
- closures disguised as “footprint optimization,”
- layoffs dressed up as “flexibility,”
- guarantees that can be revoked at management’s convenience,
- and any settlement that shifts structural risk entirely onto workers.

If management, owners, and investors want peace, they need to come back with a package that **keeps every German plant open and rules out compulsory redundancies**.

If they cannot do that, then they are not seeking compromise. They are asking the workforce to absorb the company’s strategic mistakes.

## Bottom line
We do not need more lectures about realism. We need a real settlement.

And the reality is this: **the company can restructure without plant closures and without forced layoffs if the burden is shared fairly and if management stops treating worker protections as negotiable collateral.**

We will not move off that line because others keep repeating pressure points. We will use every co-determination right, every supervisory-board vote, and every mobilization tool available to defend it.
