## To the works council
You keep saying “no closures, no compulsory redundancies, no dismantling of guarantees” as if repeating red lines closes the structural gap. It does not.

I agree with you on three points:
- a cosmetic deal would be worthless,
- the company needs a durable restructuring,
- and the numbers must be real.

Where I disagree is your conclusion that closures are not a legitimate option. That is simply **not proven**. The expert’s ranges show that a no-closure package may cover a large part of the gap, but **not automatically all of it**. And until we see the plant-level cost base, utilisation, and residual gap, you are asking management to rule out a necessary instrument without evidence.

So let’s be precise:
- If voluntary exits, redeployment, working-time measures, productivity gains, and selective capacity consolidation truly close the gap, I will consider that.
- If they do **not**, then closures and stronger structural measures remain on the table.

What I will not accept is a package that preserves every site, excludes compulsory reductions entirely, and still expects the company to become competitive. That is not a restructuring plan. That is a delay strategy.

## To the union
You are right that labour-side contributions are part of any serious settlement. I acknowledge that.

But your position still underestimates the scale of the problem. Temporary working-time reductions, bonus moderation, voluntary exits, and productivity commitments can help — yes. They may even help materially. But you have not shown that they can deliver **durable** enough savings on their own to restore the brand’s return-on-sales trajectory.

And I will say this plainly: if your package is built around:
- no closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- full job security through 2030,
- and only limited employee sacrifice,

then you are not solving the structural cost problem. You are just shifting the burden into the future.

If you want us to move off closures, then show me the quantified recurring relief you can actually deliver, and show me that it is enough. Otherwise, your position is not a solution; it is a boundary.

## To the state government
I respect your political role and your concern for the regional base. But you cannot turn that into a permanent veto over economically necessary change.

I agree with you on one point: a destructive closure-first approach would be bad if it were merely punitive or symbolic. But that is not what is being proposed. What is being proposed is a restructuring to restore competitiveness, margin discipline, and future investment capacity.

You say non-destructive instruments have not been exhausted. Fine. Then let’s exhaust them — but with a deadline and with a quantified review. What I cannot accept is a framework that makes closures politically impossible regardless of the numbers.

So yes:
- no closures as a **starting assumption** is acceptable for a short, defined phase,
- but no closures as an **absolute rule** is not.

If the numbers show a residual gap that only closure-equivalent action can close, the company must not be trapped by politics.

## To the controlling family
You want long-term value. So do I.

But long-term value is not protected by preserving an uncompetitive structure and hoping goodwill will fix it. It is protected by real, durable cost reduction and capacity rationalisation. I agree that conflict is value-destructive. I do not disagree with that.

What I disagree with is the implication that the most value-destructive thing is closure. In some cases, the most value-destructive thing is **failing to restructure deeply enough** and leaving the company with a permanently impaired cost base.

If the family wants stability, it should back a settlement that genuinely changes the economics. That means accepting that some combination of:
- labour-cost relief,
- capacity reduction,
- and possibly site-level rationalisation

may be required. What it should not do is force management into a compromise that looks orderly but leaves the business weak.

## To institutional investors
You are the closest to the economic truth here, and I agree with your insistence on hard, enforceable savings.

Where I push back is your tendency to treat closure-equivalent measures as if they are just another option to be weighed lightly. They are not lightly chosen. They are required when the structural gap is large enough.

The expert has already said what matters:
- around €600m–€900m, a no-closure package may be possible;
- above that, closure-equivalent capacity withdrawal becomes much more likely.

That is exactly why I am not accepting blanket exclusions now. The threshold is not rhetorical; it is quantitative. So I ask you the same question back:
- What savings level do you require?
- What level of headcount reduction do you consider credible?
- And are you prepared to support a package that avoids closures only if it truly closes the gap?

If yes, we can work with that. If no, then say plainly that you are only comfortable if the company retains the option to rationalise footprint and employment structurally. Because that is where the economics may lead.
