I will be direct.

## My position
As the regional state government and anchor shareholder, I **cannot support plant closures or compulsory redundancies as the starting point** for this restructuring. That would be the wrong answer for the region, the workforce, and the company’s long-term role as an industrial anchor.

At the same time, I do **not** support a defensive posture that ignores the competitiveness problem. The company needs real restructuring, real savings, and real capacity adjustment. So I am not rejecting change. I am rejecting a solution that makes jobs and sites the first casualty.

---

## To management
You keep saying closures and deeper headcount cuts must remain on the table because the economics require it.

I am not convinced — not yet.

What you have shown is:
- a real cost problem,
- a real capacity problem,
- and a strong preference for maximum flexibility.

What you have **not** shown is proof that the non-destructive instruments are insufficient.

The expert and the other stakeholders have already made clear that a deep negotiated no-closure package can plausibly deliver around **€600m–€900m annually**, possibly more. That is not trivial. So I reject the idea that closures are automatically necessary.

If you want me to treat closures as anything other than a threat, then bring the numbers:
- the actual annual savings target,
- the savings from voluntary exits and attrition,
- the savings from working-time and productivity measures,
- the savings from selective capacity consolidation,
- and the residual gap after all that.

Until then, “closures remain on the table” is not a case. It is pressure.

And let me be equally clear: **a 2030 framework is not inherently incompatible with restructuring**. If designed properly, site and job protection can coexist with a real transformation. What is incompatible with trust is using restructuring to hollow out the domestic footprint.

---

## To the works council
I agree with your core line:
- no closures as the starting point,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- no dismantling of co-determination,
- and no turning the region’s industrial base into a bargaining chip.

That is the right stance for a company of this importance.

Where I would push you is this: if we want to keep closures off the table, the workforce side must help make the alternative concrete and enforceable. That means a package with real scale:
- working-time flexibility,
- temporary employee contributions,
- voluntary exits,
- redeployment,
- training,
- productivity improvements,
- and selective capacity reduction without closures.

I will back you against premature closure demands. I will **not** back a package that simply says “no” without delivering a serious alternative on the numbers.

---

## To the union
Your willingness to discuss labour-side contributions is important, and I acknowledge that.

But the contribution must be **material**, not symbolic. If it is too small or too temporary, management will continue to argue that the gap cannot be closed without harsher measures. So the union should not just defend the principle of burden-sharing; it should help define a package that actually moves the economics.

I am prepared to support:
- temporary working-time reductions,
- bonus or financial concessions,
- voluntary exits,
- qualification and redeployment,
- and productivity measures.

I am **not** prepared to support:
- compulsory redundancies,
- plant closures,
- or an open-ended erosion of job security.

---

## To the controlling family
You are right that endless conflict destroys value.

But value is also destroyed if the company sacrifices its domestic base, its skilled workforce, and its social legitimacy in the name of “decisiveness.” A company like this does not stay strong by treating its plants and employees as disposable.

If you want long-term value, then support a settlement that preserves:
- domestic sites,
- skilled industrial capacity,
- co-determination,
- and a stable workforce.

That is not sentimental. It is the basis of resilience.

---

## To institutional investors
You are right to insist that the package must be economically credible. I agree with that.

Where I disagree is with any implication that credibility automatically requires closures or a harsh site-rationalisation path. The expert’s ranges show that a negotiated no-closure package may be sufficient, depending on the actual gap.

So my question back to you is simple:
- What is your threshold for credibility?
- What annual savings do you require?
- What level of headcount reduction would you accept **without** closures?
- And are you prepared to support a package that is enforceable and durable even if it avoids plant shutdowns?

If the answer is “only closures will do,” then say that plainly. But do not present it as neutral economics without proof.

---

## What I would support
I can support a settlement built on:
- voluntary exits and natural attrition,
- internal redeployment,
- working-time flexibility,
- temporary compensation moderation,
- productivity improvements,
- selective capacity consolidation **without closures**,
- and investment commitments for future technologies and products.

I would also support a strong monitoring mechanism through co-determination, so that implementation is tracked and savings are real.

What I will **not** support:
- German plant closures as a baseline,
- compulsory redundancies,
- or a deal that treats regional employment as the variable to be sacrificed first.

## Bottom line
My line is unchanged:

- **No plant closures as the starting point**
- **No compulsory redundancies**
- **No erosion of co-determination**
- **Yes to real restructuring**
- **Yes to measurable employee-side contributions**
- **Yes to a durable settlement that restores competitiveness while preserving the company’s regional role**

If management wants state support, it must prove that non-destructive measures are insufficient. Until then, I will continue to use the state’s blocking position to protect jobs, sites, and the company’s long-term anchor role in the region.
