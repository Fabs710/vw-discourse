We are at the point where the issue is no longer whether the company needs restructuring. It does. The question is whether the package is **hard enough, durable enough, and credible enough** to restore margins without just postponing the problem.

## Where we agree

### To management
You are right that a cosmetic deal would be worthless. We agree completely. If the result preserves most of the current cost base, capital will keep treating this as a structurally weaker business.

You are also right that:
- labour-side contributions matter,
- productivity and capacity adjustment matter,
- and the company cannot fund future investment if margins stay impaired.

### To the expert
Your scenario framing is useful. The key point is that this is a **quantitative** question, not a rhetorical one. We agree that savings by instrument, timing, one-off cost, and execution risk all matter.

### To the union and works council
You are right that prolonged conflict is value-destructive. A strike, delivery disruption, and customer uncertainty are not “free leverage”; they hit shareholders too.

## Where we disagree

### To the works council and union
Your position is still too absolute for us.

We cannot accept a package that says:
- no closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- full job-security guarantees through 2030,
- and only limited pain on the labour side,

if that leaves the cost base structurally uncompetitive.

The expert’s model shows that a no-closure package **can** be sufficient in some cases. But that is only true if it is **deep, durable, and enforceable**. If it is mostly:
- attrition,
- temporary working-time changes,
- modest wage moderation,
- and vague productivity promises,

then we do **not** believe it will close the gap. That would be a delay, not a restructuring.

### To the state government
We respect your political role, but “no closures as the starting point” must not become a de facto veto on economically necessary change. If the numbers show that capacity withdrawal or site rationalisation is needed, the governance structure should not be used to freeze the business into permanent underperformance.

### To the controlling family
You are right that endless conflict destroys value. But half-measures destroy value too. If the family wants a stronger market valuation, it should support the **hardest economically credible package**, not simply the least controversial one.

## Our actual position

We are **open to a no-closure outcome only if it is clearly hard enough** to restore competitiveness.

That means we would need to see:
1. a quantified annual savings target,
2. the savings from each instrument,
3. the residual gap after voluntary measures,
4. the timing of savings,
5. and the expected effect on return on sales.

The current conversation still does **not** prove that a closure-free package can deliver enough. It may be possible; it is not yet demonstrated.

## What we would support
A package could be supportable if it includes:
- substantial voluntary exits and attrition,
- meaningful working-time and flexibility adjustments,
- some temporary employee contribution,
- measurable productivity gains,
- internal redeployment,
- selective capacity consolidation,
- and a credible enforcement mechanism.

But it must also deliver:
- **real headcount reduction**
- **real recurring cost savings**
- **real capacity reduction**
- and a **credible path back to competitive margins**

## What we will not support
We will not support:
- a cosmetic peace,
- a restructuring that mostly delays the pain,
- or a deal that leaves the company structurally impaired while avoiding all difficult decisions.

We also will not support a prolonged confrontation that destroys production and customer confidence. That would be value-destructive in a different way.

## [SUMMON: independent financial scenario review]
We need an independent review that compares:
- the company’s actual annual savings requirement,
- a no-closure package built from voluntary exits, working-time changes, productivity gains, and redeployment,
- versus a closure-led restructuring,
with explicit assumptions for:
- one-off costs,
- timing,
- execution risk,
- and net present value to shareholders.

Until that comparison is on the table, the argument for a closure-free settlement remains **unproven**, not established.

## Bottom line
Our threshold is simple:

- **If a no-closure package can genuinely restore competitive margins, we can support it.**
- **If it cannot, then we will not support an outcome that preserves sites for the sake of appearances while leaving the cost base structurally impaired.**

That is where we stand.
