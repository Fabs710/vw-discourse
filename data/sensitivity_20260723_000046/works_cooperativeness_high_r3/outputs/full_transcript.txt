[Works Council (Betriebsrat) (Plant-level employee representation under co-determination) — Round 1]
We are entering this discussion with one clear principle: **there will be no acceptance of plant closures in Germany and no compulsory redundancies**.

The workforce has already carried a great deal of the burden of transformation. What management is now proposing goes beyond restructuring and into a unilateral dismantling of proven industrial sites, jobs, and trust. That is not an acceptable basis for a deal.

Our priorities are clear:

1. **Keep every German plant open.**
2. **Rule out operational layoffs.**
3. **Secure binding job and site guarantees through 2030.**
4. **Preserve co-determination and the existing job-security framework.**
5. **Use alternative restructuring tools instead** — temporary capacity adjustments, working-time solutions, internal redeployment, training, and a negotiated contribution from labour, but only under fair and binding conditions.

We are not refusing change. We are refusing a restructuring that makes workers pay for management’s strategic mistakes through closures and job destruction. If the company needs savings, then management must first present a plan that protects the industrial footprint, preserves livelihoods, and shares the burden fairly across all stakeholders.

If the proposal includes closures, forced layoffs, or the breaking of the historic employment protections, then we will oppose it clearly and decisively.

[Industrial Union (Trade union and collective-bargaining counterpart) — Round 1]
We share the core line already stated by the works council: **no plant closures, no compulsory redundancies, and no destruction of the collective wage framework**.

Management’s current approach is not a restructuring in the collective-bargaining sense; it is a demand that the workforce absorb the main pain while ownership and management seek to preserve strategic room for themselves. That is not acceptable.

Let me be very clear on what we can and cannot support:

- **We can support** a negotiated contribution from labour.
- **We can support** temporary capacity reductions.
- **We can support** working-time adjustments, redeployment, training, and productivity measures.
- **We can support** forgoing bonuses or other negotiated financial contributions if they are part of a balanced package.
- **We can support** a durable settlement that secures the sites and gives the company breathing room.

But we **cannot** support:
- plant closures in Germany,
- compulsory layoffs,
- open-ended insecurity,
- or a one-sided cost-cutting package that shifts the burden almost entirely onto employees.

If management genuinely wants a solution, then it must put something credible on the table in return:
1. **Binding site guarantees**
2. **Job security guarantees**
3. **A fair sharing of sacrifice**
4. **Concrete investment commitments for future products and the transition**

The company’s competitiveness problem is real. But competitiveness will not be restored by breaking trust, gutting the workforce, and turning industrial relations into a zero-sum exercise. That path would damage morale, productivity, and the long-term viability of the brand.

So our position is straightforward: **we are prepared to negotiate hard on labour costs and capacity, but only within a framework that protects jobs, protects sites, and preserves the collective bargaining standards that define fair industrial relations.** If that framework is rejected, then the conflict will escalate.

- no agreement without a **real reduction in cost and capacity**,
- no agreement without a **meaningful employee contribution**,
- no agreement without a package that can actually lift margins and fund investment.

If the workforce side wants to avoid closures, then it must come forward with an alternative that delivers the same structural savings. If it cannot, then closures remain on the table.

## Short answer
**In principle, yes — a meaningful part of the required savings can often be achieved without plant closures or compulsory redundancies in a large German carmaker.**

## 1) What can realistically be achieved without closures or compulsory redundancies?

### A. Natural attrition and hiring freezes
This is often the lowest-friction lever in German manufacturing.

Typical effect:
- avoids replacement of retirees and voluntary leavers,
- reduces headcount gradually,
- preserves formal employment continuity.

Limits:
- works slowly,
- savings are delayed,
- does not quickly fix overcapacity at a plant level.

**Assessment:** useful, but rarely sufficient alone.

---

### B. Voluntary exit programs
These can be substantial in large German employers, especially if supported by attractive severance and pension-related arrangements.

Typical effect:
- accelerates workforce reduction,
- can be targeted to older cohorts or non-core functions,
- politically easier than involuntary redundancies.

Limits:
- expensive upfront,
- may remove skilled workers the company still needs,
- effectiveness depends on acceptability and scale.

**Assessment:** can generate meaningful savings, but the cost of the exit program itself must be netted against wage-bill savings.

---

### C. Working-time reductions and flexibility measures
Examples:
- reduced weekly hours,
- temporary short-time arrangements,
- shift adjustments,
- lower overtime,
- temporary plant underutilization.

Typical effect:
- lowers labour cost per period,
- can align output with lower demand,
- avoids immediate job cuts.

Limits:
- if output falls too much, unit costs can rise,
- helps mainly in cyclical or short-run stress,
- may preserve too much fixed capacity if demand remains structurally lower.

**Assessment:** very useful for bridging, less powerful as a long-term structural fix.

---

### D. Wage moderation / temporary concessions
Examples:
- bonus deferral,
- reduced variable pay,
- temporary allowance cuts,
- lower agreed wage growth,
- one-off employee contributions.

Typical effect:
- lowers cash labour cost,
- can be negotiated at group or site level,
- helps bridge a specific restructuring period.

Limits:
- usually time-limited,
- affects morale and bargaining climate,
- cannot usually offset a large overcapacity problem by itself.

**Assessment:** can contribute, but the savings magnitude is often modest relative to total restructuring needs.

---

### E. Redeployment and internal mobility
Examples:
- moving workers from declining functions to growing ones,
- retraining for EV, battery, software, logistics, quality, or maintenance roles,
- shifting production between plants.

Typical effect:
- preserves employment,
- avoids dismissals,
- helps reallocate labour where demand exists.

Limits:
- only works if there are real internal vacancies or expansion areas,
- training takes time,
- does not solve excess capacity if the receiving units are already full.

**Assessment:** important, but it redistributes labour rather than eliminating excess labour cost.

---

### F. Productivity and process improvements
Examples:
- line balancing,
- automation,
- scrap reduction,
- logistics improvements,
- fewer indirect labour hours,
- tighter shift planning.

Typical effect:
- improves output per worker,
- can generate recurring savings,
- often politically easier than headline cuts.

Limits:
- implementation risk,
- savings are uncertain and may take time,
- rarely enough to offset a major structural cost gap alone.

**Assessment:** necessary in almost any credible package, but not a substitute for capacity adjustment if the gap is large.

---

## 2) What plant closures and compulsory redundancies do that other tools often do not

If a company has **permanent structural overcapacity**, closures and involuntary headcount reduction have three advantages:

1. **Fast removal of fixed costs**
   - plant overhead,
   - maintenance,
   - utilities,
   - logistics duplication,
   - supervisory and indirect labour.

2. **Clearer capacity matching**
   - output is concentrated in fewer sites,
   - utilisation rises at remaining plants,
   - unit costs usually improve more decisively.

3. **More certainty for investors and lenders**
   - the cost base is visibly reduced,
   - the savings are easier to verify,
   - the restructuring is less dependent on behavioural assumptions.

That said, these instruments also create:
- severance and shutdown costs,
- political and industrial conflict,
- implementation delay,
- reputational damage,
- risk of production disruption.

So closures are economically powerful, but they are not “free efficiency.” They are a costly way of forcing adjustment.

---

## 3) The critical question: how big is the structural gap?

This is the real issue. To judge whether non-closure measures are enough, one would need at least:

- current domestic capacity by plant,
- utilisation rates,
- fixed and variable labour costs,
- overhead allocation,
- expected demand by model and powertrain,
- benchmark productivity versus peer plants,
- cash cost of voluntary exit and retraining,
- savings from wage measures,
- timeline to realization.

Without that data, no one can honestly say whether the residual gap is:
- **small enough** to close through negotiated labour-side measures, or
- **large enough** that a plant closure becomes economically hard to avoid.

In practice, if the company’s German cost base is materially above peer benchmarks and the market is shrinking, then:
- **some combination of headcount reduction, wage moderation, and capacity consolidation is usually required**
- and if voluntary measures do not reach the needed scale, management will argue for closures or compulsory reductions.

## 4) German institutional context matters

In Germany, several institutional features shape what is feasible:

- **co-determination** makes unilateral management action difficult,
- **works councils** influence implementation at plant level,
- **collective bargaining** constrains wage and working-time changes,
- **protection against dismissal** makes forced cuts slower and more contentious,
- **social plans** and severance obligations increase the cost of layoffs,
- **political shareholders** can raise the hurdle for closures.

So compared with a less regulated setting, German restructuring often relies more heavily on:
- negotiated exits,
- internal redeployment,
- working-time instruments,
- phased reductions,
- and time-limited concessions.

This makes a no-closure settlement more plausible than outsiders sometimes assume. But it also means that if a very large structural correction is needed, the process can become slow and expensive unless a negotiated deal covers a lot of ground.

## What is established
- In large German automotive groups, **labour-side measures can produce substantial recurring savings** if they are broad and sustained:

- A **deep no-closure package** is most plausible when:
  - the required savings gap is moderate rather than extreme,
  - the company can combine multiple instruments,

  - faster and more certain fixed-cost reduction,
  - but higher industrial-relations conflict,
  - greater one-off restructuring costs,
  - higher legal and operational risk,
  - and potentially larger production-disruption losses.

  1. annual recurring savings,

- lower one-off cost

Cons:
