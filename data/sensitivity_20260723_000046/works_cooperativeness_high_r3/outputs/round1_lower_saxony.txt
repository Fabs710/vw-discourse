We share the diagnosis that the company cannot simply preserve the status quo. The cost and capacity problem is real, and the state government is not blind to the competitive pressure from shrinking European demand, Chinese competition, and the need to finance future investment.

But I need to be equally clear: **the answer cannot be plant closures in Germany, nor a restructuring that permanently weakens regional employment and industrial capability.**

### Where I agree
- I agree that **a durable solution** is required, not cosmetic savings.
- I agree that **labour must contribute** in a fair and negotiated way.
- I agree that **working-time measures, redeployment, training, and temporary capacity adjustments** belong on the table.
- I agree that the company must regain competitiveness if it is to remain an anchor of the regional economy.

### Where I disagree
I do **not** accept the framing that closures are simply “necessary if the numbers require it.” That is too one-sided and too destructive. This company is not a purely financial object; it is a major industrial institution with obligations to employees, regions, and the long-term stability of the German automotive base.

A package that relies on:
- plant closures,
- compulsory redundancies,
- or the stripping away of hard-won employment protections

would be unacceptable from the state’s perspective. It would damage the regional economy, undermine trust in social partnership, and create political and industrial conflict that would itself weaken the company.

### What is required instead
If management wants the state to support a restructuring, then it must bring forward a package that meets three conditions:

1. **Sites and jobs are preserved as far as possible**
   - no closure of German plants as the starting point;
   - no compulsory redundancies;
   - maximal use of natural attrition, internal redeployment, and voluntary instruments.

2. **The burden is shared fairly**
   - labour can contribute through negotiated flexibility and cost moderation;
   - management and ownership must also accept responsibility;
   - investment priorities and capital allocation must be aligned with the restructuring, not treated as separate.

3. **There is a credible long-term industrial strategy**
   - binding commitments for future products and technologies;
   - a clear plan for German production as part of the transition;
   - protection of co-determination and the existing governance balance.

### On management’s position
I must say plainly that “closures remain on the table” is not a constructive negotiating posture if the goal is a stable agreement. It may be a bargaining threat, but it cannot be the basis for a durable settlement in a company of this importance.

If management insists on making plant closures the price of any agreement, then it will not find the state government supportive. We have a blocking position and we will use it if necessary. We are not prepared to be bypassed, and we are not prepared to sign off on a strategy that hollows out the company’s domestic footprint.

### On the union and works council line
The workforce representatives are right to insist that restructuring must not become a disguised mass dismissal program. Their demand for site and job protection is legitimate. At the same time, I would encourage them to stay open to serious operational flexibility if it preserves sites and strengthens the company’s long-term position.

### Bottom line
The state government is prepared to support a negotiated restructuring, but only one that:
- **avoids closures as far as possible,**
- **rules out forced redundancies,**
- **preserves co-determination,**
- **and secures the company’s role as a regional economic anchor.**

If management cannot offer that, then it should not expect approval, political backing, or a smooth passage through the company’s governance structures.
